\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A grower who needs the number of ripe fruit on a block wants one record for each physical fruit, with its class. A camera does not supply that record. It supplies images, and in most field protocols the same fruit appears in several of them: in consecutive video frames, in photographs taken from both sides of a row, or in images taken while walking around a single tree. Detection finds fruit in each image. It does not say whether a fruit found in one image is the fruit found in another. The step that makes that decision determines whether a sum of detections is an inventory or an overcount.

The size of the problem is known from studies that measured it directly. In a mango orchard, one image of each side of 16 trees counted 54\% of the fruit that were hand-counted, and one image of one side counted 27\%; tracking every fruit through a multi-view sequence and triangulating it counted 101.4\% \citep{stein2016image}. On 21 mango trees, video tracking recorded 62\% of the harvest count and two images per tree recorded 40\% \citep{wang2019mango}. On a 192-fruit video, the same tracker produced 9.9\% double counts and 7.3\% missed fruit, which netted to a 2.6\% overcount \citep{wang2019mango}. The last figure shows why a total count can look correct when the underlying identity decisions are not: a duplicate and a miss cancel.

In oil palm the problem takes a specific form. Plantations estimate output with a bunch census in which staff count and classify bunches on each palm by the number of months until harvest. The SawitMVC dataset photographed 953 palms from four or eight sides and annotated 9{,}823 unique bunches in four census classes, with links that record which boxes in different images show the same bunch \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. A census built from such photographs needs both the identity decision and a class label for each unique bunch.

From the perspective of counting unique fruit, earlier reviews leave three things open. First, they organize the literature by estimation route or methodological approach \citep{anderson2021technologies,barriguinha2021vineyard,magro2023computational,rong2026research}, by network architecture or task \citep{maheswari2021intelligent,mohimont2022computer}, or, for oil palm, by task, sensing modality, or processing stage \citep{khan2021oil,lai2023oil,kamal2024machine}; none takes the identity of a fruit across observations as the organizing variable, although several discuss occlusion and extrapolation from partial counts. Second, reviews of tracking, re-identification, and 3D reconstruction outside agriculture provide the mechanisms but not the agricultural evidence. Third, three of the nine closest reviews do not state how their studies were found, one of them although it describes itself as systematic, and a fourth describes its literature base only by keyword counts (Table~\ref{tab:position}). As a result, a reader cannot tell which association mechanism fits a given acquisition protocol, or what evidence supports it.

To address these issues, this article presents a systematic mapping review of image-based fruit counting published between 2012 and 2026. Reporting follows PRISMA~2020 where its items apply to a mapping review. Scopus was the single database, searched on 28 September 2026, and \nIncluded studies are included, of which \nMulti combine several observations of the same fruit.

This review makes three contributions. First, it provides a systematic map of the \nIncluded studies, with the search strings reported in full and the screening decisions and codes provided as supplementary material. Second, it defines a design space that separates acquisition, observation, association, attribute assignment, and inventory, and classifies association into six mechanisms (no association, statistical correction, appearance matching, temporal tracking, geometric or 3D association, and learned association), each tied to the conditions it assumes. Third, it synthesizes the \nMulti multi-observation studies by mechanism, with the comparisons that were made on the same data reported in two tables, followed by the evidence on class attributes, depth, and oil palm.

Accordingly, the review addresses four questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
\item Which mechanisms have been used to decide that observations in different images belong to the same fruit, and what acquisition conditions does each assume?
\item What evidence shows how well each mechanism controls duplicates and misses, and how was that evidence measured?
\item How are class attributes, mainly maturity, attached to a unique fruit rather than to each detection?
\item What does the oil-palm imaging literature cover, and what is missing for a bunch-level, class-wise census from images?
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the search, screening, and coding. Section~\ref{sec:framework} sets out the design space and reports how the multi-observation studies are distributed over it. Sections~\ref{sec:acq} to~\ref{sec:class} follow its stages: acquisition (Section~\ref{sec:acq}), association, mechanism by mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:mech}), and class attributes on unique fruit (Section~\ref{sec:class}). Section~\ref{sec:depth} examines depth and other sensing modalities, and Section~\ref{sec:palm} the oil-palm case. Section~\ref{sec:agenda} answers the four questions, places the review against earlier reviews (Section~\ref{sec:position}), and draws the gaps and a measurement agenda; Section~\ref{sec:limits} states the limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Review method}\label{sec:method}

The review is a systematic mapping review with a configurative synthesis \citep{petersen2015mapping,grant2009typology}: it builds a classification from a broad body of work and does not pool effect sizes, because the included studies use different crops, references, and metrics. Reporting follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA~2020) where its items apply to a mapping review \citep{page2021prisma,page2021elaboration}. Procedural details that a reader needs only to audit the map are given in the Supplementary Material.

Five sets target agricultural fruit perception, two retrieve reviews, and two retrieve methods papers from outside agriculture, of which only the 25 most cited records of each search were retained, because their role is to supply transferable mechanisms, not to map those fields. \subsection{Inclusion and exclusion criteria}\label{sec:criteria}
Records published from 2012 to 2026 were included if they met at least one of the following: (i) an image-based method for fruit on the plant, or on the ground under the plant for oil-palm loose fruit, that evaluated counting, yield estimation, detection with a class attribute, localization, or sizing; (ii) a review of these topics; or (iii) a methods paper outside agriculture on tracking, re-identification, multi-view association, 3D reconstruction, or object counting, or a general detection, segmentation, or depth-estimation model on which such pipelines are built. Records were excluded if: (i) the object was not fruit on the plant (for example trees, leaves, or flowers only); (ii) imaging was postharvest or laboratory-only, except oil-palm mill grading, which is part of the case; (iii) yield was modelled without fruit-level detection; (iv) the work addressed only picking or manipulation; (v) fruit were detected without counting or any evaluation beyond detection; (vi) the sensor was not an imaging sensor; or (vii) the record had no English title and abstract.

\subsection{Study selection}\label{sec:screening}
Records passed through three stages: title screening, eligibility assessment on the title and abstract, and eligibility assessment on the full text of the reports that could be retrieved, with the reason for every exclusion after title screening recorded (Fig.~\ref{fig:prisma}). Screening was carried out by one reviewer assisted by a large language model, which recorded a decision, a group, and the supporting passage for each record; multi-observation and oil-palm studies and all exclusions were checked in a second pass blind to the first, and the reviewer approved the decisions. On random samples screened by the first author without the model's decisions, agreement was \pHumTitles\% for titles ($\kappa$ = \kHumTitles) and \pHumElig\% for eligibility codes ($\kappa$ = \kHumElig) (Supplementary Section~S1).

The searches returned \nRetrieved records, of which \nUnique were unique and \nTitles reached title screening. Of the \nAssessed records assessed on the title and abstract, \nEligExcl were excluded, most often because fruit were detected without counting or other evaluation (criterion v, \nExclFive records). \nOther further reports were identified outside the search, mainly from the reference lists of the closest reviews. Of the \nSought reports sought in full text, \nNotRetrieved could not be retrieved, and of the \nFTAssessed assessed in full, \nFTExcl were excluded, leaving \nIncluded studies (Fig.~\ref{fig:prisma}). Records grew from \nYearFirst in 2012 to \nYearPeak in 2025, and multi-observation studies from at most \nMultiEarlyMax per year up to 2019 to \nMultiPeak in 2025 (Fig.~\ref{fig:tahun}).

Each multi-observation study was coded from the full text for (i) association mechanism, one or more of the six defined in Section~\ref{sec:mechdefs}; (ii) acquisition design; (iii) whether counts are reported per class and, if so, how the class is attached to a unique fruit; (iv) the main result; (v) the count reference; and (vi) the level of the reported metric (count, identity, or detection). The rule that matters most for reading the results is that a study using several mechanisms was assigned each of them, so mechanism totals are occurrences, not exclusive counts; a tracker with an appearance descriptor, such as DeepSORT, counts as appearance matching combined with temporal tracking. The full coding rules are given in Supplementary Section~S2, and the codes of all \nMulti studies in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}. Oil-palm studies were coded for task, setting, modality, use of several views, and whether class labels are attached to instances.

\subsection{Appraisal and synthesis}
No formal quality score was assigned. For every comparison reported, the review records three properties that decide what a number can support: the count reference (hand count on the tree, harvest, packhouse, or annotated images), whether the compared methods were run on the same trees or data, and whether the result is a total count, a per-tree count, or an identity metric. The synthesis is a structured narrative organized by the issues on which studies agree or disagree within each stage of the design space (Section~\ref{sec:framework}); comparisons made on the same data are collected in Tables~\ref{tab:acq} and~\ref{tab:assoc}, and descriptive statistics of the coded variables are reported once, in Section~\ref{sec:landscape}.

\section{A design space for turning observations into records}\label{sec:framework}

This section (i) describes the five stages that lead from images to a class-wise inventory, (ii) names the ways in which a fruit is counted twice, (iii) defines the six association mechanisms by the condition each assumes, and (iv) reports how the \nMulti multi-observation studies are distributed over mechanisms, acquisition designs, crops, platforms, and sensors.

\subsection{From images to an inventory}
Fig.~\ref{fig:kerangka} shows the five stages of the pipeline that ends in a class-wise inventory. Acquisition fixes how many views of each plant exist, whether they are ordered in time, and whether pose, depth, or baseline are known. Observation produces detections or masks in each image; a fruit may produce several observations, and a detector may also split one fruit or merge two. Association decides which observations belong to the same physical fruit. Attribute assignment gives the unique fruit a class, size, or mass. The inventory aggregates unique fruit by class and by tree, row, or block. Detection accuracy, which most papers report, describes the second stage. The count, which the user needs, depends on all five.

\subsection{Three sources of double counting}\label{sec:dupsources}
\citet{liu2019monocular} named three ways in which a fruit is counted twice in image sequences of a row: the same fruit detected in consecutive images, the same fruit tracked, lost, and picked up again as a new track, and the same fruit seen from the two sides of a tree. \citet{wang2019mango} took the same three sources as the starting point for their mango tracker. Misses have two sources: fruit never visible from any camera position, and fruit visible but not detected. A mechanism controls some of these sources and not others, which is why the mechanisms below are described by what they assume, not only by how they compute.

\subsection{Six association mechanisms and the conditions they assume}\label{sec:mechdefs}
\textit{No association.} Each image is counted separately. This is correct only if acquisition guarantees that no fruit appears twice, or if duplicates are accepted.

\textit{Statistical correction.} Image counts are summed or averaged and then related to hand or harvest counts by a fitted factor or regression. No individual fruit is identified. It assumes that the ratio between observed and true counts is stable across the trees to which it is applied \citep{nuske2014modeling,linker2015estimation}.

\textit{Appearance matching.} Observations are linked when their appearance descriptors are similar, either hand-crafted or learned for re-identification. It assumes that fruit differ enough in appearance to be told apart.

\textit{Temporal tracking.} Observations in consecutive frames are linked by predicted motion and overlap, usually with a Kalman filter and an assignment step \citep{bewley2016simple}; a counting line or region then decides when a track is counted. It assumes that consecutive frames overlap and that motion between them is small and smooth.

\textit{Geometric or 3D association.} Observations are linked through a common coordinate frame: triangulation with known camera poses, structure from motion (SfM), simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), registered point clouds, or an implicit 3D representation such as a neural radiance field. It assumes that poses, scale, and depth or reconstruction are accurate enough that two different fruit are not placed at the same position.

\textit{Learned association.} A network is trained to decide correspondence directly from linked examples. It assumes that training data with identity links exist and represent the conditions of use.

A study may combine mechanisms; \nCombined of the \nMethod multi-observation method studies do (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}). In tables and figures a plus sign joins the mechanisms that a study combines, for example appearance + tracking. A study that combines them should still show which one removes the duplicates, because each fails under different conditions.

\subsection{Quantitative landscape of the multi-observation studies}\label{sec:landscape}
Of the \nMulti multi-observation studies, \nMultiData are dataset papers and \nMethod propose or evaluate a method; the statistics below refer to these \nMethod, and their crops, platforms, and sensors are given in Supplementary Section~S4. Because a study may combine mechanisms, mechanism values are occurrences.

\textit{(i) The mechanism mix has shifted from correcting sums to linking detections in video.} Temporal tracking appears in \nTrack of the \nMethod studies (\pTrack\%), geometric association in \nGeom (\pGeom\%), appearance matching in \nApp (\pApp\%), statistical correction in \nStat (\pStat\%), learned association in \nLearn, and no association in \nNone (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}a). Of the \nEarly studies published before 2018, \nEarlyGeom used geometric association, \nEarlyStat statistical correction, and \nEarlyTrack temporal tracking; since 2023, temporal tracking has appeared in \pTrackRecent\% of the \nRecent new studies, and appearance matching, mostly inside trackers that carry an appearance descriptor, in \pAppRecent\%.

\textit{(ii) Acquisition is mostly video.} \nVideo studies acquire video along a path, \nDiscrete use discrete views, \nScan use 3D scans, and \nRevisit revisit the same plants over time (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}b). Acquisition and mechanism are coupled (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}): video feeds temporal tracking alone (\nTrackOnly studies), appearance matching combined with tracking (\nAppTrack), and tracking combined with geometric association (\nTrackGeom), whereas discrete views lead mainly to geometric association (\nDiscreteGeom of the \nDiscrete) and statistical correction (\nDiscreteStat).

\textit{(iii) Classes are rarely counted on unique fruit.} Only \nPerClass of the \nMethod studies report counts per class (Section~\ref{sec:class}).

\textit{(1) Single images per side see half of the fruit or less, and adding sides raises recall at the cost of new errors.} \citet{stein2016image} counted 27\% of the hand-counted mangoes from one side and 54\% from two sides, and \citet{wang2019mango} counted 40\% of the harvest with one image per side. \citet{gongal2016apple} reported that about 40\% of apples were invisible from one side of a tall-spindle row; dual-side imaging reduced the count error from 41--42\% to 18\%. The added side brings its own errors. When \citet{qureshi2023seeing} merged one-sided scans of apple fruitlets into a two-sided map, counting accuracy rose from 73.7\% to 81.17\% but precision fell from 0.907 to 0.803, because misalignment of the two scans created fruitlets that did not exist. \citet{gongal2016apple} found the two registered instances of one apple slightly displaced and neighbouring apples overlapping in 3D, which forced a distance buffer. Apples wrongly taken as duplicates added to the undercount caused by missed apples, and false-positive detections and missed duplicates, which both add to the count, offset that undercount slightly. A second side therefore converts a visibility problem into an identity problem.

\textit{(2) A calibrated count can be precise without removing duplicates.} In the study of \citet{stein2016image}, the dual-view counts correlated with the harvest counts more tightly ($R^2=0.94$) than the multi-view counts ($R^2=0.90$), but the dual-view counts had to be divided by a slope of 0.54, whereas the multi-view slope was 1.014. The authors stated that the stability of the dual-view calibration across blocks, orchards, varieties, and pruning regimes was not known. Where calibration was tested across orchards and seasons, it did not hold. \citet{anderson2021estimation} found that correcting multi-view counts with hand counts of sampled trees did not improve the estimate against packhouse counts in 16 of 20 mango orchards, because the occlusion factor of the calibration trees did not represent the orchard. \citet{koirala2021attempting} found no canopy feature that predicted the number of hidden fruit, and models trained on one season did not transfer to the next. Correlation with a reference therefore shows that a count is repeatable, not that duplicates were removed.

\textit{(3) More views help only when the extra observations are linked.} \citet{vijayakumar2023tree} found that adding the count from the second side of citrus trees to a yield model did not reduce the per-tree error (mean absolute percentage error 25.72\% against 23.45\% with one side; $p=0.29$) and named double counting between the two sides as a cause. \citet{felippomes2026video} counted apples from both sides of 420 trees with two RGB-D sensors and a tracker, but processed the two sides independently, without matching fruit between them. Using both sides lowered the error per 5~m stretch (least-squares mean RMSE 46.73 apples against 58.69 and 59.36 for the single sides), but the counts reached yield through a fitted correction, so the duplicates between the sides were absorbed by the calibration rather than identified. \citet{arizasentis2025comparative} recovered more grape bunches with multiple-view UAV flights (27--74\% of the annotated bunches against 13--23\%) but reported that motion blur broke identity continuity in those flights. Across these studies, views add recall, and the gain in the count depends on whether the observations from different views are associated.

\textit{(4) Acquisition decides which mechanisms are possible.} Video along a row gives the frame-to-frame overlap that temporal tracking needs. Discrete views around a tree, as in SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc} or the eight direction frames that \citet{nenavath2024tree} sampled from a walk-around video of a mango tree, have wide baselines and no frame-to-frame continuity, so tracking does not apply and association must rely on appearance, geometry, or learning. Several groups have treated view selection itself as the problem \citep{roy2017active,zaenker2021viewpoint,zaenker2023graph}; where they checked fruit estimates against a reference, they did so in simulation or on artificial trees.

Taken together, the comparisons show that the choice of acquisition is a choice of which errors the association step must remove: one view per side leaves fruit unseen, and every added view creates duplicates that only an association mechanism, not a calibration, can identify (Section~\ref{sec:mech}).

\subsection{No association and statistical correction}\label{sec:mech-stat}
Statistical correction replaces identity with a fitted factor: it never decides which fruit was counted twice, so it is accurate only while the ratio between observed and true counts stays as it was when the factor was fitted. It is used in \nStat of the \nMethod method studies; no association occurs in \nNone, and only alongside another mechanism.

The earliest multi-image systems calibrated their counts against harvest or hand counts. \citet{nuske2014modeling} registered images from a vehicle driving along vineyard rows and related berry counts to harvest yield; with calibration data from a prior harvest the estimate of total yield was within 4\%, and within 3\% with destructive samples taken at the time of imaging. \citet{linker2015estimation} photographed 42 apple trees from two sides at three heights; the detections in the six images of a tree corresponded to about one third of its yield, and after calibration on 14 trees the overall estimate for the 42 trees was within 10\% of the actual yield, although single-tree errors were sometimes large. The authors described the result as a self-calibrating system that would need the grower to count 15--20 trees. \citet{song2014automatic} mapped repeated observations of pepper fruit to common coordinates and grouped them with a statistical clustering step. Regression from partial views remains common in UAV work \citep{apoloapolo2020cloud,li2023blueberry}.

Within the conditions of calibration, the method is repeatable: \citet{stein2016image} found the dual-view counts more tightly correlated with true counts than any other design. Its weakness is transfer. \citet{anderson2021estimation} found that correcting multi-view counts with an occlusion factor from hand-counted trees did not improve the estimate in 16 of 20 mango orchards and increased the average error from 11\% to 17\% in the nine orchards with all methods; the error on trellised canopies (25.2\%) was several times that on other canopy systems (3.4--8.2\%). \citet{koirala2021attempting} tried to replace the factor with a model of hidden fruit learned from canopy features; the model reached $R^2=0.98$ against the harvest counts of 98 trees in its training season, but no visual feature correlated with the number of hidden fruit, and the models were not robust on the following season. Some studies limit duplicates by acquisition instead: \citet{nenavath2024tree} subtracted the fruit at the corners of eight direction frames, which appear in two neighbouring frames, and \citet{villacres2024assessing} projected fruit between the cameras of a calibrated rig.

Collectively, these studies show that statistical correction gives repeatable estimates within one orchard and season, and that the factor fails when the canopy, season, or orchard changes, because the share of hidden and duplicated fruit changes with them. It is the baseline against which the other mechanisms should be tested (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).

\subsection{Temporal tracking}\label{sec:mech-track}
Temporal tracking removes duplicates between consecutive frames as long as its motion model holds, and its reported failures are the conditions that break that model. It is the most common mechanism (\nTrack of the \nMethod method studies, and \pTrackRecent\% of those published since 2023; Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}a). Most studies follow tracking by detection: a detector finds fruit in each frame, a Kalman filter predicts where each track should appear next, and an assignment step links detections to tracks \citep{bewley2016simple,zhang2022bytetrack}. Four issues recur.

\textit{(1) The motion model and the frame rate.} Fruit on a plant are static, so all apparent motion comes from the camera \citep{zhang2022deep,saraceni2024agrisort}. \citet{saraceni2024agrisort} built AgriSORT on this premise, estimated camera motion from optical flow, and dropped the velocity terms of the usual filter because box motion on rough vineyard terrain is not linear; SORT lost almost all identities in their sequence with fast non-linear motion. The frame rate decides how often the filter is corrected. \citet{wei2025multi} reported that at 5 frames per second the random initialization of the filter degraded the tracks unless initial velocities were tuned for each dataset, and \citet{qi2025assessment} found that tracking accuracy on tomato video fell as the frame rate was reduced. Camera shake on a legged robot \citep{zhang2024stablesort} and large displacements in low-frame-rate UAV video \citep{xieli2025pinesort} required explicit camera-motion compensation.

\textit{(2) Motion alone versus motion with appearance.} In the same-data comparisons, trackers that add a learned appearance descriptor did not outperform trackers that use motion alone. On citrus video the counting error of DeepSORT was higher than that of SORT (MAE 1.212 against 0.45) \citep{zhang2022deep}. On apples the three trackers reached similar higher-order tracking accuracy (HOTA 0.680--0.689), but DeepSORT needed 128~ms per image against about 15~ms, and the authors attributed the lack of gain to the similar appearance of apples \citep{genemola2023video}. On tomato clusters, trackers with a re-identification module produced the highest multiple-object tracking accuracy at a constant frame rate (BotSort, MOTA 84.6\%), but ByteTrack, without one, gave the highest counting accuracy (95.5\%), and the re-identification trackers degraded more when the frame rate varied \citep{qi2025assessment}. The same issue appears outside agriculture: DanceTrack was built because targets with uniform appearance and diverse motion defeat trackers that rely on appearance \citep{sun2022dancetrack}. Appearance therefore helps identity metrics under steady sampling, not counts of look-alike fruit.

\textit{(3) The counting rule.} A counting rule decides when a track becomes a fruit. Counting every track identity inflates the count with every identity switch, which is why \citet{qi2025assessment} counted in a fixed region rather than taking the maximum identity number. \citet{zhang2022deep} counted citrus only within a counting region, and this variant gave the lowest error in Table~\ref{tab:assoc}, with about half the errors of the same tracker counting over the whole frame. \citet{parico2021real} found the unique-identity rule more reliable than a counting line for pears, because flickering detections made the line miss fruit.

\textit{(4) What tracking does not resolve.} A track survives only for a fixed time without detections: \citet{wang2019mango} kept a lost mango track for 15 frames, and a fruit hidden for longer was counted again, which produced 9.9\% repeated counts that offset 7.3\% misses. Fruit seen from both sides of a row appear in two separate passes, and no tracker links them. \citet{wang2019mango} expected this source to grow in narrower canopies; \citet{liu2019monocular} dropped fruit lying behind the trunk centroid, which removed far-side duplicates but also undercounted fruit visible from one side only; and \citet{wei2025multi} matched the two sides of each apple tree afterwards by GPS position with a distance threshold of one fruit diameter.

Taken together, tracking is the tested default for video because static fruit and a moving camera fit its motion model, but its count depends on the frame rate, on the counting rule, and on a separate geometric step for the second side of the row (Section~\ref{sec:mech-geom}).

\subsection{Appearance matching}\label{sec:mech-app}
Appearance has not been shown to identify look-alike fruit on its own; it contributes when combined with motion or position, and it is weakest where a tracker needs it most, under occlusion and frame drops. Appearance matching occurs in \nApp of the \nMethod method studies, \nAppTrack of them inside trackers that carry an appearance descriptor. \citet{kirk2021robust} trained the appearance network of a DeepSORT-based strawberry tracker with a ripeness branch and reported a count error of 3\% against 21\% for a baseline tracker; they also noted that occlusion can change the apparent ripeness of a fruit, for example when the red part is covered, and therefore pooled the class over all previous observations of a track. \citet{xia2022culling} matched pairs of fruit patches between sequence images with a trained matching network and a one-to-one assignment, reaching F1 0.816 for apples and 0.864 for oranges in identifying repeated fruit; the matching used greyscale patches, compared all pairs by brute force, and covered one side of the row. \citet{zheng2023object} combined appearance and position in a learned distance to cluster strawberry detections from overlapping UAV images. In every case appearance was paired with position or motion, which agrees with the tracker comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:mech-track} and with person re-identification, where discriminative features are learned from benchmarks with many labelled identities \citep{ye2022deep}.

\subsection{Geometric or 3D association}\label{sec:mech-geom}
Geometric association is the only mechanism that has removed all three sources of double counting, including the second side of a row, and it has done so without a fitted factor; its failures come from the pose, scale, and depth on which it rests. It is used in \nGeom of the \nMethod method studies (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}). Four routes recur, followed by the conditions under which they fail.

\textit{(1) Triangulation with poses from navigation sensors or stereo.} \citet{wang2013automated} registered sequential stereo images from an autonomous vehicle at night; the yield error was $-3.2$\% for about 480 red-apple trees and, after calibration with a small hand-measured sample, 1.2\% for about 670 green-apple trees. \citet{stein2016image} matched mango detections between images along epipolar lines computed from a navigation system, triangulated each tracked fruit, and used LiDAR to assign it to its tree; the multi-view count of 16 validation trees was within 1.4\% of the harvest count without per-tree calibration.

\textit{(2) Structure from motion from a single camera.} \citet{liu2018robust} tracked fruit in 2D, localized the tracks in 3D by structure from motion, and rejected double-counted tracks by their proximity in 3D: tracks that are hard to tell apart in the image plane, because they come from different parts of the sequence, lie close together in 3D. The correction reduced the L1 count error from 593 to 203 for oranges and from 673 to 322 for apples. Their later version added trunk landmarks to separate the two sides of the tree, and found that clustered fruit and the relative scale of the reconstruction prevented a fixed distance threshold from merging duplicate landmarks \citep{liu2019monocular}.

\textit{(3) Merging the two sides of a row.} \citet{roy2019vision} merged counts from both sides of apple rows into one reconstruction, and \citet{hani2020comparative} compared that merge with a plain sum of the two sides on the same three datasets: the sum gave 103.86--147.81\% of the harvested count, and the merge 95.56--97.83\% (Table~\ref{tab:assoc}). \citet{gongal2016apple} matched apples between the two sides of a tall-spindle row by their 3D positions within a buffer of three quarters of a fruit diameter and reported an error of 21.1\% in identifying the duplicates. \citet{qureshi2023seeing} aligned the two sides of a trellis with a calibration board; misalignment added fruitlets that did not exist (Section~\ref{sec:acq}).

\textit{(4) Counting in a 3D map.} \citet{genemola2020fruitb} projected Mask R-CNN detections into an SfM point cloud of 11 apple trees and improved F1 from 0.816 to 0.881, because a fruit had to be seen in at least two images to be reconstructed, which removed single-image false positives. \citet{rapadorincon2023development} tracked partial point clouds of tomatoes in a 3D world model and reached a best count error of 5.06\%. Similar count errors hid different tracking quality: two configurations with a mean absolute percentage error of 11.35\% and 11.36\% reached a higher-order tracking accuracy (HOTA) of 66.68\% and 56.09\%, which the authors attributed to the larger number of frames and tracks at the later height step, and under- and over-counts of different plants cancelled each other out in the signed mean percentage error. \citet{lee2026lidarcamera} fused detections with LiDAR SLAM point clouds in a pear plot (96.2\% counting accuracy), and \citet{nellithimaru2019rols} built object-level SLAM from stereo for grape counting. Implicit representations are the latest variant: FruitNeRF clusters fruit points from a semantic neural radiance field \citep{meyer2024fruitnerf}, and Gaussian splatting was reported to count persimmons with 96\% accuracy \citep{hong2025fruitgaussian}; both take camera poses from structure from motion \citep{kerbl20233d}.

\textit{Where geometry fails.} The reported failures concern the inputs, not the principle. \citet{stein2016image} traced their largest errors to missing image frames and platform oscillation, which skewed the epipolar lines, and to bunched fruit, which made nearby fruit exchange identities; they expected the approach to help with heavily bunched fruit such as grapes only if the camera could be positioned around the bunches. In the study of \citet{genemola2020fruitb}, neighbouring apples merged into one 3D detection, most remaining duplicates were apples seen from both sides of the row whose detections did not overlap enough, and SfM was too slow for real-time use. \citet{meyer2025fruitnerf} attributed their lower F1 on a real apple set to noisy poses and manual registration of both sides. Scale is undefined for monocular reconstruction unless another sensor fixes it \citep{liu2019monocular}. Foliage moves between views, which violates the static-scene assumption of SfM and SLAM; dynamic-scene methods exist \citep{bescos2021dynaslam}, but no included study measured how much foliage motion degrades counts. Assigning fruit to trees is a separate geometric problem \citep{zineelabidine2021assigning}.

Overall, geometric association has produced counts close to the reference without a fitted factor when pose, scale, and depth are reliable, and its errors grow with fruit clustering and with any error in pose or registration; the implicit representations are still evaluated mostly on synthetic scenes and a few real trees.

\subsection{Learned association}\label{sec:mech-learn}
Learned association moves the identity decision into a trained network, which needs identity labels across observations; the included studies show promising results on their own data and no gain yet over fruit-specific geometric pipelines on shared data. A learned component decides the correspondence in \nLearn of the \nMethod method studies, in three ways.

First, a network matches fruit between scans or images: \citet{fusaro2026horticultural} matched fruit between sessions with an attention-based network on point-cloud descriptors, evaluated on strawberries, and the matching network of \citet{xia2022culling} and the learned distance of \citet{zheng2023object} (Section~\ref{sec:mech-app}) belong to the same group. Second, a network learns identity inside a 3D representation. FruitNeRF++ lifts 2D instance masks into 3D through embeddings trained with a contrastive loss; on a real apple set it reached F1 0.765, below the 0.881 of the fruit-specific method of \citet{genemola2020fruitb}, and the authors noted that occlusion by a leaf or branch can split one fruit into two instances \citep{meyer2025fruitnerf}. \citet{zhao2026adapting} combined contrastive learning with voting in a 3D Gaussian representation without explicit matching between views, and reported F1 of 0.971 and 0.977 on synthetic plum and mango scenes. Third, a network replaces the association step of a tracker: \citet{santos2026multi} used a learned point tracker in orange video, counted 978 fruit against 879 annotated (11.26\% error), and stated that the result did not improve on their earlier pipeline; their worst sequence (43.44\% error) came from re-identification errors that they attributed to poor pose estimation. \citet{lei2025spatio} associated apples across four sessions with pre-trained visual features, position, and a topology cost, without training a network, and reached 96.9\% counting accuracy for 1{,}790 apples.

Outside agriculture, learned association is established for people and vehicles, within one camera \citep{braso2020learning} and across cameras \citep{ristani2016performance,tang2019cityflow}, because benchmarks with identities across observations exist. The obstacle in fruit is the same: only a handful of fruit datasets in the corpus provide such identities, among them GrapeMOTS \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots}, the tomato tracking set of \citet{qi2025assessment}, the 4D orchard set of \citet{lei2025spatio}, and SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}.

\subsection{How the evidence was measured}\label{sec:mech-measure}
Most of the evidence for every mechanism is a count error, and a count error cannot show that duplicates were removed: an estimate can match the reference because duplicates and misses offset each other \citep{rapadorincon2023development,wang2019mango}. Identity metrics were developed to separate the two. HOTA balances detection and association accuracy \citep{luiten2021hota}, and IDF1 was designed for applications in which identities must be preserved, within and across cameras \citep{ristani2016performance}. Of the \nMethodMetric multi-observation method studies whose metrics were coded from the full text, \nMetIdent (\pMetIdent\%) report such a metric (multiple-object tracking accuracy (MOTA), IDF1, HOTA, or identity (ID) switches), \nMetCount (\pMetCount\%) report agreement between estimated and reference counts, \nMetDet (\pMetDet\%) report detection quality, and \nMetNone (\pMetNone\%) report no quantitative metric (Fig.~\ref{fig:metrik}); \nMetCountOnly report count agreement and nothing else. Identity metrics need identity labels across observations, which video tracking datasets provide; for merging the two sides of a row or matching fruit between sessions, association was measured directly only in a few studies, such as the error in identifying duplicates of \citet{gongal2016apple} and the matching precision of \citet{lei2025spatio}.

The reference also varies. Hand counts on the tree measure what the camera could have seen, harvest and packhouse counts include fruit hidden from every view, and annotated images measure only what an annotator saw. In the coded records, the count reference is annotated images or video in \nRefAnnot of the \nMethod method studies, a hand count on the plant in \nRefTree, a harvest count in \nRefHarvest, and a packhouse count in \nRefPack; \nRefNone state no count reference in the source that was read (a study may use more than one). Results from different references are not comparable, which is why Tables~\ref{tab:acq} and~\ref{tab:assoc} report the reference or metric level for every row.

\section{Class attributes on unique fruit}\label{sec:class}

A class-wise inventory needs one label per physical fruit, and the included studies show that labels are least stable exactly where classes meet: a fruit at an intermediate stage is classified differently in different frames, and a tracker that splits the track when the label changes turns a class error into a duplicate. This section answers the third question by following where the label is decided: in each image, during tracking, by a vote over frames, or once in a fused model.

\textit{Labels decided in each image.} The \nClassSingle single-image class-wise counting studies detect and count fruit by class in one image, mostly tomatoes (\nClassSingleTomato) and mostly by maturity (\nClassSingleMaturity). \citet{wang2022online} detected red and green tomatoes in a plant factory (mAP 99.3\%), \citet{ni2020deep} segmented blueberries by maturity and counted berries per cluster ($R^2=0.886$), and \citet{eron2024computer} assigned coffee cherries to ripeness classes by clustering their colour. \citet{shimomoto2025predicting} stitched greenhouse video of sweet pepper into one panorama per row and assigned each fruit a ripeness stage from its hue. Against a grower's counts the automatic counts correlated at $r=0.96$ for the 1--40\% stage but only at $r=0.63$ for the 41--80\% stage. The authors ruled out disproportionate misses at that stage and attributed the gap to classification from the visible surface: a partly occluded fruit whose visible part was mostly red could be counted in the 81--100\% stage. These studies count classes in the image, not classes of unique fruit.

\textit{Labels carried by the tracker.} In the \nPerClass multi-observation studies that report counts per class, the class is most often carried by the tracker (\nClassDuring studies). The per-class errors are larger than the total error. In the strawberry study of \citet{ogundele2025real}, which used a class-aware ByteTrack, the semi-ripe class had the lowest count agreement ($R^2=0.752$, MAPE 16.25\%) against 0.950 for ripe and 0.910 for unripe fruit, and the authors attributed it to the mottled, transitional look of that stage. \citet{wang2025tomato} obtained a mean counting error of 6.6\% for all tomatoes but 12.3\%, 16.9\%, and 17.3\% for the ripe, semi-ripe, and unripe classes, and showed fruit switching between the unripe and semi-ripe classes as one cause of duplicate counts. \citet{kirk2021robust} observed that occlusion can change the apparent ripeness of a strawberry and pooled the class over all earlier observations of a track. \citet{zheng2025robust}, who assigned the class after tracking, counted ripe and unripe tomatoes with accuracies of 89.74--93.22\% on three beds.

\textit{Labels decided by a vote.} An explicit rule for disagreeing frames is stated in \nClassVote studies. \citet{lin2026tomato} tracked tomatoes and gave each the maturity class that received most votes over its frames; after the vote, and with boxes taken from the annotations, 38--81\% of the boxes received the correct class depending on the dataset, classifier, and features, and the authors reported that the vote reduced the effect of transient errors. \citet{qi2024improved} gave each tomato cluster the ripeness level that occurred most often across frames. \citet{pai2024maturity} motivated a vote by inconsistent labels of the same tomato across frames but voted among models on single boxes, without tracking.

\textit{Labels decided once in a fused model.} Geometric association offers a route without frame-to-frame disagreement, because each fruit appears once in the fused model. \citet{zhang2022measurement} registered two views of each greenhouse tomato plant into one coloured point cloud, detected fruit in it (precision 86.19\%, recall 83.39\% against 277 fruit on 50 plants), and classified the points of each fruit into four ripening stages. The label is decided once per fruit, but the classifier was trained and tested on separate image samples (96.09\% accuracy), not checked against reference classes of the fused fruit.

\textit{What follows.} Two consequences follow. First, class and identity errors interact: a class-aware tracker that splits a track when the label flips creates a duplicate, and a vote or a fused model removes the flip only if association has already gathered the observations of one fruit. In only \nClassVote of the \nPerClass studies is a rule stated for disagreeing frames, \nClassUnstated do not state how the class is attached, and none reports a class confusion matrix computed on unique fruit. Second, the intermediate class is the weak point. The oil-palm census has the same structure: the SawitMVC authors noted irreducible label ambiguity at the boundary between the two-month and three-month classes \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}, and a bunch detector trained on images that each held one class lost up to half of its mAP50 on videos that mixed ripeness classes \citep{junior2023video}. An inventory that reports only a total hides both effects (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).

\section{Depth and other modalities}\label{sec:depth}

Depth can serve two stages of the design space, observation and association, and the evidence for the second is conditional: depth places observations of one fruit together only when its error is small relative to the spacing between fruit, and that error grows with range, sunlight, and registration error. The \nDepth depth and 3D sensing studies use RGB-D cameras (\nDepthRgbd), stereo (\nDepthStereo), LiDAR (\nDepthLidar), or other non-RGB signals, mostly on apples (\nDepthApple), and their main use is observation.

\textit{Depth for observation.} Depth removes background and adds a channel to the detector. \citet{afonso2020tomato} used depth after inference to discard tomatoes from the row behind, and \citet{barbole2024comparative} masked grape clusters by depth before segmentation (pixel accuracy up to 94.81\%). Fusing aligned RGB and near-infrared images raised the average precision of a kiwifruit detector from 88.4\% to 90.7\% \citep{liu2020improved}, and fusing RGB and depth gave AP50 0.961 for pears \citep{ling2026boosting}. Depth also gives metric size: root-mean-square errors of about 4--5~mm for on-tree mangoes \citep{wang2017tree} and 2.35~mm for apple fruitlets \citep{sapkota2024immature}. \citet{neupane2021evaluation} compared eight depth cameras against a 20~mm error requirement for fruit localization and sizing.

\textit{Depth for association.} In association, depth enters through geometric association (Section~\ref{sec:mech-geom}). \citet{liu2019monocular} used the depth of each fruit relative to the trunk to reject far-side duplicates, \citet{zheng2025robust} filtered detections by depth and used robot odometry in inter-frame prediction for tomatoes, and \citet{wei2025multi} matched apples from the two sides of a tree when their positions were closer than one fruit diameter (70--85~mm). Such a rule needs position errors below the fruit size. For four stereo cameras, the depth residual grew with the distance between camera and object \citep{neupane2021evaluation}. An oil-palm dataset recorded with a stereo depth camera on bunches on the palm accepted depth readings within $\pm$10~cm of a tape measurement at 1.5--2.0~m and within $\pm$20~cm at 3.0--4.0~m \citep{goh2025outdoor}. \citet{wang2017tree} found that their RGB-D camera could not be used in direct sunshine. \citet{qureshi2023seeing} reported that sun glare degraded stereo matching and that wind disrupted registration, which is why their platform straddled the canopy. On the same 420 apple trees, the Azure Kinect gave more stable results across scanning dates than the ZED~2 \citep{felippomes2026video}.

\textit{Depth without a depth sensor.} Monocular depth networks now predict depth from a single image \citep{ranftl2021vision}; one included study paired RGB images with predicted depth to train a detector for \textit{Camellia oleifera} fruit (mAP@0.5 93.33\%) \citep{chen2026ciafnet}, but none used predicted depth for cross-view association against a counted reference.

The evidence therefore supports depth as an aid to separating neighbouring fruit at close range, and as a component of geometric association when its error is small relative to fruit spacing. For tall crops imaged from the ground, the reported growth of depth error with range is the condition to check first (Section~\ref{sec:palm}).

\section{Oil palm}\label{sec:palm}

A bunch-level census of oil palm photographed from a few positions around each palm removes the conditions that temporal tracking and appearance matching rely on and leaves the conditions for geometric association untested, and the oil-palm imaging literature has so far built detectors that are evaluated image by image: high scores come from harvested bunches, and they fall when classes are mixed in one frame and among standing palms. This section answers the fourth question by first stating why the census is difficult, then tracing how detection has developed, and then stating what one linked dataset shows and what is missing.

\subsection{Why the census challenges each mechanism}
Bunches of the same class on one palm look alike, which weakens appearance matching (Section~\ref{sec:mech-app}). When a census photographs a palm from a few discrete positions around the trunk, as in SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}, the frame-to-frame continuity that temporal tracking needs is lost (Section~\ref{sec:mech-track}); video recorded while circling a palm keeps it \citep{suharjito2025oil}. Fronds occlude bunches differently from each side: in SawitMVC, 25.4\% of bunches were annotated in only one of the photographed sides. The bunches also sit metres from a ground camera, and for four stereo cameras measured indoors the depth residual grew with the distance between camera and object \citep{neupane2021evaluation}. An RGB-D dataset of bunches on the palm accepted depth readings within $\pm$20~cm of a tape measurement at 3.0--4.0~m \citep{goh2025outdoor}, and no included study measured whether such errors allow neighbouring bunches to be told apart (Section~\ref{sec:depth}). Statistical correction needs two stable ratios: how many times a bunch is seen, which SawitMVC measured, and how many bunches are hidden from every side, which no included study measured for oil palm. Neighbouring census classes differ by colour and by size and structure \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}, so a class error on one view can split one bunch into two records of different classes (Section~\ref{sec:class}).

\subsection{How detection developed}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sawit} shows that the \nPalm oil-palm studies concentrate on ripeness: \nPalmCls classify the ripeness of images of single bunches, \nPalmDet detect bunches in a scene, \nPalmSensor study non-RGB sensing of ripeness, \nPalmQual address quality, defects, mass, or pose, \nPalmLoose detect loose fruit, \nPalmCount count bunches or fruit in single images, and \nPalmData are dataset papers. The setting is on the palm in \nPalmOnTree studies, at a mill, conveyor, or laboratory in \nPalmMill, and on the ground or at a collection point in \nPalmGround; \nPalmUnstated sources do not state it, and the number taken from a UAV is \nPalmUav. \nPalmViews use more than one view of the same bunch or palm, for example three to four photographs of each bunch \citep{omar2024outdoor}. Six further oil-palm reports, five studies that link observations across images and the SawitMVC dataset, were coded with the multi-observation studies and are discussed below.

\textit{From colour features to whole-image classifiers.} Early work classified images of single bunches from colour features with neural networks \citep{fadilah2012intelligent} or support vector machines. \citet{sabri2018evaluation} found the YCbCr and YUV colour models better than six others on about 500 bunch images. Convolutional classifiers followed, often on mobile devices. \citet{puttinaovarat2024oil} classified on-tree bunches as ripe or unripe with 96.12\% accuracy, whereas \citet{usman2026integration} reported 94.66\% $\pm$ 0.54\% over four classes in leave-site-out cross-validation on 10{,}000 photographs. Non-RGB sensing (thermal, hyperspectral, laser bio-speckle, and LED multispectral imaging) was tested mostly on harvested bunches \citep{makky2021pre,zolfagharnassab2022classification,bensaeed2014oil,salambue2018investigation,shiddiq2024multispectral}. A classifier assigns one class to a whole image, which presumes that the image holds one bunch.

\textit{Detectors on video frames and field images.} Detection of several bunches per image appeared with two-stage detectors, which counted bunches in images collected from the internet \citep{prasetyo2020automatic} and on conveyors \citep{aji2021automatic}, and became common with single-stage YOLO detectors. \citet{junior2023video} trained YOLOv4 variants on smartphone video of harvested bunches at a grading site. Their best model reached mAP50 97.64\% on test videos of one class, whereas the best model on videos mixing classes reached 70.21\%, which the authors traced to training images that each held one class. \citet{suharjito2023real} graded piles of harvested bunches with YOLOv4 (test mAP50 up to 99.98\% on a frame-level split), but quantized models on a phone reached an average F1 of only 0.52--0.64 on new videos, and the underripe and ripe classes were the least consistent. YOLOv8 then became the common detector. \citet{noordin2025real} reported mAP 0.952 for a YOLOv8n model on a handheld device, trained and evaluated on 4{,}194 images without stating which split the score refers to, and reported lower accuracy on palms taller than about six feet. \citet{naftali2024palm} detected bunches in five classes in frames from 440 plantation videos with a depthwise-separable YOLOv8s (mAP50 0.75, with videos kept apart between training and test), and the same model reached 0.994 on an earlier dataset of harvested bunches; the plantation data were later released with mAP50 0.735--0.745 for YOLOv8n and YOLOv8s on palms 1--3~m tall \citep{suharjito2025oil}. \citet{jumrat2026detection} reached mAP50 0.988 for three classes on 427 images under industrial conditions, with most errors between half-ripe and ripe bunches. \citet{hutapea2025comparison} reported mAP50 0.995 for YOLOv8s on training data from 80 of 250 images. On SawitMVC, an untuned YOLO26m reached AP50 0.531 on 141 test palms photographed from four to eight sides, with per-class AP50 of 0.354--0.739 \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}.

Taken together, scores near 0.99 come from harvested bunches and close-ups, and they fall when classes are mixed in one frame, when quantized models run on a phone on new videos \citep{suharjito2023real}, or when the bunches hang on standing palms, where one plantation dataset gave about 0.75 and SawitMVC 0.531. Setting, split protocol, and deployment change together in these studies, so the drop cannot be assigned to any one of them. Class errors fell between ripe and underripe or half-ripe bunches in harvested data \citep{suharjito2023real,jumrat2026detection}, whereas on standing palms the least ripe class, the smallest bunches, had the lowest AP50 \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. Every one of these detectors is evaluated on single images or frames.

\subsection{Counting, the one linked dataset, and what is missing}
Counting is the gap. The \nPalmCount oil-palm studies that count in single images counted bunches in plantation video frames \citep{naftali2024palm}, bunches in images collected from the internet \citep{prasetyo2020automatic}, and fruit within bunches \citep{hamdani2026automated}. Counting per frame does not produce a census, because a bunch visible in several frames is counted in each. Five oil-palm studies link observations across images. Two counted harvested bunches on a conveyor with a detector and a centroid tracker \citep{shiddiq2023counting,aji2021automatic}. Two tracked the palm, not the bunch, and took the most frequent per-frame count of the loose fruit under it \citep{daud2022loose,daud2023detection}. One linked repeated photographs of 809 tagged bunches through the tag \citep{kassim2012oil}. None of them associates bunches automatically across views of the same palm. The plantation dataset of \citet{suharjito2025oil} was recorded around each palm, but its released boxes are not linked across frames.

The only oil-palm dataset in the corpus with cross-view identity links is SawitMVC, in which a bunch appears in 1.89 photographed sides on average (18{,}540 boxes for 9{,}823 unique bunches on 953 palms). \citet{indriani2026sawitmvc} counted the bunches of 141 test palms per class with two deliberately untuned baselines. Summing the annotated boxes of all sides placed 50.00\% of the per-class counts within one bunch of the reference. Dividing that sum by the ratio of boxes to bunches in the training split (1.89) raised this to 95.57\% (mean absolute error per palm and class, averaged over classes, 0.356 bunches), and a support vector regression on per-side counts raised it to 96.81\% (0.303). All four classes of a palm were within one bunch on 86.52\% and 88.65\% of palms. With boxes from the detector instead of the annotations, the ratio and the regression placed 72.34\% and 75.35\% of per-class counts within one bunch, and all four classes on 30.50\% and 33.33\% of palms. The authors concluded that detection, rather than the counting stage, was the dominant source of error.

These baselines set the bar for an association method, within limits that the dataset states. A within-one-bunch criterion is lenient when a test palm carries on average 0.85 to 5.28 bunches per class, and the duplication ratio was fitted and tested on palms from the same two plantations and the same month, so it is the in-distribution case of the statistical correction that failed to transfer elsewhere (Section~\ref{sec:mech-stat}). The ratio was fitted on annotated boxes and applied unchanged to detected boxes, where the mean signed error rose from $+0.009$ to $+0.381$ bunches, so the drop with the detector mixes detection error with a ratio that does not match the detector's output. The reference counts bunches annotated in at least one photograph, not bunches hidden from every side, so none of these numbers measures a full census. The dataset also comes from one collection period, mostly one variety (89.6\% of palms), and ten phone models, and its authors report an ambiguous boundary between two of the four classes without a measured inter-annotator agreement. The missing experiment is therefore an association method evaluated on palms photographed from several sides, with identity links, census classes, and a per-class count reference, compared with the ratio and regression baselines on both annotated and detected boxes, with the ratio refitted on detector output, tested on another plantation or season, and checked against a count of all bunches on a subset of palms (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:agenda}

The evidence reviewed in Sections~\ref{sec:acq} to~\ref{sec:palm} supports one argument: the count a system reports is decided by the step that links observations of the same fruit, and the conditions that step assumes are set by how the images were taken. This section traces how the choice of mechanism has shifted, answers the four questions, places the review against earlier reviews, and states five gaps with the measurement that would close each.

\subsection{How the choice of mechanism has shifted}
Before 2018, geometric association and statistical correction accounted for almost all multi-observation studies; since 2023, temporal tracking has appeared in \pTrackRecent\% of new studies (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}a). Within geometric association, implicit 3D representations are the latest variant, and \nLearn studies learn part of the association (Sections~\ref{sec:mech-geom} and~\ref{sec:mech-learn}). The trajectory runs from correcting a sum, to linking detections in video, to placing fruit in a 3D frame. Class-wise identity has not followed: only \nPerClass of the \nMethod method studies report counts per class (Section~\ref{sec:class}).

\subsection{Answers to the four questions}
\textit{Which mechanisms have been used, and what does each assume?} Six mechanisms are in use, and each removes duplicates only while its assumption holds (Table~\ref{tab:mechsyn}). Statistical correction assumes a stable ratio between observed and true counts; the ratio changed with orchard, canopy, and season \citep{anderson2021estimation,koirala2021attempting}, so the factor must be refitted and cannot say which fruit was counted twice. Temporal tracking assumes ordered, overlapping frames and a camera whose motion the filter can predict; its reported failures are low frame rates, camera shake, occlusions longer than the track lifetime, and the second side of the row. Appearance matching assumes fruit that can be told apart, which fruit of one class on one plant often cannot. Geometric association assumes reliable pose, scale, and depth and a static scene; it is the only mechanism that removed duplicates between the two sides of a row, and its failures come from missing frames, oscillation, clustered fruit, and misregistration. Learned association assumes identity-labelled training data that resemble the conditions of use, which few fruit datasets provide. The mechanisms are therefore not interchangeable: the acquisition protocol selects which assumptions can hold before any method is chosen (Section~\ref{sec:acq}).

\textit{How well does each mechanism control duplicates and misses, and how was that measured?} The comparisons on the same data give three findings. First, on look-alike fruit, adding a learned appearance descriptor to a motion-based tracker did not improve the count, and it cost time and robustness to frame-rate changes \citep{zhang2022deep,genemola2023video,qi2025assessment}. Second, a geometric step after tracking or summation removed duplicates that the earlier step left: the error of tracked counts fell by about half to two thirds after structure-from-motion correction \citep{liu2018robust}, and merging the two sides of a row in 3D brought counts from 103.86--147.81\% to 95.56--97.83\% of the harvest \citep{hani2020comparative}. Third, the net count error hides offsetting errors: 9.9\% repeated counts and 7.3\% misses netted to a 2.6\% overcount \citep{wang2019mango}, and over- and under-counts cancelled in a 3D tracker \citep{rapadorincon2023development}. Because most studies report only count agreement, against references that range from annotated images to harvest counts, the strength of the evidence for any mechanism is weaker than its count errors suggest (Section~\ref{sec:mech-measure}).

\textit{How are classes attached to unique fruit?} Mostly by carrying a per-detection class along a track. Per-class errors exceed the total error, the intermediate class was the weakest in two studies \citep{ogundele2025real,shimomoto2025predicting}, and a label that flips between neighbouring classes across frames can split one fruit into two records, so class errors become identity errors \citep{wang2025tomato}. A vote over frames or a decision in a fused 3D model avoids the flip only if association has already gathered the observations of one fruit, and only \nClassVote studies state such a rule.

\textit{What does the oil-palm literature cover, and what is missing?} It has moved from colour features to convolutional classifiers and then to detectors on field images and video frames, and most of it addresses ripeness. Detector scores near 0.99 come from harvested bunches and close-ups; among standing palms one plantation dataset gave about 0.75 mAP50 and SawitMVC 0.531 AP50 (Section~\ref{sec:palm}). Counting is done per image or on conveyors. No study associates bunches across views of a palm. A census from a few discrete views removes what temporal tracking and appearance matching rely on, frame continuity and distinct appearance, and whether depth at the height of the bunches is accurate enough for geometric association is untested. On the one dataset that links bunches across sides, a duplication ratio fitted on the same plantations placed 95.57\% of per-class counts on annotated boxes within one bunch, and 72.34\% on detected boxes \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. An association method therefore has to beat that ratio, not the naive sum, and has to be tested on detected boxes and on another plantation, where the ratio has not been tested.

\subsection{Position relative to earlier reviews}\label{sec:position}
Table~\ref{tab:position} lists the nine earlier reviews in the map that are closest to this one. They organize the evidence by estimation route or methodological approach \citep{anderson2021technologies,barriguinha2021vineyard,magro2023computational,rong2026research}, by network architecture or task \citep{maheswari2021intelligent,mohimont2022computer}, or, for oil palm, by task \citep{khan2021oil}, sensing modality and classifier \citep{lai2023oil}, or processing stage \citep{kamal2024machine}. Reviews of tracking and re-identification outside agriculture \citep{luo2021multiple,ye2022deep} and of feature tracking and 3D reconstruction \citep{cao2018survey,li2025reviewb} provide the mechanisms but not the agricultural evidence. This review adds an organization by the identity decision, which makes three things visible that a route-, architecture-, or task-based organization does not: the coupling between acquisition design and admissible mechanism, the dependence of class-wise counts on identity, and the missing discrete-view case. \subsection{Five gaps}
\textit{(i) Identity is measured in a minority of studies.} \nMetIdent of the \nMethodMetric multi-observation method studies (\pMetIdent\%) report an identity metric (Section~\ref{sec:mech-measure}, Fig.~\ref{fig:metrik}). A study that claims to remove duplicates should report, on annotated sequences or view sets, the numbers of duplicate records, missed fruit, false merges of two fruit into one, and identity switches, in addition to the count error. \citet{wang2019mango} and \citet{rapadorincon2023development} show that this is feasible.

\textit{(ii) Mechanisms are rarely compared on the same data.} Table~\ref{tab:assoc} contains 14 such comparisons, six of which compare trackers only. Comparisons across mechanisms, for example tracking against geometric association on the same trees, are rare, and comparisons against a calibrated statistical-correction baseline are rarer, although such a baseline is the reference method in commercial mango estimation \citep{koirala2021attempting}. A new mechanism should be tested against a statistical correction fitted on separate trees and, where possible, another season.

\textit{(iii) Class-wise identity is almost absent.} \nPerClass multi-observation studies report counts per class, and in \nClassVote of them the source states how label disagreement between frames is resolved (Section~\ref{sec:class}). The required measurement is a per-class count error computed on unique fruit, together with a confusion matrix of classes on unique fruit.

\textit{(iv) Discrete-view protocols lack mechanisms.} Most methods assume video (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}b and Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}). Census protocols photograph a plant from a few fixed positions, as in oil palm \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc} or in multi-sided mango and citrus imaging \citep{nenavath2024tree,zhu2024citrus}. For such data, temporal tracking does not apply, and the evidence on appearance matching, geometric association, and learned association comes mostly from other acquisition designs (Section~\ref{sec:acq}). Wide-baseline association with known or estimated pose is the missing experiment.

\textit{(v) Oil palm has detection and ripeness evidence but no identity evidence.} A bunch-level census from images needs an evaluation on palms photographed from several sides, with identity links, class labels in census classes, and a per-class count reference (Section~\ref{sec:palm}). One dataset now provides this, from two plantations in one month, with a reference limited to bunches annotated in at least one photograph \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}; a split by plantation or a second dataset is needed to test whether any counter transfers, and a count of all bunches on some palms is needed to measure what no photograph shows.

\subsection{A minimum reporting set}
At minimum, a study that claims a duplicate-aware, class-wise inventory should report: (a) the acquisition protocol (views per plant, ordering, pose source, depth source, baseline); (b) the mechanism and the component that removes duplicates; (c) the reference type (hand count on the tree, harvest, or annotated images); (d) the count error per plant and per class; (e) identity metrics on annotated data; and (f) a statistical-correction baseline fitted on separate plants.

\section{Limitations of this review}\label{sec:limits}

Seven limitations apply. First, Scopus was the only database; work indexed only in Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, or preprint servers is missing unless Scopus also indexes it. Second, the map rests on the reports whose full text could be retrieved. Of the \nSought reports that passed the abstract stage, \nNotRetrieved (\pNotRetrieved\%) could not be retrieved, among them \nNotRetrievedMulti multi-observation studies and all \nOther reports found outside the search, and none of them is part of the map. The counts in this review therefore describe the retrieved literature; if retrieval depended on the publisher or the access model, the sources that were easier to obtain are over-represented.

Third, abstracts were not available from the database export used and were retrieved from other bibliographic services; \nNoAbstract records without an abstract were first decided from titles, sources, and machine-generated summaries. Fourth, screening and coding were done by one reviewer assisted by a large language model, and the second pass at the abstract and full-text stages was also run by the model. It shows how reproducible the decisions are but does not replace an independent second reviewer, and the first author's blind samples gave only moderate agreement ($\kappa$ = \kHumTitles for titles and \kHumElig for eligibility codes).

Fifth, the title stage was the least reliable step: in a random sample of \nSampleTitles titles, \nSampleTitlesEligible excluded records met an inclusion criterion once their abstracts were read, and although the exclusions from the two core query sets were re-read from their abstracts, the map is likely to miss eligible studies among the remaining title exclusions, mainly in the supporting groups. Sixth, rule-based codes for crop, modality, and platform were spot-checked but not verified for every record. Seventh, the set of methods papers from outside agriculture is purposive, limited to the most cited records of each search, and results were not pooled, so the numbers in Tables~\ref{tab:acq} and~\ref{tab:assoc} should be compared only within a row.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

This review asked how image-based fruit counting decides that observations in different images belong to the same fruit. It mapped \nIncluded studies published between 2012 and 2026, coded the \nMulti multi-observation studies among them by association mechanism and acquisition design, and read the comparisons made on the same data against the assumptions of each mechanism (Tables~\ref{tab:mechsyn} and~\ref{tab:assoc}; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}).

What is established is that counting fruit from images is an identity problem as much as a detection problem, and that each mechanism fails where its assumption breaks. A fitted correction factor stays accurate only within the orchard and season in which it was fitted. Motion-based tracking removes duplicates between consecutive frames, and on look-alike fruit, appearance descriptors did not improve its counts. A geometric step removed the duplicates that tracking and summation left, including fruit seen from both sides of a row, as long as pose and registration were reliable. What is not established is how often duplicates and misses offset each other inside a correct-looking total, because \nMetIdent of the \nMethodMetric method studies measure identity directly; whether any mechanism works on a few discrete views of a plant; and how class labels should be decided for a unique fruit whose appearance changes between views.

For practice, the acquisition design narrows the choice. For video along a row, a motion-based tracker with an explicit counting rule is the tested default, and fruit seen from both sides need a separate geometric merge. For a few discrete views around a plant, temporal tracking does not apply; association must rest on geometry with estimated pose, on appearance combined with position, or on learned correspondence, and should be tested against a statistical correction fitted on separate plants. For class-wise inventories, the class should be decided once per unique fruit, with the rule for disagreeing views reported. For oil palm, detectors trained and tested among standing palms, not on harvested bunches, are the starting point.

Five priorities follow: (i) measure duplicates, misses, false merges, and identity switches, not only the count error; (ii) compare mechanisms on the same data, including a statistical-correction baseline fitted on separate plants and, where possible, another season; (iii) report per-class counts and a class confusion matrix on unique fruit; (iv) test wide-baseline association on discrete-view protocols; and (v) evaluate bunch-level, class-wise counting on oil palms photographed from several sides, against the regression baseline already available for such data.
