\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A grower who needs the number of ripe fruit on a block wants one record for each physical fruit, with its class. A camera does not supply that record. It supplies images, and in most field protocols the same fruit appears in several of them: in consecutive video frames, in photographs taken from both sides of a row, or in images taken while walking around a single tree. Detection finds fruit in each image. It does not say whether a fruit found in one image is the fruit found in another. The step that makes that decision determines whether a sum of detections is an inventory or an overcount.

The size of the problem is known from studies that measured it directly. In a mango orchard, one image of each side of 16 trees counted 54\% of the fruit that were hand-counted, and one image of one side counted 27\%. Tracking every fruit through a multi-view sequence and triangulating it counted 101.4\% \citep{stein2016image}. On 21 mango trees, video tracking recorded 62\% of the harvest count, whereas two images per tree recorded 40\% \citep{wang2019mango}. On a 192-fruit video, the same tracker produced 9.9\% double counts and 7.3\% missed fruit, a net overcount of 2.6\% \citep{wang2019mango}. The last figure shows why a total count can look correct when the underlying identity decisions are not: a duplicate and a miss cancel.

In oil palm the problem takes a specific form. Plantations estimate output with a bunch census in which staff count and classify bunches on each palm by the number of months until harvest \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. The SawitMVC dataset contains 953 palms photographed from four or eight sides and 9{,}823 unique bunches annotated in four census classes, with links that record which boxes in different images show the same bunch \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. A census built from such photographs needs both the identity decision and a class label for each unique bunch.

From the perspective of counting unique fruit, earlier reviews leave two gaps. First, they organize the literature by estimation route or methodological approach \citep{anderson2021technologies,barriguinha2021vineyard,magro2023computational,rong2026research}, by network architecture or task \citep{maheswari2021intelligent,mohimont2022computer}, or, for oil palm, by task, sensing modality, or processing stage \citep{khan2021oil,lai2023oil,kamal2024machine}. None takes the identity of a fruit across observations as the organizing variable, although several discuss occlusion and extrapolation from partial counts. Second, reviews of tracking, re-identification, and 3D reconstruction outside agriculture provide the mechanisms but not the agricultural evidence. As a result, a reader cannot tell which association mechanism fits a given acquisition protocol, or what evidence supports that mechanism.

To address these issues, this article presents a systematic mapping review of image-based fruit counting published between 2012 and 2026, reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement where its items apply. Scopus was the single database, searched on 28 September 2026, and \nIncluded studies met the criteria, of which \nMulti combine several observations of the same fruit.

This review makes three contributions. First, it provides a systematic map of the \nIncluded studies, with the search strings reported in full and the screening decisions and codes provided as supplementary material. Second, it defines a design space that separates acquisition, observation, association, attribute assignment, and inventory, and classifies association into six mechanisms (no association, statistical correction, appearance matching, temporal tracking, geometric or 3D association, and learned association), each tied to the conditions it assumes. Third, it synthesizes the \nMulti multi-observation studies by mechanism, with the comparisons that were made on the same data reported in two tables, followed by the evidence on class attributes, depth, and oil palm.

Accordingly, the review addresses four questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
\item Which mechanisms have been used to decide that observations in different images belong to the same fruit, and what acquisition conditions does each assume?
\item What evidence shows how well each mechanism controls duplicates and misses, and how was that evidence measured?
\item How are class attributes, mainly maturity, attached to a unique fruit rather than to each detection?
\item What does the oil-palm imaging literature cover, and what is missing for a bunch-level, class-wise census from images?
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the search, screening, and coding. Section~\ref{sec:framework} sets out the design space and reports how the multi-observation studies are distributed over it. Sections~\ref{sec:acq} to~\ref{sec:class} follow its stages: acquisition (Section~\ref{sec:acq}), association examined mechanism by mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:mech}), and class attributes on unique fruit (Section~\ref{sec:class}). Section~\ref{sec:depth} examines depth and other sensing modalities, and Section~\ref{sec:palm} the oil-palm case. Section~\ref{sec:agenda} answers the four questions, positions the review relative to earlier reviews (Section~\ref{sec:position}), identifies the gaps, and proposes a measurement agenda. Section~\ref{sec:limits} states the limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Review method}\label{sec:method}

The review is a systematic mapping review with a configurative synthesis \citep{petersen2015mapping,grant2009typology}: it builds a classification from a broad body of work and does not pool effect sizes, because the included studies use different crops, count references, and metrics. Reporting follows PRISMA~2020 where its items apply to a mapping review \citep{page2021prisma,page2021elaboration}, and the completed checklist is given in Supplementary Section~S5. The review protocol was not registered. Procedural details that a reader needs only to audit the map are given in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Search strategy}
Scopus was the single bibliographic database, searched on 28 September 2026 (Coordinated Universal Time); it indexes the journals and proceedings in which agricultural engineering and computer vision work is published. The search comprised 13 search strings in nine query sets (Table~\ref{tab:queries}). Five sets target agricultural fruit perception, two retrieve reviews, and two retrieve methods papers from outside agriculture. For the latter two sets, only the 25 most cited records of each search were retained, because their role is to supply transferable mechanisms, not to map those fields. All searches were limited to 2012--2026. One included review, published online before the search date, is assigned to a 2027 volume. The complete strings are given in Appendix~\ref{app:queries}.

\subsection{Inclusion and exclusion criteria}\label{sec:criteria}
Records published from 2012 to 2026 were included if they were at least one of the following: (i) an image-based method for fruit on the plant, or on the ground under the plant for oil-palm loose fruit, that evaluated counting, yield estimation, detection with a class attribute, localization, or sizing; (ii) a review of these topics; or (iii) a methods paper outside agriculture on tracking, re-identification, multi-view association, 3D reconstruction, or object counting, or a general detection, segmentation, or depth-estimation model on which such pipelines are built.

Records were excluded if: (i) the object was not fruit on the plant (for example trees, leaves, or flowers only); (ii) imaging was postharvest or laboratory-only, except oil-palm mill grading, which is part of the case; (iii) yield was modelled without fruit-level detection; (iv) the work addressed only picking or manipulation; (v) fruit were detected without counting or any evaluation beyond detection; (vi) the sensor was not an imaging sensor; or (vii) the record had no English title and abstract.

\subsection{Study selection}\label{sec:screening}
Records passed through three stages: title screening, eligibility assessment on the title and abstract, and eligibility assessment on the full text of the reports that could be retrieved, with the reason for every exclusion after title screening recorded (Fig.~\ref{fig:prisma}). A report that repeated a study already included through another report, such as a book chapter that reprinted a journal article, was linked to that study and not counted again (\nFTExclEight reports). Reports that share data or a pipeline but are not reprints of one another were counted separately (Supplementary Section~S2).

Screening was carried out by one reviewer assisted by a large language model, which recorded a decision, a group, and the supporting passage for each record. Multi-observation and oil-palm studies and all exclusions were checked in a second pass, also run by the model but blind to the first, and the reviewer approved the decisions. On random samples screened by the first author without the model's decisions, agreement was \pHumTitles\% for titles ($\kappa$ = \kHumTitles) and \pHumElig\% for eligibility codes ($\kappa$ = \kHumElig) (Supplementary Section~S1).

The searches returned \nRetrieved records, of which \nUnique were unique and \nTitles reached title screening. Of the \nAssessed records assessed on the title and abstract, \nEligExcl were excluded, most often because fruit were detected without counting or other evaluation (criterion v, \nExclFive records). A further \nOther reports were identified outside the search, mainly from the reference lists of the closest reviews. Of the \nSought reports sought in full text, \nNotRetrieved could not be retrieved, and of the \nFTAssessed assessed in full, \nFTExcl were excluded, leaving \nIncluded studies (Fig.~\ref{fig:prisma}). Records grew from \nYearFirst in 2012 to \nYearPeak in 2025, and multi-observation studies from at most \nMultiEarlyMax per year up to 2019 to \nMultiPeak in 2025 (Fig.~\ref{fig:tahun}).

Each multi-observation study was coded from the full text for (i) association mechanism, one or more of the six defined in Section~\ref{sec:mechdefs}; (ii) acquisition design; (iii) whether counts are reported per class and, if so, how the class is attached to a unique fruit; (iv) the main result; (v) the count reference; and (vi) the level of the reported metric (count, identity, or detection). The rule that matters most for reading the results is that a study using several mechanisms was assigned each of them, so mechanism totals are occurrences, not exclusive counts. A tracker with an appearance descriptor, such as DeepSORT, counts as appearance matching combined with temporal tracking. The full coding rules are given in Supplementary Section~S2, and the codes of all \nMulti studies in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}. Oil-palm studies were coded for task, setting, modality, use of several views, and whether class labels are attached to instances.

\subsection{Appraisal and synthesis}\label{sec:appraisal}
No formal quality score was assigned. A comparison was treated as evidence on the same data only when three conditions held: the authors evaluated two or more arms on the same test images, videos, or plants; the arms differed in the tracker, an association component, the mechanism, the counting rule, or the acquisition design; and a quantitative result was reported for each arm. Comparisons of detectors alone and numbers taken from other papers were excluded. Every multi-observation method study whose full text could be extracted was read for such comparisons. For every comparison reported, the review records three properties that decide what a number can support: the count reference (hand count on the tree, harvest, packhouse, or annotated images), whether the compared methods were run on the same trees or data, and whether the result is a total count, a per-tree count, or an identity metric.

Results are given with the metric that each study used. Count agreement is reported as counting accuracy, the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), or the root-mean-square error (RMSE). Identity is reported as the multiple-object tracking accuracy (MOTA), the higher-order tracking accuracy (HOTA), the identification F1 score (IDF1), or the number of identity switches. Detection is reported as precision, recall, F1, or the average precision at an intersection-over-union threshold of 0.5 (AP50), whose mean over classes is mAP50; mAP is written without a threshold where the study did not state one. Rates and scores are given as percentages, converted where a study reported fractions, whereas F1, $R^2$, and $r$ are given as fractions.

The synthesis is a structured narrative organized by the issues on which studies agree or disagree within each stage of the design space (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). Comparisons made on the same data are collected in Tables~\ref{tab:acq} and~\ref{tab:assoc}, and descriptive statistics of the coded variables are reported once, in Section~\ref{sec:landscape}.

\section{A design space for turning observations into records}\label{sec:framework}

This section (i) describes the five stages that lead from images to a class-wise inventory, (ii) names the ways in which a fruit is counted twice, (iii) defines the six association mechanisms by the condition each assumes, and (iv) reports how the \nMulti multi-observation studies are distributed over mechanisms, acquisition designs, crops, platforms, and sensors.

\subsection{From images to an inventory}
Fig.~\ref{fig:kerangka} shows the five stages of the pipeline that ends in a class-wise inventory. Acquisition fixes how many views of each plant exist, whether they are ordered in time, and whether pose, depth, or baseline are known. Observation produces detections or masks in each image; a fruit may produce several observations, and a detector may also split one fruit or merge two. Association decides which observations belong to the same physical fruit. Attribute assignment gives the unique fruit a class, size, or mass. The inventory aggregates unique fruit by class and by tree, row, or block. Detection accuracy, which most papers report, describes the second stage. The count, which the user needs, depends on all five.

\subsection{Three sources of double counting}\label{sec:dupsources}
\citet{liu2019monocular} named three ways in which a fruit is counted twice in image sequences of a row: the same fruit detected in consecutive images; the same fruit tracked, lost, and picked up again as a new track; and the same fruit seen from the two sides of a tree. \citet{wang2019mango} took the same three sources as the starting point for their mango tracker. Misses have two sources: fruit never visible from any camera position, and fruit visible but not detected. A mechanism controls some of these sources and not others, which is why the mechanisms below are described by what they assume, not only by how they compute.

\subsection{Six association mechanisms and the conditions they assume}\label{sec:mechdefs}
\textit{No association.} Each image is counted separately. This is correct only if acquisition guarantees that no fruit appears twice, or if duplicates are accepted.

\textit{Statistical correction.} Image counts are summed or averaged and then related to hand or harvest counts by a fitted factor or regression. No individual fruit is identified. It assumes that the ratio between observed and true counts is stable across the trees to which it is applied \citep{nuske2014modeling,linker2015estimation}.

\textit{Appearance matching.} Observations are linked when their appearance descriptors are similar, either hand-crafted or learned for re-identification. It assumes that fruit differ enough in appearance to be told apart.

\textit{Temporal tracking.} Observations in consecutive frames are linked by predicted motion and overlap, usually with a Kalman filter and an assignment step \citep{bewley2016simple}; a counting line or region then decides when a track is counted. It assumes that consecutive frames overlap and that motion between them is small and smooth.

\textit{Geometric or 3D association.} Observations are linked through a common coordinate frame: triangulation with known camera poses, structure from motion (SfM), simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), registered point clouds, or an implicit 3D representation such as a neural radiance field. It assumes that poses, scale, and depth or reconstruction are accurate enough that two different fruit are not placed at the same position.

\textit{Learned association.} A network is trained to decide correspondence directly from linked examples. It assumes that training data with identity links exist and represent the conditions of use.

A study may combine mechanisms; \nCombined of the \nMethod multi-observation method studies do (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}). In tables and figures a plus sign joins the mechanisms that a study combines, for example appearance + tracking. A study that combines them should still show which one removes the duplicates, because each fails under different conditions.

\subsection{Quantitative profile of the multi-observation studies}\label{sec:landscape}
Of the \nMulti multi-observation studies, \nMultiData are dataset papers and \nMethod propose or evaluate a method; the statistics below refer to these \nMethod studies, and their crops, platforms, and sensors are given in Supplementary Section~S4. Because a study may combine mechanisms, mechanism values are occurrences.

\textit{(i) The mechanism mix has shifted from correcting sums to linking detections in video.} Temporal tracking appears in \nTrack of the \nMethod studies (\pTrack\%), geometric association in \nGeom (\pGeom\%), appearance matching in \nApp (\pApp\%), statistical correction in \nStat (\pStat\%), learned association in \nLearn, and no association in \nNone (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}a). Of the \nEarly studies published before 2018, \nEarlyGeom used geometric association, \nEarlyStat statistical correction, and \nEarlyTrack temporal tracking; since 2023, temporal tracking has appeared in \pTrackRecent\% of the \nRecent new studies, and appearance matching, mostly inside trackers that carry an appearance descriptor, in \pAppRecent\%.

\textit{(ii) Acquisition is mostly video.} Of these studies, \nVideo acquire video along a path, \nDiscrete use discrete views, \nScan use 3D scans, and \nRevisit revisit the same plants over time (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}b). Acquisition and mechanism are coupled (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}): video feeds temporal tracking alone (\nTrackOnly studies), appearance matching combined with tracking (\nAppTrack), and tracking combined with geometric association (\nTrackGeom), whereas among the \nDiscrete studies with discrete views, geometric association appears in \nDiscreteGeom and statistical correction in \nDiscreteStat.

\textit{(iii) Classes are rarely counted on unique fruit.} Only \nPerClass of the \nMethod studies report counts per class (Section~\ref{sec:class}).

\textit{(1) One view misses a share of the fruit that depends on the canopy, and adding sides raises recall at the cost of new errors.} On mango, one or two images per side recorded 27--54\% of the fruit (Section~\ref{sec:intro}; \citealp{stein2016image,wang2019mango}). Dense canopies and clustered fruit gave shares of the same order. \citet{li2025field} found that each of three views of a blueberry plant showed 47.3--53.87\% of the berries harvested from it. \citet{victorino2020yield} counted 4.3--6.3 grape bunches per metre in images of the sunlit side of non-defoliated vines, against 11.8--12.1 counted in the field. \citet{rist2018high} found 66 berries per grape bunch in one-side 3D scans, against 137 in a 360$^\circ$ scan of the same bunches.

Open canopies gave higher shares: \citet{bresilla2019single} counted 85--95\% of the apples and pears of a tree in one image taken from about 2~m, depending on the training system. \citet{silwal2016hierarchical} imaged shallow tall-spindle apple canopies from both sides and missed 38 of 1{,}827 apples (2\%), mainly fruit not visible from either side. The same count held 130 false positives (7\%), mainly single apples counted more than once. A share measured on one canopy therefore does not transfer to another.

\citet{gongal2016apple} reported that about 40\% of apples were invisible from one side of a tall-spindle row, and imaging both sides reduced their count error from 41--42\% to 18\%. The added side brings its own errors. When \citet{qureshi2023seeing} merged one-sided scans of apple fruitlets into a two-sided map, counting accuracy rose from 73.7\% to 81.17\% but precision fell from 90.7\% to 80.3\%, because misalignment of the two scans produced spurious fruitlets. \citet{gongal2016apple} found that the two registered instances of one apple were slightly displaced and that neighbouring apples overlapped in 3D, so a distance buffer was needed. In that count, apples wrongly taken as duplicates added to the undercount caused by missed apples, whereas false-positive detections and missed duplicates raised the count and offset that undercount slightly. A second side therefore converts a visibility problem into an identity problem.

\textit{(2) A calibrated count can be precise without removing duplicates.} In the study by \citet{stein2016image}, the dual-view counts correlated with the harvest counts more tightly ($R^2=0.94$) than the multi-view counts ($R^2=0.90$) but had to be divided by a slope of 0.54, whereas the multi-view slope was 1.014. \citet{payne2013estimation} made the same point with manual counts in images of 15 mango trees: one side held 38.1--45.7 fruit on average, the four sides summed to 167.3, and the trees carried 61.7, yet $R^2$ against the tree count stayed between 0.81 and 0.93 for every side and every sum. The authors stated that the stability of the dual-view calibration across blocks, orchards, varieties, and pruning regimes was not known. Where calibration was tested across orchards and seasons, it did not hold (Section~\ref{sec:mech-stat}). Correlation with a reference therefore shows that a count is repeatable but cannot show that duplicates were removed.

\textit{(3) More views help only when the extra observations are linked.} \citet{vijayakumar2023tree} found that adding the count from the second side of citrus trees to a yield model did not reduce the per-tree error (MAPE 25.72\% against 23.45\% with one side; $p=0.29$) and identified double counting between the two sides as a cause. \citet{felippomes2026video} counted apples from both sides of 420 trees with two red-green-blue-depth (RGB-D) sensors and a tracker, but processed the two sides independently, without matching fruit between them. Using both sides lowered the error per 5~m stretch (least-squares mean RMSE 46.73 apples against 58.69 and 59.36 for the single sides). Because the counts were converted to yield through a fitted correction, the duplicates between the sides were absorbed by the calibration rather than identified.

\citet{mirhaji2021fruit} summed detector counts from two or four sides of 100 orange trees. Two sides underestimated dense canopies, four sides overestimated thin ones, and the authors attributed the overestimate to oranges repeated in images taken from different angles. Choosing two or four sides by canopy area lowered the best MAPE against the harvest count from 15.0\% (four sides) to 9.4\%, so the summing rule was tuned to the canopy rather than the duplicates removed.

\citet{singh2025development} predicted blueberry yield from ripe-berry counts in images of two sides of the same plants. Averaging two predictions made from one side each gave $R^2=0.80$, whereas a prediction from the combined count of both sides gave 0.71. \citet{arizasentis2025comparative} recovered more grape bunches with multiple-view flights of an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) (27--74\% of the annotated bunches against 13--23\%) but reported that motion blur broke identity continuity in those flights. Across these studies, views add recall, and the gain in the count depends on whether the observations from different views are associated.

\textit{(4) Acquisition decides which mechanisms are possible.} Video along a row gives the frame-to-frame overlap that temporal tracking needs. Discrete views around a tree, as in SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc} or the eight direction frames that \citet{nenavath2024tree} sampled from a walk-around video of a mango tree, have wide baselines and no frame-to-frame continuity. Tracking therefore does not apply, and association must rely on appearance, geometry, or learning. \citet{zhang2022complete} avoided association by stitching UAV views taken on a ring around each holly tree into one panorama and counting fruit once on it; the share of the fruit that reached the panorama fell from 99.19\% at a radius of 0.6~m to 92.53\% at 2.0~m. Several groups have treated view selection itself as the problem \citep{roy2017active,zaenker2021viewpoint,zaenker2023graph}. Where they checked fruit estimates against a reference, they did so in simulation or on artificial trees.

Fig.~\ref{fig:hasil}a places four comparisons on one scale, the count as a percentage of the reference count: two from Table~\ref{tab:acq} and two in which linking was compared with summing (Table~\ref{tab:assoc}, rows 5 and 8). The references are harvest counts in three studies and a manual count of the video in the fourth, so values should be read within a study. Single views and one image per side fell below the reference (27--54\% of the harvest count), and summing sides or frames rose above it (103.86--170.7\%). In every study, linking moved the count towards the reference, and it came closest where a geometric step was used (95.56--101.4\%).

The acquisition design decides which errors the association step must remove: one view per side leaves fruit unseen, and every added view creates duplicates that only an association mechanism, not a calibration, can identify (Section~\ref{sec:mech}).

\subsection{No association and statistical correction}\label{sec:mech-stat}
Statistical correction replaces identity with a fitted factor: it never decides which fruit was counted twice, so it is accurate only while the ratio between observed and true counts stays as it was when the factor was fitted. It is used in \nStat of the \nMethod method studies; ``no association'' occurs in \nNone. It stands alone only where physical tags, not images, identified each bunch \citep{kassim2012oil}.

The earliest multi-image systems calibrated their counts against harvest or hand counts. \citet{nuske2014modeling} registered images from a vehicle driving along vineyard rows and related berry counts to harvest yield. Because the berries were first linked by registration, the study is counted under geometric association, and only its calibration step belongs here. With calibration data from a prior harvest, the estimate of total yield was within 4\%, and within 3\% with destructive samples taken at the time of imaging.

\citet{linker2015estimation} applied the same idea to apple trees, photographing 42 trees from two sides at three heights. The detections in the six images of a tree corresponded to about one third of its yield. After calibration on 14 trees, the overall estimate for the 42 trees was within 10\% of the actual yield, although single-tree errors were sometimes large. The authors described the result as a self-calibrating system that would need the grower to count 15--20 trees. In a different approach, \citet{song2014automatic} mapped repeated observations of pepper fruit to common coordinates and grouped them with a statistical clustering step. Regression from partial views remains common in UAV work \citep{apoloapolo2020cloud,li2023blueberry}.

Within the conditions of calibration, the method is repeatable: \citet{stein2016image} found the dual-view counts more tightly correlated with true counts than any other design. Its weakness is transfer. \citet{anderson2021estimation} found that correcting multi-view counts with an occlusion factor from hand-counted trees did not improve the estimate in 16 of 20 mango orchards and increased the average error from 11\% to 17\% in the nine orchards with all methods. The error on trellised canopies (25.2\%) was several times that on other canopy systems (3.4--8.2\%).

Models of the hidden share held only within the season or plots on which they were fitted. \citet{koirala2021attempting} tried to replace the factor with a model of hidden fruit learned from canopy features. The model reached $R^2=0.98$ against the harvest counts of 98 trees in its training season, but no visual feature correlated with the number of hidden fruit, and the models did not hold in the following season. Image features predicted the hidden share in a narrower setting: \citet{victorino2022overcoming} estimated the visible share of grape bunches (16--64\%) from canopy porosity and visible bunch area, with $R^2=0.80$ on a validation set drawn from the same vineyard plots.

The factor also changes with canopy architecture within one site. \citet{islam2022ground} fitted a separate calibration factor to grader counts at harvest for each training system of four stone-fruit crops, and the factors ranged from 0.796 to 3.875, for example 1.882 and 3.295 for two peach systems, whereas the standard error within each system was 10.21\% or less. \citet{payne2014estimating} applied one two-side imaging protocol to mango in two orchards and seasons, and the summed image counts held 84\% of the tree count with $R^2=0.51$ on 50 trees in the first and 59\% with $R^2=0.92$ on 21 trees in the second. \citet{linker2018machine} transferred an apple detector between two years with different cameras and lighting, but the count adjustments fitted on one year were slightly worse than no adjustment on the other.

Within one orchard, a learned conversion did hold across seasons. \citet{apoloapolo2020deep} trained a recurrent network on fruit counts from UAV images of the two faces of 20 orange trees, left out one of three seasons in turn, and estimated the total yield with a mean error of 7.22\%, against 13.74\% for a technician's estimate. Some studies limit duplicates by acquisition instead, either by subtracting the fruit at the corners of eight direction frames, each of which appears in two neighbouring frames \citep{nenavath2024tree}, or by projecting fruit between the cameras of a calibrated rig \citep{villacres2024assessing}.

These studies show that statistical correction gives repeatable estimates within one orchard, and that the factor fails when the canopy or the orchard changes, because the share of hidden and duplicated fruit changes with them. Between seasons, the conversion held in one study \citep{apoloapolo2020deep} and failed in two \citep{koirala2021attempting,linker2018machine}. It is the baseline against which the other mechanisms should be tested (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).

\subsection{Temporal tracking}\label{sec:mech-track}
Temporal tracking removes duplicates between consecutive frames as long as its motion model holds, and its reported failures are the conditions that break that model. It is the most common mechanism (\nTrack of the \nMethod method studies, and \pTrackRecent\% of those published since 2023; Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}a). Most studies follow tracking by detection: a detector finds fruit in each frame, a Kalman filter predicts where each track should appear next, and an assignment step links detections to tracks, as in simple online and realtime tracking (SORT; \citealp{bewley2016simple}) and ByteTrack \citep{zhang2022bytetrack}. Four issues recur.

\textit{(1) The motion model and the frame rate.} Fruit on a plant are static, so all apparent motion comes from the camera \citep{zhang2022deep,saraceni2024agrisort}. \citet{saraceni2024agrisort} built AgriSORT on this premise, estimated camera motion from optical flow, and dropped the velocity terms of the usual filter because box motion on rough vineyard terrain is not linear. In their sequence with fast non-linear motion, SORT lost almost all identities. The frame rate decides how often the filter is corrected. \citet{wei2025multi} reported that at 5 frames per second the random initialization of the filter degraded the tracks unless initial velocities were tuned for each dataset. \citet{qi2025assessment} found that tracking accuracy on tomato video fell as the frame rate was reduced. Camera shake on a legged robot \citep{zhang2024stablesort} and large displacements in low-frame-rate UAV video \citep{xieli2025pinesort} required explicit camera-motion compensation.

\textit{(2) Which failure the tracker is built to repair.} Generic trackers differ in the failure they repair, and the comparisons on the same data favour whichever repair the data needed (Table~\ref{tab:assoc}, rows 1--3). Against plain motion matching, a re-identification descriptor usually helped. \citet{piazolo2026enhanced} counted grape bunches in UAV video with a counting accuracy of 95.2\% for DeepSORT against 32.7\% for SORT, which had 73.5 identity switches against 17.1. \citet{villacres2023apple} removed annotated apple boxes at random and found that the descriptor mattered once detections went missing: with all boxes the two trackers were close (MOTA 93.0\% and 94.6\%), whereas with 60\% of boxes DeepSORT kept 69.3\% and SORT fell to 13.8\%.

Against ByteTrack, which keeps low-confidence boxes of partly occluded fruit, the result reversed between studies. On apples, ByteTrack reached MOTA 68.17\% at about 15~ms per image, against 57.35\% for DeepSORT at 128~ms, and the authors attributed the absence of a gain to the similar appearance of apples \citep{genemola2023video}. On tomato clusters, BoT-SORT with re-identification reached the highest MOTA at a constant frame rate (84.6\%), but ByteTrack gave the highest counting accuracy (95.5\%), and the re-identification trackers degraded more when the frame rate varied \citep{qi2025assessment}.

Each repair addresses a different failure: a descriptor re-links fruit after a track was lost, ByteTrack prevents some losses, and camera-motion compensation keeps the motion prediction valid on a shaking platform. Which of them wins depends on missed detections, frame rate, and platform motion, and few studies report these alongside the result. DanceTrack was built for the same reason outside agriculture, because uniform appearance and diverse motion defeat trackers that rely on either cue alone \citep{sun2022dancetrack}.

\textit{(3) The counting rule changes the count more than the tracker.} A counting rule decides when a track becomes a fruit, and counting every track identity adds a fruit at each identity switch. \citet{zhou2024advancing} counted strawberries in 30 videos with three trackers: taking the largest identity number gave relative errors of 110.2--356.5\%, and mapping identities to a counter on the same tracks gave 6.7--23.8\%. The best native tracker (StrongSORT) was not the best after the change (DeepSORT). \citet{zhang2025row} found the same reversal on 11 videos: with every identity counted, ByteTrack reached 40.12\% accuracy against 37.59\% for DeepSORT, and with a two-line filter the order flipped at 94.20\% and 96.65\%. \citet{zheng2023efficient} reported MAPE of 298.4\% for the largest identity, 75.3\% for a single line, and 8.9\% for their non-uniform counter on the same citrus tracks.

\citet{rong2023tomato} tracked tomato clusters with ByteTrack in four greenhouse videos holding 91--167 hand-counted clusters each. With three detectors, the largest identity number reached 145--361, whereas a rule that counted an identity once when it crossed a counting region gave 96--187. The authors stated that no tracker they tried solved the identity shifts and therefore rejected the largest identity as the yield. Tracker settings act in the same way. \citet{ripa2022orchard} varied the minimum track length, the track lifetime, and an extra counting region at the image edge of a DeepSORT counter on one kiwifruit video with 341 hand-counted fruit, and the count ranged from 305 to 572; the settings were tuned on that video.

A counting line without a tracker fails on its own: \citet{tenorio2021automatic} counted cherry-tomato clusters in three videos with a line rule and with a tracker, using the same detector, and the error fell from $-59$, $-30$, and $-43$ clusters to $-5$, $-2$, and $-1$. They noted that a narrow band around the line missed boxes that moved further between frames, whereas a wide band counted one cluster several times. A single line is not always better than counting identities: \citet{parico2021real} found that flickering detections made the line miss pears.

Fig.~\ref{fig:hasil}b sets out the two studies that applied several rules to the same tracks. Changing the rule moved the error by 100.2 to 376.6 percentage points for every tracker. Changing the tracker, together with the detector in one of the studies, moved it by 14.9 to 63.0 points under a counting line or a rule built by the authors. When the largest identity number was counted, the same change moved it by 175.6 and 246.3 points, because each identity switch then adds a fruit. A tracker comparison is therefore interpretable only with its counting rule stated.

\textit{(4) What tracking does not resolve.} A track survives only for a fixed time without detections: \citet{wang2019mango} kept a lost mango track for 15 frames, and a fruit hidden for longer was counted again. These repeated counts offset the missed fruit in the net count (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). Fruit seen from both sides of a row appear in two separate passes, and no tracker links them. \citet{wang2019mango} expected this source to grow in narrower canopies. Two studies handled it with geometry. \citet{liu2019monocular} dropped fruit lying behind the trunk centroid, which removed far-side duplicates but also undercounted fruit visible from one side only. \citet{wei2025multi} matched the two sides of each apple tree afterwards by position from a global positioning system, with a distance threshold of one fruit diameter.

Tracking is the tested default for video because static fruit and a moving camera fit its motion model, but its count depends on the frame rate, on the counting rule, and on a separate geometric step for the second side of the row (Section~\ref{sec:mech-geom}).

\subsection{Appearance matching}\label{sec:mech-app}
Appearance has not been tested as the only cue for identifying fruit. It contributed when combined with motion or position, and its gains were largest when the descriptor was built or trained for the fruit. Generic person re-identification descriptors improved on plain motion matching but not consistently on other trackers (Section~\ref{sec:mech-track}). Appearance matching occurs in \nApp of the \nMethod method studies, \nAppTrack of them inside trackers that carry an appearance descriptor.

Three studies trained the appearance cue on the fruit itself. \citet{kirk2021robust} trained the appearance network of a DeepSORT-based strawberry tracker with a ripeness branch and reported a count error of 3\% against 21\% for a baseline tracker. They also noted that occlusion can change the apparent ripeness of a fruit, for example when the red part is covered, and therefore pooled the class over all previous observations of a track. \citet{xia2022culling} matched pairs of fruit patches between sequence images with a trained matching network and a one-to-one assignment, reaching F1 0.816 for apples and 0.864 for oranges in identifying repeated fruit. The matching used greyscale patches, compared all pairs by brute force, and covered one side of the row. \citet{zheng2023object} combined appearance and position in a learned distance to cluster strawberry detections from overlapping UAV images.

Descriptors built for the fruit improved trackers in every comparison in which they were tested. Speeded-up robust features (SURF) with a vector of locally aggregated descriptors cut identity switches on apples from 835 to 603 against BoT-SORT \citep{yang2024automatic}. Binary keypoint descriptors (ORB) added to ByteTrack raised MOTA from 62.6\% to 63.7\% on Camellia fruit \citep{dong2025fruit}. A Siamese tracker fine-tuned on grape bunches lowered the bunch-count error from 28.3\% to 23.6\% against DeepSORT fine-tuned on the same tracks \citep{ahmedtaristizabal2024field}. Replacing the person re-identification network of DeepSORT with one trained on cherry-tomato crops lowered the count RMSE on one video from 18.57 to 11.21 \citep{meng2024real}. Each of these tested the authors' own descriptor.

\citet{zabawa2022image} matched grape bunches between three vertically overlapping images by correlation of image patches, restricted to the same image columns and to a disparity-based depth of 0.3--1.3~m. They matched bunches rather than berries because single berries were too small and too similar to tell apart, and the automatic berry count per plant agreed with the manual count at 91.6\% and 89.0\% in two training systems.

Matching between sessions shows where appearance stops working. \citet{mirbod2021field} matched apples on 30 trees between monthly imaging sessions with scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT) descriptors and camera pose. They matched 73\% of detected fruit between two passes on one day, 71--74\% between August and October, and 55\% between July and August, and did not attempt June to July because the texture of the fruitlets had changed completely. In every case appearance was paired with position or motion, consistent with work on person re-identification, where discriminative features are learned from benchmarks with many labelled identities \citep{ye2022deep}.

\subsection{Geometric or 3D association}\label{sec:mech-geom}
Geometric association is the only mechanism that has removed all three sources of double counting, including the second side of a row. It reached the reference without a fitted factor while the fruit were visible from some view, and a factor was still fitted where foliage or unthinned clusters hid fruit from every view \citep{wang2013automated}. Its failures come from the pose, scale, and depth on which it rests. It is used in \nGeom of the \nMethod method studies (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}). Four routes recur, followed by the conditions under which they fail.

\textit{(1) Triangulation with poses from navigation sensors or stereo.} \citet{wang2013automated} registered sequential stereo images from an autonomous vehicle at night. The yield error was $-3.2$\% for about 480 red-apple trees and, after calibration with a small hand-measured sample, 1.2\% for about 670 green-apple trees. \citet{stein2016image} matched mango detections between images along epipolar lines computed from a navigation system, triangulated each tracked fruit, and used light detection and ranging (LiDAR) to assign it to its tree. The multi-view count of 16 validation trees was within 1.4\% of the harvest count without per-tree calibration.

\textit{(2) Structure from motion from a single camera.} \citet{liu2018robust} tracked fruit in 2D, localized the tracks in 3D by structure from motion, and rejected double-counted tracks by their proximity in 3D. Two tracks of one fruit come from different parts of the sequence, so they are hard to detect as duplicates in the image plane but lie close together in 3D. The correction reduced the L1 count error from 593 to 203 for oranges and from 673 to 322 for apples. In their later version, the authors added trunk landmarks to separate the two sides of the tree and found that clustered fruit and the relative scale of the reconstruction prevented a fixed distance threshold from merging duplicate landmarks \citep{liu2019monocular}.

\citet{bellocchio2022novel} replaced the depth sensor with a monocular depth network. They placed tracked mango fruit in a map built from structure-from-motion poses and predicted depth, and treated a detection within 0.08~m of a mapped fruit as already counted. Against the harvest counts of 18 trees, the mean absolute error was 33.80\%, every tree was undercounted, and the depth and map modules could not be evaluated because the dataset has no ground-truth depth or fruit positions.

\textit{(3) Merging the two sides of a row.} \citet{roy2019vision} merged counts from both sides of apple rows into one reconstruction. \citet{hani2020comparative} compared that merge with a plain sum of the two sides on the same three datasets. With the ResNet50 detector, the sum gave 103.86--147.81\% of the harvested count, and the merge 95.56--97.83\% (Table~\ref{tab:assoc}).

\citet{gongal2016apple} matched apples between the two sides of a tall-spindle row by their 3D positions within a buffer of three quarters of a fruit diameter and reported an error of 21.1\% in identifying the duplicates. \citet{qureshi2023seeing} aligned the two sides of a trellis with a calibration board; misalignment produced spurious fruitlets (Section~\ref{sec:acq}). \citet{tsoulias2023situ} aligned LiDAR scans of both sides of 12 apple trees by iterative closest point and clustered fruit points into apples. Against manual counts, $R^2$ was 0.99 at a mean fruit diameter of 76.1~mm and 0.88 at 35.5~mm, which the authors attributed to fewer points per small apple under occlusion.

\textit{(4) Counting in a 3D map.} \citet{genemola2020fruitb} projected Mask R-CNN detections into an SfM point cloud of 11 apple trees and improved F1 from 0.816 to 0.881. A fruit had to be seen in at least two images to be reconstructed, which removed single-image false positives. \citet{dong2023three} reconstructed apple trees from oblique UAV images and segmented single apples in the point cloud. Against hand counts on 36 test trees, the weighted MAPE was 3.41\% in a simple multi-axis canopy, 8.63\% in a vertical wall, and 12.75\% in a vertical-axis canopy, where the authors traced the errors to occlusion and to separating single apples from clusters. \citet{ni2021three} projected berry masks from 120 images of each blueberry cluster onto a photogrammetric point cloud and merged 3D patches whose overlap exceeded 0.3 into one berry. Their count accuracy over 40 clusters was 97.3\%.

\citet{rapadorincon2023development} tracked partial point clouds of tomatoes in a 3D world model and reached a best count error of 5.06\%. In that study, similar count errors hid different tracking quality: two configurations with a MAPE of 11.35\% and 11.36\% reached a HOTA of 66.68\% and 56.09\%, which the authors attributed to the larger number of frames and tracks at the later height step. In the signed mean percentage error, under- and over-counts of different plants also cancelled each other out.

SLAM has also served as the map: detections were fused with LiDAR SLAM point clouds in a pear plot (96.2\% counting accuracy; \citealp{lee2026lidarcamera}), and object-level SLAM was built from stereo for grape counting \citep{nellithimaru2019rols}. Implicit representations are the latest variant: FruitNeRF clusters fruit points from a semantic neural radiance field \citep{meyer2024fruitnerf}, and Gaussian splatting was reported to count persimmons with 96\% accuracy \citep{hong2025fruitgaussian}. Both take camera poses from structure from motion \citep{kerbl20233d}.

\textit{Where geometry fails.} The reported failures concern the inputs, not the principle. \citet{stein2016image} traced their largest errors to missing image frames and platform oscillation, which skewed the epipolar lines, and to bunched fruit, which made nearby fruit exchange identities. They expected the approach to help with heavily bunched fruit such as grapes only if the camera could be positioned around the bunches. In the study by \citet{genemola2020fruitb}, neighbouring apples merged into one 3D detection, most remaining duplicates were apples seen from both sides of the row whose detections did not overlap enough, and SfM was too slow for real-time use. In the blueberry clusters of \citet{ni2021three}, one mask that covered two berries in a single image out of 120 was enough to merge them, and a berry whose patches overlapped less than the threshold was split in two.

\citet{meyer2025fruitnerf} attributed their lower F1 on a real apple set to noisy poses and manual registration of both sides.

Scale is undefined for monocular reconstruction unless another sensor fixes it \citep{liu2019monocular}. Foliage moves between views, which violates the static-scene assumption of SfM and SLAM. Dynamic-scene methods exist \citep{bescos2021dynaslam}, but no included study measured how much foliage motion degrades counts. Assigning fruit to trees is a separate geometric problem \citep{zineelabidine2021assigning}.

Geometric association has produced counts close to the reference without a fitted factor when pose, scale, and depth are reliable and the fruit are visible from some view, and its errors grow with fruit clustering and with any error in pose or registration. The implicit representations are still evaluated mostly on synthetic scenes and a few real trees.

\subsection{Learned association}\label{sec:mech-learn}
Learned association moves the identity decision into a trained network, which needs identity labels across observations. The included studies report gains on their own data but no advantage yet over fruit-specific geometric pipelines on shared data. A learned component decides the correspondence in \nLearn of the \nMethod method studies, in three ways.

First, a network matches fruit between scans or images: \citet{fusaro2026horticultural} matched fruit between sessions with an attention-based network on point-cloud descriptors and evaluated it on strawberries. The matching network of \citet{xia2022culling} and the learned distance of \citet{zheng2023object} (Section~\ref{sec:mech-app}) belong to the same group. Second, a network learns identity inside a 3D representation. FruitNeRF++ lifts 2D instance masks into 3D through embeddings trained with a contrastive loss. On a real apple set it reached F1 0.765, below the 0.881 of the fruit-specific method of \citet{genemola2020fruitb}, and the authors noted that occlusion by a leaf or branch can split one fruit into two instances \citep{meyer2025fruitnerf}. \citet{zhao2026adapting} combined contrastive learning with voting in a 3D Gaussian representation without explicit matching between views, and reported F1 of 0.971 and 0.977 on synthetic plum and mango scenes.

Third, a network replaces the association step of a tracker: \citet{santos2026multi} used a learned point tracker in orange video, counted 978 fruit against 879 annotated (11.26\% error), and stated that the result did not improve on their earlier pipeline. Their worst sequence (43.44\% error) came from re-identification errors that they attributed to poor pose estimation. \citet{lei2025spatio} associated apples across four sessions with pre-trained visual features, position, and a topology cost, without training a network, and reached 96.9\% counting accuracy for 1{,}790 apples.

Outside agriculture, learned association is established for people and vehicles, within one camera \citep{braso2020learning} and across cameras \citep{ristani2016performance,tang2019cityflow}, because benchmarks with identities across observations exist. The obstacle in fruit is the same: only a handful of fruit datasets in the corpus provide such identities, among them GrapeMOTS \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots}, the tomato tracking set of \citet{qi2025assessment}, the four-session orchard set of \citet{lei2025spatio}, PFuji-Size, which stores each of 615 apples on six trees as one point cloud reconstructed from images taken from both sides of the row \citep{genemola2021pfuji}, and SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}.

\subsection{How the evidence was measured}\label{sec:mech-measure}
Most of the evidence for every mechanism is a count error, and a count error cannot show that duplicates were removed: an estimate can match the reference because duplicates and misses offset each other \citep{rapadorincon2023development,wang2019mango}. Identity metrics were developed to separate the two. HOTA balances detection and association accuracy \citep{luiten2021hota}, and IDF1 was designed for applications in which identities must be preserved, within and across cameras \citep{ristani2016performance}.

Of the \nMethodMetric multi-observation method studies whose metrics were coded from the full text, \nMetIdent (\pMetIdent\%) report such a metric: MOTA, IDF1, HOTA, or the number of identity switches. Agreement between estimated and reference counts is reported in \nMetCount (\pMetCount\%), detection quality in \nMetDet (\pMetDet\%), and no quantitative metric in \nMetNone (\pMetNone\%) (Fig.~\ref{fig:metrik}). Because a study may report several types, the shares sum to more than 100\%, and \nMetCountOnly report count agreement and nothing else. Identity metrics need identity labels across observations, which video tracking datasets provide. For merging the two sides of a row or matching fruit between sessions, association was measured directly only in a few studies, such as the duplicate-identification error reported by \citet{gongal2016apple} and the matching precision reported by \citet{lei2025spatio}.

The reference also varies. Hand counts on the tree measure what the camera could have seen, harvest and packhouse counts include fruit hidden from every view, and annotated images measure only what an annotator saw. In the coded records, the count reference is annotated images or video in \nRefAnnot of the \nMethod method studies, a hand count on the plant in \nRefTree, a harvest count in \nRefHarvest, and a packhouse count in \nRefPack (a study may use more than one). In \nRefNone, the source that was read states no count reference. Results from different references are not comparable, which is why Tables~\ref{tab:acq} and~\ref{tab:assoc} report the reference or metric level for every row.

In answer to the first two questions, the included studies use six mechanisms whose assumptions are met by different acquisition designs (Section~\ref{sec:acq}), and the evidence that a mechanism controls duplicates and misses rests mostly on count agreement. Most comparisons on the same data vary the tracker or a component of the authors' own method (Table~\ref{tab:assoc}), and identity and count metrics ranked the same trackers differently \citep{qi2025assessment,ishizuka2024eggplant}. Section~\ref{sec:agenda} turns these observations into gaps.

\section{Class attributes on unique fruit}\label{sec:class}

A class-wise inventory needs one label per physical fruit, and the included studies show that labels are least stable where classes meet. A fruit at an intermediate stage is classified differently in different frames, and a tracker that splits the track when the label changes turns a class error into a duplicate. This section answers the third question by following where the label is decided: in each image, during tracking, by a vote over frames, or once in a fused model.

\textit{Labels decided in each image.} The \nClassSingle single-image class-wise counting studies detect and count fruit by class in one image. Most concern tomatoes (\nClassSingleTomato), and most classify by maturity (\nClassSingleMaturity). \citet{wang2022online} detected red and green tomatoes in a plant factory (mAP 99.3\%), \citet{ni2020deep} segmented blueberries by maturity and counted berries per cluster ($R^2=0.886$), and \citet{eron2024computer} assigned coffee cherries to ripeness classes by clustering their colour. \citet{maceachern2023detection} trained six You Only Look Once (YOLO) networks on wild blueberry images with green, red, and blue classes, and the intermediate red class had the lowest AP50 in every network (48.40--69.39\%); merging red and green into one unripe class raised mAP50 by 9.73\% on average.

\citet{shimomoto2025predicting} stitched greenhouse video of sweet pepper into one panorama per row and assigned each fruit a ripeness stage from its hue. Against a grower's counts, the automatic counts correlated at $r=0.96$ for the 1--40\% stage but only at $r=0.63$ for the 41--80\% stage. The authors ruled out disproportionate misses at that stage and attributed the gap to classification based on the visible surface: a partly occluded fruit whose visible part was mostly red could be counted in the 81--100\% stage.

The intermediate class was the weakest in other single-image studies as well. \citet{pisharody2025precise} reported a test confusion matrix over four tomato ripeness levels in which the intermediate level had a true-positive rate of 0.627, against 0.922--0.972 for the others. \citet{rahim2021highly} obtained count agreement of $R^2=0.75$ and 0.74 for half-ripened tomatoes on two test sets, against 0.90--0.94 for the ripe and green classes. \citet{ramos2017automatic} found that the semi-ripe coffee count failed their agreement criterion ($R^2=0.356$) and merged that class with the ripe fruit. These studies count classes in the image, not classes of unique fruit.

\textit{Labels carried by the tracker.} Of the \nPerClass multi-observation studies that report counts per class, \nClassUnstated do not state how the class is attached. Where it is stated, the class is most often carried by the tracker from the per-frame detections without a rule for disagreeing frames (\nClassDuring studies). The per-class errors are larger than the total error. In the strawberry study by \citet{ogundele2025real}, which used a class-aware ByteTrack, the semi-ripe class had the lowest count agreement ($R^2=0.752$, MAPE 16.25\%) against 0.950 for ripe and 0.910 for unripe fruit, and the authors attributed it to the mottled, transitional look of that stage. \citet{wang2025tomato} obtained a mean counting error of 6.6\% for all tomatoes but 12.3\%, 16.9\%, and 17.3\% for the ripe, semi-ripe, and unripe classes, and showed that fruit switching between the unripe and semi-ripe classes was one cause of duplicate counts.

Two studies moved the class decision away from single frames. \citet{kirk2021robust} used a class description pooled over all earlier observations of a track as a matching cue for re-identification, because occlusion can change the apparent ripeness of a strawberry. \citet{zheng2025robust} instead assigned the class after tracking and counted ripe and unripe tomatoes with accuracies of 89.74--93.22\% on three beds.

\textit{Labels combined across frames.} An explicit rule that combines the labels of one fruit from different frames is stated in \nClassVote studies, either as a vote or as an average. \citet{lin2026tomato} tracked tomatoes and gave each tomato the maturity class with the most votes over its frames. With boxes taken from the annotations, 38--81\% of the boxes were assigned the correct class after the vote, depending on the dataset, classifier, and features. The authors reported that the vote reduced the effect of transient errors. The same rule appears in \citet{qi2024improved}, who gave each tomato cluster the ripeness level that occurred most often across frames, and in \citet{bolouri2024cottonsense}, who tracked cotton fruit in four growth stages and gave each fruit the label that was most frequent across its frames.

Other studies averaged the label instead of voting. \citet{tenorio2021automatic} averaged the maturity of each tracked tomato cluster over the frames in which it was matched. \citet{rong2025end} updated the maturity of each tracked cherry-tomato cluster as a moving average over frames, with a weight of 0.5 for frames in which the cluster was predicted unoccluded and 0.1 for occluded frames. Their count in the denser of two greenhouses reached 87.1\% of 202 hand-counted clusters in total but 61.4\% in the 40--60\% maturity bin. \citet{pai2024maturity}, by contrast, cited inconsistent labels of the same tomato across frames as the reason for a vote, but voted among models on single boxes, without tracking.

\textit{Labels decided once in a fused model.} Geometric association offers a route without frame-to-frame disagreement, because each fruit appears once in the fused model. \citet{zhang2022measurement} registered two views of each greenhouse tomato plant into one coloured point cloud, detected fruit in it (precision 86.19\%, recall 83.39\% against 277 fruit on 50 plants), and classified the points of each fruit into four ripening stages. The label was decided once per fruit, but the classifier was trained and tested on separate image samples (96.09\% accuracy), not checked against reference classes of the fused fruit.

In the blueberry reconstruction of \citet{ni2021three} (Section~\ref{sec:mech-geom}), each 3D berry was called mature when more of its patches came from masks classified as mature than as immature. The result was checked as the mature share per cluster ($R^2=0.908$ against manual classification of 40 clusters), not per berry. The earlier report of \citet{ni2020blueberry} applied the same patch vote.

\textit{What follows.} This evidence has two consequences. First, class and identity errors interact: a class-aware tracker that splits a track when the label flips creates a duplicate, and a vote, an average, or a fused model removes the flip only if association has already gathered the observations of one fruit. In only \nClassVote of the \nPerClass studies is a rule stated for disagreeing frames, and none reports a class confusion matrix computed on unique fruit. Second, the intermediate class is the weak point, and the oil-palm census shares it. The SawitMVC authors noted irreducible label ambiguity at the boundary between the two-month and three-month classes \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. A bunch detector trained on images that each held one class lost up to half of its mAP50 on videos that mixed ripeness classes \citep{junior2023video}. An inventory that reports only a total hides both effects (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).

\section{Depth and other modalities}\label{sec:depth}

Depth can serve two stages of the design space, observation and association, and the evidence for the second is conditional. Depth places observations of one fruit together only when its error is small relative to the spacing between fruit, and that error grows with range, sunlight, and imperfect registration. The \nDepth depth and 3D sensing studies use RGB-D cameras (\nDepthRgbd), stereo (\nDepthStereo), LiDAR (\nDepthLidar), or signals other than red-green-blue (RGB) images, mostly on apples (\nDepthApple), and their main use is observation.

\textit{Depth for observation.} Depth removes background and adds a channel to the detector. \citet{afonso2020tomato} used depth after inference to discard tomatoes from the row behind, and \citet{barbole2024comparative} masked grape clusters using depth before segmentation (pixel accuracy up to 94.81\%). \citet{devanna2025yield} removed depth points beyond a threshold as bunches of farther rows and counted grape bunches in images of one side of 13 vines. The count differed from the visual count of the same images by 12.2\% but from the post-harvest count by 33.9\% ($R^2=0.073$), which shows how much of the error lies in bunches that no image of that side contains. Fusing aligned RGB and near-infrared images raised the average precision of a kiwifruit detector from 88.4\% to 90.7\% \citep{liu2020improved}, and fusing RGB and depth gave AP50 96.1\% for pears \citep{ling2026boosting}.

Comparisons on the same data show that the gain depends on how depth enters the detector. On peaches from an Azure Kinect, adding depth to RGB changed mAP from 93.3\% to 92.7\% for unbagged and from 72.4\% to 72.3\% for bagged fruit, whereas adding infrared raised both \citep{luo2022multi}. \citet{chen2024mlp} reported mAP50 of 95.24\% for RGB tomato images from an Azure Kinect, 91.02\% with depth and near-infrared added as input channels, and 95.48\% with fusion at the feature level, and attributed the loss to noise in the added channels. On 1{,}000 apple images from a RealSense D455, \citet{kaukab2024improving} reported AP50 of 91.5\% for RGB, 36.3\% for depth alone, and 92.5\% for RGB and raw depth concatenated. The value rose to 96.4\% only after the depth holes were filled and the background beyond 1~m was removed.

Depth that suppressed fruit of the next row also changed the counts after tracking. \citet{rong2023tomato} added depth as a fourth input channel to a tomato-cluster detector and raised mAP50 from 85.8\% to 89.0\% when training from scratch. With that detector, the largest track identity fell in every test video, for example from 313 to 290, because fewer clusters on the opposite ridge were detected. \citet{wu2023ndmfcs} removed apples of the back row with a depth threshold of 3.0~m before tracking along a fruiting wall, and on the same 10 videos the identity-switch rate fell from 4.6\% to 3.5\% and the counting accuracy against manual counts rose by 4.2\% to 95.0\%.

Depth separates touching fruit only with a suitable grouping step. On 637 test citrus in Kinect V2 images, region growing and Euclidean clustering of depth points grouped adjacent fruit of similar depth into one cluster (F1 0.6950 and 0.6633), whereas density clustering after a depth filter reached 0.9197 \citep{lin2019field}. \citet{devanna2025boosting} separated grape bunches by depth gradient with an instance mAP of 59.0\%, against 51.5\% for a segmentation method without depth, but noisy depth split the largest bunches into fragments. Fitting spheres to clustered peach point clouds raised recall for touching fruit from 69.23\% to 89.74\% over direct segmentation, whereas fruit partly hidden by leaves stayed at 74.29\% \citep{wu2019automatic}.

Depth also yields metric size, with RMSE of about 4--5~mm for on-tree mangoes \citep{wang2017tree} and 2.35~mm for apple fruitlets \citep{sapkota2024immature}. In the field, the width and length of grape clusters measured from RealSense D435 depth at 1.5~m had mean absolute errors of 2.9~cm in a controlled outdoor setup and 3.6~cm in a commercial vineyard, with lower errors in images taken with a background behind the clusters \citep{kurtser2020field}. \citet{neupane2021evaluation} compared eight depth cameras against a 20~mm error requirement for fruit localization and sizing.

\textit{Depth for association.} In association, depth enters through geometric association (Section~\ref{sec:mech-geom}). \citet{liu2019monocular} used the depth of each fruit relative to the trunk to reject far-side duplicates. \citet{zheng2025robust} filtered detections by depth and used robot odometry in inter-frame prediction for tomatoes. \citet{wei2025multi} matched apples from the two sides of a tree when their positions were closer than one fruit diameter (70--85~mm).

Such a rule needs position errors below the fruit size. For four stereo cameras, the depth residual grew with the distance between camera and object \citep{neupane2021evaluation}. On apple trees, the pooled standard deviation of the localization error of six RGB-D cameras against a LiDAR reference was about 9.15~mm at 0.8~m and 14.14~mm at 1.6~m (300 tests at each distance; \citealp{fu2026mt}). An oil-palm dataset of bunches on the palm, recorded with a stereo depth camera, accepted depth readings within $\pm$10~cm of a tape measurement at 1.5--2.0~m and within $\pm$20~cm at 3.0--4.0~m \citep{goh2025outdoor}.

\citet{wang2017tree} found that their RGB-D camera could not be used in direct sunshine. \citet{choi2017performance} attributed noise in Kinect v2 depth maps of immature citrus to sunlight; with the same camera and scenes, the depth algorithm found 84.1\% of 492 fruit, against 87.2\% for RGB and 96.0\% for near-infrared. The limit depends on the sensing principle: \citet{parr2023grape} recorded time-of-flight depth of grape bunches with a smartphone in direct sunlight, whereas the structured-light camera of earlier grape studies had not worked in sunlight. \citet{qureshi2023seeing} reported that sun glare degraded stereo matching and that wind disrupted registration, which explains why their platform straddled the canopy. On 420 apple trees, the Azure Kinect gave more stable results across scanning dates than the ZED~2 \citep{felippomes2026video}.

\textit{Depth without a depth sensor.} Monocular depth networks now predict depth from a single image \citep{ranftl2021vision}. One included study paired RGB images with predicted depth to train a detector for \textit{Camellia oleifera} fruit (mAP50 93.33\%) \citep{chen2026ciafnet}. One study used predicted depth for association against a counted reference. \citet{bellocchio2022novel} merged mango detections in a map built from predicted depth and structure-from-motion poses (Section~\ref{sec:mech-geom}) and reported a mean absolute error of 33.80\% against the harvest counts of 18 trees. The depth step was not evaluated separately, so the share of that error due to predicted depth is unknown.

\citet{ma2026gdyey} used predicted depth in single images to drop small, distant grape bunches, on the grounds that such bunches appear in other images and would be counted twice; the rule removes candidates without linking them. On 50 images against a weighed yield of 206.2~kg, the mean absolute error per image was 2.02~kg from counts alone and 1.01~kg with the depth-based estimate.

The evidence therefore supports depth as an aid to removing background and, with a suitable grouping step, to separating neighbouring fruit at close range. As an extra detector input, its gain depended on how the depth was cleaned and fused. It serves geometric association when its error is small relative to fruit spacing. For tall crops imaged from the ground, the reported growth of depth error with range is the condition to check first (Section~\ref{sec:palm}).

\section{Oil palm}\label{sec:palm}

A bunch-level census of oil palm photographed from a few positions around each palm removes what temporal tracking and appearance matching rely on and leaves geometric association untested. The oil-palm imaging literature has so far built detectors that are evaluated image by image: high scores come from harvested bunches, and they fall when classes are mixed in one frame and when bunches hang on standing palms. This section answers the fourth question by first stating why the census is difficult, then tracing how detection has developed, and finally stating what one linked dataset shows and what is missing.

\subsection{Why the census challenges each mechanism}
The census images themselves show why each mechanism is strained (Fig.~\ref{fig:fotosawit}). In the four photographs of one palm in SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}, the same bunch appears in several of them, at a different position in each and partly behind fronds, and its boxes share a colour only because annotators linked them. Among the bunch crops, the two-month and three-month bunches, whose boundary the dataset authors described as ambiguous, are both dark and partly hidden.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{F11_foto_sawit}
\caption{Oil-palm census imagery in SawitMVC: (a) one palm photographed from four sides, with the boxes of one bunch drawn in the same colour in every photograph; (b) bunch crops of the four census classes, from about one month (B1) to about four months (B4) before harvest. Adapted from \citet{indriani2026sawitmvc} under a CC BY 4.0 licence.}\label{fig:fotosawit}

Bunches of the same class on one palm look alike, which weakens appearance matching (Section~\ref{sec:mech-app}). When a census photographs a palm from a few discrete positions around the trunk, as in SawitMVC \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}, the frame-to-frame continuity that temporal tracking needs is lost (Section~\ref{sec:mech-track}); video recorded while circling a palm keeps it \citep{suharjito2025oil}. Fronds occlude bunches differently from each side: in SawitMVC, 25.4\% of bunches were annotated in the photograph from only one side.

The bunches also sit metres from a ground camera, and indoor measurements with four stereo cameras showed that the depth residual grew with the distance between camera and object \citep{neupane2021evaluation}. An RGB-D dataset of bunches on the palm accepted depth readings within $\pm$20~cm of a tape measurement at 3.0--4.0~m \citep{goh2025outdoor}, and no included study measured whether such errors allow neighbouring bunches to be told apart (Section~\ref{sec:depth}). Statistical correction needs two stable ratios: how many times a bunch is seen, which SawitMVC measured, and how many bunches are hidden from every side, which no included study measured for oil palm. Neighbouring census classes differ in colour, size, and structure \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}, so a class error on one view can split one bunch into two records of different classes (Section~\ref{sec:class}).

\subsection{How detection developed}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sawit} shows that the \nPalm oil-palm studies concentrate on ripeness. Of these, \nPalmCls classify the ripeness of images of single bunches, \nPalmDet detect bunches in a scene, \nPalmSensor study non-RGB sensing of ripeness, and \nPalmQual address quality, defects, mass, or pose; \nPalmLoose detect loose fruit, \nPalmCount count bunches or fruit in single images, and \nPalmData are dataset papers. The setting is on the palm in \nPalmOnTree studies, at a mill, conveyor, or laboratory in \nPalmMill, and on the ground or at a collection point in \nPalmGround; \nPalmUnstated sources do not state it, and \nPalmUav used images taken from a UAV. More than one view of the same bunch or palm is used in \nPalmViews, for example three to four photographs of each bunch \citep{omar2024outdoor}.

Six further oil-palm reports, five studies that link observations across images and the SawitMVC dataset, were coded with the multi-observation studies and are discussed below. SawitMVC is counted among the method studies, not the dataset papers, because it also reports a counting baseline with a fitted duplication ratio.

\textit{From colour features to whole-image classifiers.} Early work classified images of single bunches from colour features with neural networks \citep{fadilah2012intelligent} or support vector machines. \citet{sabri2018evaluation} found the YCbCr and YUV colour models, which separate brightness from colour, better than six others on about 500 bunch images. Convolutional classifiers followed, often on mobile devices. \citet{puttinaovarat2024oil} classified on-tree bunches as ripe or unripe with 96.12\% accuracy, whereas \citet{usman2026integration} reported 94.66\% $\pm$ 0.54\% over four classes in leave-site-out cross-validation on 10{,}000 photographs. Non-RGB sensing (thermal, hyperspectral, laser bio-speckle, and light-emitting-diode multispectral imaging) was tested mostly on harvested bunches \citep{makky2021pre,zolfagharnassab2022classification,bensaeed2014oil,salambue2018investigation,shiddiq2024multispectral}. A classifier assigns one class to a whole image, which presumes that the image holds one bunch.

\textit{Detectors on video frames and field images.} Detection of several bunches per image appeared with two-stage detectors, which counted bunches in images collected from the internet \citep{prasetyo2020automatic} and on conveyors \citep{aji2021automatic}. It became common with single-stage YOLO detectors. \citet{junior2023video} trained YOLOv4 variants on smartphone video of harvested bunches at a grading site. Their best model reached mAP50 97.64\% on test videos of one class, whereas the best model on videos mixing classes reached 70.21\%, which the authors traced to training images that each held one class. \citet{suharjito2023real} graded piles of harvested bunches with YOLOv4 (test mAP50 up to 99.98\% on a frame-level split), but quantized models on a phone reached an average F1 of only 0.52--0.64 on new videos, and the underripe and ripe classes were the least consistent.

YOLOv8 then became the common detector. \citet{noordin2025real} reported mAP 95.2\% for a YOLOv8n model on a handheld device. The authors trained and evaluated the model on 4{,}194 images without stating which split the score refers to, and noted lower accuracy on palms taller than about six feet. \citet{naftali2024palm} detected bunches in five classes in frames from 440 plantation videos with a depthwise-separable YOLOv8s (mAP50 75\%, with videos kept apart between training and test), and the same model reached 99.4\% on an earlier dataset of harvested bunches. The plantation data were later released with mAP50 73.5--74.5\% for YOLOv8n and YOLOv8s on palms 1--3~m tall \citep{suharjito2025oil}.

With one class, scores on standing palms were higher. \citet{lai2022real} photographed 49 palms at bunch height from 3~m, kept the images of each palm in either the training or the validation set, and reported mAP 87.9\% for ripe bunches on 20 palms in another area of the estate. \citet{jumrat2026detection} reached mAP50 98.8\% for three classes on 427 images under industrial conditions, with most errors between half-ripe and ripe bunches. \citet{hutapea2025comparison} reported mAP50 99.5\% for YOLOv8s on training data from 80 of 250 images. On SawitMVC, an untuned YOLO26m reached AP50 53.1\% on 141 test palms photographed from four or eight sides, with per-class AP50 of 35.4--73.9\% \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}.

Scores near 99\% come from harvested bunches and close-ups. They fall when classes are mixed in one frame, when quantized models run on a phone on new videos \citep{suharjito2023real}, or when the bunches hang on standing palms, where one plantation dataset gave about 75\% for five classes and SawitMVC gave 53.1\% for four, against 87.9\% for one ripe class \citep{lai2022real}. Setting, split protocol, and deployment change together in these studies, so the drop cannot be assigned to any one of them.

Class errors occurred between ripe and underripe or half-ripe bunches in harvested data \citep{suharjito2023real,jumrat2026detection}. In phone images from farmers' fields, the third of four ripeness fractions was classified correctly in 47\% of test cases, against 75--89\% for the others \citep{harmiansyah2024detection}, whereas on standing palms the least ripe class, the smallest bunches, had the lowest AP50 \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. Every one of these detectors is evaluated on single images or frames.

\subsection{Counting, the one linked dataset, and what is missing}
Counting is the gap. The \nPalmCount single-image counting studies on oil palm counted bunches in plantation video frames \citep{naftali2024palm}, bunches in images collected from the internet \citep{prasetyo2020automatic}, and fruit within bunches \citep{hamdani2026automated}. Counting per frame does not produce a census, because a bunch visible in several frames is counted in each. Five oil-palm studies link observations across images. Two counted harvested bunches on a conveyor with a detector and a centroid tracker \citep{shiddiq2023counting,aji2021automatic}. Two tracked the palm rather than the bunch and took the most frequent per-frame count of the loose fruit under it \citep{daud2022loose,daud2023detection}. One linked repeated photographs of 809 tagged bunches through the tag \citep{kassim2012oil}. None of them associates bunches automatically across views of the same palm.

The closest case is a stereo pair: \citet{warni2026stereo} matched detections of loose fruit on the ground between the left and right images of a stereo camera with the Hungarian algorithm, so that one fruit was not counted twice. At distances of 20--120~cm, their MAPE in distance was 0.86--2.84\% depending on camera height. That range is shorter than the 3.0--4.0~m at which an RGB-D dataset of bunches on the palm accepted depth errors of $\pm$20~cm \citep{goh2025outdoor}. The plantation dataset reported by \citet{suharjito2025oil} was recorded around each palm, but its released boxes are not linked across frames.

The only oil-palm dataset in the corpus with cross-view identity links is SawitMVC, in which a bunch has a box in 1.89 photographs on average (18{,}540 boxes for 9{,}823 unique bunches on 953 palms). \citet{indriani2026sawitmvc} counted the bunches on 141 test palms per class with two deliberately untuned baselines. Summing the annotated boxes from all sides placed 50.00\% of the per-class counts within one bunch of the reference. Dividing that sum by the ratio of boxes to bunches in the training split (1.89) raised the share to 95.57\% (mean absolute error per palm and class, averaged over classes, 0.356 bunches). A support vector regression on per-side counts raised it to 96.81\% (0.303). Counts for all four classes of a palm were within one bunch on 86.52\% and 88.65\% of palms.

With boxes from the detector instead of the annotations, the ratio and the regression placed 72.34\% and 75.35\% of per-class counts within one bunch, and all four classes on 30.50\% and 33.33\% of palms. The authors concluded that detection, rather than the counting stage, was the dominant source of error.

These baselines are the reference that an association method has to beat, within limits that the dataset states. A within-one-bunch criterion is lenient when a test palm carries on average 0.85 to 5.28 bunches per class. The duplication ratio was also fitted and tested on palms from the same two plantations and the same month, so it is the in-distribution case of the statistical correction that failed to transfer elsewhere (Section~\ref{sec:mech-stat}). The ratio was fitted on annotated boxes and applied unchanged to detected boxes, where the mean signed error rose from $+0.009$ to $+0.381$ bunches, so the drop with the detector mixes detection error with a ratio that does not match the detector's output.

The reference counts bunches annotated in at least one photograph, not bunches hidden from every side, so none of these numbers measures a full census. The dataset also comes from one collection period, mostly one variety (89.6\% of palms), and ten phone models, and its authors reported an ambiguous boundary between two of the four classes without a measure of inter-annotator agreement. The missing experiment is therefore an association method evaluated on palms photographed from several sides, with identity links, census classes, and a per-class count reference. It should be compared with the ratio and regression baselines on both annotated and detected boxes, with the ratio refitted on detector output. The method should also be tested on another plantation or season and checked against a count of all bunches on a subset of palms (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:agenda}

The evidence reviewed in Sections~\ref{sec:acq} to~\ref{sec:palm} supports one argument: the count a system reports is decided by the step that links observations of the same fruit, and the conditions that step assumes are set by how the images were taken. This section traces how the choice of mechanism has shifted, answers the four questions, positions the review relative to earlier reviews, and states five gaps with the measurement that would close each.

\subsection{How the choice of mechanism has shifted}
Before 2018, geometric association and statistical correction accounted for almost all multi-observation studies, whereas since 2023 temporal tracking has appeared in \pTrackRecent\% of new studies (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}a). Within geometric association, implicit 3D representations are the latest variant, and in \nLearn studies part of the association is learned (Sections~\ref{sec:mech-geom} and~\ref{sec:mech-learn}). The trajectory runs from correcting a sum to linking detections in video and then to placing fruit in a 3D frame. Class-wise identity has not followed: only \nPerClass of the \nMethod method studies report counts per class (Section~\ref{sec:class}).

\subsection{Answers to the four questions}
\textit{Which mechanisms have been used, and what does each assume?} Six mechanisms are in use, and each removes duplicates only while its assumption holds (Table~\ref{tab:mechsyn}). Statistical correction assumes a stable ratio between observed and true counts. The ratio changed with orchard and canopy \citep{anderson2021estimation,islam2022ground}, and between seasons it held in one study and failed in two (Section~\ref{sec:mech-stat}), so the factor must be refitted and cannot identify which fruit was counted twice. Temporal tracking assumes ordered, overlapping frames and a camera whose motion the filter can predict. Its reported failures arise from low frame rates, camera shake, occlusions longer than the track lifetime, and the second side of the row (Section~\ref{sec:mech-track}).

Appearance matching assumes fruit that can be told apart, a condition that fruit of one class on one plant often fail to meet. Generic descriptors helped mainly by re-linking fruit after missed detections, whereas descriptors built for the fruit were tested only by their authors. Geometric association assumes reliable pose, scale, and depth and a static scene. It is the only mechanism that removed duplicates between the two sides of a row, and its failures come from missing frames, oscillation, clustered fruit, and misregistration. Learned association assumes identity-labelled training data that resemble the conditions of use, which few fruit datasets provide. The mechanisms are therefore not interchangeable: the acquisition protocol determines which assumptions can hold before any method is chosen (Section~\ref{sec:acq}).

\textit{How well does each mechanism control duplicates and misses, and how was that measured?} The comparisons on the same data give four findings (Table~\ref{tab:assoc}). First, no tracker family won consistently: a generic appearance descriptor improved on plain motion matching, especially when detections went missing, but its ranking against ByteTrack reversed between studies and between identity and count metrics \citep{villacres2023apple,genemola2023video,qi2025assessment}. Second, where both were varied, the counting rule changed the error more than the tracker and reversed the ranking of trackers \citep{zhou2024advancing,zhang2025row}. Third, a geometric step after tracking or summation removed duplicates that the earlier step left, including fruit seen from both sides of a row \citep{liu2018robust,hani2020comparative}.

Fourth, the net count error hides errors that cancel: repeated counts and missed fruit offset each other in a mango tracker \citep{wang2019mango}, and over- and under-counts of different plants cancelled in a 3D tracker \citep{rapadorincon2023development}. Because most studies report only count agreement against references that range from annotated images to harvest counts, the evidence for any mechanism is weaker than its count errors suggest (Section~\ref{sec:mech-measure}).

\textit{How are classes attached to unique fruit?} Mostly by carrying a per-detection class along a track. Per-class errors exceed the total error, and the intermediate class was the weakest in tracked and single-image counts alike \citep{ogundele2025real,shimomoto2025predicting,rong2025end,maceachern2023detection,pisharody2025precise,rahim2021highly,ramos2017automatic}. A label that flips between neighbouring classes across frames can split one fruit into two records, so class errors become identity errors \citep{wang2025tomato}. A vote or an average over frames, or a decision in a fused 3D model, avoids the flip only if association has already gathered the observations of one fruit, and only \nClassVote studies state such a rule.

\textit{What does the oil-palm literature cover, and what is missing?} Mostly ripeness detection, whose high scores come from harvested bunches and drop for multi-class detection on standing palms. Counting is done per image or on conveyors, and no study associates bunches across views of a palm (Section~\ref{sec:palm}). A census from a few discrete views removes the frame continuity and distinct appearance that tracking and appearance matching rely on. The depth accuracy that geometric association would need at bunch height is untested. On the one dataset that links bunches across sides, a duplication ratio fitted on the same plantations already gives correct per-class counts for most palms on annotated boxes \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. An association method therefore has to beat that ratio, not the naive sum, on detected boxes and on another plantation.

\subsection{Position relative to earlier reviews}\label{sec:position}
Table~\ref{tab:position} lists the sixteen earlier reviews in the map that are closest to this one. They organize the evidence by sensor and detection method \citep{gongal2015sensors}, by estimation route or methodological approach \citep{anderson2021technologies,barriguinha2021vineyard,he2022fruit,magro2023computational,rong2026research}, by input features and prediction model \citep{liu2026shaping}, by counting method \citep{huang2023survey}, or by network architecture or task \citep{koirala2019deepb,maheswari2021intelligent,mohimont2022computer,miranda2023fruit}.

The oil-palm reviews organize it by task \citep{khan2021oil}, by sensing modality \citep{lai2023oil,goh2025fresh}, or by processing stage \citep{kamal2024machine}. Two further reviews in the map, outside Table~\ref{tab:position}, organize it by network architecture \citep{pugazhendi2026advances} and, for oil palm, by capture device, features, and model \citep{naghipour2024review}. Reviews of tracking and re-identification outside agriculture \citep{luo2021multiple,ye2022deep} and of feature tracking and 3D reconstruction \citep{cao2018survey,li2025reviewb} provide the mechanisms but not the agricultural evidence.

Five of the agricultural reviews name double counting. \citet{gongal2015sensors} tie the 3D localization accuracy needed for crop-load estimation to avoiding duplicate counts, and \citet{koirala2019deepb} note that multi-view tracking is computationally complex and double-counts fruit whose track is lost under occlusion. \citet{miranda2023fruit} state that fruit must be tracked during scanning to prevent double counting and divide the remedies into tracking in video and projection into 3D. \citet{liu2026shaping} report that SORT and DeepSORT suffer identity switches and double counting under wind and high fruit density, and \citet{ma2026frontiers} describe a Kalman-filter tracker of mango detections used to remove duplicate counts.

None of them organizes the evidence by how duplicates are removed. This review organizes the evidence by the identity decision, which makes visible three things that a route-, architecture-, or task-based organization does not: the coupling between acquisition design and admissible mechanism, the dependence of class-wise counts on identity, and the missing discrete-view case. The column ``Stated method'' records what each review states about how its studies were found.

\subsection{Five gaps}
\textit{(i) Identity is measured in a minority of studies.} Only \nMetIdent of the \nMethodMetric multi-observation method studies (\pMetIdent\%) report an identity metric (Section~\ref{sec:mech-measure}, Fig.~\ref{fig:metrik}). A study that claims to remove duplicates should report, on annotated sequences or view sets, the numbers of duplicate records, missed fruit, false merges of two fruit into one, and identity switches, in addition to the count error. \citet{wang2019mango} and \citet{rapadorincon2023development} show that this is feasible.

\textit{(ii) Comparisons on the same data vary trackers more than mechanisms, and mostly test the authors' own method.} Most comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:assoc} rank trackers or add a component to the authors' pipeline, and baselines are often run with default settings. Comparisons across mechanisms, for example tracking against geometric association on the same trees, are rare, and comparisons against a calibrated statistical-correction baseline are rarer, although such a baseline is the reference method in commercial mango estimation \citep{koirala2021attempting}. A new mechanism should be tested against a statistical correction fitted on separate trees and, where possible, on data from another season.

\textit{(iii) Class-wise identity is almost absent.} Counts per class are reported in \nPerClass multi-observation studies, and in \nClassVote of them the source states how label disagreement between frames is resolved (Section~\ref{sec:class}). The required measurement is a per-class count error computed on unique fruit, together with a class confusion matrix on the same fruit.

\textit{(iv) Discrete-view protocols lack mechanisms.} Most methods assume video (Fig.~\ref{fig:mek}b and Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}). Census protocols photograph a plant from a few fixed positions, as in oil-palm imaging \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc} or in multi-sided mango and citrus imaging \citep{nenavath2024tree,zhu2024citrus}. For such data, temporal tracking does not apply, and the evidence on appearance matching, geometric association, and learned association comes mostly from other acquisition designs (Section~\ref{sec:acq}). Wide-baseline association with known or estimated pose is the missing experiment.

\textit{(v) Oil palm has detection and ripeness evidence but no identity evidence.} A bunch-level census from images needs an evaluation on palms photographed from several sides, with identity links, class labels in census classes, and a per-class count reference (Section~\ref{sec:palm}). One dataset now provides this, from two plantations in one month, with a reference limited to bunches annotated in at least one photograph \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}. A split by plantation or a second dataset is needed to test whether any counter transfers, and a count of all bunches on some palms is needed to measure what no photograph shows.

\subsection{A minimum reporting set}
The five gaps share one cause: results turned on conditions that the studies did not report. At minimum, a study that claims a duplicate-aware, class-wise inventory should report the following items, each of which changed a result in the evidence reviewed above:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item the acquisition protocol: views per plant, ordering, frame rate, platform, pose source, depth source, and baseline, because frame rate and platform motion decided which tracker won (Section~\ref{sec:mech-track});
\item the mechanism, the component that removes duplicates, and the counting rule, because changing the rule on the same tracks moved the error by 100.2 to 376.6 percentage points (Fig.~\ref{fig:hasil}b);
\item detection quality on the same images, alongside the count, because missed detections decided whether an appearance descriptor helped \citep{villacres2023apple}, and, on SawitMVC, detection rather than counting dominated the error \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc};
\item the reference type (hand count on the plant, harvest, packhouse, or annotated images) and whether it includes fruit hidden from every view (Section~\ref{sec:mech-measure});
\item the count error per plant and per class, both signed and absolute, because signed errors of different plants cancelled in a net total \citep{rapadorincon2023development};
\item identity metrics on annotated data, with the numbers of duplicate records, missed fruit, false merges, and identity switches (gap (i)); and
\item a statistical-correction baseline fitted on separate plants and, where possible, on data from another season (gap (ii)).
\end{enumerate}
Reported together, these items would let a reader attribute a change in the count to detection, association, the counting rule, or the reference, which no single count error can do.

\section{Limitations of this review}\label{sec:limits}

Seven limitations apply. First, Scopus was the only database. Work indexed only in Web of Science or IEEE Xplore, or posted only on preprint servers, is missing unless Scopus also indexes it. Second, the map rests on the reports whose full text could be retrieved. Of the \nSought reports that passed the abstract stage, \nNotRetrieved (\pNotRetrieved\%) could not be retrieved, and none of them is part of the map.

The reports not retrieved are unevenly spread across groups: multi-observation studies account for \nNotRetrievedMulti of them, single-view counting studies for \nNotRetrievedSingle, oil-palm imaging studies for \nNotRetrievedPalm, depth or non-RGB detection studies for \nNotRetrievedDepth, class-attribute studies for \nNotRetrievedClass, earlier reviews for \nNotRetrievedReview, and transferable methods for \nNotRetrievedTransfer. Of the reports found outside the search, \nOtherRetrieved of \nOther were retrieved and entered the map. The synthesis of association mechanisms therefore rests on nearly all eligible multi-observation studies, whereas the map of oil-palm imaging and the comparison with earlier reviews (Table~\ref{tab:position}) cover only the retrieved part. If retrieval depended on the publisher or the access model, the sources that were easier to obtain are over-represented there.

Third, abstracts were not available from the database export used and were retrieved from other bibliographic services. Records without an abstract (\nNoAbstract) were first assessed from titles, sources, and machine-generated summaries. Fourth, screening and coding were done by one reviewer assisted by a large language model, and the second pass at the abstract and full-text stages was also run by the model. That second pass shows how reproducible the decisions are but does not replace an independent second reviewer, and the first author's blind samples yielded only moderate agreement ($\kappa$ = \kHumTitles for titles and \kHumElig for eligibility codes).

Fifth, the title stage was the least reliable step: in a random sample of \nSampleTitles titles, \nSampleTitlesEligible excluded records met an inclusion criterion once their abstracts were read. Although the exclusions from the two core query sets were re-read from their abstracts, the map is likely to miss eligible studies among the remaining title exclusions, mainly in the supporting groups. Sixth, rule-based codes for crop, modality, and platform were spot-checked but not verified for every record. Seventh, the set of methods papers from outside agriculture is purposive, limited to the most cited records of each search. Results were not pooled, so the numbers in Tables~\ref{tab:acq} and~\ref{tab:assoc} should be compared only within a row.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

The question was how image-based fruit counting decides that observations in different images show the same fruit. Of the \nIncluded studies mapped, the \nMulti that combine observations were read in full and placed in a design space of five stages and six association mechanisms (Fig.~\ref{fig:kerangka}). The comparisons made on the same data were then read against the assumption of each mechanism (Tables~\ref{tab:mechsyn} and~\ref{tab:assoc}; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c1matrix}).

Two results are established. First, the count is set by the step that links observations, not by the detector alone: on the same data, one image per side undercounted, summing counts made over many frames overcounted, and linking moved the count towards the reference in each of the four studies that compared them (Fig.~\ref{fig:hasil}a). Second, each mechanism has a known domain of validity. A fitted factor is accurate where it was fitted, a motion-based tracker within the lifetime of a track, and a geometric merge across the two sides of a row when poses can be trusted (Sections~\ref{sec:mech-stat} to~\ref{sec:mech-geom}). Three questions remain open. The size of the errors that cancel inside a correct-looking total is unknown, because most studies report only count agreement (Section~\ref{sec:mech-measure}). It is also open whether any mechanism works on a few discrete views of a plant, and how one label should be decided for a fruit whose appearance changes between views.

In practice, the acquisition design narrows the choice. For video along a row, a motion-based tracker with an explicit counting rule is the tested default, and fruit seen from both sides need a separate geometric merge. For a few discrete views around a plant, temporal tracking does not apply. Association must rest on geometry with estimated pose, on appearance combined with position, or on learned correspondence, and should be tested against a statistical correction fitted on separate plants. For class-wise inventories, the class should be decided once per unique fruit, with the rule for disagreeing views reported. For oil palm, detectors trained and tested on standing palms, not on harvested bunches, are the starting point.

The five gaps in Section~\ref{sec:agenda} set five priorities, and most can start on data that already exist. Identity metrics (i) and comparisons across mechanisms against a statistical-correction baseline (ii) can be computed on the datasets with identity links listed in Section~\ref{sec:mech-learn}. Per-class counts with a confusion matrix on unique fruit (iii) and wide-baseline association on discrete views (iv) can be tested on SawitMVC, whose bunches carry census classes and links across four to eight sides. For the oil-palm census (v), a split of SawitMVC by plantation can test transfer between its two plantations, whereas a second season and a count of every bunch on some palms, to measure what no photograph shows, need new data \citep{indriani2026sawitmvc}.
