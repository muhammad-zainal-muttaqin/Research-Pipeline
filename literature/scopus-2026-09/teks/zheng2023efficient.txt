Abstract
The accurate detection and counting of fruits in natural environments are key steps
for the early yield estimation of orchards and the realization of smart orchard
production management. However, existing citrus counting algorithms have two
primary limitations: the performance of counting algorithms needs to be improved,
and their system operation efficiency is low in practical applications. Therefore, in
this paper, we propose a novel end‐to‐end orchard fruit counting pipeline that can
be used by multiple unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in parallel to help overcome the
above problems. First, to obtain on‐board camera images online, an innovative
UAV live broadcast platform was developed for the orchard scene. Second, for this
challenging specific scene, a detection network named Citrus‐YOLO was designed to
detect fruits in the video stream in real‐time. Then, the DeepSort algorithm was used
to assign a specific ID to each citrus fruit in the online UAV scene and track the fruits
across video frames. Finally, a nonuniform distributed counter was proposed to
correct the fruit count during the tracking process, and this can significantly reduce
the counting errors caused by tracking failure. This is the first work to realize online
and end‐to‐end counting in a field orchard environment. The experimental results
show that the F1 score and mean absolute percentage error of the method are
89.07% and 12.75%, respectively, indicating that the system can quickly and
accurately achieve fruit counting in large‐scale unstructured citrus orchards.
Although our work is discussed in the context of fruit counting, it can be extended
to the detection, tracking and counting of a variety of other objects of interest in
UAV application scenarios

INTRODUCTION
China is a large producer and consumer of fruit, and its annual fruit
planting area and output rank first in the world (He, 2020). In the
process of fruit production and processing, preproduction yield
estimation is an extremely important task. Accurate orchard yield
prediction can not only provide effective information with which to
assist managers' and farmers' decision‐making in different stages
such as harvest, storage, transportation logistics, and marketing, but
it can also assist them in making appropriate management adjust-
ments for the next production cycle to further improve orchard fruit
yield and quality. Although yield prediction is important, due to the
lack of efficient and accurate fruit yield prediction methods, it is
generally carried out by farmers based on past experience, manual

needs to be solved, and it is of great significance in respect of
improving the profit of the fruit industry.
An important prerequisite of intelligent orchard yield estimation
is the accurate computation of orchard fruit numbers. Therefore,
many scholars have devoted their research to automatic yield
mapping processes. With the development of computer vision
algorithms, fruit counting for a single image has been realized. To
extend the counting method to trees and entire orchards, some
scholars (Bargoti & Underwood, 2017; Das et al., 2015; Roy, Dong,
et al., 2018; Roy, Kislay, et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2013) collected
images or videos from one or both sides of a tree, then used
algorithms to detect and count fruits offline. In addition, in recent
years, some scholars (Apolo‐Apolo, Martínez‐Guanter, et al., 2020;
Apolo‐Apolo, Pérez‐Ruiz, et al., 2020; Häni et al., 2020) attempted to
combine three‐dimensional reconstruction algorithms such as struc-
ture from motion (SFM) and simultaneous localization and mapping
(SLAM) to undertake the fruit counting task. Although the above
methods have good fruit counting accuracy, they usually require
expensive equipment, and a step‐by‐step and complex processing
flow. In particular, it can take more than 10 h to process three‐
dimensional information during the specific steps of such methods,
which
leads
to
an
inability
to
perform
subsequent
counting
operations coherently. That is, each step of the yield estimation
algorithms is separated, thus affecting the operational efficiency of
orchard counting/yield estimation. Moreover, such methods cannot
meet the requirements of efficient and real‐time operations in smart
agriculture in the future.
In existing fruit counting research, image acquisition has often
been realized with cameras held by people or attached to cars. This
means that the labor is still inefficient, because the photography
requires a specific platform, resulting in a time‐consuming method
and possible aggravation of soil compaction problems (Apolo‐Apolo,
Martínez‐Guanter, et al., 2020). In contrast, using an unmanned aerial
vehicle (UAV), with its high flexibility and low cost, is a good solution.
At present, UAVs have been successfully applied to precision
agriculture for monitoring and counting animals and fruits (Apolo‐
Apolo, Martínez‐Guanter, et al., 2020; Apolo‐Apolo, Pérez‐Ruiz, et al.,
2020; Beltrán‐Esteve & Reig‐Martínez, 2014; Girshick, Donahue,
et al., 2014; Hodgson et al., 2016; Martínez et al., 2016; Méndez
et al., 2019; Rivas et al., 2018). The above studies have proved that
using UAVs for agricultural tasks is an efficient and promising
method. Images from UAVs can provide unique and rich perspectives
and information, so as to realize the detection and counting of citrus
fruits in large‐scale unstructured orchards. However, in most studies,
the counting results are obtained through the offline processing of
UAV images or videos acquired in advance. This kind of method often
has shortcomings such as a complex operation process and inability
to work in real‐time. In addition, existing research has focused on the
development of algorithms, while ignoring the embedding of data
transmission between sensors and processors in the counting system,
which makes it impossible to achieve a complete end‐to‐end
production estimation system. These methods are not only complex
but are also unable to directly make corresponding adjustments
during field experiments to obtain the optimal counting accuracy.
Therefore, in the actual operation process, we found it necessary
to develop an end‐to‐end yield estimation system for the following
three reasons:
①Adjustment in field operation: Different orchards have
different unstructured environment characteristics, such as differ-
ences in tree height and shape, fruit tree density, fruit shape and
color, light conditions, wind speed, and other factors. In the actual
counting operation, according to the characteristics of the orchard
environment, we often need to make multiple adjustments (such as
flight speed, camera distance, and flight height) to obtain the best
estimation
accuracy.
However,
when
we
make
corresponding
adjustments to the collected video and then move to the orchard's
on‐site laboratory, it is often difficult to ensure that the above
environmental factors are consistent with the previous video
collection. This will lead to difficulty in obtaining the best estimation
accuracy in line with the on‐site experimental environment. By using
the end‐to‐end production estimation system proposed in this study,
we can not only greatly reduce the processing time required to obtain
accurate results, but also directly and quickly make corresponding
adjustments on site to obtain the optimal production estimation
accuracy (Apolo Apolo, Martínez‐Guanter, et al., 2020).
②Timeliness of counting operation: The time from citrus
maturity to picking is generally 30–50 days. If the fruit is harvested
too late, it can become soft and vulnerable to damage, and there may
be increased fruit dropping due to the decline of the peel hardness. In
addition, late harvesting affects the storage and shelf life of fruits,
causing greater economic losses (Gao et al., 2022; INRA & DIJON,
2015). Therefore, there is a requirement for us to complete high‐
precision orchard yield estimation more quickly, so as not to delay
citrus picking, sales, and the formulation of related strategies. It
should be noted that the time required for counting includes not only
the time for algorithm processing but also the time for hardware
adjustment and algorithm optimization mentioned in point ①.
③Later extension: The realization of an orchard counting system
with multiple UAVs can not only greatly improve the operation
efficiency of yield estimation, but can also help to integrate the UAVs
into a complete closed‐loop platform and constantly improve the
system accuracy. In addition, we can combine the system with other
intelligent agricultural systems, such as fruit‐picking robots, orchard
intelligent
navigation
cars,
and
so
on,
to
feedback
effective
information in real‐time so as to better realize intelligent operations
in orchards.
Unlike previous studies, in this study emperor citrus and orange
were taken as the experimental objects, and an online detection and
counting pipeline of unstructured orchard citrus fruits based on a
UAV was proposed. Several key components of the system were

RELATED WORK
Large‐scale fruit counting in orchards is an important but challenging
task due to severe occlusion, perspective distortion, complex lighting,
and different distributions of target sizes. In recent years, many
works have been proposed to solve fruit counting problems in
various situations (Chen et al., 2020). The counting methods of these
works can be divided into those based on density maps and those
based on object numbers.
(1) Counting based on density maps: Some scholars developed
methods based on deep learning to estimate the number of
humans or pigs in a single image density map (Sindagi & Patel,
2017; Tian, Guo, et al., 2019; Zan et al., 2018). These methods
expressed the counting problem as a density map estimation,
which has the advantages of dealing with server occlusion and
perspective distortion. Methods based on density maps will lose
detailed personal information and discard the accurate location
information of each target, resulting in losing the ability to
correlate targets across time and being unsuitable for video‐
based counting.
(2) Counting based on object number: With the development of
computers, cameras, and image analysis technology, many
scholars have developed fruit‐number‐based methods to esti-
mate crop yield (Apolo‐Apolo, Pérez‐Ruiz, et al., 2020). Accurate
detection and counting of fruits are the keys to realizing
intelligent yield estimation in orchards. To achieve this goal, the
following two difficult problems need to be solved.
(a) Fruit detection: Most of the current research has achieved the
detection of various fruits using traditional image processing
techniques, machine learning, or deep learning methods. How-
ever, for orchard yield estimation operations, it has been found
that in unstructured orchard environments, there are complex
lighting conditions such as direct light and backlight, and diverse
fruit growth states such as fruit overlapping and blocking by
branches and leaves, resulting in great difficulties for the accurate
detection of fruits (Zheng et al., 2021). Although deep learning
algorithms have good robustness and generalization ability, the
accuracy of existing fruit detection algorithms needs to be
further improved because of the above difficulties and the
important prerequisite of accurate orchard yield estimation, that
is, accurate fruit identification.
(b) Fruit counting: Most of the fruit counting algorithms proposed in
existing studies perform fruit counting using a single image and
cannot be applied to fruit counting for a single tree or a whole
orchard. In recent years, some scholars have photographed fruit
trees from multiple visual angles, and then used algorithms to
detect and count fruits offline. However, the accuracy of such
methods often failed to meet the requirements, due to the
problem of repeat counting of fruits. In addition, many scholars
have tried to use three‐dimensional reconstruction algorithms,
such as SFM and SLAM, combined with machine learning or
neural network technology to achieve fruit counting in orchards.
However, the equipment used in such methods was expensive,
and the real‐time performance of the algorithms was difficult to
guarantee. Therefore, a new fruit‐counting method needs to be
proposed to ensure the accuracy and real‐time performance of
the algorithm, which is very important for deployment and
application in practical work.

Fruit detection
In precision agriculture, machine learning is one of the most widely
used decision‐making technologies in yield estimation, soil manage-
ment, plant disease management, and so on. In the past decade,
machine learning has been utilized in fruit yield estimation in a
number of studies. Payne et al. (2013) proposed a pixel segmentation
method for mangoes based on RGB and YCbCr, and conducted
texture segmentation for adjacent pixels. The experimental results
showed that the squared correlation coefficient (r2) values calculated
for four different angle images and a single angle image were 0.91
and 0.74, respectively. Dorj et al. (2017) used a watershed algorithm
to segment citrus fruits after converting RGB images into HSV color
space, achieving an r2 value of 0.93. Lu et al. (2018) proposed a
method to detect immature citrus fruits in tree images using texture
and intensity distribution, with an algorithm accuracy of 80.4%. Wang
et al. (2018) developed a recognition and calculation method for
immature green citrus fruits, combing the LBP features and an
Adaboost‐based classifier, with an accuracy of 85.6%. He, Xiong,
et al. (2020) proposed a green citrus fruit detection method based on
a deep bounding box regression forest (DBBRF), with an average
identification accuracy and a time of 87.6% and 0.759 s, respectively.
The above studies mainly combine machine learning and basic
features such as color, texture, and shape to realize fruit detection

et al. (2019) used DenseNet to deal with a low‐resolution feature layer

enhanced and network performance being improved. Lu et al. (2019)
proposed a citrus recognition method based on an improved YOLOV3‐
LITE lightweight neural network, introducing a GIoU border regression
loss function and using MobileNet‐V2 as the backbone network, with
an average accuracy of 91.13%. Xiong et al. (2020) proposed a visual
detection method using a UAV and the YOLOV2 model to rapidly
detect green mangoes on the canopy surface, with the results showing
that the error rate was 1.10% by comparison with the fruit numbers of
10 mango trees calculated using manual counting and model prediction.
Liang et al. (2020) first used the YOLOV3 model combined with the
UNet network to segment fruit stem pixels, with an average detection
accuracy of 96% for litchi and a segmentation accuracy of 95% for fruit
stems. Yang et al. (2020) proposed an integrated system for detecting
citrus fruits and branches and estimating their sizes; Mask RCNN was
used as the citrus fruit recognition model with an average recognition
accuracy of 88.15%.
The above studies show that the research on fruit detection has
gradually transitioned to deep‐learning‐based methods due to their
powerful feature extraction capabilities. However, so far, research on
the application of deep learning methods to UAV videos in orchard
scenes is still in the early stages. In addition, the recognition accuracy
and real‐time performance of the existing methods still need to be
further improved.
Unlike the above methods, a network named Citrus‐YOLO was
proposed in this study to solve the detection problems in respect of
small, overlapping, and occluded citrus fruits from the viewpoint of a
UAV, and TensorRT was used to compress the model size and speed
up the algorithm for easier deployment.

Fruit counting
In recent years, many scholars have carried out research in respect of
fruit counting for a tree or an orchard. Wang et al. (2019) used a
YOLO network to detect mangoes and a Kalman filter to track
mangoes, achieving real‐time counting of mangoes with a recall rate
of 62%. Vasconez et al. (2020) used Faster R‐CNN and SSD to detect
a variety of fruits and used a Kalman filter to trace and remove
duplicate fruits. The method could only be used to calculate dozens
of fruits with low accuracy. Based on the Euclidean distance and
intersection ratio, Gao et al. (2021) used the improved Hungarian
algorithm
and
YOLOV4‐tiny
to
perform
apple
matching.
The
experimental results showed that the tracking confidence was greatly
enhanced after algorithm improvement. However, this method did
not work for a long time and only performed counting for videos
within 30 s. Mekhalfi et al. (2020) proposed a counting method using
LAB color space and Adaboost to determine the location of kiwi fruit.
The disadvantages of this method were its inability to achieve real‐
time operation and its poor robustness.
Some scholars have attempted to combine three‐dimensional spatial
information to achieve the task of fruit counting. Apolo‐Apolo, Martínez‐
Guanter, et al. (2020) and Apolo‐Apolo, Pérez‐Ruiz, et al. (2020)
developed a fruit detection system based on UAV image reconstruction,
with a standard error of 6.59% for fruit counting. Although this method
achieves good counting results, many expensive commercial software
and high‐performance computers are used to process precaptured UAV
images. However, the operation of the method was complicated and it
was unable to meet the requirements of real‐time working. Häni et al.
(2020) first used the Gaussian mixed model (GMM) algorithm to cluster
and segment apples, then used the SFM to reconstruct the point cloud of
fruits and finally completed fruit counting for an orchard. Liu et al. (2018)
developed a fruit counting pipeline using a monocular camera to achieve
fruit tracking and eliminate repeating fruits with FCM, a Kalman filter and
the SFM algorithm. Gené‐Mola et al. (2020) achieved accurate counting
of fruits on both sides of a row of trees utilizing mask R‐CNN and SFM.
Méndez et al. (2019) used 3D lidar for modeling and the K‐means
algorithm for fruit detection and counting. In the case of tree pruning,
there was a significant regression relationship (R2 = 0.63, p = 0.01)
between the actual number and the number determined by the
algorithm. However, the algorithm was slow and unstable in a complex
environment. Although the above methods can effectively eliminate
duplicate counting of fruits using 3D information, they had the
disadvantages of being slow, unable to operate in real‐time and requiring
expensive sensors.
In addition, some scholars have proved that images captured by
UAVs can be used to monitor and count animals and fruits (Apolo‐
Apolo, Martínez‐Guanter, et al., 2020; Apolo‐Apolo, Pérez‐Ruiz, et al.,
2020; Hodgson et al., 2016; Méndez et al., 2019; Rivas et al., 2018).
The counting of orchard fruits based on aerial views is a promising
approach. Counting of fruits in large‐scale unstructured orchards is
difficult without developing algorithms for scenarios such as complex
lighting, small targets, severe occlusion, overlapping, and double‐
counting trajectories.
In contrast to these works, we learned from the approaches
proposed by Liu et al. (2018), Wang et al. (2019), and Vasconez et al.
(2020), whose systems detected and calculated the number of apples
in a single image and tracked fruits with image series using estimated
camera movement. Based on these methods, we proposed a new
citrus counting system based on a UAV with abilities of accurate
online detection and counting for a large‐scale unstructured citrus

MATERIALS AND METHODS
A schematic diagram of the proposed citrus counting system for
large‐scale orchards is shown in Figure 1. The pipeline consists of a
UAV system, a control transmission system, and a visual counting
system. During the counting operation, the UAV system transmits
high‐quality video to Android devices LightBridge, OcuSync, and
other transmission protocols. Then, the control transmission system
pushes the video stream to the Nginx server. Finally, the visual
counting system processes the video stream to realize the detection
and counting of orchard fruits. The details of each process will be
introduced later.
3.1
|
Hardware
The UAV models used in this study were the Phantom 4 Pro v2 and
the Phantom 4 Advanced (DJI Technology Co., Ltd.). The Phantom
4 series comprises popular and intelligent consumer‐grade aerial
photography UAVs. DJI GO 4 software on a mobile phone can not
only be used to obtain flight and sensor parameters, and so on, but it
can also be used to customize the RTMP interface to push streams to
third parties for live broadcast services. The idea of live broadcasting
was used to complete the online collection and transmission of citrus
videos. A personal PC with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060,
11.0 GRAM and Ubuntu18.04OS was used as the image acquisition
platform.

Vision solution
Starting from video streaming acquisition by the UAV's onboard
camera, four stages were required to achieve citrus counting in the
orchard. In the first stage, a UAV live broadcast platform was built to
transmit high‐quality online videos. In the second stage, the Citrus‐
YOLO network was used to detect citrus. In the third stage, citrus
were tracked across frames using a Kalman filter, and the detection
results were matched to trajectories using a combination of IoU,
depth appearance, and Mahalanobis distance. In the fourth stage, the
proposed NUDC was adopted to realize the global calculation of the
number of citrus in the orchard.
3.3.1
|
Stage 1: The UAV live broadcast platform
Online acquisition of UAV video is an important prerequisite for
subsequent related processing. With the continuous development of
information technology and communication technology, the rise of
live broadcast platforms could provide a suitable solution for this
research. Based on the mainstream open source architecture Nginx‐
rtmp‐module, we built a UAV live broadcast platform for the orchard
scene. The architecture of the live broadcast platform is shown in
Figure 3 and includes three modules: the pushing flow end, the flow
media server, and the pulling flow end. The three modules were
connected in turn by the RTMP push protocol and HLS pull protocol.
In this study, the DJGO 4 application developed by Shenzhen DJI Co.
Ltd. was used as the pushing flow end. The Nginx server was adopted
as the flow media server. Nginx is a high‐performance web server
platform based on the BSD open‐source protocol. It has the
characteristics of less resource occupation, high stability, and large
data processing capacity. A computing platform was used as the
pulling flow end.
In the actual counting work, Wi‐Fi was first enabled through the
mobile phone to ensure that the computing platform and the mobile
phone were in the same intranet. Then, through the LightBridge/
OcuSync protocol, video captured by the UAV's on‐board camera
was transmitted to the controller. The video streaming was pushed to
the Nginx server by the self‐developed SDK application installed on
the smartphone. Finally, the computing platform realized the online
acquisition of video streaming by accessing the Nginx server.

From the viewpoint of the UAV, the citrus fruits in the videos not only

As shown in Figure 4, we propose Citrus‐YOLO to overcome the
above limitations. The improvements included replacing PANeT with
BiFPN in the feature enhancement and fusion network and adding a
small object detection head to the prediction network. A more
efficient multiscale fusion method is adopted in the improved
detection network, which can prevent the loss of small target
information. The proposed Citrus‐YOLO network effectively solves
the shortcomings of YOLOV5 in the missed detection of occluded
citrus and the false detection of background objects.
Replacing PANeT with BiFPN: PANet (Liu et al., 2018) was adopted
as a parameter aggregation method in the YOLOV5. Although PANet
proves the feasibility of bidirectional fusion, it ignores the fact that
the contribution of features at all levels may be different, and the
bidirectional fusion method used is relatively simple. Inspired by Tan
et al. (2020), we improved PANet with more efficient reuse and
fusing of multilayer features, as shown in Figure 5b.
Compared with PANet, BiFPN has two improvements: (1) PANet is
simplified by deleting redundant nodes at both ends of the network
to reduce unnecessary calculation; (2) jump connections are added
between original feature nodes and output nodes at the same layer of
the network, and the reuse and fusion of multilayer features are
strengthened.
Increase small‐object detection head: YOLOV5 performs multiple
downsampling operations, which makes it difficult for deep feature
maps to learn the feature information of small targets. To increase
the robustness of YOLOV5 for small citrus fruits, we added a small‐
object detection head to the network after splicing the shallower
feature map and the deep feature map.
Taking the network input of 608 × 608 as an example, the
downsampling in YOLOV5 is used five times, so the final feature map
sizes are 19 × 19, 38 × 38, and 76 × 76. In the three feature maps, the
largest (76 × 76) is responsible for detecting small targets. Corre-
sponding to 608 × 608, the receptive field of each feature map has a
size of 608/76 = 8 × 8. If the width or height of the target in the
original image are less than 8 pixels, it is difficult for the network to
learn the feature information of the target. In addition, the resolution
of images from the UAV is very large, and the downsampling factor of
YOLOV5 is relatively large; it is therefore difficult for the network to

Stage 3: Tracking between frames
The fruit candidate region output from our deep learning fruit
detection stage is used as the input for the tracking stage. This part of
our pipeline is used to obtain cross‐frame associations and is a
necessary step to obtain fruit counts for the entire image sequence as
opposed to an individual image.
After citrus fruits were detected, a Kalman filter was used to
realize the cross‐frame tracking of fruits. The Kalman filter is a widely
used optimal tracking algorithm for linear systems. Since the frame
rate of the UAV video reaches 24 frames/s, there are few changes in
the position of fruit targets between consecutive video frames, and
the changes in position can basically be regarded as uniform motion
(Han et al., 2020). Therefore, it can be assumed that the fruit tracking
system changes linearly with time. The operation of the Kalman filter
includes two steps: prediction and updating of the target track (Han
et al., 2020).
In the prediction stage, the Kalman filter uses estimated
information for the previous video frame to predict the current
frame. The results of the detection network are used to predict the
corresponding fruit tracking position and its covariance matrix.

Stage 4: Counting strategy
Although the large field of view of the UAV allows us to complete the
counting work quickly, it also brings about changes in the citrus‐ID.
When using a UAV to count citrus fruits in orchards, it is found that if
the maximum value of the citrus‐ID is used as the final counting
result, there will be a large number of repeated counts of fruits, which
will lead to great counting errors, as shown in Figure 6. To solve this
problem, the single line counter (SLC), a method recently used in
pedestrian detection, was introduced. However, in the actual
counting work, we found that although this method can avoid the
repeated counting of fruits, it has serious missed counting issues,
which leads to great counting errors, as shown in Figure 7.
To solve these problems, we proposed a counting scheme named
the NUDC. As shown in Figure 8, we defined dense count lines in the
middle region, and the number of count lines gradually decreased
from the middle to both sides. The reason for this is that the fruits in

4.1

Training procedure
To properly compare the performance of the algorithms, the same
training platform and similar hyperparameter settings were used for
each algorithm. The running environment included a desktop
computer with an Intel i7‐10700 (2.90 GHz × 16) octa‐core CPU,
NVIDIA GeForce GTX 3090 graphical processing unit (GPU) and
Ubuntu 16.04. The software included CUDA 10.0.130, CUDNN
7.4.2, NVIDIA driver 410.78, Opencv 3.4.5, Ubuntu16.04LTS, and
Pytorch. Each model was trained for 100 epochs.
(1) YOLOV5 and Citrus‐YOLO: YOLOV5_l6.pt was adopted as the
pretraining model. The size of each image was 1280 × 1280,
the batch size was 32, the initial learning rate was 0.01, the
momentum was 0.937 and the weight decay coefficient was
0.0005. K‐means was used during training to obtain the best
anchor aspect ratio. Image enhancement methods such as mosaic
and flip were adopted.
(2) YOLOX
and
ByteTrack: YOLOX_x.pth
was

as
the
pretraining model. The size of each image was 1280 × 1280,
the batch size was 32, the lr_per_img was 7.8125e−05, the
momentum was 0.9, the weight decay coefficient was 0.0005
and the NMS threshold was 0.65. Image enhancement methods
such as mosaic, mixup and flip were adopted.

Analysis of experimental results
For citrus orchards, ByteTrack had the smallest IDSR compared to the
other two methods. However, as mentioned above, Bytetrack and
YOLOV5_DeepSORT exhibited serious missed detections of small
and overlapping fruits, which led to high L1 loss. We found that the
low IDSR of Bytetrack may also be caused by serious missed
detections, while our method overcame this problem well. In
addition, the proposed Citrus‐YOLO method exhibited good detec-
tion performance for image sequences, and can effectively achieve
continuous tracking of fruits.
Figures 11 and 12 show the counting results for the three
algorithms before and after the stone pillar works. For the orange
orchard, Bytetrack's IDSR became the largest compared to the other
two methods. The reason for the results being different from those for
the citrus orchard is the presence of stone pillars in the orange
orchards. As shown in Figures 11a and 12a, the IDs of the reappearing
fruits were almost all changed, which led to the double counting of the
fruits. Although Bytetrack algorithm performed well with the MOT20
data set, it cannot be applied to orchard counting. This is because the
movement pattern of fruits is complex relative to the camera, and due
to the existence of occluded objects, such as stone pillars, passing
people, and so on, the algorithm cannot work well when only using
Kalman filtering. In contrast, our pipeline solved this problem by
adopting the DeepSORT algorithm, with the counting results of
the algorithm shown in Figures 11c and 12c. Compared with

Analysis of experimental results
During the online counting experiment, a likely reason for the difference
in the counting accuracy between the two methods was that there were
loopholes and packet losses of image data when the communication
network was not in good condition during the process of online
transmission of UAV images; that is, some image data were discarded
before they were transmitted to the counting system. Figure 14 shows
the counting effect diagram of the algorithm in a poor‐condition
communication network. When the algorithm detected the kth frame
image of the UAV video as shown in Figure 14a, due to the loss of image

not match the position predicted by the tracker, resulting in the failure of
some fruit tracking across frames. This phenomenon also confirms
our hypothesis that when the UAV is working, the communication
fluctuation of the network will have a certain impact on the stable
transmission of the UAV image sequence, resulting in an increase in the
counting errors during the online operation.

Discussion
This study provides valuable insights into automatic counting
operations for fruits in emperor citrus and orange orchards in UAV
application scenarios, especially for the construction of a UAV live
broadcast platform, the detection and counting of fruits, the field
operation performance and the resource consumption of the
algorithm.
To address the limitation of UAVs only performing offline
operations, an orchard UAV live broadcast platform based on the
Nginx‐RTMP‐Module architecture was built to transmit UAV high‐
quality video streams online. Nevertheless, the UAV live broadcast
platform had a delay of 8–15 s and a packet loss rate of 3.4%. When
the communication network was in poor condition, the uniform
motion model assumed by the Kalman filter often failed to work,
which led to repeated counting of some fruits. Compared to the
offline counting scheme, the online algorithm achieved 39.67% and
9.67% increases in IDs and MAPE, respectively.
The Citrus‐YOLO network, using Bi‐FPN to replace PAN‐Net and
adding a small target detection layer, was proposed for fruit detection
with real‐time UAV videos, and was able to effectively prevent
missed detection of small and severely occluded fruits. Compared
with YOLOV5 and YOLOX, Citrus‐YOLO's F1 values increased by
1.62 and 7.88 percentage points, respectively. However, the Citrus‐
YOLO network had only 52.63 FPS due to the increase in
computational work. To further improve the real‐time performance
of the detection algorithm in the inference stage, 640 × 640 pixels
images and a model accelerated by TensorRT were adopted in this
study. Compared with the model without acceleration, the FPS of
the accelerated model was increased to 158.73 with a loss of
0.47% mAP.
For the counting of fruits, the DeepSORT algorithm was first
adopted to achieve continuous tracking of fruits in UAV videos. Then,
a NUDC was proposed to suppress repeat counting due to tracking
failures or UAV flight instability, and experiments were designed to
compare the counting performance of Bytetrack, YOLOV5_Deep-
SORT and the proposed method. The experimental objects were two
orchard scenes with different characteristics. Without occluders,
Bytetrack had the smallest IDSR. However, Bytetrack and YOLOV5_-
DeepSORT exhibited serious missed detections of small targets and
overlapping fruits in the UAV field of view, resulting in their high L1
loss. In the presence of occluders such as stone pillars, compared to
the other two methods, Bytetrack's IDSR was the largest. Occlusion
was the essential reason for this phenomenon in ByteTrack. When
fruits blocked by stone pillars reappeared, their IDs were almost all
changed, leading to double counting of fruits. Although our method
can overcome the above problems well, repeated counting still occurs

hardware resource consumption will also increase with the increasing

In general, the reasons for the failure of online fruit counting are
as follows: (1) When the communication network signal is poor, the
orchard live broadcast platform cannot stably transmit the UAV
videos, resulting in failure of fruit tracking. Optimizing the quality of
the network communication or adopting a higher‐quality online
transmission method are problems that need to be solved in the
future. (2) When the fruit reappears after being blocked for a long
time, its ID often changes, which leads to repeated counting of fruit.
(3) In this study, only fruits from one side of the tree were counted,
and there was a problem in respect of missing detection of some
fruits. Therefore, to effectively solve the problems of fruit occlusion
and missing detection in the traditional single‐view, research on
multiview fruit online counting based on the cooperative operation of
multiple UAVs will be the focus of future work.

CONCLUSIONS
In this study, a new UAV fruit counting pipeline was proposed, which
can accurately and quickly detect and count citrus fruits in large‐scale
unstructured orchards. As far as we know, this is the first orchard
fruit counting method that can be realized online and support
multiple UAVs working in parallel. This work can greatly reduce time
consumption in the development, optimization, and practical applica-
tion of the algorithm. In addition, it has high operating efficiency, and
can provide technical support for the counting and yield estimation of
large‐scale unstructured orchards.

average IDs and MAPE of the offline counting method were 18% and
5.28%, respectively, while the average IDs and MAPE of the online
counting method were 57.67% and 14.95%, respectively.
A detection network named Citrus‐YOLO was designed to detect
fruits in real‐time from UAV video. As a result of adding one more
detection head and replacing PAN‐Net with Bi‐FPN, the Citrus‐
YOLO network can effectively prevent the missing detection of small
and severely occluded fruits. The experiments show that the F1 value
of the Citrus‐YOLO network reached a high value of 94.51% with the

1.62% and 7.88%, respectively. To further improve the real‐time
performance of the Citrus‐YOLO network, smaller images and
TensorRT were adopted in this study. The experimental results show
that the FPS of the accelerated Citrus‐YOLO network increased to
142.86 with a loss of 0.47% mAP. The proposed Citrus‐YOLO
network had better detection accuracy and speed, and can effectively
meet the needs of actual orchard operations.
To suppress the repeated counting caused by tracking failure or
UAV flight instability, the NUDC was proposed in combination with
the DeepSORT algorithm to achieve continuous tracking and global
counting of fruits in UAV images. In two orchard scenes with
different characteristics, an experiment was designed to compare the
counting performance of ByteTrack, YOLOV5_DeepSORT and the
proposed method. IDSR, count/ground truth, L1 loss and error mean
were used as evaluation metrics. The experimental results show that
the average error means of ByteTrack and YOLOV5_DeepSORT were
71.34% and 8.22%, respectively, while the mean for our method was
3.135%. Therefore, our method has wider applicability and better
counting performance. To evaluate the effectiveness of Citrus‐YOLO
and NUDC, a set of comparative experiments was designed. MAPE
and MAE were used as evaluation metrics. The experimental results
show that the MAPE and MAE of our proposed pipeline were only
7.05% and 25.87, respectively.
Moreover, to reflect the counting performance of our method in
actual deployment, field experiments in respect of fruit counting
were carried out in a citrus orchard. The experimental results show
that under suitable lighting conditions, the F1 value and MAPE of the
algorithm were 89.07% and 12.75%, respectively. When working
with one, two, and three UAVs, it took about 0.05, 0.06, and 0.10 s to
process one frame of UAV image, respectively, and the required GPU
memory was 2.93, 8.09, and 10.78 g, respectively.
Finally, a group of experiments was designed to compare the
performance of the most advanced citrus counting methods and the
method proposed in this paper. The experimental results show that
the diversity score obtained using our method is relatively better than
the method proposed by Zhang et al., which shows that it has better
detection and counting performance.
