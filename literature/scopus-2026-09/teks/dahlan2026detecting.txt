Abstract— The detection of oil palm fresh fruit bunches 
(FFB) by ripeness stage is crucial to maximizing oil yield and 
maintaining quality in the palm oil industry. Manual grading of 
FFB which relies on human vision is often time-consuming, 
inconsistent, and prone to error, especially when performed 
under challenging field conditions. This research aims to detect 
real-time oil palm fruit ripeness using the You Only Look Once 
(YOLO) object detection model, evaluate its accuracy, and 
assess its overall performance. Both YOLOv9 and YOLO11 
models were evaluated to identify the most suitable version for 
field deployment, as YOLO11 offers improved speed and 
accuracy over earlier YOLO versions. The dataset contains 
annotated images categorized into four classes which are unripe, 
underripe, ripe, and overripe from multiple sources. Model 
training and testing were conducted using the Ultralytics 
framework and Roboflow for preprocessing and augmentation. 
During validation, the YOLO11n model achieved a mean 
Average Precision (mAP50) of 88% and mAP50–95 of 68.3%. 
In the future, aim to further improve detection accuracy and 
expand the dataset for greater robustness in diverse field 
conditions. 
Keywords— Oil palm FFB, Object detection, You Only Look 
Once algorithm, TensorFlow Lite, Mobile app development. 
I.
INTRODUCTION
The agriculture sector, particularly in the oil palm 
industry, plays a significant role in Malaysia’s economy as it 
contributes significantly to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) 
and creates employment opportunities. Malaysia has remained 
one of the world’s top palm oil producers, with its crude palm 
oil (CPO) production reaching approximately 18.55 million 
tonnes in 2023, a modest increase from 2022.  
One factor that influences palm oil yield is  harvesting FFB 
at the right time. However, manual grading of  FFB that poses 
several drawbacks remains a  common practice in Malaysia. 
Currently, the ripeness of oil palm FFB is identified visually 
based on the FFBs color and a specific quantity of separated 
fruits that have fallen to the ground [1], [2]. As the manual 
harvesting requires the workers to visually identify each of the 
ripeness oil palm FFB which is time-consuming because of 
the size area of palm oil, distance and lighting makes it is 
tough to detect the ripeness [3], [4]. A study by [2] found that 
the oil palm industry needs advanced technology equipment 
to reduce dependency on human labour, as in the last few 
years many owners faced labour shortage crisis due to 
COVID-19 pandemic [5]. The traditional approach which 
relies on human experience on identify the ripeness of FFB 
could be minimized with the automation of the manual 
inspection via mobile application FFB ripeness detection as it 
less the load of work and workers are needed to check the oil 
palm FFB because with the mobile camera to capture more oil 
palm FFB it can detect and class. This study proposes an 
automated system leveraging deep learning technology to 
detect and classify the stages of maturity FFB.  
Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) has classified 16 
classes of FFB stages in the oil: ripe, underripe, unripe, 
overripe, empty, rotten, wet, long stalk, unfresh or old, dirty, 
small, diseased, pest damaged, dura, small stalk, and 
parthenocarpy [6]. But most of the research only focuses on 
three to four maturity stages of FFB, ripe, underripe, overripe, 
and unripe.  
II.
LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Deep Learning
Deep Learning (DL) is a sophisticated subset of machine
learning that leverages artificial intelligence to analyze and 
interpret complex datasets through multiple layers of 
interconnected nodes, mirroring the neural networks of the 
human brain. DL is a very useful tool in various industries 
including agriculture as it performs better in tasks such as 
classification, object detection, segmentation and prediction. 
Among the key DL techniques are Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CNNs), YOLO and Single Shot MultiBox Detector 
(SSD). CNNs are particularly noted for their effectiveness in 
feature extraction from images, making them highly suitable 
for tasks like image recognition [7]. YOLO and SSD, on the 
other hand, a simple architecture that allows it to directly 
produce the bounding box’s location and class through the 
neural network [8], [9]. With the current technology in the 
agriculture sector, DL is used to classify and identify the stage 
of fruit ripeness through image classification and object 
detection tasks. 
As shown in Table I, CNN, YOLO and SSD have different 
DL implementations, however these three algorithms share a 
common feature for image analysis in deep learning models. 
These three models use neural network architectures to 
process the image as the input and extract the relevant 
characteristics. Moreover, it improves the accuracy and 
computational efficiency of the tasks such as in object 
detection and classification. Also, these algorithms can be 
trained and modified using the specific datasets, which allows 
the models to adjust into various domains and needs. In this 
paper, we chose YOLO because it is very fast. Furthermore, 
YOLO was chosen because it requires multiple object 
detection in an image using the bounding boxes and the 
architecture complexity is simpler than the other two.

B. Object Detection - YOLO 
In computer vision, object detection includes identifying 
and locating specific objects within an image or video. 
Compared to image classification, the object's detection is 
recognized, and its position is determined by using the 
bounding boxes or segmentation masks. The YOLO model, 
recognized for object detection and image segmentation, was 
created in 2015 by Joseph Redmon and Ali Farhadi at the 
University of Washington [10]. YOLO is categorized under 
single-stage object detection models. It is frequently used for 
real-time object detection tasks. In a single evaluation, it 
produces bounding boxes and class predictions [11].  
 
Based on research by [12], the essential component of 
YOLO object detection algorithm is its small size and fast 
calculation speed. YOLOv9 is an advanced object detection 
model designed for high accuracy with low computational 
cost. It introduces two key improvements, Generalized 
Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (GELAN) and 
Programmable Gradient Information (PGI). GELAN acts as a 
part of the backbone and PGI help to reduce the issue of losing 
information during training [13]. YOLO11 is the latest 
version, building on YOLOv9 with faster performance, fewer 
parameters, and improved accuracy.  
C. YOLO11  
 
YOLO11 is one of the latest models of the YOLO version 
for real-time object detection. It is designed to improve the 
accuracy of the object detection and enhance in detecting 
small objects while preserving the real-time inference speed. 
The flow of the YOLO11 architecture starts with the FFB 
image input then the backbone to extract the important 
features such as the colour of the oil palm FFB. Next, the neck, 
where the feature fusion, from the extracted feature in 
backbone combined and mixed to pass it to the next stage to 
predict. Lastly, the head predicts the FFB ripeness class label 
and the bounding boxes for each object in the image [14]. 
 
Although YOLOv9 provides strong accuracy with 
GELAN and PGI enhancements, the latest YOLO11 
architecture further optimizes inference speed, parameter 
efficiency, and small-object detection. Because real-time field 
usage requires quick and accurate predictions on mobile 
devices, this study compares YOLOv9 and YOLO11 to justify 
the selection of the most suitable model for oil palm FFB 
detection. 
III. 
METHODOLOGY 
In this object detection system, the YOLOv9 and YOLO11  
models were implemented together with TensorFlow. One of 
the pre-trained models of YOLO11 was used. Next, the 
preprocessing in TensorFlow includes the resizing image as 
YOLO11 model expect image input 640 x 640 pixel. 
Therefore, the images need to preprocess to match and only 
during training the data augmentation be used. Then, the data 
is organized to process it at once and prefetching to load the 
next batch of data in advance. Lastly, the data sent to the 
model to predict or train. 
A. Data Collection 
There are two types of datasets that were collected from 
Mendeley [15], Kaggle [16] and Roboflow [17] which are 
image and video data. Before the beginning of image 
augmentation, a total of 2,672 images of oil palm FFB were 
gathered. The datasets were categorized into four classes, 
unripe, underripe, ripe and overripe as shown in Fig. 1.

B. Data Pre-Processing and Augmentation   
The datasets that are in video form are converted into 
image frames using VLC Media Player. The recording ratio 
that was used in VLC Media Player to extract the videos is 30. 
For videos with 30 frames per second (fps), a recording ratio 
of 10 means that it will take pictures every 10/30 or 0.33 
seconds. Then all the datasets were combined according to 
their classes and uploaded to Roboflow. In Roboflow, the 
images are manually labelled for all the classes in the 
annotation process. After doing all the annotations class by 
class, the datasets going through the pre-processing were all 
the images were resized to 640x640 and set in .JPG format. 
Moreover, the auto-orient was applied and used the Adaptive 
Equalization for auto-adjust contrast for the pre-processing. 
As for the image augmentations, also was done in Roboflow 
and from a total of 2,672 images to 6,412 images where the 
datasets split into around 70:20:10 of ratio for training, valid 
and testing.

The findings align with cognitive vision theory, where 
visual understanding progresses from low-level perception to 
focused attention and final decision-making. YOLO follows 
this same hierarchical structure. The backbone extracts basic 
features, the neck integrates and highlights important regions, 
and the head produces the final ripeness classification. 
YOLO11’s improved performance is consistent with 
computational perception principles stating that stronger 
feature fusion and attention mechanisms lead to more accurate 
human-like visual decisions. 
The YOLO model replicates human visual cognition by 
first perceiving visual cues such as color and texture (feature 
extraction), then focusing attention on critical regions of the 
fruit bunch (feature fusion) and finally making a classification 
decision like how workers judge ripeness. Thus, the system 
mirrors the human perception.

V. CONCLUSION & FUTURE WORK 
In conclusion, the object detection app, which employs 
YOLO11, demonstrates strong performance with an overall 
accuracy of 88%. This reasonable accuracy reflects the 
system’s effectiveness in detecting the oil palm FFB across 
various categories. The system achieves notable accuracy 
rates for overripe with 96%. However, unripe shows slightly 
lower accuracy at 75%, indicating that further refinement may 
be needed to enhance performance for this category. The 
results confirm that YOLO11n provides higher precision and 
slightly better mAP compared to YOLOv9c, supporting its 
suitability for real-time deployment in mobile applications. 
Despite the high accuracy achieved by the proposed object 
detection 
system, 
several 
limitations 
and 
ethical 
considerations must be acknowledged. The system’s 
performance is affected by several visual challenges in real 
field conditions. Lighting variation (uneven brightness, 
shadows, glare) reduces color consistency. Occlusion from 
palm leaf or overlapping fruitlets leads to missed detections, 
reflected in the lower recall for underripe and unripe classes. 
Background confusion also occurs because unripe and 
underripe fruit share similar colors with surrounding 
vegetation, resulting in lower precision for these classes 
compared to ripe and overripe. These factors demonstrate that 
lighting, occlusion, and background similarity remain key 
obstacles for reliable FFB detection.  
Lastly, the use of automated FFB detection system can 
significantly impact labor usage in oil palm plantation. 
Although automation may improve efficiency, safety and 
productivity, it may also reduce the demand for manual labor. 
Replacing manual grading tasks may negatively affect 
workers who depend on such roles. Therefore, plantations 
must manage this transition carefully. Ethical deployment 
should involve training and upskilling programs for workers. 
Furthermore, the accuracy of automated FFB detection 
models can be affected by dataset bias. If certain environments 
or fruit appearances are poorly represented, the system may 
behave unfairly or inconsistently. Addressing these concerns 
requires diverse datasets, continuous performance monitoring, 
and transparent evaluation. These measures help to ensure 
responsible and fair use of automation in agriculture.
