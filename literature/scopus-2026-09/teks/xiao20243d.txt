A B S T R A C T  
Phenotypic traits at the organ scale hold significant importance in the realm of plant breeding, notably in 
evaluating genetic diversity, selecting innovative cultivars, and forecasting potential yield. Ground platforms, 
despite delivering accurate results in organ-scale phenotypic studies, frequently exhibit limitations concerning 
efficiency and adaptability, and can adversely affect the soil. The Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) platform 
emerges as an innovative medium, distinguished by its high-throughput and adaptability, thus offering immense 
prospects in phenotypic research. Nonetheless, its precision within the organ scale warrants further investigation. 
As a notable economic plant species, cotton (Gossypium spp.) provides the textile industry with its essential raw 
material through the fruit it bears, the cotton boll. This study, employing the characterization of cotton boll 
breeding materials as an example, scrutinizes the capability of UAV platforms in executing precise enumeration 
and yield estimation of three-dimensional (3D) cotton bolls in agricultural fields. An innovative route, the Cross- 
circling oblique (CCO) route, was employed in conjunction with lightweight UAVs for reconstructing a 3D point 
cloud of cotton bolls in the field, and juxtaposed against the traditional nadir route. A comprehensive process was 
also developed for characterizing the quantity, volume, and spatial distribution of cotton bolls, further facili­
tating yield estimation. The CCO-produced point cloud demonstrated a superior completeness compared to the 
nadir-produced point cloud, proving capable of effectively mitigating occlusion in the middle and lower sections, 
thereby procuring a more comprehensive 3D cotton boll. In terms of cotton boll counting (R2 = 0.92 vs R2 =
0.73) and yield estimation (seed cotton: R2 = 0.7 vs R2 = 0.62, lint cotton: R2 = 0.75 vs R2 = 0.55), the precision 
of the CCO-derived point cloud surpassed that of the nadir-derived point cloud considerably. Notably, yield 
estimations were more precise when based on cotton boll count for seed cotton, and on boll volume for lint 
cotton. Utilizing the high-precision CCO-produced cotton boll point cloud, an analysis was conducted on the 
spatial distribution of cotton bolls for all breeding materials, and the yield was subsequently projected. The 
contribution of this study lies in the high-throughput capture of organ-scale traits of field cotton bolls using the 
UAV-based CCO route, enabling accurate yield estimation. This approach may also be applicable to extracting 
organ-scale traits of other field crops. It demonstrates the immense potential of combining UAV platforms with 
the CCO route for high-throughput acquisition of crop organ-scale traits, which could become a powerful tool in 
plant breeding.   
1. Introduction 
Phenotypic traits at the organ scale play a crucial role in plant 
breeding (Jin et al., 2021; Song et al., 2021). These traits, which 
encompass characteristics such as the size, shape, and function of indi­
vidual plant organs including leaves, flowers, and fruits, serve as crucial 
indicators of a plant’s health, productivity, and adaptability (Marsh 
et al., 2021). Such invaluable data can guide breeders in identifying and 
preserving genetic variations, assist in the selection of plants with 
favorable traits, and offer substantial utility in forecasting potential 
yields (Maman et al., 2004; Boyles et al., 2017).

fibers integral to various cotton textile production (Ye et al., 2023). 
Concurrently, its seeds are substantial sources for oil extraction and feed 
production, underlining cotton’s vital role across fiber, oil, and feed 
industries (Wang et al., 2023). Cotton bolls, the fruit of cotton, serve as 
crucial metrics for assessing cotton yield and as direct indicators for 
evaluating qualities such as cotton’s quality, resistance to diseases and 
pests, among other significant traits. Such traits inherently impact cot­
ton’s economic value and cultivation benefits. The cotton bolls pheno­
typing decisively influences cotton’s genetic enhancement, novel variety 
screening, and production management. A thorough comprehension of 
the cotton boll phenotyping enables more accurate prediction and 
evaluation of cotton yield and quality, facilitating the selection of su­
perior cotton varieties and underpinning the science of cotton cultiva­
tion management (Conaty et al., 2022). A major challenge is that 
prevailing cotton boll phenotyping are predominantly reliant on manual 
measurement and observation (Sun et al., 2020). This methodology, 
while laborious and time-consuming, often fails to meet the demands of 
modern cotton production for accuracy and efficiency (Jung et al., 
2018). An exigent necessity is the development of a high-throughput 
cotton boll traits analysis method to rapidly obtain abundant, accurate 
cotton boll traits. This progress would bolster the enhancement of cotton 
variety selection and management strategies. 
Crucial characteristics of cotton encompass the number, volume, 
distribution, and yield of cotton bolls (Sun et al., 2020). Predominantly, 
two-dimensional (2D) image-based technologies have been adopted for 
identifying cotton bolls, such as a field detection method found on 
regional semantic image segmentation proposed by Li et al. (2016) and 
weakly supervised deep learning models to segment and count cotton 
bolls from proximal imagery proposed by Adke et al. (2022). Never­
theless, these techniques only capture the number of cotton bolls within 
an image’s range, and fail to provide the number at the plot scale or 
reveal other traits. This 2D image technology extends to recognizing 
other plant organs, such as in the detection of mango fruit (Stein et al., 
2016) and apples (Bargoti and Underwood, 2017). However, the effi­
cacy of such image-based methods is inherently limited by their 2D 
nature, which precludes them from addressing issues in three- 
dimensional (3D) space, like the occlusion problem among plant or­
gans (Xiao et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2021). Consequently, when two 
cotton bolls are closely adjacent or one is obscured by another, 2D image 
technology may falter in the correct identification and counting process. 
This limitation inevitably constrains the accuracy and reliability of these 
technologies in estimating and analyzing cotton boll characteristics. 
Sensing technologies that use 3D are primarily mounted on ground 
mobile platforms to obtain high-precision 3D structures of plant organs 
at close range (Deery et al., 2020). Light detection and ranging (LiDAR) 
are the most commonly used 3D sensor for characterizing plant organ 
scales in field conditions (Yan et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). For 
example, Malambo et al. (2019) placed a terrestrial LiDAR on a sprayer 
to capture point clouds of sorghum panicle in the field and accurately 
estimate their characteristics (panicle length: Pearson correlation (r) =
0.88, root mean square error (RMSE) = 3.10 cm; panicle width: r = 0.79, 
RMSE = 1.67 cm; plant height: r = 1.00, RMSE = 0.80 cm). LiDAR offer 
high precision but are expensive. Another type of 3D sensor is the depth 
camera. For example, Dube et al. (2020) installed multiple RealSense 
depth cameras on a field vehicle to collect 3D data of cotton bolls during 
three seasons. Based on the distribution and cumulative frequency of 
boll structures, the irrigation volume and the impact of varieties on the 
distribution of cotton bolls were determined. Depth cameras are rela­
tively cheaper than LiDAR but have lower resolution and are susceptible 
to sunlight interference (Kazmi et al., 2014). Multiple-view geometry 
methods are passive 3D reconstruction techniques that can reconstruct 
3D point clouds from RGB images with overlapping views (Hui et al., 
2018; Gil-Docampo et al., 2020). They offer low cost and high precision. 
Researchers often mount multiple RGB sensors on field mobile platforms 
to acquire a collection of plant multi-view images and reconstruct highly 
accurate 3D plant structures using multiple-view geometry methods 
(Salas Fernandez et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2020). For example, Sun et al. 
(2020) mounted five RGB cameras respectively on the top and sides of a 
field vehicle to capture multi-view images of cotton plants and recon­
structed a 3D point cloud of the cotton plants. Additionally, they used 
clustering-based methods to analyze the number, distribution, and yield 
of cotton bolls. Although the combination of multiple-view geometry 
methods and ground mobile platforms allows cost-effective acquisition 
of high-precision 3D structures at the scale of plant organs, ground 
platforms still have limitations, including low efficiency, soil compac­
tion damage, and reliance on manual operations (Sun et al., 2020). 
In recent years, the swift progress of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles 
(UAVs) has facilitated high-throughput acquisition of plot-scale plant 
traits such as plant height (Che et al., 2020) and canopy coverage (Cheng 
et al., 2022). Unlike ground platforms, UAVs offer high image capture 
efficiency, negate the need for manual intervention, and prevent soil 
compaction. The UAV nadir route, a technique in which the lens is 90◦
vertically down to acquire images, is the most commonly used UAV 
technique for acquiring plot-scale traits (Shu et al., 2021; Shu et al., 
2022). The method has also been used for the detection of some simple 
plant organs, such as panicles, which are often located at the top of the 
stem for easy identification. As an illustration, Chang et al. (2021) uti­
lized a UAV to gather RGB point cloud data of sorghum panicles from a 
height of 10 m, successfully estimating the length (R2 = 0.61) and width 
(R2 = 0.83) of panicles compared with manual measurements. None­
theless, this UAVs flight route fails to accurately portray complex plant 
organs as it excludes organ data from the mid and lower sections due to 
its top-down perspective. Some researchers have attempted cotton boll 
counting and yield estimation via this method (Jung et al., 2018; Yeom 
et al., 2018), but have faced data loss concerning mid and lower cotton 
bolls due to obstruction (Dube et al., 2020). 
Acquiring complete 3D point clouds of cotton bolls from the field in a 
high-throughput and cost-effective manner, along with accurate detec­
tion of the number, volume, distribution, and yield estimation, remains 
a significant challenge. On the one hand, ground platforms offer high 
accuracy but lack efficiency and flexibility, while UAVs nadir route 
provide efficiency and flexibility but fall short in capturing complete 3D 
point clouds (Dube et al., 2020). Hence, the unresolved challenge lies in 
developing a UAVs platform that integrates high precision, high 
throughput, and flexibility. On the other hand, current cotton boll 
recognition algorithms primarily rely on white cotton fiber bolls, green 
leaves, and brown stems as reference. For example, Dube et al. (2020) 
utilized depth cameras and RGB cameras to obtain RGB 3D point clouds 
of cotton and used color filters to identify white cotton fiber bolls, 
exploiting their distinct color differences from green leaves and brown 
stems. However, these algorithms overlook the variations in cotton va­
rieties found in breeding materials, such as brown cotton bolls or leaves 
in shades of purple and red. Moreover, cotton bolls, with their pliable 
structures and considerable variations in shape, are more likely to cause 
occlusion when compared to rigid fruit structures such as mangoes. 
Consequently, an urgent need arises for a more robust algorithm to cater 
to cotton breeding materials and enable accurate segmentation of cotton 
bolls from complex environments. 
Cross-circling oblique (CCO) route is an innovative method of obli­
que photography for UAVs, involving multiple single circular flight 
routes (Xiao et al., 2023). Currently, this technology finds primary ap­
plications in urban aerial photography and 3D reconstruction, enabling 
comprehensive and precise 3D structural reconstruction of the target 
object by capturing information from a 360◦perspective. CCO routes 
employ nonlinear movement, providing considerable efficiency advan­
tages over traditional five-dimensional oblique routes (Xiao et al., 
2023). In the traditional five-dimensional oblique route, the UAV moves 
linearly and necessitates a substantial image overlap between the front 
and back, leading to an increased image count. Implementing the CCO 
route enables the UAV platform to attain high throughput, precision, 
and flexibility, thereby opening novel avenues for reconstructing and 
characterizing 3D cotton bolls in the field.

The purpose of this study was to obtain high-quality 3D point clouds 
of various cotton breeding materials in the field using a UAV platform 
that offers high precision, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness. Addition­
ally, the study aimed to develop a complete process for characterizing 
cotton boll traits, including the number of bolls, volume, distribution, 
and yield. The specific objectives were as follows: (1) to compare the 
accuracy of estimating the number and volume of cotton bolls using both 
the CCO route and the nadir route on a UAV platform; (2) to develop a 
comprehensive process for characterizing diverse breeding cotton bolls, 
encompassing white and brown fiber bolls, as well as green, purple, 
yellow leaves, and branches; (3) to analyze the distribution of various 
cotton breeding materials using the complete cotton boll point cloud 
reconstructed through CCO route; (4) to predict the yield of all cotton 
breeding materials in the field using the optimal yield estimation model 
extracted from CCO-derived traits. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Study area and experimental setup 
A selection of 320 representative upland cotton germplasm materials 
were planted at the Cotton Experimental Base of the Biotechnology 
Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, 
situated in Yazhou District, Sanya City, Hainan Province (109◦12′6.030″, 
18◦23′10.338″). Eighty-six of these varieties were selected for this 
experiment and replicated once for a total of 172 plots (Fig. 1). The 
planting period extended from 2021 to 2022. Seed sowing was carried 
out around November 1, 2021, and the harvest was conducted in April 
2022. The area, Sanya City, is characterized by a tropical marine 
monsoon climate, with an average temperature range of 22–30 ◦C. A 
total rainfall of 456.5 mm was recorded in 2021, while the rainfall was 
measured with 289.5 mm in 2022. The field experiment comprised 
subplots measuring 3 m in length and 0.6 m in width, encompassing an 
area of 2.4 m2 each. Each subplot was planted with a single row of 10 
cotton plants. The sowing of seeds was achieved through manual film 
covering and spot sowing. Cotton was cut from the top in March 2022 to 
avoid overcrowding and to keep the height of the cotton at about 1 m. 
Defoliants were also used to facilitate cotton harvest. All other field 
management procedures at the experimental site, including watering, 
fertilizing, weeding, and pest control, were executed in accordance with 
local conventional cotton cultivation methods to ensure the normal 
growth of the cotton materials.

a total of 172 plots. The selected cotton included white and brown

2.3. UAV data pre-processing 
2.3.1. Reconstruction of 3D cotton point cloud 
The software chosen for reconstructing 3D point clouds of cotton 
based on UAV images was Agisoft Metashape professional software 
(Agisoft LLC, St. Petersburg, Russia; version 1.7.0). The software utilizes 
the standard Structure from Motion-Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS) pro­
cess to reconstruct 3D point clouds from multiple-view images. Structure 
from Motion (SfM) estimates camera positions to create a sparse point 
cloud, and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) refines this into a dense 3D model 
by analyzing images from different viewpoints (Schonberger and Frahm, 
2016; Iglhaut et al., 2019). For the reconstruction of dense and precise 
point clouds, high-quality settings were utilized for the both photo 
alignment and dense point cloud generation, following the specific 
recommendations for vegetation (Liu et al., 2021). Statistical filtering 
was employed for noise removal. In order to speed up the calculation, 
the voxel-based downsampling method was used to downsample the 
cotton point cloud, and the voxel size was set to 0.006 m.

cotton was adjusted to align with the long side of the plot. Initially, the

2.3.3. Plot segmentation of 3D cotton point cloud 
Two steps were implemented for plot segmentation: row and column 
segmentation of the cotton point cloud. The dense planting of cotton 
poses challenges in determining plot boundaries from the front view. 
Thus, in row segmentation, the 3D cotton boll point cloud was flipped to 
the backside, allowing for a clearer selection of coordinates for four 
boundary points (Fig. 4). The cotton boll point cloud was divided into 
four rows based on the Y coordinates of the four boundary points. Given 
that cotton is cultivated on terraces, each row of 3D cotton bolls was 
initially divided into N + 1 equal sections based on their x-axis range 
where N represents the total number of plots in a row. The point with the 
lowest Z value was identified in each section, deemed the lowest point 
(red point in Fig. 5a). Upon dividing each row of 3D cotton bolls into 
plots based on the lowest point, competition between varieties emerged, 
resulting in certain varieties encroaching upon the living space of 
neighboring varieties and leading to unsatisfactory segmentation

2.3.4. Soil removal of 3D cotton point cloud 
Upon acquiring the 3D point cloud of the cotton plot, the plant point 
cloud was vertically partitioned into 100 equal segments to generate its 
vertical distribution profile (Fig. 6). A Gaussian fitting curve with a term 
value of four was employed. The initial local minimum point, 
descending from the top, was adopted as the threshold for soil seg­
mentation, ensuring comprehensive segmentation of the soil within the 
plot. 
2.4. Cotton boll detection and characterization 
Upon acquiring clean 3D point clouds for each individual cotton plot, 
an automated procedure was implemented to ascertain the count, 
location, and volume of cotton bolls within each plot. This process 
comprised two primary steps. Firstly, features pertaining to both color 
and morphological structure were extracted to facilitate supervised 
classification, distinguishing the point clouds associated with cotton 
bolls from those associated with branches and leaves. Subsequently, 
these 3D point clouds of cotton bolls were subjected to further clustering 
to accurately determine the quantity, position, and volume of the cotton 
bolls. 
2.4.1. Cotton boll segmentation  
(1) Super-voxel clustering of cotton point cloud 
Super-voxels are a higher-level representation of 3D data that groups 
points with similar properties to facilitate tasks such as segmentation, 
object recognition, and feature extraction. Super-voxels calculation on 
3D point clouds of cotton provides advantages, including improved 
feature extraction through voxel grouping with similar properties and 
preservation of object boundaries. This leads to more efficient differ­
entiation between cotton bolls and leaf/branch point clouds, while also 
reducing calculation complexity. Two commonly employed super-voxel 
clustering methods are the Voxel Cloud Connectivity Segmentation 
(VCCS) algorithm (Papon et al., 2013) and the Color-based Region 
Growth Segmentation (CRGS) (Zhan et al., 2009). The CRGS method 
was selected due to the VCCS’s tendency to cluster single cotton boll 
point clouds into numerous super-voxels during testing, which hinders 
the representation of the cotton boll’s structural and morphological 
features. Conversely, the CRGS method successfully preserves the 
structural and morphological features of single cotton boll after the 
clustering process by using the merging algorithm for over- and under- 
segmentation control.

similarity and spatial proximity. In this algorithm, the point cloud is first 
partitioned into multiple clusters based on spatial distance and color

(2) Feature extraction 
To distinguish among clusters of cotton bolls, cotton branches, and 
cotton leaves, a 126-dimensional feature vector was extracted from the 
clusters. This vector includes color, shape, and spatial characteristics. In 
this study, cotton of different varieties exhibited different colors in their 
bolls and leaves, making the color features indispensable. A 102-dimen­
sional color feature, extracted from RGB and HSV spaces, was presented. 
Here, the R, G, B, H, S, V channels were each represented by 16-bin 
histograms, supplemented by a 6-dimensional vector composed of the 
mean values of R, G, B, H, S, V. Studies suggested that HSV space can 
better resist the interference of light. Shape and spatial features were 
also essential, given the potential confusion caused by the color 
resemblance between brown cotton bolls and branches, as well as the 
white appearance that some branches and leaves may adopt under 
sunlight, possibly leading to misidentification with white cotton fiber 
bolls. 
Cotton bolls exhibited significant shape differences compared to 
branches and leaves, thus the three eigenvalues (λ0>λ1 > λ2) from the 
covariance matrix of the clusters are chosen as shape features (Sun et al., 
2020). These three eigenvalues denoted the variance in the three prin­
cipal component directions of the clusters. Given that cotton bolls 
clusters approximated a circular shape across three directions, the three 
eigenvalues of this cluster should be similar (λ0≈λ1≈λ2). As the shape of 
branches and leaves is linear, their three eigenvalues would exhibit 
notable differences. In branches, the length significantly exceeds both 
width and height, resulting in a pronounced disparity among the ei­
genvalues: λ0≫λ1≈λ2. For leaves, the length and width greatly surpass 
the thickness, leading to eigenvalues: λ0≈λ1≫λ2. 
The global radius-based surface descriptor (GRSD) (Warchoł 2018) 
was chosen to describe the spatial characteristics of the cluster. The 
radius-based surface descriptor (RSD) encodes the radial relationship of 
the point and its neighborhood. The point has the largest RSD value 
when it is located on a flat surface and the smallest RSD value when it is 
located on a curved surface. GRSD is a 21-dimensional histogram 
calculated by computing RSD for all points of the cluster, which is used 
to portray the spatial characteristics of the cluster.  
(3) Deep-forest based classifier 
A deep-forest classifier (Zhou and Feng, 2018) was used for binary 
classification of cotton bolls and branches/leaves. The deep forest 
classifier is an alternative to deep learning, which stacks multiple layers 
of random forests in a cascading manner to achieve better feature rep­
resentation and learning performance. Compared to deep learning, its 
advantages include fewer required sample sizes, almost no need to 
adjust hyperparameters, and the ability to determine model complexity 
in a data-related manner without the need for backpropagation. The 
method divides the training into two stages: Multi-Grained Scanning and 
Cascade Forest. Multi-Grained Scanning generates features, and the 
prediction results are obtained through multiple layers of Cascade For­
est. Specifically, in the first stage, a sliding window is used to divide into 
multi-instance feature vectors, which are output as class probability 
vectors through forest transformation. Then merge the class probability 
vectors to generate new features. In the second stage, the input features 
are output as a class probability vector through the forest, and connected 
to the original input as the next layer output. After multiple cascades of 
forests, the final class probability vector is output. Finally, the average 
class probability vector of multiple forest outputs is calculated, and the 
largest class probability is taken as the prediction result. The algorithm 
flow chart is shown in Fig. 7. 
In this study, four representative varieties of cotton were selected to 
train the deep-forest model, including white and brown cotton fiber 
bolls, as well as green, purple, red, and yellow leaves and branches. After 
super-voxel clustering, 598 clusters of cotton bolls were combined with 
1,738 other clusters (leaves and branches) to form the training set for the 
deep forest model. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to assess the 
model accuracy on the training set. Subsequently, three different vari­
eties of cotton were selected as the test set to evaluate the model’s 
generalization ability. The test sets included test plot 1 with white cotton 
fiber bolls, yellow, and green leaves. test plot 2 with white cotton fiber 
bolls, and purple leaves; and test plot 3 with brown cotton bolls, yellow, 
and green leaves. 
2.4.2. Cotton boll clustering and characterization 
Following the segregation of the cotton boll point cloud from the rest 
(branches and leaves), further segmentation was performed to isolate 
individual cotton boll 3D point clouds, thereby facilitating their char­
acterization. A preliminary segmentation of the cotton boll point cloud 
was achieved using clustering methodologies, followed by an additional 
division of the adhering cotton bolls. Subsequently, a comprehensive 
characterization of the cotton bolls, including their respective number, 
position, and volume, was taken in each plot. 
The 3D density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise 
(DBSCAN) algorithm was used to cluster cotton boll point clouds into 
clusters. The algorithm classified the point clouds larger than density D 
in the boll point cloud into a cluster. Because the un-adhering cotton 
bolls have gaps between them, its density will be significantly smaller 
than that of the cotton boll. There are two parameters in the algorithm to 
determine the density D, which are the neighborhood radius R and the 
minimum number of points Minpts. Density D means that the number of 
points in the neighborhood radius R is greater than the minimum 
number of points Minpts. After testing, the radius R and Minpts were set 
to 1.5 cm and 10 in this study, respectively. 
The cotton boll point cloud, post-clustering, has been divided into 
individual cotton bolls and adhering cotton bolls. In order to identify the 
adhering cotton bolls, the convex hull volumes of all cotton bolls were 
calculated and sorted in ascending order. Statistical filtering was used to 
remove outliers for each individual boll as well as adherent bolls to 
ensure the accuracy of volume calculations. It was observed that the 
sorted convex hull volumes of the cotton boll initially increased slowly, 
and there is a sudden increase at a certain position, which is the location 
of the two adhering cotton bolls. There are multiple positions where the 
volume suddenly increases sometimes, which could be due to multiple 
adhering cotton bolls. In order to determine the position of the first 
sudden increase, the difference sequence F and the average difference 
d of the cotton boll’s convex hull volume were calculated. By testing, the 
first position in F equal to or greater than 5*d was found to be the first 
burst point, i.e., the adherent cotton boll. 
After the identification of adhered cotton bolls, the quantity within 
each cluster was determined. For each plot, the average of the top 50 % 
of individual cotton boll volumes was utilized as the representative 
average volume. This is predicated on two primary considerations: the 
potential obstruction of cotton bolls by leaves, leading to incomplete 
reconstruction and underestimation of their convex hull volumes. Uti­
lizing the larger-volume cotton bolls for average calculation compen­
sates for this bias. Furthermore, in clusters of adhered cotton bolls, gaps 
between bolls are included in the total convex hull volume calculation, 
resulting in an aggregate volume exceeding the sum of individual vol­
umes. Employing the average volume of larger bolls enables a more 
precise estimation of the number in attached clusters. The cotton boll

3. Result 
3.1. 3D reconstruction of cotton point cloud 
A total of 172 cotton plots were reconstructed both derived from 
CCO and nadir routes. Fig. 8 depicted the CCO-derived and nadir- 
derived point clouds for three selected test plots. Test plot 1 included 
white cotton fiber bolls, yellow leaves, and green leaves. Test plot 2 
included white cotton fiber bolls and purple leaves. Test plot 3 included 
brown cotton bolls, yellow leaves, and green leaves. The CCO-derived 
point clouds for cotton exhibited significantly higher density 
compared to the nadir-derived point clouds, thereby enabling a more 
accurate characterization of the morphological structure of cotton bolls. 
Most of the nadir-derived point clouds for cotton bolls were incomplete 
due to obstructions caused by branches and leaves when observed from 
the nadir perspective. In comparison to test plots 2 and 3, the recon­
structed cotton bolls in test plot 1 exhibited higher completeness, sug­
gesting the influence of leaf occlusion on both CCO and nadir flight 
routes. 
3.2. Cotton boll segmentation and clustering 
The cotton boll point clouds were separated from branches and 
leaves using Deep-forest classifier based on color, shape, and spatial 
structure features. Fig. 9 showed the segmentation results of the cotton 
bolls in three test plots. Visually, the white and brown cotton fiber bolls 
were effectively separated from point clouds of different colors repre­
senting branches and leaves. From a statistical perspective (Table 1), 
when using the deep learning classifier based on color, shape, and 
spatial structure features, the training set achieved a classification ac­
curacy of 100 %, and the validation set reached 98.69 %. The model also 
demonstrated strong generalization ability on test set (test plots 1, 2, 3). 
The discrimination accuracy for white cotton fiber bolls was as high as 
100 % and 99.78 %, respectively. The discrimination accuracy for 
brown cotton bolls also reached 95.90 %. When the Deep-forest classi­
fier relied solely on color features, the discrimination accuracy for white 
cotton fiber bolls remained largely unchanged, while the discrimination 
accuracy for brown cotton bolls showed a slight decrease (94.63 %).

3.3. Cotton boll counting and yield estimation 
The proposed algorithm achieved good accuracy in calculating boll 
numbers based on both the CCO-derived and nadir-derived cotton point 
clouds (R2 > 0.73 for both, Fig. 12). The accuracy of cotton boll counting 
using CCO-derived cotton point clouds was higher than that of the nadir- 
derived cotton point clouds (R2: 0.92 vs 0.73, RMSE: 7.27 vs 13.09, 
rRMSE: 8.57 % vs 15.40 %). The convex hull volume of the cotton bolls 
calculated from CCO-derived data was significantly larger than that of 
the nadir-derived calculation (average convex hull volume: 8729.00 cm3 
vs 3196.36 cm3). This is because the nadir-derived cotton point clouds 
miss many parts of the cotton bolls during reconstruction. This indicated 
that the CCO flight route was more advantageous than the nadir flight 
route for a complete 3D characterization of cotton bolls. Combined with 
the proposed algorithm, it can accurately characterize cotton bolls. 
The number and volume of cotton bolls calculated from CCO-derived 
and nadir-derived point clouds were both used to estimate the yield of 
seed cotton (Fig. 13) and lint cotton (Fig. 14). Overall, in estimating both 
seed and lint cotton, the overall accuracy of traits calculated using the 
CCO-derived point cloud was significantly higher compared to those 
calculated using the nadir-derived point cloud. Specifically, the best 
estimation for seed cotton was the boll numbers calculated from the 
CCO-derived point cloud (R2 = 0.70, rRMSE = 18.17 %). The precision 
of the CCO-calculated volume to estimate seed cotton was slightly lower 
than that of the CCO-calculated number of bolls (R2 = 0.67, rRMSE =
18.97 %), but higher than the precision of the nadir-calculated trait to 
estimate seed cotton (R2 < 0.62, rRMSE > 20.27 %). In contrast, the best 
for estimating lint was the boll volume calculated by CCO-derived point 
cloud with R2 = 0.75 and rRMSE = 19.30 %. The estimation of lint 
cotton based on the number of cotton bolls calculated by CCO-derived 
point cloud had slightly lower accuracy (R2 = 0.65, rRMSE = 22.57 
%), but it was higher than the accuracy of estimations based on the 
nadir-derived point cloud (R2 < 0.55, rRMSE > 25.90 %). Estimating 
seed cotton yield based on the number of bolls was more accurate while 
estimating lint based on boll volume was more accurate. This may be 
due to the fact that seed cotton is more correlated with the number of 
bolls because it contains the weight of the seed, while lint cotton does 
not contain the weight of the seed, so it is more correlated with the boll 
volume. The above results showed that the CCO-derived point cloud 
combined with the proposed algorithm has great advantages for the 
estimation of seed and lint cotton.

The combination of CCO routes and proposed algorithms could effi­

horizontal distribution, it was observed that the number and size of fiber 
bolls increase as they approach the main stem (Fig. 15c and Fig. 15e 
respectively). Particularly in the first and second segments adjacent to 
the main stem, the formation of cotton fiber bolls exhibited a pro­
nounced increase (surpassing 30 % of the total), and the quality was 
noteworthy, contributing to over 24 % of the overall volume. However, 
when moving away from the main stem, the number of cotton bolls 
decreased and their size became smaller. This is due to the fact that the 
boll formation time of the bolls close to the main stem is synchronized 
with the most favorable climatic conditions for boll development 
(Gwathmey and Clement, 2010). In addition, cotton bolls close to the 
main stem received more organic and inorganic nutrients than those on 
the outside (Kannan, 2010). In terms of vertical distribution, the number 
of cotton bolls was highest in the center, followed by the lower part and 
lowest in the upper part (Fig. 15d). The peak in the middle of Fig. 15d 
was shifted toward the lower part, which was due to the fact that the 
counts start from the ground. These horizontal and vertical distribution 
patterns are consistent with the statistics conducted by the expert in the 
field (Pettigrew, 2004; Zhang et al., 2021). However, there was no clear 
pattern in the vertical distribution of boll volume (Fig. 15f), which 
contradicts the known results (Sun et al., 2020). This may be due to the 
fact that late rains and winds resulted in greater deformation of the 
upper and lower bolls than the middle bolls, which resulted in the upper 
and lower boll volume being close to those of the middle layer. 
3.5. Cotton yield mapping 
Cotton yields were estimated for 172 plots (Fig. 16) by the best yield 
estimation model in section 3.3. Seed cotton was estimated by CCO- 
calculated bolls number while lint cotton was estimated by CCO- 
calculated bolls volume. The average weight of seed cotton in 172 
plots was 540.36 g, while the average lint quality was 168.86 g. There 
was a remarkable consistency in the weight of seed cotton over lint (R2 
= 0.93). Cotton yields varied considerably across varieties, ranging from 
127.08 g to 952.68 g for seed cotton and from 72.53 g to 291.40 g for lint 
cotton. The yield of cotton was mainly influenced by climate and 
variety.

The study proposed a complete algorithmic process for character­
izing 3D cotton bolls, including their quantity and volume. The 
involvement of multiple cotton varieties and complex scenarios (with 
cotton bolls classified as white and brown, and leaves classified as green, 
brown, and purple) poses challenges in characterizing the cotton bolls. 
The deep-forest model that integrates color, morphology, and spatial 
features were employed to distinguish cotton bolls from branches/ 
leaves. The results demonstrated that the deep-forest classification 
model, considering color, morphology, and spatial features compre­

(98.69 %) and test set (>95.90 %). However, the color-based deep-forest 
classification model also achieves acceptable results on the validation 
set (97.67 %) and test set (>94.63 %). Specifically, distinguishing white 
cotton fiber bolls from branches/leaves relies solely on color features, 
while the inclusion of morphology and spatial features only improves 
the accuracy of distinguishing brown cotton bolls. Previous research has

models. Without specular reflections, the colors of cotton bolls and

area for cotton boll breeding, hence the density of the cotton bolls was 
relatively low (with a plant spacing of 30 cm). However, we are opti­
mistic about the application of our pipeline in more densely planted 
cotton boll plots, as our improved method can effectively separate 
multiple adhered cotton bolls, as demonstrated in Figure S2. Addition­
ally, our current cotton boll plot was partially obscured by leaves, 
leading to incomplete cotton boll reconstruction. We speculate that in 
denser plots without leaf occlusion, more complete three-dimensional 
cotton bolls could be reconstructed, indicating that our pipeline may 
exhibit superior performance. In the future, we plan to conduct more in- 
depth experiments in Xinjiang, China, testing the effectiveness of our 
pipeline in denser cotton plots. 
Three-dimensional deep learning is a potential method for the next 
stage of exploration. The potential advantage of 3D deep learning in the 
characterization of 3D cotton bolls lies in its ability to highly capture 
spatial information and automate feature learning (Jiang et al., 2020). 
This means it can identify and interpret the complex three-dimensional 
shapes and structures of cotton bolls, providing accurate reconstruction 
and counting. Deep learning can also automatically learn the complex 
features of cotton bolls, not depending on manually designed features, 
greatly increasing its applicability and accuracy (Saeed et al., 2023). 
However, this method also has some potential drawbacks. Firstly, 
training a 3D deep learning model usually requires a large amount of 
labeled data, which may be difficult to obtain in practice (Hinterstoisser 
et al., 2018). Secondly, the computational and memory requirements of 
3D deep learning models are high, which may create significant demand 
for computational resources (Zhang and Zhu, 2018). In addition, the 
interpretability of 3D deep learning models is poor, which may make it 
difficult to understand the internal decision-making process of the 
model (Li et al., 2022). 
The environment greatly influences the 3D characterization of cotton 
bolls, especially light and wind (Hu et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2020; Xiao 
et al., 2021). Lighting conditions are among the environmental factors 
that most directly affect 3D reconstruction. The intensity, angle, and 
quality of sunlight can directly affect image acquisition, and thus affect 
the quality and precision of the reconstruction (Seifert et al., 2019). If 
the light is too strong or too weak, it might cause overexposure or un­
derexposure in the images, affecting their quality (Feng et al., 2021). 
Changes in lighting conditions can also cause variations in the color and 
texture of the object’s surface (Zhang et al., 2018). In this study, to avoid 
the influence of specular reflection on the 3D reconstruction and 
discrimination of cotton, images were taken on a cloudy day, which 
mainly results in diffused reflection. Strong wind may cause the crop to 
sway, preventing precise matching between consecutive images, leading 
to stratification of the point cloud in 3D reconstruction or even failure 
(Zhu et al., 2020). A relatively windless environment were chosen in this 
study for image acquisition, ensuring the accuracy of the model’s 
reconstruction. 
This article possesses certain limitations. Within the process of plot 
segmentation, a small number of neighborhoods are interconnected, and 
the segmentation method in this article cannot completely separate 
them. This, in turn, may result in errors during the subsequent cotton 
boll counting phase. The complete separation of these interlinked 
neighborhoods poses a notably formidable task. It is our anticipation 
that advancements in deep learning may pave the way for the resolution 
of this issue in future endeavors. Environmental factors, such as wind, in

UAV, as one of the low-altitude remote sensing technologies, have 
significant application value in obtaining crop phenotypic traits on a 
plot-scale. The strong timeliness and operability of UAV effectively 
compensate for the lack of spatial and temporal resolution in the 
application of satellite remote sensing in precision agriculture. The 
introduction of the CCO method provides a new approach for UAV 
technology to obtain phenotypic traits at the crop organ-scale. This 
paper uses this method to reconstruct the 3D cotton bolls of 172 plots 
and proposes an algorithm to precisely characterize the 3D distribution 
of cotton bolls, realizing the high-throughput estimation of seed cotton 
and lint cotton in the field. The method can be effectively used in the 
screening of cotton boll breeding varieties, greatly improving the effi­
ciency of breeding variety selection. 
5. Conclusion 
UAV-based CCO routes have been employed to generate 3D point 
clouds of cotton bolls in the field, which are subsequently contrasted 
with those obtained through traditional nadir routes. Furthermore, a 
robust procedure has been proposed to characterize cotton boll traits. 
The CCO-derived 3D point cloud of cotton bolls exhibits greater 
completeness compared to the nadir-derived cotton bolls, accompanied 
by a markedly improved accuracy in the estimation of the cotton boll 
number and volume. The proposed procedure can identify cotton bolls
