Abstract
2
Accurate citrus yield estimation is crucial for fruit yield control and quality enhancement. An automated citrus 
3
orchard yield estimation model was developed by capturing full-surface video streams of individual fruit trees during 
4
their enlargement stage using a rotating camera system. This model employs a YOLOv8n-SC detector integrated with 
5
the DeepSORT multi-object tracking algorithm. The impact of various image data on estimation accuracy was 
6
investigated under distinct illumination conditions: Sunny conditions, Overcast conditions, and Artificial illumination. 
7
Different shooting angles were also examined: Overhead shooting, Horizontal shooting, and Low-angle shooting.The 
8
YOLOv8n model was enhanced by incorporating a small object detection head to reduce feature information loss. The 
9
CBAM attention mechanism was introduced into the C2f module to strengthen extracted features and positional data. 
10
Additionally, the WIoU loss function replaced CIoU to improve model convergence performance. Results indicate 
11
YOLOv8n-SC achieved a recall rate of 97.6% and a mean average precision mAP50 of 93.7% on the total dataset, 
12
outperforming the original YOLOv8n model by 2.0% and 3.5% respectively. Optimal detection performance for 
13
YOLOv8n-SC was observed under Artificial illumination, with an mAP50 of 98.2%. Under Artificial illumination with 
14
Horizontal shooting, tracking accuracy reached 90.8% and tracking precision 94.0%. Average counting accuracy was 
15
89.46% with a mean absolute error of 9.98. These tracking and counting data were more stable compared to Overhead 
16
shooting and Low-angle shooting. This estimation system effectively counts individual citrus fruits, providing a 
17
valuable technical reference for citrus yield prediction.
18

1.
Introduction 
2
As the global leader in cultivation area and production, citrus is a pivotal economic crop in China, significantly 
3
impacting farmers' livelihoods. Presently, citrus yield estimation is primarily conducted via manual sampling, a 
4
process recognized as laborious and time-consuming [1], consequently failing to meet contemporary industry demands. 
5
Advancing citrus yield estimation towards automation is therefore considered highly significant for enhancing 
6
agricultural production efficiency and promoting smart agriculture [2].
7
Object detection algorithms are considered fundamental for accurate yield estimation [3], enabling precise fruit 
8
identification, localization, and stable tracking within complex environments. Traditional object detection methods, 
9
such as image segmentation [4] and color segmentation [5], are often compromised by environmental interferences 
10
like occlusion and varied illumination, limiting their generalizability [6]. Conversely, deep learning-based object 
11
detection [7], utilizing convolutional neural networks, offers advantages in terms of lightweight architecture, 
12
scalability, and high speed, rendering it more suitable for real-time applications. He and Xiong [8] proposed a green 
13
fruit detection method named deep bounding box regression forest (DBBRF) to address the challenge of detecting 
14
green citrus in natural environments, achieving a mAP50 of 87.6% with an average execution time of 0.759 seconds. 
15
Riza and Musahada [9,10] demonstrated that the original YOLOv7 model outperforms its tiny and X counterparts in 
16
citrus fruit detection, achieving an mAP50 of 90.6% along with strong precision and sensitivity metrics. Lu and Chen 
17
achieve 93.6% accuracy in detecting green citrus fruit in natural environments using a YOLOv5 backbone network 
18
[11], surpassing mainstream lightweight models while reducing parameters and model size. Wu proposes the 
19
automatic fruit counting system [12], which utilizes YOLOv4-tiny and threshold-based abatement of abnormal fruit 
20
detection to improve fruit counting accuracy from 89.1% to 95.0% in modern apple orchards.
21
The escalating scale of agricultural production coupled with diminishing rural labor has amplified the urgency for 
22
automated orchard yield estimation. However, existing image-based estimation methods have been found to be 
23
insufficient for high-precision counting [13]. Consequently, a video stream-based object tracking estimation approach 
24
is proposed in this paper.
25
Traditional object tracking techniques, such as optical flow and frame differencing [14], are often limited by 
26
challenges posed by illumination variations and background interference. While LiDAR-based object tracking 
27
methods [15] acquire high-precision three-dimensional spatial data capable of capturing target shape and position, 
28
they are susceptible to occlusion and noise interference, thus hindering high-precision estimation requirements. Deep 
29
learning, characterized by robust generalization, efficient and precise representation, and ease of deployment, 
30
facilitates not only automated yield estimation but also intelligent agricultural applications including maturity 
31
discrimination [16] and mechanized harvesting [17]. Wang, Walsh, and Koirala proposed a method for on-tree mango 
32
fruit detection and counting that combines deep learning, a Kalman filter, and the Hungarian algorithm, achieving a 
33
bias-corrected Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 18.0 fruits per tree, outperforming traditional dual-view imaging 
34
methods[18]. Jin proposes the CO-YOLO model for recognizing the growth patterns of Camellia oleifera fruits in 2D 
35
images, achieving a precision of 90.6% and improving mAP50 by 4.8% compared to YOLO11s while significantly 
36
reducing model size and computational complexity [19]. Li proposes a video stream inspection method utilizing the

Preprint not peer reviewed

1
YOLOv5 network and an improved ByteTrack algorithm to accurately classify and count dragon fruit flowers and 
2
fruits, achieving an overall recognition accuracy of 95.0% and a counting speed of 56 frames per second [20].
3
Existing video data collection predominantly involves inter-row parallel shooting [21,22], a methodology 
4
primarily suited for trellis-trained or espaliered fruit trees. Conversely, contemporary fruit tree orchards typically 
5
feature uniformly spaced plantings where branches extend radially, resulting in fruit distribution at various heights 
6
and orientations within the canopy. Rotating capture around individual trees can provide comprehensive surface image 
7
data, thereby mitigating estimation errors arising from fruit occlusion and enhancing the model's detection capability 
8
and counting precision. This paper aims to construct an estimation model that integrates deep learning object detection 
9
algorithms with multi-object tracking and counting algorithms, thereby facilitating automated citrus counting based 
10
on video streams.

12
2.1.
Data Sources
13
2.1.1.
Data Collection Sites
14
As citrus growth is dependent on effective accumulated temperature, Jiangsu is not considered an ideal citrus 
15
cultivation region; consequently, the fruit enlargement stage is entered later due to its colder climate. Therefore, 
16
experiments were commenced in late September at the Hongmeiren greenhouse cultivation base in Wutao Village, 
17
Jiangxin Isle, Zhenjiang City, Jiangsu Province (119°58’E, 32°21’N). Video stream data were collected from 20 citrus 
18
trees during their fruit enlargement stage. The citrus trees were cultivated using a high-density planting method, 
19
characterized by a row spacing of 2.5–3 meters, a plant spacing of 2–2.5 meters, and an approximate plant height of 
20
2 meters.
21
2.1.2.
Data Acquisition Methods
22
A handheld camera system was constituted by mounting a sports camera and a high-power LED light source onto 
23
a telescopic pole (maximum 1.5 meters). Raw video stream data were acquired using a DJI OSMO Action4 sports 
24
camera (as shown in Figure 1a).
25
Full-surface image acquisition of fruit trees was achieved by rotating the handheld camera system 360° around 
26
each tree. Experimental conditions encompassed varied illumination conditions (Sunny conditions, Overcast 
27
conditions, Artificial illumination) and distinct shooting angles (Overhead shooting, Horizontal shooting, Low-angle 
28
shooting) [23]. The camera's distance from the tree trunk's central point (rotation radius) was approximately 1.2 m to 
29
1.5 m, adjusted according to tree width and inter-tree spacing, based on the average canopy height H. Based on height 
30
H, Horizontal shooting was defined as horizontal capture [24]. Overhead shooting involved a downward angle of 30°, 
31
while Low-angle shooting utilized an upward angle of 30° (as shown in Figure 1b). The acquired data comprehensively 
32
covered various illumination conditions, including direct light, back light, side light, and Artificial illumination.

2.2.
Fruit Detection Based on Improved YOLOv8n
2
YOLO series models are characterized by their compact size [25], strong flexibility, and high accuracy. Object 
3
detection tasks can be completed in a single forward pass, which significantly enhances real-time detection processing 
4
speed. Nevertheless, false negatives or false positives can still occur when affected by occlusion and illumination 
5
variations. To overcome these shortcomings, a small object detection algorithm, YOLOv8n-SC, based on YOLOv8n, 
6
was proposed.
7
2.2.1.
Additional Detection Head
8
In the YOLOv8n model, the head output (Head) is responsible for object detection and classification tasks. By 
9
default, three detection heads (Detect) are provided for the detection of large, medium, and small objects [26], 
10
respectively. Citrus fruits typically constitute a very small proportion of an image, with their bounding box area often 
11
being less than 0.1% of the total image area. This characteristic presents multiple challenges for micro-object detection. 
12
Furthermore, issues such as occlusion, blurring, and color distortion contribute to sub-optimal fruit recognition 
13
performance by the original detection heads. To address these issues, a new detection head was introduced, specifically 
14
optimized for micro-object features with unique anchor boxes and prediction layers [27]. As illustrated in Figure 3, a 
15
small object output layer with a 160×160 detection feature map size was incorporated. This detection head is designed 
16
to collaborate with the existing three detection heads, thereby forming a more robust detection module. By reducing 
17
the receptive field, citrus object detection can be performed in parallel across multiple detection heads [28]. This 
18
approach effectively minimizes feature information loss during the downsampling process, consequently reducing 
19
instances of missed citrus detections.

The Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) is a lightweight [29], generic module that can be seamlessly 
2
integrated into the architecture of any convolutional neural network model with negligible computational overhead. 
3
Given the small size of citrus fruits during their enlargement stage and their color similarity to green leaves, CBAM 
4
attention was introduced into the backbone network's C2f module [30]. This C2f_CBAM module [31] was constructed 
5
to prevent the model from losing effective feature information during the sampling process. Its structure is presented 
6
in Figure 4.

15
CBAM comprises two components: the Channel Attention Module (CAM) and the Spatial Attention Module 
16
(SAM). These modules respectively focus on capturing target feature information and positional information [32]. 
17
Within the YOLOv8n model, an increase in feature channel numbers is observed with deeper layers, leading to 
18
information redundancy during channel concatenation. CAM is designed to focus on the most salient feature channels, 
19
strengthening target features through dynamic weighting and suppressing irrelevant channel features. This enhances 
20
the model's ability to select crucial features of citrus during the enlargement stage. SAM effectively suppresses noise 
21
via trainable weight parameters that are multiplied with the input data. This process prioritizes important local 
22
information, thereby improving the model's target localization capability.

2.3.1.
State Estimation and Fruit Matching
2
As the camera rotates circularly, citrus fruits are observed to exhibit varying occlusion states, leading to 
3
considerable differences in identified fruit counts even between proximate frames. Consequently, the multi-object 
4
tracking algorithm DeepSORT is introduced to facilitate real-time tracking and counting of citrus within complex 
5
environments. Its framework structure is presented in Figure 6.
6
7
8
9

Fig.6-The structure of DeepSORT citrus tracking frame 
21
Given the minimal positional changes of fruit targets between consecutive video frames, their motion can be 
22
approximated as uniform. Kalman filtering, an estimation algorithm for linear motion states, enables accurate state 
23
estimation through prediction and update cycles [32]. During the prediction stage, the current state is initially estimated 
24
using the state and control input from the preceding moment. In the update stage, state estimation is further optimized 
25
by integrating actual observations and correcting predictions. This methodology is particularly well-suited for 
26
processing noisy data, offering real-time uncertainty management within dynamic systems, thereby enhancing 
27
tracking stability and precision.
28
Association calculation is a critical step for matching detection results with predicted trajectory states. The 
29
optimization of DeepSORT is primarily based on the cost matrix derived from the Hungarian algorithm. An additional 
30
cascaded matching process is performed prior to IoU matching, which integrates appearance features and Mahalanobis 
31
distance, rendering it suitable for object detection in dynamic environments. During target tracking, deviations 
32
between predicted and true positions can be effectively quantified by calculating the Mahalanobis distance. When 
33
target motion patterns fluctuate frequently or targets become occluded, cosine distance is introduced to capture 
34
appearance features, thus maintaining a robust matching capability. These two distinct measurement methodologies 
35
serve to complement each other in facilitating accurate target matching.
36
2.3.2.
Tracking and Counting

Preprint not peer reviewed

1
For each track within the DeepSORT algorithm, an initialized timer (age) of zero is assigned. This counter is 
2
incremented by one upon an unsuccessful match and reset to zero following a successful match. The 'age' value 
3
signifies the time elapsed since the last successful correspondence for that track. A maximum age (max_age) of 90 
4
frames is pre-defined; should a track fail to be re-matched within this duration, it is subsequently deleted.
5
The detailed algorithm process, as illustrated in Figure 6, is outlined below:
6
(1) For each citrus fruit within a video frame, corresponding track information is created. Kalman filter motion 
7
variables are initialized, and their associated detection box information is predicted.
8
(2) Tracks from Step 1, alongside targets detected by YOLOv8n-SC in the current iteration, are submitted for 
9
cascade matching. Citrus targets are then classified into three distinct states: unmatched detections, unmatched tracks, 
10
and matched tracks.
11
(3) To address potential omissions from Step 2, these are combined with unconfirmed tracks from Step 1 and re-
12
matched using IoU matching, similarly resulting in three states.
13
(4) Unconfirmed tracks and those confirmed but with an 'age' exceeding 90 are subsequently deleted from the 
14
set of unmatched tracks.
15
(5) Matched tracks are merged and outputted following a Kalman filter update. Unmatched targets from Step 3 
16
are designated as newly established tracks. Tracks confirmed in Step 4 but with an 'age' less than or equal to 90 are 
17
also considered for a new round of tracking [33]. These three categories collectively form the basis for the next tracking 
18
iteration.
19
(6) Steps 1-5 are iteratively executed until the video frame sequence concludes. Numerical IDs, commencing 
20
from 1, are assigned to fruits based on their detection order within the video frame, thereby achieving automated citrus 
21
counting.

4.
Conclusions
2
The following outlines the main conclusions derived from the experiment, which proposed the YOLOv8n-SC 
3
model integrated with the DeepSORT algorithm for citrus fruit detection and counting during their enlargement stage, 
4
exploring estimation accuracy under varying illumination conditions and shooting angles.
5
(1) The improved YOLOv8n-SC model demonstrated superior comprehensive detection performance compared to 
6
YOLOv8n, Faster RCNN, and YOLOv10n models. This substantiates the effectiveness of the model enhancements 
7
and its capability for citrus fruit yield estimation during the enlargement stage.
8
(2) Illumination conditions significantly influence citrus yield estimation. Strong light on Sunny conditions resulted 
9
in reflections and shadows, which reduced detection accuracy. Under Overcast conditions, with more uniform 
10
illumination, all detection metrics exhibited notable improvements. Artificial illumination provided optimal detection 
11
performance, achieving an mAP50 of 98.2%, by effectively highlighting fruits and minimizing background interference.
12
(3) Video shooting angles exerted a significant impact on citrus yield estimation precision. Horizontal shooting video 
13
streams demonstrated optimal tracking performance, achieving a MOTA of 90.8% and a MOTP of 94.0%. Overhead 
14
shooting video streams were found to contain an insufficient number of visible targets due to fruit occlusion from the 
15
limited perspective, thereby compromising the reliability of counting results. Conversely, Low-angle shooting video 
16
streams were deemed unsuitable for citrus estimation due to severe ID switching issues and lower overall estimation 
17
capability.
18
(4) Among the evaluated multi-object tracking algorithms for citrus fruits, the YOLOv8n-SC + DeepSORT model 
19
combination exhibited the highest estimation precision, with a counting accuracy of 89.46%. This represents an 
20
improvement of 4.65% and 3.09% in counting accuracy when compared to YOLOv8n-SC integrated with SORT or 
21
Tracktor, respectively.
