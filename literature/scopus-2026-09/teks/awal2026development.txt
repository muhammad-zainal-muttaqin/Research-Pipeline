a b s t r a c t
Greenhouse robots must perform navigation and fruit sensing under irregular canopy boundaries, occlusion, 
changing observation conditions, and constrained row-transition spaces. This study developed a simulation-
driven ROS 2–Gazebo workﬂow for evaluating an omnidirectional mobile robot integrating LiDAR-based crop-
row navigation, AprilTag-guided multi-row mission control, and ripeness-speciﬁc cherry-tomato counting. The 
system combined LiDAR row centering, PTZ-RGB and front RGB cameras, AprilTag-based mission control, YOLO 
instance segmentation, ByteTrack, and canonical-identity-based counting. The workﬂow was evaluated in a pro-
cedurally generated greenhouse with heterogeneous plant structures and known plant-level fruit distributions. 
The AprilTag-guided mission reproduced the intended multi-row route at commanded speeds of 0.2–1.0 m/s, 
with mean cross-track errors of 0.045–0.056 m and RMSE values below 0.10 m. Fruit-counting performance was 
strongly aﬀected by robot speed. At 0.2 m/s, the system recovered 90.1% of the ground-truth fruit count with a 
count-level F1-score of 0.898. Performance declined above 0.4 m/s because of reduced observation time, canopy 
occlusion, and unstable temporal association. Additional errors arose from canopy obstruction and confusion 
between orange and red fruits. The proposed workﬂow enables repeatable evaluation of navigation, mission 
control, perception, and counting functions before physical deployment. It supports early identiﬁcation of tech-
nical limitations and speed-accuracy trade-oﬀs, thereby reducing trial-and-error, shortening the lead time to 
real-greenhouse experiments, and decreasing the number and duration of physical tests required for precision-
agriculture technology development.
1.  Introduction
Agricultural production is increasingly aﬀected by rural labor short-
ages, aging farming populations, and the declining availability of work-
ers for physically demanding tasks. Globally, agrifood systems employed 
approximately 1.3 billion people in 2021, while agriculture alone ac-
counted for 892 million workers in 2022 [1]. The eﬀect of automa-
tion varies among regions, but robotic systems are increasingly con-
sidered important for reducing repetitive labor, improving operational 
eﬃciency, and maintaining production under limited labor availability 
[2,3]. Japan represents a particularly severe case because its farming 
population is rapidly declining and aging [4]. These conditions have 
encouraged the development of agricultural robots for monitoring, har-
vesting, and other ﬁeld operations [5].

Reliable agricultural robotics remains challenging because crop en-
vironments contain irregular plant structures, variable illumination, oc-
clusion, plant motion, and changing layouts. These factors aﬀect both 
autonomous navigation and vision-based sensing. Simulation-driven de-
velopment can reduce testing cost and risk by allowing navigation 
and perception algorithms to be evaluated repeatedly before physical 
deployment. Robotics simulators provide controlled environments for 
rapid development, debugging, and reproducible experimentation [6]. 
Gazebo is widely used because it supports robot dynamics, virtual sen-
sors, and integration with ROS-based control systems [7,8].
ROS–Gazebo simulation has been applied to several agricultural 
robotics tasks. Li et al. [9] integrated crop-row navigation, multi-
camera perception, tracking, and visual SLAM for phenotyping. Betrò 
et al. [10] investigated GNSS-free LiDAR navigation using structured

vineyard landmarks, while Martini et al. [11] developed a synthetic-
data framework for visual navigation in row crops. Iqbal et al. [12] used 
ROS simulation for LiDAR-based phenotyping and crop-row navigation, 
and Singh et al. [13] developed a greenhouse digital twin for straw-
berry detection and robotic manipulation. Other studies have improved 
simulation realism through plant-motion modeling and high-quality im-
age rendering [14,15]. More recent work has further advanced LiDAR-
based inter-row navigation for structured cultivation: Jiang et al. [16] 
combined 3D LiDAR SLAM with NDT–ICP point-cloud registration for 
orchard row navigation, and Fujinaga [17] developed a LiDAR-based 
navigation method for high-bed cultivation that keeps the robot at a 
constant distance from the bed and was validated in both virtual and 
real greenhouse environments.
Despite these advances, many existing studies focus mainly on nav-
igation, harvesting, or isolated perception tasks. Fewer studies inves-
tigate a complete greenhouse-monitoring workﬂow that combines mo-
bile navigation, controllable camera viewpoints, ﬁducial-guided mission 
control, fruit segmentation, multi-object tracking, and counting within 
one simulation environment. This integration is important because re-
liable fruit monitoring depends not only on visual-model accuracy, but 
also on robot path stability, camera orientation, acquisition speed, oc-
clusion, and temporal association across image frames.
ROS 2 provides a suitable architecture for this integration through 
its modular communication framework, DDS-based middleware, and 
Quality of Service mechanisms for heterogeneous sensors [18]. Fiducial 
markers such as AprilTag can support controlled mission transitions by 
providing tag identiﬁcation and relative pose information from monoc-
ular images [19]. In greenhouse navigation, such markers can be used 
to trigger forward and backward row traversal, camera reorientation, 
lateral row transitions, and mission termination.
Vision-based fruit detection has also advanced through real-time 
deep-learning models. The YOLO family is widely used in agricul-
tural applications because of its favorable balance between accuracy 
and inference speed [20]. Recent versions, including YOLOv11, pro-
vide improved feature extraction and accuracy–eﬃciency trade-oﬀs for 
small and partially occluded objects [21–23]. In particular, instance-
segmentation models have recently been tailored to fruit ripeness as-
sessment, including tomato-speciﬁc approaches that address occlusion, 
overlap, and variable illumination in greenhouse and ﬁeld conditions 
[24,25]. However, detection or segmentation alone is insuﬃcient for 
fruit counting from video because the same fruit may appear in multi-
ple frames. ByteTrack addresses this problem by associating detections 
temporally, including lower-conﬁdence detections that may otherwise 
interrupt object tracks [26]. Previous agricultural studies have shown 
that tracking improves counting robustness under occlusion, illumina-
tion variation, and viewpoint changes [27–29].
In previous work, a PTZ-camera-based plant phenotyping system was 
developed using an overhead rail robot for high-resolution cucumber 
monitoring [30]. The system achieved high YOLOv8 detection perfor-
mance and demonstrated the beneﬁt of adjustable camera viewpoints. 
However, the ﬁxed rail structure limited ﬂexibility in dynamic or recon-
ﬁgurable greenhouse environments. The robotic platforms used in that 
study are shown in Fig. 1.
In contrast to the ﬁxed-rail platforms in Fig. 1, which constrain mo-
tion to a predeﬁned track, the present study adopts a wheeled omnidi-
rectional base to enable ﬂexible, reconﬁgurable operation; what carries 
over from the previous work is the concept of active PTZ-camera view-
point control, not the rail hardware. Building on that work, the present 
study develops a ground-based omnidirectional mobile robot equipped 
with 3D LiDAR, a PTZ-RGB camera, and a front RGB camera. The system

robot operating conditions. The framework also supports separate eval-
uation of LiDAR row-centering, AprilTag detection, complete multi-row 
mission execution, model performance, and fruit-counting accuracy.
1.1.  Objectives and contributions
The objective of this study was to develop and evaluate a virtual 
greenhouse platform that supports repeatable, pre-deployment integra-
tion and diagnosis of the interactions among robot navigation, mission 
control, viewpoint selection, segmentation, tracking, and counting. To 
this end, the study makes three principal contributions:
• An integrated ROS 2–Gazebo simulation workﬂow that uniﬁes 
LiDAR-based crop-row centering, AprilTag-guided multi-row mis-
sion control with active PTZ viewpoint selection, and a YOLO seg-
mentation, ByteTrack, and canonical-identity counting pipeline into 
a single reproducible monitoring loop.
• A procedurally generated greenhouse environment with exact plant- 
and fruit-level ground truth, providing a heterogeneous yet repro-
ducible benchmark for the controlled evaluation of navigation and 
perception.
• A joint, cross-speed evaluation and diagnosis of navigation accu-
racy, AprilTag-guided mission behavior, segmentation performance, 
and fruit-counting consistency, together with an analysis of the 
simulation-to-real limitations that this virtual evaluation reveals.
By combining mobile navigation, active sensing, ﬁducial-guided mis-
sion control, and temporal fruit perception in one virtual environment, 
the proposed framework provides a practical basis for developing and 
de-risking autonomous greenhouse-monitoring robots before real-world 
deployment.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 de-
scribes the robot platform, simulation environment, navigation frame-
work, and perception methods. Section 3 presents the experimental re-
sults and discussion, including an analysis of the simulation-to-real gap 
and study limitations. Finally, Section 4 summarizes the main ﬁndings 
and outlines directions for future work.
2.  Materials and methods
2.1.  System and simulation setup
The simulation and control system were implemented on a Linux-
based workstation running Ubuntu 22.04.3 LTS (Jammy Jellyﬁsh). The 
workstation was equipped with an Intel Core i5-13400F CPU, 16 GB 
RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 GPU. Python 3.10.12 was used 
for implementing the control, data-processing, and experiment-support 
scripts.
The robotic software framework was developed using ROS2 Humble, 
while the greenhouse simulation environment was executed in Gazebo 
Classic version 11.10.2. Gazebo Classic was selected because it provides 
stable integration with ROS2 Humble and supports physics-based sim-
ulation, URDF/Xacro-based robot models, simulated sensors, and re-
producible virtual greenhouse environments. The mobile robot model 
was described using URDF/Xacro and launched together with the gen-
erated greenhouse world through a ROS2 launch system. The simula-
tion pipeline initialized the Gazebo server and client, loaded the virtual 
greenhouse environment, spawned the robot model, and executed the 
navigation and perception modules within the same simulated environ-
ment.
To ensure reproducibility, the main Gazebo world parameters were 
explicitly deﬁned. The simulation used the ODE physics engine with a 
maximum step size of 0.001 s, a real-time factor of 1, and a real-time 
update rate of 1000 Hz. Gravity was set to 9.81 m∕s2 in the negative 
vertical direction. The rendering environment used a ﬁxed directional 
sun-like light source with shadow casting enabled. The light source was 
conﬁgured with a diﬀuse component of (0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 1) and a specular 
component of (0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 1), while the scene ambient and background 
values were set to (0.4, 0.4, 0.4, 1) and (0.7, 0.7, 0.7, 1), respectively. These 
lighting parameters were kept constant throughout all experiments to 
provide repeatable visual conditions for navigation and fruit-detection 
evaluation.
The simulated environment was conﬁgured as a controlled virtual 
greenhouse rather than a fully dynamic outdoor scene. Wind eﬀects 
were not activated, and no time-varying illumination, weather distur-
bance, or day–night transition was applied. This conﬁguration was se-
lected to isolate the performance of the robot navigation, LiDAR-based 
row-following control, and perception pipeline under consistent simu-
lation conditions. The resulting setup enabled repeatable evaluation of 
the proposed system while maintaining a clear boundary between con-
trolled simulation performance and future real-greenhouse validation.

2.2.  Virtual greenhouse environment generation
To construct a greenhouse environment suitable for evaluating 
vision-based fruit detection and counting, a cherry tomato plant model 
with three ripeness levels was adopted: green (unripe), orange (interme-
diate), and red (ripe). These visual categories provide controlled appear-
ance variation for detector training and evaluation while maintaining a 
consistent simulation structure. The plant models and greenhouse envi-
ronment were procedurally generated using the tomato farm simulation 
framework provided by Espejel et al. [31], which supports the creation 
of randomized tomato plant conﬁgurations and structured greenhouse 
layouts for robotic simulation studies.
The simulated greenhouse was assembled and evaluated in Gazebo 
Classic. Instead of manually modeling each plant and greenhouse as-
set from scratch, the adopted framework was used to generate crop 
rows, plant placement, fruit-texture categories, and canopy structures in 
a repeatable virtual environment. The generated world was conﬁgured 
using the following parameters: seed value = 1000, model name pre-
ﬁx = cherry_tomato, number of rows = 6, row length = 10 m, inter-
row spacing = 1.2 m, intra-crop spacing = 0.5 m, and world size = 9 ×
13 m. These parameters were selected to create a structured greenhouse 
layout that supports controlled testing of navigation and perception 
pipelines while remaining reproducible for repeated experiments.
Plant arrangement and fruit distribution were randomized within the 
constraints of the generator to introduce visual diversity among simu-
lated plants. Table 2 summarizes the main parameters used to generate 
the virtual greenhouse environment. The use of a ﬁxed seed ensures that 
the same environment can be regenerated, while the randomized plant 
conﬁgurations provide variation in plant structure, fruit placement, and 
canopy appearance across the simulated crop rows.
It should be noted that the generated tomato morphology is a sim-
pliﬁed representation of real greenhouse tomato plants. Because the 
adopted generator relies on predeﬁned plant, branch, fruit, and truss 
templates, it does not fully reproduce the biological variability observed

2.3.  Ground-truth fruit distribution statistics for the generated plant 
community
To characterize the procedurally generated greenhouse environ-
ment, descriptive statistics were calculated from the ground-truth plant 
and fruit distribution. Since the morphological assumptions and simpli-
ﬁcations of the plant generator have been described in the previous sub-
section, the purpose of this analysis is not to claim botanical realism, but 
to document the numerical distribution of fruits used as the controlled 
test condition for navigation and perception experiments. The virtual 
greenhouse consisted of six crop rows, with 21 plant instances in each 
row, resulting in a total of 126 plants. Each fruit instance was assigned 
to one of three ripeness classes: green, orange, or red. These statistics 
were used to quantify the variability introduced by the procedural gen-
erator and to conﬁrm that the simulated environment contained hetero-
geneous fruit-density and ripeness conditions. The generated environ-
ment contained 1320 ground-truth fruits in total. Red fruits were the 
dominant ripeness class, accounting for 828 fruits or 62.7% of the to-
tal population. Orange fruits accounted for 336 fruits or 25.5%, while 
green fruits accounted for 156 fruits or 11.8%. The mean fruit count 
per plant was 10.48 ± 11.66, indicating substantial variation among plant 
instances. In addition, 54 out of 126 plants contained no fruits, corre-
sponding to 42.9% of the plant population. To further quantify the nu-
merical heterogeneity of the generated fruit population, the coeﬃcient 
of variation of fruit count per plant and the normalized Shannon en-
tropy of the ripeness-class distribution were calculated. The coeﬃcient 
of variation was selected because it expresses dispersion relative to the 
mean and provides a dimensionless measure of plant-to-plant variability 
in fruit abundance. It was calculated as

At the row level, the fruit distribution also showed clear variation.

Overall, these ground-truth statistics show that the procedural gen-

and plant-level sparsity. These characteristics provide controlled vari-

mentation, and counting workﬂows. However, as noted in the previous 
subsection, the generated plant community should be interpreted as a 
controlled synthetic robotic testbed rather than a biologically faithful 
representation of a real tomato greenhouse.

The physical robot platform was designed with four Mecanum 
wheels to support omnidirectional mobility in narrow greenhouse rows. 
Each wheel has an outer diameter of 152 mm and a width of 55.52 mm, 
with rollers mounted at a 45◦ angle. In the Gazebo simulation, how-
ever, the wheel joints were represented as ﬁxed visual and struc-
tural components because the robot motion was implemented using 
the libgazebo_ros_planar_move.so plugin. This plugin applies pla-
nar base motion directly from velocity commands and publishes odom-
etry feedback, allowing the robot to move in the simulated environment 
without explicitly modeling individual wheel rotation, roller contact, 
and wheel-ground friction. Therefore, the wheels in the URDF serve pri-
marily to preserve the mechanical appearance and structural layout of 
the platform, while the planar motion plugin represents the commanded 
holonomic motion of the mobile base.
This modeling approach was selected because the objective of the 
simulation was to evaluate greenhouse navigation, LiDAR-based row 
following, and perception-system integration, rather than to analyze 
detailed wheel-terrain interaction or Mecanum roller dynamics. By ab-
stracting the low-level wheel contact model, the simulation avoids insta-
bility caused by complex roller-ground interactions and provides a re-
peatable platform-level motion response for evaluating navigation and 
sensing performance. Nevertheless, this simpliﬁcation is acknowledged

in the simulated greenhouse.

2.6.  Object detection and counting method
2.6.1.  Dataset preparation and model training
The dataset used for model training was generated from Gazebo-
based simulated greenhouse environments created using two 
procedural-generation seeds, 1001 and 1002. These environments 
provided diﬀerent plant arrangements, fruit locations, and ripeness 
distributions. The images were manually annotated using Roboﬂow and 
exported in YOLO segmentation format. Each fruit instance was labeled 
into one of three ripeness classes: cherry_tomato_green, cherry_tomato_or-
ange, or cherry_tomato_red. The dataset was split into 70% training, 20% 
validation, and 10% testing subsets. Fig. 12 presents a representative 
example of the annotated simulated dataset, illustrating how individual 
cherry tomato fruits were labeled at the instance level and assigned to 
the three ripeness classes.
Two dataset conﬁgurations were prepared for model comparison. 
The original non-augmented conﬁguration contained 755 images, com-
prising 529 training images, 151 validation images, and 75 test images. 
The dataset partition was completed before augmentation to prevent 
augmented derivatives of the same image from appearing in diﬀerent 
subsets. Augmentation was applied exclusively to the 529-image train-
ing subset and generated three training outputs per original image,

initial learning rate of 0.01, and an input resolution of 640 × 640 pixels.

model for ripeness-class fruit segmentation before the subsequent fruit-
counting evaluation. Model performance was evaluated using mask pre-
cision, recall, F1-score, mAP50, and mAP50–95. Since the downstream 
counting workﬂow depends on stable instance-level segmentation, mask 
mAP50–95 was used as the primary segmentation metric, while pre-
cision, recall, and F1-score were also considered to account for false-
positive and missed-fruit behavior.
It should be noted that the dataset was generated from simu-
lated greenhouse environments and was used primarily to validate the 
proposed robotic perception–navigation workﬂow. The trained model 
served as a functional perception component for evaluating fruit detec-
tion, ripeness classiﬁcation, and counting in simulation. Future transfer 
to real greenhouse conditions will require additional real-world train-
ing data and domain adaptation to account for lighting variation, plant 
diversity, camera noise, and fruit morphology.
2.6.2.  Evaluation criteria for model selection
The trained YOLO segmentation models were evaluated to select a 
base model for the subsequent fruit-counting experiment. Model selec-
tion was performed using the validation split, while the held-out test 
split was reserved for ﬁnal evaluation. Following common object detec-
tion and instance segmentation evaluation practice [34], the comparison 
used mask precision, mask recall, mask F1-score, mask mAP50–95, and 
bounding-box mAP50–95. Mask mAP50–95 was used as the primary 
segmentation-quality metric because it averages performance across 
multiple IoU thresholds, while precision, recall, and F1-score were used 
to assess false-positive and missed-fruit behavior that could aﬀect count-
ing accuracy [35].
In addition to segmentation accuracy, video-processing speed was 
evaluated using robot-camera video sequences. Mean inference time 
per frame and pipeline FPS were reported to assess the practical suit-
ability of each model for robot-based perception. Since the subsequent 
counting workﬂow uses tracking-by-detection, stable and balanced de-
tections are important for maintaining object continuity across frames 
[36]. Therefore, the ﬁnal model was selected based on the overall bal-
ance between segmentation quality, precision–recall behavior, localiza-
tion performance, and processing speed, rather than the highest mAP 
value alone.
2.6.3.  Fruit counting test setup and evaluation
Fruit counting was evaluated using recorded robot-camera image se-
quences from all six crop rows in the generated greenhouse environ-
ment. Because the plant community was procedurally generated, each 
row contained diﬀerent fruit distributions and visual conditions. There-
fore, evaluating all rows allowed the counting workﬂow to be assessed 
across the complete generated environment rather than using only se-
lected sample rows. For each row, image sequences were acquired under 
one static condition and ﬁve robot traveling speeds: 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 
and 1.0 m/s. Thus, the evaluation covered six acquisition conditions 
across six greenhouse rows.
The selected segmentation model was applied to each sequence to de-
tect fruit instances and assign ripeness classes. A tracking-by-detection 
strategy based on ByteTrack was then used to associate detections across 
consecutive frames and reduce repeated counting of the same fruit. Fruit 
counts were updated based on tracked identities rather than per-frame 
detections. Speciﬁcally, a fruit was counted only when the centroid of 
its tracked instance entered a predeﬁned counting window, and each 
track was counted at most once. To further reduce repeated counting 
caused by raw-ID fragmentation, a canonical-ID layer was used to pre-
serve the counted state when a fruit track was re-associated after a tem-
porary tracking interruption. The same segmentation model, tracking

3.  Results and discussion
3.1.  AprilTag detection evaluation
The AprilTag detection module was evaluated under three condi-
tions: camera-to-tag distance, viewing angle, and robot speed. A single 
AprilTag marker was placed at a known ﬁxed position in the simula-
tion environment, and the robot state, camera-to-tag geometry, com-
mand velocity, odometry velocity, and detection result were recorded 
during each test. A detection was considered successful when the target 
AprilTag was detected and a valid pose estimate was available in the 
recorded frame. The detection success rate 𝑆 was computed as deﬁned 
in Eq.  (11), with 𝑁valid the number of successful detections and 𝑁total
the total number of recorded frames in each evaluation condition.
For the distance evaluation, the robot was positioned at diﬀerent 
camera-to-tag distances while maintaining the tag approximately within 
the camera view. The recorded samples were grouped into distance in-
tervals, and the success rate was calculated for each interval. For the 
viewing-angle evaluation, the robot pose was varied relative to the tag 
while the camera-to-tag distance was also recorded. Since the camera 
was mounted on the right side of the robot, the viewing angle was cal-
culated based on the corrected camera heading rather than the robot 
heading. For the speed evaluation, the robot was commanded to move 
at diﬀerent linear speeds while observing the AprilTag. Since the robot 
could move in both forward and reverse directions, the signed com-
manded velocity was converted into absolute speed. Frames recorded 
while the robot was stopped or not moving within the intended speed 
interval were excluded from the speed-based calculation.

This near-ﬁeld limitation is geometric rather than a failure of the

at close range, and using the existing LiDAR range measurement as a 
complementary cue to anticipate the stopping point and pre-empt the 
transition before the robot enters the near-ﬁeld blind zone.
In the viewing-angle evaluation, the detector achieved 100% success 
at 5–15◦ and 15–30◦, showing that the system was robust when the tag 
was observed at moderate viewing angles. The success rate decreased 
at 0–5◦ and 30–45◦, with values of 40.10% and 39.22%, respectively. 
These results suggest that detection reliability was not determined by 
viewing angle alone, but also by camera framing and tag visibility dur-
ing the recorded motion. Therefore, the viewing-angle result should be 
interpreted together with the mean camera-to-tag distance and the vis-
ibility condition during the test.
In the speed evaluation, the highest detection success rate was ob-
tained at an absolute commanded speed of 0.207 m/s, with a suc-
cess rate of 90.57%. At higher speeds, the success rate varied between 
52.63% and 83.33%. The lowest success rate occurred at 0.864 m/s, 
while the success rate increased again to 75.00% at 1.045 m/s. This 
non-monotonic trend indicates that robot speed was not the only factor 
aﬀecting detection performance; camera-to-tag distance and viewing an-
gle also inﬂuenced the result. Nevertheless, compared with the static dis-
tance test, the speed evaluation showed lower and more variable success 
rates, indicating that robot motion reduced AprilTag detection stability.
Overall, the AprilTag evaluation conﬁrms that the detector per-
formed reliably when the marker was observed within an appropri-
ate distance and viewing-angle range. The distance test showed near-
perfect detection beyond 0.50 m, while the viewing-angle and speed 
tests revealed that detection performance became more sensitive when 
the tag visibility changed during robot motion. These ﬁndings indicate 
that AprilTag-based localization is suitable as a supporting reference in 
the simulated robot system, but the marker should be placed within a 
practical viewing distance and should remain suﬃciently visible to the 
side-mounted camera during robot motion.
3.2.  LiDAR-based navigation evaluation
In the simulated greenhouse environment, the proposed LiDAR-
based navigation controller was evaluated in ﬁve crop-row scenarios 
and one wall-corridor reference scenario. The crop rows represent nav-
igation conditions with irregular plant boundaries, while the wall cor-
ridor provides a more structured reference case with continuous and 
well-deﬁned side boundaries. For each scenario, the robot was tested in 
both forward and backward directions. One manually driven ground-
truth (GT) trajectory was recorded for each case, followed by three au-
tonomous repetitions using the proposed controller.
Before evaluation, the recorded trajectories were preprocessed to re-
move invalid samples, suppress discontinuities, trim stationary sections 
at the beginning and end of each run, and reduce high-frequency noise. 
To ensure a fair comparison, each autonomous trajectory was aligned 
to the same start and end points as the corresponding GT trajectory 
and resampled into an equal number of points along the normalized 
path progress. Therefore, the reported trajectory error represents the 
repeatability of the path shape relative to the GT reference, rather than 
diﬀerences caused by unequal recording duration or stopping position.
Fig. 14 shows the trajectory RMSE for all scenarios after prepro-
cessing and start–end normalization. Overall, the robot followed the GT 
trajectory closely in both forward and backward directions. The RMSE 
remained below 0.012 m for all tested rows and wall-corridor cases. 
This indicates that the proposed navigation method produced repeat-
able path-following behavior across diﬀerent simulated greenhouse row 
conditions.
A quantitative summary of the navigation performance is presented 
in Table 6. In the crop-row scenarios, the forward-direction RMSE 
ranged from 0.0040 m to 0.0111 m, while the backward-direction RMSE 
ranged from 0.0011 m to 0.0021 m. The backward runs generally 
showed lower trajectory RMSE than the forward runs after normaliza-
tion. This suggests that, under the evaluated simulation conditions, the 
robot was able to reproduce the return path with very high consistency 
once the start and end points were standardized.

part of the reduction is associated with the start–end alignment and 
normalized resampling applied during trajectory post-processing, 
which standardizes the return path; the backward advantage should 
therefore be interpreted as a combination of this favorable starting 
condition and the normalization procedure, rather than as a purely 
physical gain in navigation accuracy.

robot to follow the desired corridor path without relying solely on yaw

diﬀerences among rows are mainly attributed to variations in local 
boundary geometry and the distribution of LiDAR returns from plant 
structures.

3.3.  Selection of a base model for ripeness-class fruit segmentation
Before evaluating fruit counting performance, a base segmentation 
model was selected for ripeness-class fruit segmentation. In this study, 
the model was required to localize individual cherry tomato fruits, gen-
erate instance masks, and assign each fruit instance to one of three 
ripeness classes: green, orange, or red. Therefore, the model selection 
process compared YOLOv8-seg and YOLOv11-seg models with three 
model scales: nano (n), small (s), and medium (m). Each model was 
trained using both non-augmented and augmented datasets.
The selection was not based only on the highest segmentation mAP 
value. Since the selected model was used in the subsequent ByteTrack-
based fruit-counting evaluation, the model also needed to provide stable 
instance-level detections, balanced precision–recall behavior, and suﬃ-
cient processing speed for video-based robot perception. In this context, 
false-positive detections may lead to overcounting, while missed detec-
tions may lead to undercounting. Therefore, mask precision, mask re-
call, mask F1-score, mask mAP50–95, bounding-box mAP50–95, mean 
inference time, and pipeline FPS were considered together.
Table 8 summarizes the validation performance and video-based in-
ference speed of all trained models. The validation metrics were ob-
tained from the best epoch selected based on mask mAP50–95, while 
the speed metrics were calculated from robot-camera video inference. 
The pipeline FPS represents the mean end-to-end throughput, including 
preprocessing, inference, and postprocessing.
Fig. 16 compares the mask mAP50–95 values among all model con-
ﬁgurations. The augmented YOLOv11m-seg model achieved the highest 
mask mAP50–95 value of 0.416. However, this model also produced the 
lowest mask precision among all conﬁgurations, with a precision value 
of 0.646, and a relatively low mask F1-score of 0.706. In addition, its 
bounding-box mAP50–95 was only 0.336. These results indicate that al-
though the model obtained the highest mask mAP50–95, its detection 
behavior was less balanced for downstream tracking-based counting.
Fig. 17 shows the speed–accuracy trade-oﬀ using pipeline FPS and 
mask F1-score. The nano-scale models generally provided higher pro-
cessing speed, but their segmentation performance was lower than 
that of the small and medium models. The medium-scale models 
achieved stronger mask-level performance while still maintaining real-
time processing capability. Among all candidates, the non-augmented 
YOLOv8m-seg model achieved the highest mask F1-score of 0.780, with 
mask precision of 0.790, mask recall of 0.770, mask mAP50–95 of 0.374, 
bounding-box mAP50–95 of 0.651, and pipeline throughput of 54.10 
FPS.
Although augmented models and YOLOv11 variants achieved strong 
performance in several metrics, the ﬁnal model selection prioritized the 
stability required for tracking-based counting rather than the highest in-
dividual metric. In particular, the augmented YOLOv11m-seg model was 
not selected despite having the highest mask mAP50–95 because its low

3.4.  Fruit counting performance and qualitative error analysis
Fruit-counting performance was evaluated at the individual-plant 
level using all 126 plants distributed across six greenhouse rows. Each 
plant was evaluated under a static-image condition and ﬁve robot trav-
eling speeds ranging from 0.2 to 1.0 m/s. The same ground-truth plant 
community was used for all acquisition conditions and contained 1320 
fruits in total, consisting of 828 red, 336 orange, and 156 green fruits.
Table 9 summarizes the counting performance under each acquisi-
tion condition. The best overall performance was obtained at 0.2 m/s, 
at which the system counted 1189 fruits, corresponding to 90.1% of the 
ground-truth total. At this speed, the plant-level MAE and RMSE were 
2.02 and 4.16 fruits, respectively, and the count-level F1-score reached 
0.898. The 0.2 m/s condition also outperformed the static-image con-
dition, which recovered 81.2% of the ground-truth count and produced 
an F1-score of 0.822. This result suggests that slow camera motion pro-
vided temporal redundancy and multiple views of each plant, allowing 
some fruits that were not visible or conﬁdently detected in a single static 
image to be recovered in subsequent frames.

Fig. 18 presents the distribution of plant-level absolute counting 
errors. Unlike Table 9, which reports aggregate performance metrics, 
the ﬁgure shows the variability among individual plants. The 0.2 m/s
condition exhibited the lowest central error and a comparatively nar-
row error distribution, whereas both error magnitude and dispersion 
increased at higher traveling speeds.
A Friedman test applied to the matched plant-level absolute errors 
conﬁrmed a signiﬁcant eﬀect of acquisition condition (𝜒2(5) = 162.95, 
𝑝< 0.001, Kendall’s 𝑊= 0.259). Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons 
showed that the 0.2 m/s condition produced signiﬁcantly lower absolute 
errors than the static condition (𝑝= 0.040) and all higher moving speeds 
(𝑝< 0.001). No signiﬁcant diﬀerence was found between the static and 
0.4 m/s conditions (𝑝= 0.158). These results support the selection of 
0.2 m/s as the preferred speed for accurate counting and suggest that 
approximately 0.4 m/s represents the practical upper operating bound-
ary for the present perception and tracking conﬁguration.
The reduction in performance at higher speeds was driven primar-
ily by missed fruits rather than excessive counting. At 0.6 and 1.0 m/s, 
count-level precision remained relatively high at 0.980 and 0.961, re-
spectively, whereas recall decreased to 0.447 and 0.319. This indicates 
that many fruits were not detected or maintained as stable tracks for 
suﬃcient time to enter the counting region. Shorter observation dura-
tion, partial fruit visibility, and obstruction by the leaf canopy likely 
contributed to the observed undercounting. Because these factors aﬀect 
detection and tracking continuity, they cannot be fully corrected by the 
counting logic after a fruit has disappeared from the camera view.
The canonical-ID diagnostic logs showed that raw ByteTrack iden-
tities were frequently fragmented during video processing. Among the 
counted canonical tracks, 71.2% were associated with more than one 
raw tracker ID, and 62.0% underwent at least one relinking event af-
ter their initial count. Maintaining the counted state at the canonical-
ID level was therefore important. When ByteTrack assigned a new raw 
ID to a previously counted fruit, the relinked track retained the same 
canonical identity and was not counted again. The canonical-ID layer 
consequently reduced the risk of duplicate counting caused by tempo-
rary tracking interruptions. However, these diagnostic results should not 
be interpreted as a standard multi-object-tracking evaluation because 
frame-level ground-truth identities were not available.
Ripeness-class predictions were also temporally inconsistent for 
some tracks. Approximately 26.1% of counted canonical tracks received 
more than one ripeness-class label during their lifetime, while red–
orange alternation occurred in 18.4% of the counted tracks. This be-
havior is consistent with the visual similarity of fruits near the orange-
to-red transition. Nevertheless, the available plant-level ground truth 
cannot determine whether every class-count diﬀerence resulted from a 
true class switch, a missed fruit, or an additional detection. The quan-
titative results were therefore complemented by representative visual 
cases obtained at the selected speed of 0.2 m/s.

3.5.  Simulation-to-real gap, limitations, and planned physical validation
The Gazebo environment provided a controlled and repeatable plat-
form for integrating the navigation, perception, tracking, and fruit-
counting modules. Procedural generation also enabled diﬀerent plant 
and fruit distributions to be evaluated while retaining exact plant-level 
ground truth. Nevertheless, the reported results represent controlled-
simulation performance and should not be interpreted as direct esti-
mates of performance in a real greenhouse [39].
Several diﬀerences between the simulated and real environments 
may aﬀect system performance. First, simulated illumination remained 
relatively stable, whereas real greenhouses exhibit sunlight variation, 
shadows, reﬂections, glare, and changes in camera exposure. These ef-
fects may reduce segmentation conﬁdence and increase confusion be-
tween visually similar orange and red fruits. Second, the simulated 
plants and greenhouse structures used a limited set of meshes and tex-
tures and therefore did not reproduce the full variability of real fruit 
shape, surface appearance, leaf condition, background clutter, and ma-
terial reﬂectance.
The current LiDAR simulation also did not include a sensor-speciﬁc 
noise and measurement-dropout model. Real LiDAR measurements may 
contain range uncertainty, missing returns, outliers, and unstable re-
sponses from thin leaves, stems, and reﬂective greenhouse structures. 
Consequently, the simulated crop-row boundaries were likely more sta-
ble than those expected during physical operation, and the reported nav-
igation performance may represent an optimistic condition. In addition, 
the simulated plants were static, whereas real leaves and fruit clusters 
may move because of airﬂow, vibration, irrigation, or robot contact. 
Such motion may alter LiDAR boundary measurements, temporarily oc-
clude fruits, interrupt detections, and increase tracker-ID fragmentation.
The AprilTag-guided mission also assumes that the required mark-
ers remain visible, clean, and correctly positioned. In a real greenhouse, 
tags may be partially or fully occluded by leaves, stems, fruit clusters, 
tools, or workers. Dust, water droplets, mud, condensation, fading, and 
physical damage may also reduce image contrast and prevent reliable 
tag recognition. Variations in viewing angle, camera exposure, motion 
blur, and tag displacement may further delay or suppress the mission-
state transitions that depend on tag detection. Therefore, practical de-
ployment should include redundant markers, improved tag placement, 
periodic tag maintenance, and fallback behavior when an expected tag 
cannot be detected.
Another limitation is that the current navigation algorithm does not 
explicitly handle dynamic obstacles that may interrupt the mission. The 
simulated environment did not include moving workers, carts, tools, 
animals, or other robots crossing the robot path. The present controller 
primarily maintains row centering and executes predeﬁned AprilTag-
triggered transitions; it does not perform dynamic-obstacle prediction, 
collision avoidance, or autonomous path replanning. In physical opera-

tion, a temporary obstruction could block the LiDAR ﬁeld of view, con-
ceal an AprilTag, or interfere with lateral row-transition motion. Future 
implementations should therefore incorporate safety stopping, obstacle 
classiﬁcation, local replanning, and mission-resumption logic before real 
greenhouse deployment.
A further limitation concerns the spatial extent of the counting region 
of interest (ROI). The ROI restricts counting to a lateral band around 
the current row, and the canonical-identity layer prevents the same 
tracked fruit from being counted more than once over time. However, 
neither mechanism explicitly rejects fruits that physically belong to an 
adjacent row but happen to fall within the ROI in the image plane. In 
dense plantings, a robust detector may therefore register fruits from a 
neighboring row and inﬂate the count for the current row. The present 
ROI plus canonical-identity strategy thus suppresses temporal duplicate 
counting, but does not fully eliminate spatial leakage between adjacent 
rows. A depth-based validation step, described in the planned physical-
validation study below, is proposed to address this leakage using the 
depth-sensing capability already present on the robot.
These limitations may inﬂuence the absolute navigation, segmen-
tation, and counting performance, although the controlled simulation 
remains useful for comparing acquisition conditions and identifying sys-
tem behavior. In particular, the preferred counting speed of 0.2 m/s and 
the approximate upper operating boundary of 0.4 m/s are speciﬁc to the 
present camera, LiDAR, plant model, tracking conﬁguration, and sim-
ulated mission environment, and require validation under real green-
house illumination, plant motion, marker degradation, sensor noise, and 
dynamic-obstacle conditions.
The choice of simulation platform also shapes how well these results 
transfer to reality. Gazebo Classic, used in this study, provides mature 
ROS and ROS 2 integration and a lightweight footprint that runs on 
modest hardware, but it oﬀers only basic rendering and idealized sen-
sor models, and it has reached end-of-life. NVIDIA Isaac Sim, by con-
trast, provides GPU-accelerated PhysX physics, RTX ray-traced photore-
alistic rendering, and higher-ﬁdelity camera and LiDAR sensor models 
with conﬁgurable noise, together with built-in domain-randomization 
and synthetic-data generation (Replicator); these capabilities come at 
the cost of substantially higher GPU requirements. For the present work-
ﬂow, the most relevant expected beneﬁt is more realistic lighting, occlu-
sion, and sensor noise, which should narrow the perception sim-to-real 
gap and improve the transferability of the segmentation, tracking, and 
counting results, while the mission-control and LiDAR-centering logic 
would remain largely unchanged [39,40].
To verify that these simulation results transfer to real conditions, 
a physical-validation study is planned as the immediate next phase of 
this work. It will use a wheeled Mecanum robot carrying the same sen-
sor suite modeled here—a 3D LiDAR, a PTZ-RGB camera, and a front 
RGB camera—and will run the same ROS 2 nodes used in the virtual 
workﬂow, so that only the environment and physical sensors diﬀer. The 
validation follows a staged protocol in which each subsystem is veriﬁed 
before the integrated mission is tested:
1. Sensor calibration and bring-up: camera intrinsic and extrinsic calibra-
tion, LiDAR calibration, AprilTag pose-estimation checks across the 
operating distances, and LiDAR–odometry time synchronization.
2. Single-row navigation: LiDAR row-centering along one real crop row, 
quantiﬁed by cross-track RMSE and heading error against a surveyed 
reference path at the same commanded speeds used in simulation.
3. Full multi-row mission: end-to-end AprilTag-guided forward and back-
ward traversal, PTZ reorientation, and lateral row transitions across 
several rows, assessed by mission-completion rate and transition re-
liability.
4. Fruit counting: class-wise cherry-tomato counting against manual per-
plant ground truth, evaluated with the same plant-level MAE and 
RMSE, recovery rate, and count-level precision, recall, and F1-score 
reported here, and complemented by standard multi-object-tracking 
metrics (MOTA, IDF1, and ID switches).
5. Depth-based count validation: estimating the depth of the row cur-
rently under observation from the on-board LiDAR or depth camera, 
deriving the expected fruit-depth distribution for that row, and re-
jecting detections whose estimated depth deviates beyond a thresh-
old, so that fruits belonging to adjacent rows are excluded from the 
count. Combined with the lateral ROI and canonical-identity track-
ing, this depth gate targets spatial neighbor-row leakage in addition 
to temporal duplicates, and is expected to improve counting reliabil-
ity in dense multi-row plantings.
Because the same metrics are reported in both domains, this protocol 
will allow a direct simulation-to-real comparison and quantify the real-
ity gap for navigation and counting. Particular attention will be given to 
the counting-speed guidance derived in simulation (preferred 0.2 m/s, 
approximate upper boundary 0.4 m/s), which will be re-measured un-
der real illumination, plant motion, marker degradation, sensor noise, 
and dynamic obstacles to conﬁrm or revise the recommended operating 
range.
4.  Conclusion
This study developed and evaluated a simulation-driven 
ROS 2–Gazebo workﬂow for integrating greenhouse robot navigation, 
PTZ-RGB fruit perception, temporal tracking, and ripeness-speciﬁc 
cherry-tomato counting. The main conclusions are summarized as 
follows:
1. The developed virtual greenhouse provided a controlled and repeat-
able environment for evaluating the complete navigation and per-
ception workﬂow before physical deployment, with procedural plant 
generation enabling testing under heterogeneous canopy structures 
and fruit distributions while retaining exact plant-level ground truth.
2. The LiDAR-based row-centering controller achieved accurate and 
repeatable navigation in both directions, with trajectory RMSE be-
low 0.012 m across all crop-row and wall-corridor scenarios. Back-
ward traversal showed lower RMSE (0.0011–0.0021 m) than forward 
traversal (0.0040–0.0111 m), because each backward pass started 
from an already-centered, converged state, whereas the forward runs 
included the initial row-acquisition transient; the largest error oc-
curred in the forward direction (Row 5 forward, 0.0111 m), and part 
of this diﬀerence also reﬂects the start–end normalization applied 
during trajectory post-processing. The remaining diﬀerences among 
rows are mainly attributed to variations in local boundary geometry 
and LiDAR return distribution.
3. The selected YOLOv8m-seg model, combined with ByteTrack and 
canonical-ID-based counting, enabled class-wise counting of green, 
orange, and red cherry tomatoes, with the canonical-ID layer pre-
serving the counted state when raw tracker identities changed and 
thereby reducing duplicate counting after temporary tracking inter-
ruptions.
4. Robot traveling speed signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced counting perfor-
mance: the best result was obtained at 0.2 m/s (90.1% of the ground-
truth count; count-level F1-score 0.898), remained comparatively ac-
ceptable at 0.4 m/s, and declined substantially from 0.6 m/s onward. 
The main errors arose from leaf-canopy occlusion, limited observa-
tion duration at higher speeds, and classiﬁcation inconsistency be-
tween adjacent orange and red ripeness stages, showing that count-
ing performance depends jointly on robot motion, canopy structure, 
segmentation quality, and tracking stability.
5. The main research signiﬁcance of this work is the demonstration that 
simulation can serve not only as a pre-deployment testing platform, 
but also as a diagnostic tool for identifying navigation, perception, 
tracking, and counting limitations under controlled and repeatable 
conditions.
The simulation-to-real limitations of the present study have been dis-
cussed in Section 3.5. Future work will ﬁrst improve the simulation ﬁ-
delity before conducting physical validation. Higher-ﬁdelity platforms,

such as NVIDIA Isaac Sim, may be used to provide more realistic 
lighting, materials, sensor behavior, and robot–environment interaction 
through GPU-accelerated physics, photorealistic RTX rendering, and 
higher-ﬁdelity camera and LiDAR sensor models with built-in domain-
randomization tools [40], which are expected to improve perception 
sim-to-real transfer relative to the Gazebo Classic environment used 
here. The procedural plant generator will also be extended to repre-
sent greater variation in plant height, canopy density, leaf size and ori-
entation, stem structure, fruit size, shape, color, reﬂectance, ripeness 
distribution, and occlusion level. Dynamic leaf and fruit motion, cam-
era noise, motion blur, LiDAR uncertainty, and measurement dropout 
will be incorporated to support domain randomization and more de-
manding robustness tests. The complete workﬂow will subsequently be 
validated using a physical robot in real greenhouse rows, with LiDAR–
vision–odometry fusion and real image sequences containing persistent 
fruit identities to enable object-level evaluation using standard multi-
object-tracking metrics (e.g., MOTA, IDF1, and ID switches) alongside 
the count-level precision and recall used in this study.
