Fresh Fruit Bunch Ripeness Classification Methods: A Review
Jin Yu Goh1 · Yusri Md Yunos1 · Mohamed Sultan Mohamed Ali1,2
Received: 1 November 2023 / Accepted: 12 June 2024 / Published online: 28 June 2024 
© The Author(s) 2024
Abstract
The escalating demand for palm oil necessitates enhanced production strategies. As the trend shifts towards automated 
harvesting to meet the demand, precise ripeness classification has become pivotal. Manual methods are inefficient and error-
prone because of workforce constraints. The present review scrutinizes the following non-destructive ripeness classification 
methods: spectroscopy, inductive sensing, thermal imaging, light detection and ranging, laser-light backscattering imaging, 
and computer vision. The review focuses on identifying reliable techniques capable of real-time and accurate classification 
in dynamic and unstructured environments. All aforementioned techniques are discussed in intricate detail, accompanied 
by thorough critiques. This review then presents a performance comparison and benchmarking process, providing com-
prehensive insights into the strengths and weaknesses of each technique. A compelling solution emerges in the fusion of 
light detection and ranging and computer vision techniques. This synergy capitalizes on their strengths to offset individual 
limitations, offering a potent approach. Furthermore, this fusion yields added value in terms of localization and mapping, 
rendering it exceptionally suitable for real-time classification in complex environments. This review provides insights into 
bridging the gap between automated harvesting needs and ripeness assessment precision, thereby fostering advancements 
in the palm oil industry.
Keywords  Computer vision in agriculture · Agriculture automation · Non-destructive assessment · Oil palm technology
Introduction
The oil palm industry exerts a notable global impact, play-
ing a vital role in the production of various edible and non-
edible consumer goods. Thus, global demand for fresh fruit 
bunches (FFB) has increased significantly, accounting for 
approximately 35% of global vegetable oil consumption 
(United States Department of Agriculture, 2023a). In 2022, 
there was a record-breaking global production of 78.14 mil-
lion metric tons of FFB (United States Department of Agri-
culture, 2023b), with Indonesia and Malaysia accounting for 
83.18% of that total. Because of FFB’s versatility in various 
industries, as well as increasing global demand due to popu-
lation growth and shifting dietary patterns, efficient harvest-
ing of FFB is gaining attention to cope with production rates.
Harvesting FFB from an oil palm tree requires experi-
enced workers because FFB can weigh 20–25 kg and must 
be cut from heights of up to 20 feet (Malaysian Palm Oil 
Council, n.d.). Workers are at high risk of injury during this 
process due to the force exerted to cut the FFB with a sickle 
and the impact threat when the FFB drops down (Syazwani 
et al., 2016). Likewise, the conventional harvesting process 
is labor-intensive and employs approximately 8 million indi-
viduals in Indonesia (Indonesian Palm Oil Association, n.d.). 
The integration of innovative technology for autonomous 
harvesting operations has emerged as a critical imperative 
for the field and mitigating potential human errors associated 
with decision-making tasks that directly affect productivity. 
The first stage in implementing an autonomous FFB harvest-
ing system is to develop reliable and accurate methods for 
recognizing and classifying FFB ripeness, thereby addressing 
the existing difficulties in ripeness classification. The conven-
tional approach to determining the ripeness of FFB while still 
on the tree involves visual examination by knowledgeable 
graders, which entails inspecting and classifying the FFB 
based on their color and the number of loose fruitlets on the 
ground adjacent to the tree. The quality index identification

inaccuracy of these classifications hinders optimal harvesting

Adopting ripeness classification technology is crucial for 
the oil palm industry’s long-term growth, and the methods 
should be able to operate in a real-time outdoor dynamic and 
unstructured environment. There are two types of methods 
for ripeness classification: destructive and non-destructive 
methods. Researchers have commonly used various tech-
niques under non-destructive methods, such as spectroscopy, 
inductive sensing, thermal imaging, light detection and rang-
ing (LiDAR), laser-light backscattering imaging (LLBI), and 
computer vision, for classification purposes. Because of the 
importance of this research field, several efforts have been 
made to review the use of ripeness classification techniques 
in the context of oil palm. For instance, Lai et al. (2023) 
and Yee et al. (2020) reviewed FFB ripeness detection tech-
niques, providing valuable current insight into both destruc-
tive and non-destructive methods. However, the authors did 
not evaluate the collected results with quantitative assess-
ments, which are essential for determining the performance 
metrics of the reported techniques. Additionally, Mohamed 
Ahmed Mansour et al. (2022) specifically reviewed non-
destructive methods for FFB ripeness classification and 
evaluated various classification methods with comparative 
performance indexes. However, the authors provided a gen-
eral overview of the non-destructive methods without delv-
ing into each technique’s specific technical details and real-
world scenarios, further limiting the reader’s understanding 
of the methodologies.
Despite a considerable body of research in this domain, a 
notable paucity exists in comprehensive review articles that 
explore the diverse aspects of the oil palm sector within the 
framework of ripeness classification. Unlike previous review 
articles, the present rigorous systematic review ensures a 
methodical meta-analysis that was evaluated by enabling 
qualitative and quantitative assessment benchmarks of the 
compiled literature. The present review focuses on notable 
advancements, prevailing trends, and pioneering concepts, 
thereby stimulating further research in the oil palm domain 
by using innovative ripeness classification techniques. A 
structured approach was used to select relevant sources for a 
comprehensive and informative review. Priority was given to 
journal articles published in highly regarded journals within 
the fields of FFB ripeness classification. However, there are 
limited articles because of the geographically concentrated 
research effort on FFB ripeness classification in Southeast 
Asia. To address this gap and capture the latest advance-
ments, some conference proceedings were incorporated in 
this review. These conference materials provide valuable 
insights into the potential of novel technologies for FFB 
ripeness assessment. Ultimately, this review offers high-
quality recommendations for researchers, backed by detailed 
evaluation of the existing literature, thereby propelling the 
field of oil palm research into new frontiers.

the exocarp. The fruitlets ripen within an FFB, so the exo-

accumulate as oil components, replacing chlorophyll in the 
mesocarp and changing the color from black or dark purple 
in the unripe stage to dark red or orange in the ripe stage 
(Harun et al., 2013). Several factors can influence oil quality, 
including oil content and free fatty acids (FFAs) produced 
by glyceride hydrolysis in the presence of water (Makky 
& Soni, 2014). The extraction of crude palm oil involves 
obtaining the mesocarp (Basyuni et al., 2017), which pri-
marily consists of glycerides with minor quantities of non-
glyceride components, including FFAs, moisture, trace met-
als, and impurities (Mba et al., 2015).

still attached to the FFB, mesocarp color, and exocarp color. 
Besides the fruitlet color, which is the primary indicator of 
ripeness, the quantity of loose fruitlet sockets improves the 
classification accuracy.
Ripeness Classification Techniques
To achieve accurate ripeness classification of FFB, research-
ers have explored various techniques that leverage advanced 
technology and data analysis. The present review focuses 
on non-destructive methods that classify the maturity of 
FFB without damaging or destroying it by extracting the 
external features for further study. The review includes 
the following in-depth techniques: spectroscopy, inductive 
sensing, thermal imaging, LiDAR, LLBI, and computer 
vision. Accuracy and coefficient of determination (R2) are 
crucial performance metrics used to evaluate the effective-
ness of the aforementioned techniques used for the ripeness 
classification. Accuracy measures the closeness of sensor 
measurements to actual ripeness, signifying the reliability 
of the sensor’s output. In spectroscopy, thermal imaging, 
inductive sensing, LiDAR, and LLBI, accuracy ensures 
the correct identification of components, precise distance 
measurements, accurate temperature readings, and reliable 
predictions of FFB properties. However, R2 evaluates the 
goodness of fit of regression models that establish rela-
tionships between sensor data and real-world quantities. A 
higher R2 value indicates a stronger correlation, suggesting 
a better fit for the model and higher accuracy of the sensor 
data. Both accuracy and R2 play pivotal roles in ensuring the 
effectiveness and dependability of the ripeness classification 
techniques to meet specific application requirements. This 
review provides useful insights into robust and reliable non-
destructive FFB ripeness classification methods by exploring 
the latest advancements and findings.
Spectroscopy
In the context of FFB ripeness classification, spectroscopy 
is used to determine the chemical and physical properties of 
the fruitlets by measuring the wavelengths and intensities 
of electromagnetic radiation absorbed, emitted, or diffused. 
Various molecules and elements across multiple ripeness 
stages have distinctive spectral signatures that can be used 
to identify and analyze them. When FFB is exposed to elec-
tromagnetic radiation, it absorbs or emits energy at specific 
wavelengths that correspond to the energies required for 
the FFB’s electronic, vibrational, or rotational transitions. 
Researchers have determined the FFB’s chemical composi-
tion (lipid and moisture content), molecular structure (tri-
glycerides and fatty acids), and physical properties (bulk 
density and oil viscosity) by measuring the wavelengths 
and intensities of the absorbed or emitted radiation (Pavia 
et al., 2013) (Fig. 2). Spectroscopy has numerous applica-
tions in scientific research and industry; the present review 
focuses on near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), hyperspectral 
imaging, Raman spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy,

Near Infrared Spectroscopy
NIRS is a non-destructive analytical technique that involves 
emitting near-infrared light onto an FFB and measuring how 
much light is absorbed or reflected. The resulting spectra 
can be used to identify and quantify chemical compounds 
in the fruitlet. The near-infrared region of the electromag-
netic spectrum is typically defined as wavelengths ranging 
from 700 to 2500 nm. This region is useful for spectroscopy 
because it contains high molecular vibrations that are unique 
to specific chemical bonds in molecules. By measuring the 
absorption or reflection of light at various wavelengths in 
this range, NIRS can provide information about the chemi-
cal composition of the fruitlet. For the use case of NIRS to 
classify the ripeness of oil palm FFB, the oil and moisture 
content in the mesocarp of FFB was evaluated using NIRS, 
and the result was validated with an average classifica-
tion accuracy of > 81% (Silalahi et al., 2016a, 2016b). The 
classification is made possible by comparing the absorbance 
of NIRS spectra (Fig. 2), which shows that the ripe FFB has 
a higher absorbance than the unripe FFB. Iqbal et al. (2019) 
developed a calibration model based on the NIRS approach 
using chemometrics to predict the water content and oil con-
tent of the oil palm FFB. The calibration model uses partial 
least square (PLS) regression to predict the water content 
using multiplicative scatter correction preprocessing; how-
ever, the oil content calibration statistic parameter did not 
meet the requirements that restrict the application of PLS 
on the oil content approach. The genetic algorithm neural 
network model (GANN) (Silalahi et al., 2016a, 2016b) was 
used to process the NIRS spectral data for FFB ripeness 
classification with the result of dimensional reduction from 
original NIRS spectral data using principal component anal-
ysis (PCA) validation scores of 84.5% for good classification 
accuracy. NIRS is conducted in the laboratory and requires 
specialized equipment; heavy assets and lack of portabil-
ity restrict the data-acquiring process in dynamic outdoor 
environments, causing major drawbacks to the feasibility of 
implementation in dynamic outdoor environments.

imaging technique involving the acquisition and process-
ing of FFB images in hundreds or thousands of contiguous 
narrows and closely spaced spectral bands across a broad 
spectrum of wavelengths. The acquired data is used to gen-
erate a hyperspectral data cube, in which each pixel in the 
image represents a spectrum, and its spectral signature can 
be analyzed. The spectral signature of each pixel is used to

identify and classify the fruitlet substances based on their 
chemical and physical properties. As FFB ripens, its com-
position changes, affecting not only chemical properties 
like moisture content, oil concentration, and pigment com-
position but also physical properties like fruitlet firmness, 
texture, and even subtle changes in size. These combined 
changes manifest in the fruitlet’s spectral response that are 
captured using hyperspectral imaging. Hyperspectral imag-
ing is typically conducted with specialized imaging systems, 
such as hyperspectral cameras and spectrometers, which can 
capture high-resolution images over a broad spectrum of 
wavelengths. Chu et al. (2022) extracted and analyzed each 
pixel’s spectral information by using specialized software 
that was compatible with the equipment. The hyperspectral 
imaging approach was then initiated to classify the ripeness 
of oil palm FFB for an in-line monitoring system using red, 
green, and blue (RGB) color images from a hyperspectral 
camera that focuses on identifying the color reflectivity from 
each pixel with intensity values ranging from 615 to 940 nm 
with an average 20 nm interval from 16 light-emitting diode 
(LED). The statistical results from the study demonstrated 
significant differences in ripeness at a wavelength of 660 nm. 
The captured images were processed with linear discrimi-
nant analysis (LDA) with Mahalanobis distance, yielding an 
average accuracy of 88.2% (Setiawan et al., 2019). Bensaeed 
et al. (2014) used a hyperspectral-based system to classify 
FFB ripeness by recording the reflectance at various wave-
lengths. The oil palm FFB was classified into three ripeness 
categories (ripe, underripe, and overripe) using a hyperspec-
tral device and an artificial neural network (ANN) model. 
The ANN model successfully classified the FFB with an 
accuracy of > 95%. The spectral bands from 710 to 940 nm 
demonstrated the best test accuracy between the three types 
of oil palm FFB ripeness. FFB has a stable range from 770 
to 890 nm with an accuracy of 83.3%, which considers the 
overall most optimum wavelengths to classify between the 
three ripeness categories of oil palm FFB. Studies have 
implemented a similar approach with several LEDs in vari-
ous specific wavelengths (Cherie et al., 2015; Setiawan & 
Prasetya, 2020; Utom et al., 2018) to acquire multispectral 
images. Hue, saturation, and value (HSV) color spaces in the 
acquired images were the elements for evaluation, and the 
most optimum result was obtained at a 940 nm wavelength. 
Hyperspectral imaging is prone to background noises in the 
image; thus, obtaining clear and background-free images is 
only possible in a controlled laboratory with independent 
FFB, which is a disadvantage in real-time applications.
The present review primarily explores the use of hyper-
spectral imaging for FFB ripeness classification. However, 
Hyperspectral imaging holds immense potential for assess-
ing the ripeness of various other fruits. Researchers have 
actively applied this technology to monitor the ripening 
stages of diverse fruits like strawberries, bananas, kiwifruits, 
and blackberries (Benelli et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2020; Oli-
sah et al., 2024; Pu et al., 2019). Hyperspectral imaging 
offers a non-destructive and objective method for ripeness 
assessment by analyzing the unique spectral signatures of 
these fruits at various ripeness stages.
Raman Spectroscopy
Raman spectroscopy measures inelastic light dispersion 
by irradiating an FFB with a laser source before collecting 
and analyzing the dispersed light to determine the molecu-
lar structure and composition of the FFB. When laser light 
interacts with the fruitlet, some photons are elastically dis-
persed, which means they retain the same energy and wave-
length as the incident photons. Nonetheless, a small propor-
tion of the dispersed photons are scattered inelastically, and 
these photons endure a change in energy and wavelength as 
a result of interactions with the FFB molecules. The energy 
and wavelength shift of the dispersed photons provides 
information about the vibrational modes of the fruitlet’s 
molecules, and the resultant Raman spectra can be used 
to identify and quantify the chemical composition. Raman 
spectra can reveal the types of chemical bonds present in an 
FFB, the relative concentrations of various chemical species, 
and the structural properties, such as the presence of impuri-
ties or defects (Dan et al., 2018). Raman spectroscopy is a 
popular approach for oil palm FFB because of its carotene 
content. The Raman peak averaging at 1515cm−1 is proven to 
be a crucial molecular fingerprint for carotene levels, which 
can serve as a ripeness indicator in oil palm FFB. Further 
signal analysis on the Raman peak reveals four primary 
sub-bands (lycopene, β-carotene, lutein, and neoxanthin), 
which originate from the stretching vibration of carotenoid 
molecules found in the peel of the oil palm fruitlet. The fine 
kernel nearest neighbors (KNN) classifier is found to provide 
the highest overall accuracy of 100% (Raj et al., 2021) with 
the use of limited samples (46 samples). Another study used 
a confocal Raman spectrometer to extract spectral features 
from the FFB and further analyzed with ANN to obtain a 
prediction accuracy of 95.48% (Hong et al., 2021), demon-
strating the effectiveness of this method. Tzuan et al. (2022) 
located the following eight hidden Raman peaks: protein, 
beta carotene, carotene, lipid, guanine/cytosine, chloro-
phyll-a, and tryptophan. Analysis of variance revealed six 
peak intensities from proteins to amide III (β-sheet), beta-
carotene, carotene, lipid, guanine/cytosine, and carotene; 
one peak location from lipid was found to be significant. 
An automated oil palm FFB ripeness classification system 
deployed with ANN using the seven significant features 
demonstrated an overall performance of 97.9% accuracy 
(Tzuan et al., 2022), and KNN achieved 90.9% accuracy 
(Azmi et al., 2022). Raman spectroscopy can classify the 
ripeness of FFB, which is robust against lights and noise

Fluorescence Spectroscopy
Fluorescence spectroscopy is a spectroscopic method that 
uses fluorescence to determine the properties of FFB fruitlet 
molecules. It entails the excitation of an FFB with a specific 
wavelength of light and the measurement of the emitted fluo-
rescence light at an extended wavelength. Because of the 
relaxation of excited electronic states in the fruitlet, the fluo-
rescence emission provides information about the molecular 
structure and properties of the fruitlet. The FFB’s properties 
can be determined by measuring and analyzing the fluores-
cence light emitted (Lakowicz, 2011). A fluorescence sensor 
is used to obtain the blue-to-red fluorescence ratio param-
eter from blue-green (447 nm) to far-red (685 nm) emission 
signals by using UV light emitting diode as the excitation 
light source; the classification and regression tree method 
successfully distinguished between the three categories with 
an overall classification accuracy of 89.7%. An optical probe 
consisting of a 635 nm diode laser and two photodiodes was 
used to study the relation of fruitlet firmness, which resulted 
in photodiode voltages. The output voltages were amplified 
from a commercial multi-board circuit, and firmness levels 
were obtained from the penetrometer. The linear correlation 
was calculated to get the conclusion that ripe FFB produces 
higher voltage and vice versa (Sari et al., 2019). Hazir et al., 
(2012a, 2012b) investigated the potential of flavonoids and 
anthocyanins because predictors were used to classify the 
degree of oil palm FFB ripeness. The research collected 180 
oil palm FFB samples from a private plantation in Malaysia 
and scanned them using a hand-held Multiplex ® 3 multi-
parameter fluorescence sensor. The findings show that both 
predictors can be good indicators for classifying oil palm 
FFB into under-ripe, ripe, and over-ripe maturity categories. 
The highest overall classification accuracy was 87.7% using 
a stochastic gradient boosting trees model with both predic-
tors. The positive outcomes of this research show the poten-
tial of flavonoids and anthocyanins as predictors for oil palm 
FFB ripeness. Fluorescence spectroscopy for outdoor real-
time FFB ripeness detection faces obstacles such as inter-
ference from ambient light and background fluorescence, 
variability in fruit samples, difficulties in optical access and 
alignment, sensor stability, and the computational demands 
of real-time data processing. Fluorescence spectroscopy 
must be carefully designed and optimized to detect outdoor 
FFB maturity to overcome these limitations and improve 
accuracy and reliability.
Microwave Spectroscopy
Microwave spectroscopy uses microwave radiation to exam-
ine the rotational energy levels of fruitlet molecules. It is 
based on the observation that molecules can absorb and 
emit electromagnetic radiation at specific frequencies cor-
responding to their rotational energy levels by conveying 
microwave radiation through a fruitlet containing molecules. 
As the microwave radiation interacts with the fruitlet’s mol-
ecules, the molecules can absorb the radiation and attain 
higher rotational energy levels. The assimilated energy alters 
the dipole moment of the molecule, which can be measured 
and analyzed to reveal information about the molecule’s 
rotational energy levels (Townes & Schawlow, 2012). You 
et al. (2020) used various microwave sensors used in oil 
palm monitoring for FFB ripeness classification to obtain the 
shifting resonance frequency/wavelength or the change in 
the reflection/transmission coefficient. The dielectric proper-
ties and moisture content are the important elements when 
determining the corresponding classification for FFB. Fur-
thermore, Yeow et al. (2010) developed a low-cost coaxial 
moisture sensor for determining the moisture content of oil 
palm FFB of various degrees of fruit ripeness. The sensor 
operates between 1 and 5 GHz and is suitable for single-
fruit measurement. The actual moisture content was deter-
mined using the standard oven drying method and compared 
with the predicted value of fruit moisture content obtained 
using the studied sensor. The sensor was used to monitor 
fruit ripeness based on the measurement of the phase or 
magnitude of the reflection coefficient. The profundity of 
microwave penetration can vary depending on the size of the 
fruit samples, which can consequently affect the accuracy 
of the measurements. Based on the limitations of the probe 
configuration at frequencies below 4 GHz, the minimum 
sample thickness for significant signal penetration was estab-
lished to be 3.5 mm (Yeow et al., 2010). For larger or thicker 
fruit samples, microwave radiation may not penetrate deeply 
enough to provide reliable information about the internal 
properties of the fruit. This limitation makes it difficult to 
use microwave spectroscopy to determine the maturity of 
FFB of varying sizes and thicknesses. Moreover, this method 
lacks performance evaluation in the reference papers, limit-
ing its comparability with other techniques in this review.
Inductive Sensing
Inductive sensing is an electromagnetic induction-based 
method for determining FFB ripeness classification. Induc-
tive sensors can be used to measure certain ripeness-depend-
ent physical characteristics of FFB, such as electrical con-
ductivity and physical properties. The inductive sensor used 
for FFB ripeness classification, known as a flat-type induc-
tor, consists of an electromagnetic field-generating air-cored

coil sandwiched between two non-conducting materials such 
as Perspex (Fig. 3a). When the FFB fruitlet approaches the 
sensor, the electromagnetic field interacts with the elec-
trical and magnetic properties of the fruitlet. Changes in 
the dielectric properties of the fruitlet because of ripeness 
influence the capacitance and inductance value, allowing 
for a shift in resonance frequency. The distinctive resonance 
frequency contributes to the classification of FFB ripeness. 
Misron et al. (2017) proposed a flat-type inductive concept 
based on a resonant frequency technique, which focuses on 
the resonance frequency of the air coil and the change in the 
air coil’s stray capacitance. The experiment was conducted 
by selecting three FFB and observing the change of resonant 
frequency from the 8th to the 18th week of anthesis. The 
resonant frequencies of the ripe FFB were recorded from 9.8 
to 9.9 MHz, while unripe started at 6.3 MHz. The research 
further analyzed the moisture content in the fruitlet, which 
can be determined from the capacitance of the sensor. As 
the lipid content in the fruitlet increases when the number of 
weeks progresses, the capacitance value obtained decreases. 
Harun et al. (2014) analyzed dual resonant frequencies 
by having two sets of flat-type inductive sensors measur-
ing from the top and bottom of the fruitlet. However, both 
papers lack relevant performance results that are comparable 
with those in the present review. Sinambela et al. (2020) 
designed a portable flat-type inductive sensor (Fig. 3b) to 
collect resonant frequency data, which was then analyzed 
using discriminant analysis to determine the ripeness. Unlike 
the previously mentioned papers, Sinambela et al. used a 
low frequency (270–500 Hz) that resulted in lower resonant 
frequencies obtained for ripe FFB. Based on the field test 
performance, the inductive sensor system can determine the 
oil palm ripeness with an accuracy of 100%. Furthermore, 
the proposed system can forecast the harvest time by trac-
ing the ripeness after anthesis. Because the ripe FFB had a 
lower resonant frequency, the ripeness was predicted using 
polynomial regression.
The calibration process is essential to achieve high-
accuracy FFB ripeness classification but is time-consuming 
and labor-intensive. The process requires the collection of 
reference data from various fruitlet samples representing 
multiple phases of ripeness. The inherent variability of fruit 
characteristics presents a significant challenge in achieving 
consistent and accurate ripeness classification. Furthermore, 
the sensitivity and specificity of the sensing system must be 
thoroughly evaluated to guarantee that it can reliably detect 
minor variations in ripeness levels. Fruit maturity, moisture 
content, and variations in fruit composition can affect the 
accuracy and dependability of the classification results. 
External interferences (e.g., electromagnetic interference) 
and environmental factors (e.g., temperature and humid-
ity) can influence sensor readings, necessitating the use of 
shielding techniques or signal-processing algorithms to miti-
gate these effects. Overcoming these limitations necessitates

Thermal Imaging
Thermal imaging is a technique for detecting and measuring 
thermal radiation from an FFB. Thermal camera devices can 
capture and quantify infrared radiation, which represents the 
surface temperature of FFB fruitlets. The captured thermal 
images provide a visual representation of the temperature 
radiation distribution across the observed FFB, enabling the 
identification of surface temperature gradients and thermal 
distribution variations. Oil has a lower specific heat capac-
ity and thermal conductivity than water, so ripe FFB with 
higher oil content dissipates heat less efficiently than unripe 
FFB with higher moisture content. Thus, ripe FFB tends to 
radiate higher temperatures than unripe FFB. Fauziah et al. 
(2021) presented a thermal-vision system to observe the 
thermal properties of oil palm FFB before harvest. The sys-
tem uses the multilayer perceptron ANN algorithm to pro-
cess thermal images by identifying temperature values that 
are represented by the intensity of RGB color. The R2 value 
for FFB ripeness was found to be 0.9058 when correlated 
with oil content and 0.8039 when correlated with tempera-
ture. Makky & Cherie (2021) developed a linear regression 
model to predict the ratio of oil and moisture content of 
observed FFB with an R2 of 0.6732. Moreover, a multiple-
regression model was successfully developed to estimate the 
concentration of carotene in FFB mesocarp with an R2 of 
0.8122. Zolfagharnassab et al. (2022) used the difference 
between the average temperature of the FFB and the ambient 
temperature (∆Temp) to normalize the measurement (Fig. 4). 
A classification analysis was performed to assess the poten-
tial of ∆Temp of FFB as an index for ripeness classification. 
The ∆Temp values were used in linear discriminant analysis, 
Mahalanobis discriminant analysis, ANN, and KNN algo-
rithms. Notably, the ANN method achieved the highest over-
all accuracy of 99.1%, demonstrating the promising potential 
of thermal imaging for oil palm maturity classification.
FFB ripeness is a multifaceted attribute influenced by a 
complex interplay of factors beyond just temperature. These 
factors include changes in sugar composition, particularly 
increases in non-structural, structural, and water-soluble 
sugars during the ripening (Thang et al., 2017). Additional 
factors include potential variations in acidity and anthocya-
nin content. The observed decrease in anthocyanin content 
with increasing ripeness (Hazir et al., 2012a, 2012b) is 
associated with a possible shift in FFB pH as it matures. 
Anthocyanins, as pH-sensitive pigments, may decrease in 
concentration or change color as the environment becomes 
less acidic (Luiza et al., 2022). Consequently, relying merely 
on thermal imaging to evaluate the viability of fruit may 
not provide a comprehensive understanding of the actual 
ripeness stage. Furthermore, thermal imaging requires cali-
bration and validation to establish meaningful correlations 
between temperature measurements and particular ripeness 
indicators. A potential limitation of using thermal imaging 
for oil palm FFB maturity classification lies in the inherent 
variability of fruit size. The thermal camera captures the 
entire FFB (Zolfagharnassab et al., 2022), so the measured 
average radiation temperature is influenced by bunch size. 
Compared with smaller bunches, larger bunches tend to have

LiDAR and LLBI
LiDAR has long been recognized as a powerful tool, par-
ticularly for tasks such as localization and mapping. The 
ability to measure the time it takes for the sensor light to 
return from the targeted object enables accurate mapping of 
surroundings and determination of the object’s precise posi-
tion. The LiDAR sensor can be mounted on a mobile robot 
to perform the task (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, the application 
of LiDAR has expanded significantly in the classification of 
FFB ripeness. By harnessing the distinct variations in reflec-
tion caused by varying degrees of FFB ripeness, research-
ers have unlocked the potential to derive crucial ripeness 
information by calculating the reflectance patterns in the 
form of point clouds exhibited by FFB (Fig. 5b). The point 
cloud data collected using the LiDAR sensor can be used 
to develop algorithms for accurate FFB ripeness classifica-
tion. Mat Seri et al. (2021) developed a LiDAR scanning 
system to predict the ripeness of oil palm FFB. The system 
was built using a LiDAR sensor and servo motors to obtain 
the point cloud data and intensity value reflected from the 
oil palm FFB. The authors designed the scanning mecha-
nism to overcome the vertical range limitation of the used 
sensor, which is a single-stripe laser transmitter. Ripe oil 
palm FFB tends to reflect a lower mean intensity value than 
unripe FFB. Furthermore, Husin et al. (2021) took a similar 
approach, including coordinating information on the ripe 
FFB in a distribution map. This study validated the use of 
LiDAR sensors to distinguish FFB ripeness. The LiDAR 
system was used to scan the FFB, and a reflectance percent-
age ranging from 0 to 100% was calculated based on the 
concept of linearity (Zulkifli et al., 2019). This reflectance

Apart from LiDAR, similar imaging methods such as 
LLBI have also shown promise in differentiating FFB 
ripeness levels. LLBI systems use diode lasers with 
two distinct wavelengths, a CMOS camera, and an opti-
cal component. The system analyzes the relationship 
between the reflectance intensities represented using 
RGB values in the captured FFB images and FFB height 
variations to assess ripeness (Shiddiq et  al., 2017). 
Another research investigated a hybrid system of LLBI 
and computer vision, combining the determination of 
oil content and color values to achieve > 85% accuracy 
(Mohd Ali et al., 2020). Laser-light backscattering is 
used to categorize FFB’s level of ripeness and to assess 
the maturity of plants, including watermelon, potato, 
apricot, banana, and macaw oil palm (Sunilkumar & 
Sparjan, 2013; Adebayo et al., 2016; Costa et al., 2017; 
Zulkifli et al., 2019; Mozaffari et al., 2022). The chemi-
cal and textural characteristics of the FFB can be identi-
fied through laser-light backscattering. The laser diode’s 
wavelength can be adjusted to obtain the physical charac-
teristics of the fruit. The FFB’s absorption and scattering 
characteristics are also connected to its backscattering 
profile. Similar research on cocoa pods (Lockman et al., 
2019) with LLBI revealed the disadvantage of detecting 
intact FFB on the tree because it requires a laser diode 
pointed at the specific fruit to obtain the reflectance 
data, which inhibits effectiveness in a dynamic outdoor 
environment.
There are some potential drawbacks to using LiDAR 
and LLBI techniques for FFB ripeness classification 
in outdoor dynamic environments. These techniques 
may have limitations in penetrating dense vegetation or 
opaque surfaces, hindering their ability to capture data 
from deep within objects. Additionally, environmental 
conditions such as fog, rain, or dust can affect the accu-
racy and performance of these techniques by scattering 
or absorbing laser light. The implementation costs of 
LiDAR and LLBI systems can be high, and processing 
the large volume of generated data can be computation-
ally intensive. Furthermore, these techniques primarily 
provide geometric information and may be limited in 
their ability to offer detailed spectral or color informa-
tion about the fruit bunches. Harsh weather conditions 
and mobility or range limitations may also affect their 
performance in dynamic outdoor environments.
Computer Vision
Computer vision has emerged as the preferred method for 
object classification because of its commercial viability and 
ease of implementation. With advances in imaging technology 
and computational algorithms, the use of image processing in 
FFB ripeness detection has gained significantly in recent years. 
Computer vision is a multidisciplinary field of study that seeks 
to enable machines to perceive and comprehend visual data in 
a manner analogous to human vision. The workflow for FFB 
ripeness classification through computer vision unfolds seam-
lessly in a well-orchestrated workflow (Fig. 6). The process 
begins with the acquisition of high-resolution images of palm 
oil FFB at varying ripeness stages, followed by precise image 
annotation. Each fruit bunch is carefully delineated and labeled 
based on its ripeness, constituting an annotated dataset system-
atically organized in a structured database. This dataset serves 
as the cornerstone for the ensuing machine-learning phase. 
Prior to model training, a crucial preprocessing stage refines 
the dataset to ensure its optimal format. Feature extraction then 
identifies key characteristics such as color distribution and tex-
ture. The core of the process lies in training a machine learning 
model, commonly CNN, utilizing the annotated dataset. Vali-
dation and fine-tuning enhance the model’s adaptability to new, 
unseen data. Following the training, the model is ready for 
inference, delivering real-time ripeness predictions for input 
FFB images. A popular approach in computer vision involves 
integrating technology with a mobile robot. This approach 
allows for automated, real-time classification of oil palm 
FFB directly on the plantation, as explored by Suharjito et al. 
(2021). The robot is equipped with all-terrain wheels (Fig. 5a) 
to navigate uneven terrain effectively, thereby developing 
algorithms and systems that derive meaningful characteristics 
from digital images or videos. These characteristics serve as 
the foundation for more complex tasks for real-time dynamic 
and unstructured environment ripeness classification. Image 
filtering, edge detection, feature detection, and segmentation 
are among the most important techniques used in the feature 
extraction process in computer vision. These techniques are 
used to recognize and isolate pertinent visual patterns, objects, 
or regions of interest within the visual data. Using machine 
learning and deep learning techniques, computer vision algo-
rithms (Mansour et al., 2022) can recognize and understand 
complex visual patterns for FFB ripeness, enabling machines 
to comprehend and interpret visual content. Shabdin et al. 
(2016) investigated a color classification method using the hue, 
saturation, and intensity (HSI) model. This model can catego-
rize oil palm FFB into underripe and ripe classes. The model 
uses an ANN model that achieves 70% accuracy. Fadilah et al. 
(2012) further improved accuracy to 71.66% by incorporating 
a data reduction technique called PCA into the ANN model. 
Alfatni et al., (2014, 2020) and Ibrahim et al. (2018a) explored 
another approach based on real-time analysis of RGB color 
spaces. This method involves transforming the RGB data into 
HSI. The HSI color space allows researchers to analyze the 
properties of digital color values and derive information about 
the intensity and perceived color of light. The hue value can

plays a pivotal role in ensuring an accurate and real-time 
approach, particularly within dynamic outdoor and unstruc-

disadvantages of ripeness classification techniques. Spec-

for assessing ripeness. Nevertheless, it is imperative to rec-
ognize that when deployed in real-world outdoor environ-
ments, spectroscopy encounters challenges stemming from 
environmental interferences, which may introduce potential 
inaccuracies. The adaptability of spectroscopy and the task 
of calibrating spectroscopic instruments to suit the nuanced 
conditions of outdoor and unstructured environments repre-

this technique in real time. Although inductive sensing dem-
onstrates swiftness in internal analysis, it requires calibration 
tailored to each distinct fruit type. This calibration process 
introduces complexity and may necessitate time-consuming 
adjustments in practical, real-world applications. Notably, 
the technology’s depth of penetration might not entirely 
capture the breadth of internal variations, thereby restrict-
ing its efficacy in assessing FFB ripeness within dynamic 
outdoor environments. The setup of thermal imaging is 
comparatively straightforward and demands less equipment. 
Nevertheless, its application encounters challenges because 
of the capricious nature of outdoor, unstructured environ-
ments. Susceptibility to external factors such as fluctuating 
surface temperatures and the need for precise calibration 
emerge as practical hurdles, demanding consideration dur-
ing deployment. LiDAR emerges as a standout choice for 
FFB ripeness classification in dynamic outdoor settings. Its 
remarkable advantages, including the provision of precise 
3D structural data, independence from fruit size variations, 
and adaptability to changing lighting and foliage conditions, 
align seamlessly with the demands of the palm oil indus-
try’s environment. The ability to generate real-time insights, 
capture external structural attributes, and offer consistent 
measurements positions LiDAR as a valuable tool for on-
field deployment, especially when size and shape assessment 
is critical. The drawbacks include the requirement of com-
putational power for processing 3D data and rainy condi-
tions that might potentially affect the travel of emitted light. 
Computer vision, armed with its prowess in extracting rich 
visual features, emerges as a potent option for real-world 
deployment in FFB ripeness classification. Its adaptability 
to diverse fruit types, rapid processing capabilities, and non-
invasive nature hold considerable promise. In the context of 
the palm oil industry, where variations in FFB appearance 
and dynamic outdoor environments are the norm, computer 
vision is more suited for the role. However, the nuances of 
lighting variations, complex backgrounds, and the resource-
intensive nature of model training should be navigated.

Machine Learning Models and Algorithms Comparison
The landscape of FFB ripeness classification has undergone 
significant evolution, as reflected in the diverse array of 
techniques and models employed in recent years (Table 4). 
NIRS has seen a progression from GANN in 2016 to more 
advanced ANN in 2022 by Novianty et al., indicating a 
growing reliance on sophisticated machine learning mod-
els. Hyperspectral imaging, introduced by Bensaeed et al. in 
2014 by using ANN, has exhibited a trend towards simplic-
ity with the adoption of linear regression by Cherie et al. in 
2015 and, subsequently, LDA by Setiawan et al. in 2019. 
This shift suggests a strategic move towards more interpret-
able and less complex models. On the other hand, Raman 
spectroscopy has exhibited a strong preference for ANN, 
as demonstrated by Dan et al. in 2018, Raj et al. in 2021, 
Hong et al. in 2021, Tzuan et al. in 2022, and Azmi et al. 
in 2022. This preference likely stems from ANNs’ ability 
to effectively handle the complex, non-linear relationships 
present within Raman spectral data for FFB ripeness clas-
sification. Fluorescence spectroscopy, introduced in 2012 by 
Hazir et al., Shariff et al., and Amiruddin et al., has shown 
a reduced number of recent studies, possibly indicating a 
saturation point in its application or a shift towards newer 
and more advanced techniques in the domain. Microwave 
spectroscopy, when absent from Table 4 because of the lack 
of machine learning applications in prior research, presents 
an untapped avenue for potential exploration in FFB ripeness 
classification. Inductive sensing, a relatively recent entrant in 
2020, showcases the utilization of LDA by Sinambela et al. 
This technique is yet to witness extensive exploration, pre-
senting opportunities for further development and research. 
Thermal imaging, introduced by Fauziah et al. in 2021, has 
seen a blend of traditional linear regression and advanced 
ANN in subsequent studies by Makky et al. and Cherie et al. 
in 2021 and Zolfagharnassab et al. in 2022. This combina-
tion of approaches suggests a nuanced approach to leverage 
the strengths of various models for comprehensive analysis. 
LiDAR and LLBI, as explored by Mohd Ali et al. in 2020, 
and combined PLS and PCA showcase a trend towards inte-
grating multiple techniques for a more holistic understanding 
and accurate classification. Computer vision experienced a 
dynamic shift over the years since the early use of ANN in 
2012; the field has rapidly progressed through various mod-
els such as CNN-AlexNet, SVM, and, eventually, the YOLO 
series. The consistent adoption of advanced CNN models in 
recent years, such as YOLOv3, YOLOv4, and YOLOv5, is a 
testament to the increasing confidence in the efficacy of deep 
learning for image-based classification tasks. The consen-

FFB ripeness classification.
Previous researchers have believed that the shift towards 
deep learning and advanced models in the context of FFB 
ripeness classification can be attributed to several compel-
ling reasons. These trends are reflective of the growing 
recognition among researchers of the unique capabilities 
and advantages offered by deep learning methodologies. 
Agricultural datasets, including those for FFB ripeness, are 
inherently complex, featuring intricate patterns and varia-
tions. Deep learning models, particularly CNNs, excel at 
capturing and learning from these complex, non-linear 
relationships in data. Deep learning models automatically 
learn hierarchical representations of features from raw data, 
allowing them to extract meaningful patterns and features 
without explicit manual feature engineering. This ability is 
particularly beneficial in scenarios where the relevant fea-
tures for classification may not be immediately apparent. 
Deep learning models, especially CNNs, are highly scalable. 
As datasets grow in size and complexity, these models can 
adapt and continue to provide accurate and efficient results. 
This scalability is crucial in agricultural applications where 
datasets may vary significantly because of factors such as 
climate, soil conditions, and crop varieties. Unlike conven-
tional methods, deep learning models support end-to-end 
learning, indicating that the model learns directly from the 
raw input data to the output, thereby eliminating the need 
for manual preprocessing and feature engineering. This 
process streamlines the development process and often 
leads to more efficient and accurate models. In the case of 
computer vision and spectral analysis, such as with hyper-
spectral imaging, thermal imaging, and LiDAR, deep learn-
ing models, especially CNNs, are well suited for handling 
image and spatial data. Their ability to understand spatial 
hierarchies and patterns makes them ideal for tasks such as 
image-based ripeness classification. Deep learning models 
demonstrate a remarkable ability to generalize across diverse 
conditions. This ability is crucial in agriculture, where vari-
ations in environmental factors, crop types, and growing 
conditions can be substantial. Models trained using deep 
learning techniques can adapt to these variations and provide 
robust performance across various scenarios. The increasing 
availability of computational resources, including powerful 
GPUs and TPUs, has facilitated the training of deep learn-
ing models on large-scale datasets. This accessibility has 
empowered researchers to experiment with and deploy more 
complex models, leading to advancements in the field. Deep

Although the focus of ripeness classification techniques 
has been thoroughly discussed in previous sections, the 
integration of these methods with harvesting devices holds 
immense potential for the palm oil industry. Unlike tomato 
harvesting, FFB are heavy and require careful handling dur-
ing harvest. Current harvesting technology is not yet sophis-
ticated enough to handle these complexities. Extensive 
research and development efforts are necessary to bridge 
the gap and create truly automated harvesting systems for 
the palm oil industry. The most crucial part is being able to 
classify the ripe FFB and effectively locate it with specific 
coordinates. Combining LiDAR and camera technologies 
can offer a powerful approach to overcome the challenges 
because this combined approach can potentially improve the 
efficiency and precision of harvesting operations. Fu et al. 
(2023) demonstrated the potential of LiDAR and camera 
fusion for fruit detection tasks in agricultural settings. Their 
findings suggest that this integration approach can lead to 
robust and accurate fruit recognition, paving the way for its 
future application in automated harvesting systems.
Conventional methods for FFB ripeness assessment often 
rely on destructive techniques that involve physically dam-
aging the fruit. Despite providing some level of accuracy, 
these methods come with significant drawbacks. Moreover, 
non-destructive techniques offer a more efficient approach. 
When using computer vision or machine learning in con-
junction with sensors such as cameras or LiDAR, the FFB 
remains intact on the tree throughout the assessment pro-
cess. Furthermore, non-destructive methods are generally 
less labor-intensive and require simpler equipment compared 
to destructive techniques, reducing operational costs.
Challenges and Future Prospects
Navigating the intricate world of FFB ripeness classifi-
cation presents substantial challenges that require crea-
tive solutions. Integrating these techniques into automatic 
harvesting systems holds immense potential but demands 
strategies to match the speed of harvesting machinery. The 
palm oil industry often encounters obstacles in outdoor 
environments, dealing with varying lighting conditions, 
dense foliage, and uneven terrain. These challenges have 
been addressed by combining LiDAR and computer vision 
(Kang et al., 2022). The present review discusses previ-
ous research focusing on developing hybrid fusion models 
that integrate LiDAR and computer vision, extending the 
fusion system into 3D mapping and localization, which 
introduces complexities in capturing and interpreting 
spatial information accurately. The fusion of computer 
vision and LiDAR can lead to optimized algorithms for 
efficient 3D mapping, revolutionizing on-field navigation 
and resource utilization. This exploration opens up excit-
ing possibilities for overcoming obstacles in FFB ripeness 
classification and enhancing the efficiency of harvesting 
operations in dynamic outdoor environments. Acknowl-
edging the pivotal role of data quality and size in training 
deep learning models, researchers are increasingly turn-
ing to synthetic data to bolster their datasets. This trend 
holds great promise for improving the accuracy of FFB 
ripeness classification, especially in complex and dynamic 
outdoor environments. By seamlessly blending synthetic 
data with real-world datasets, researchers can enhance the 
training process, expose models to a wider array of sce-
narios, and address challenges related to dataset size and 
diversity (Goh et al., 2024). This trend not only offers a 
cost-effective alternative for model optimization but also 
lays the groundwork for a more resilient and adaptable 
FFB ripeness classification system in diverse agricultural 
settings. Future research must refine these methodolo-
gies, focusing on improving the realism of synthetic data 
and establishing standardized benchmarks for evaluating 
model efficacy. The integration of synthetic data emerges 
as a transformative strategy, ushering in a new era of pre-
cision in FFB ripeness classification. In the lush, natu-
ral settings where FFBs thrive, the abundance of foliage, 
branches, and neighboring fruits creates visually clut-
tered backgrounds. This environmental richness poses a 
challenge in distinguishing FFB from their surroundings, 
leading to substantial occlusion. The boundaries of indi-
vidual fruit bunches become blurred or obscured, mak-
ing accurate identification and classification a tricky task. 
Besides the complexity, the images are captured from a 
considerable distance during field surveys or from aerial 
platforms such as drones. This distance results in the fruit

through the challenges posed by visually cluttered back-

Conclusion
In conclusion, the realm of FFB ripeness classification 
within the palm oil industry is a multifaceted challenge, 
demanding a strategic selection of techniques to ensure 
accurate and real-time assessments. As described in this 
comprehensive comparative analysis across spectroscopy, 
inductive sensing, thermal imaging, LiDAR, LLBI, and 
computer vision have provided a nuanced understanding 
of their distinct merits and limitations within the dynamic 
and unstructured outdoor environments inherent to this 
ripeness classification. Computer vision emerges as the 
linchpin in FFB ripeness classification, outshining other 
techniques with its unparalleled ability to discern intri-
cate visual details and swift adaptability. This adaptability 
positions it as the preferred choice for real-world appli-
cations in the palm oil industry, promising efficient and 
accurate assessments. Additionally, LiDAR proves to be a 
valuable tool, offering crucial 3D structural data and adept 
handling of diverse lighting and environmental conditions. 
This finding positions LiDAR as a strong supporting tech-
nique for FFB ripeness classification, particularly when 
external structural attributes carry significant importance. 
The challenges identified, including the optimization of 
LiDAR and computer vision fusion, the need for high-
quality datasets in computer vision, and the exploration 
of a higher classification accuracy model, call for inno-
vative solutions. Advanced sensor integration technolo-
gies, synthetic data implementation, and the deployment 
of RT-DETR as a new object detection model represent 
promising prospects for overcoming these challenges. 
The present review provides a beacon light on the current 
state of FFB ripeness classification techniques and sets the 
stage for future advancements. By addressing challenges 
and embracing prospective solutions, researchers can pave 
the way for transformative innovations, ensuring that FFB 
ripeness classification seamlessly aligns with the evolving 
demands of agricultural practice and the broader ecosys-
tem. The trajectory of research in this direction not only 
fosters innovation within the palm oil industry but also the 
overall agricultural landscape for other plantations as well, 
which serves as a pivotal component in the sustainable and 
efficient evolution of agricultural methodologies.
