Abstract. Automated fruit counting, through the use of computer
vision methods, is a very important step towards the development of
precision agriculture, and, as a consequence, has been object of study by
multiple authors. In this work, we present a novel method to detect, track
and count fruits that is scalable and robust towards problems existing in
other fruit counting methods. Namely, our approach consists in a method
that uses (1) a stereo camera that can be used reliably under direct sun-
light, (2) a fast detection algorithm, and (3) an algorithmic approach to
track fruits which is robust to fruit occlusions. We applied our proposed
solution in an apple orchard and were able to provide apple counts with
an error ranging from 15% to 57%. The characteristics of this novel app-
roach and the preliminary results achieved seem promising in order to
tackle the problem of fruit counting with occlusions at the large scale of a
whole orchard, since it addresses the problem of intermittent occlusions
that is overlooked by other approaches. Even with errors, the overview
of the entire orchard, resulting from the scalability of the process, which
can be implemented with a camera in a tractor, makes real-time mapping
possible, supporting technical decisions with major economical impact,
such as supplementary fruit thinning, water or nutrient adjustments or
harvest management.

automated agriculture, from decision support systems for agronomic manage-

In particular, there is a strong interest in automatically counting fruits in an
orchard, a task that is currently being handled by manual counting estimations,
which is time consuming, impractical on a large scale, and highly error-prone.
To address this problem, it is important to study, not just the algorithmic
techniques to process data, but the sensors to collect it, as the feasibility of any
proposed solution depends on the possible drawbacks of the tools available. In
particular, we study the case of an apple orchard, although our system is general
enough to potentially handle other kinds of fruits. This task has speciﬁc hurdles
to be surpassed: the variability of fruit colour, size, orientation, visibility, and
lightning conditions make the problem of detecting fruits challenging. Moreover,
the intermittent occlusion of fruits by other fruits, branches and leaves is a major
challenge to the problem of tracking and counting fruits.
We approach the problem in this work using a very simple setup composed
by a single stereo camera, which is more aﬀordable than most Digital Single-
Lens Reﬂex (DSLR) cameras used in many similar works and performs better
under sunlight conditions than most depth cameras studied. On the algorithmic
part, we present a multi-step solution, beginning by detecting, then tracking, and
ﬁnally counting the total number of fruits. On the detection end, we use a Deep
Learning model for fast-detection. Regarding the tracking and counting part, we
present a novel approach to fruit tracking based on 3D centroids which is robust
to intermittent occlusions that happen when a fruit is being tracked, becomes
occluded during a few frames, and reappears again later, and is computationally
less demanding than a previously proposed approach to the same problem [8].
Our solution may be summarised as follows. First, an object detector, trained
on images of fruits that we collected and manually annotated, is used to detect
fruits in each video frame. Then, fruits are tracked in 3D across the video frames
by an algorithm that we developed, in order to associate detections of the same
fruit across the video frames and count each fruit only once. Finally, the total
number of fruits can be recovered by simply counting the number of objects that
the algorithm tracked in step 2, after applying some ﬁlters.
1.2
Related Work
Gongal et al. made a review [3] about the sensors, features and methods more
commonly used for fruit detection and location and summarised the main issues
that arise when tackling these problems, namely: variable lighting conditions,
complex canopy structures, varying colour, size and shape of fruits, occlusions
of fruits, clustering of fruits, and displacement of fruits by wind.
Several authors already tackled the problem of identifying and counting
fruit in orchards, with a variety of methods. Gen´e-Mola et al. [2] used RGB
images collected with a DSLR camera and processed through a Structure-from-
Motion (SfM) [15] pipeline in order to detect and locate fruits in 3D. They
applied the Mask R-CNN deep learning model to segment apples, while using

other faster detection methods such as the YOLO [11] would improve the perfor-
mance of the system. Moreover, the high computational time needed to process
the images with SfM is one limitation that has to be taken into account. Gongal
et al. [4] used a combination of RGB images and depth maps collected with a
time-of-ﬂight depth camera to locate and count apples on an orchard. Wang et
al. [16] used a stereo camera rig composed of two DSLR cameras to detect and
count apples, by triangulating their 3D positions in the image sequence, and
then projecting them onto a global coordinate system. The biggest limitation of
both works is their fruit detection being heavily tied to colour features, which are
very sensitive to variations in light and colour. Despite the good results in the
identiﬁcation of red apples, the methods struggle to identify apples in early phe-
nological stages, when they are green and smaller in size. The solutions proposed
by Nguyen et al. [9] and Tao et al. [14] used a low-cost RGB-D camera to photo-
graph orchards and generate a 3D point cloud, and for fruit detection, they ﬁlter
the point cloud by colour and apply clustering techniques. These solutions using
depth cameras rely on infrared light and thus have limitations in outdoor con-
ditions under direct sunlight, depending on a light shield, tunnel or some other
device to block it and on the usage of LEDs to create the ideal lighting conditions.
This adds complexity to the data collection process, and makes the solution less
scalable if one intends to apply it in large scale to a whole orchard, for example,
and to various conditions such as terrain slope. Stein et al. [13] and also Liu
et al. [7] tackled the problem of counting and locating fruits with a monocular
RGB camera by training a Faster R-CNN model. The biggest limitation of both
approaches is that the tracking is done sequentially, with each detection being
matched against the ones in the next image only. This means that a single false
negative, i.e. a fruit that is not detected in a given image, inevitably breaks the
tracking of that fruit, and a new track is introduced later on when the fruit is
detected again in a subsequent image, leading the system to count two fruits
instead of one.
To summarise, most of the works relying on depth cameras based on infrared
light have limited applicability under direct sunlight, resorting to solutions such
as light tunnels to block it. On the other hand, solutions relying on monocular
RGB cameras tend to use SfM to produce 3D data and are thus computation-
ally more demanding than stereo approaches. On the detection side, approaches
relying on colour and geometric features to detect fruits through more traditional
computer vision and/or clustering techniques are strongly aﬀected by variations
in fruit colour and light conditions, whereas approaches based on Deep Learning
models are usually more robust. Therefore, we propose a solution to the fruit
counting problem that is scalable – i.e. suitable to be adopted at a whole orchard
level by a farmer –, robust to light and colour changes in outdoor conditions. We
ruled out solutions relying on infrared light and light shields, and fruit detectors
based solely on colour, and decided to go for a stereo solution in order to recover
depth maps in real time.

Materials and Methods
2.1
Description of the Data
The data for the following tasks was collected in an orchard owned by INIAV [5]
in Alcoba¸ca, Portugal (Lat. 39,549 N; Long. −8,957 W), with a stereo camera—
the ZED 2i—being held by hand, attached to a computer, by shooting video in
roughly straight lines parallel to the line of trees. No stabiliser or any other type
of helping device was used. Several lines of trees were captured, corresponding
to diﬀerent apple tree clones from the group Gala, when the fruits were close to
harvest, corresponding to the phenological stages from BBCH 81 (beginning of
ripening) to BBCH 87 (near fruit ripe for picking), according to BBCH-scale for
pome fruits.
2.2
Detecting Fruits in 2D Images
Data Annotation. Our image annotations consist of bounding boxes around
all the apples visible in each image. Video frames corresponding to the left view
of the stereoscopic camera were extracted from the ZED proprietary video ﬁles
and stored as image ﬁles. They were then manually annotated using the open-
source tool Makesense [12]. Only one in every 30 frames was annotated due to
the similarity between consecutive frames. We annotated a total of 183 images,
each one containing approximately 100 fruits, on average, convering the afore-
mentioned phenological states.
Model Training. For this work, a Darknet implementation of the YOLOv4
[1] was used. Darknet is an open source neural network framework written in
C and CUDA [10]. The YOLOv4 is an object detection Deep Learning model
belonging to the YOLO family, which was speciﬁcally designed to handle real-
time object detection [11]. Unlike many of the deep learning models used in some
of the previous works, this type of model is more suitable for in-ﬁeld operations
due to its speed. Although more recent YOLO models are available, we stick
to v4 due to its license allowing commercial usage. Our YOLO model was ﬁrst
pre-trained on the MS COCO [6] dataset—a large scale image dataset containing
330K images over 80 diﬀerent object categories. Then, we ﬁne-tuned it to our
own tasks, using our annotated dataset.
2.3
Tracking and Counting Fruits in 3D
Overview. Having a YOLO detector capable of detecting the fruits of interest
in each video frame is not suﬃcient to count them. In fact, the same fruit will
most certainly appear in several video frames, thus simply summing the num-
ber of detected boxes ends up with duplicate counts. We must track each fruit
across frames in order to understand which detection boxes (in diﬀerent frames)
correspond to the same fruit, and then count only one of them. This is where 3D
data becomes useful.

By using a Structure-from-Motion (SfM) algorithm, it is possible to assemble,
from a set of 2D images, a 3D representation of the photographed scene in
the form of a point cloud and/or a depth map. Another way of obtaining a
point cloud is by using a depth camera or a stereo camera, such as the ZED
2i. These devices are designed to output a depth map – from which a point
cloud can be directly generated –, thus avoiding the need to run computationally
expensive SfM algorithms.
Having a dense 3D representation of the scene in the form of a point cloud,
there is a matching between each pixel in a 2D image and a 3D point in space.
This allows us to project each fruit detected in a 2D frame onto its 3D repre-
sentation in a global coordinate system that is common to all frames. Therefore,
whenever the same fruit is detected in diﬀerent frames, the projection of the cor-
responding detection boxes will lead to the same region in 3D space, and so we
can conclude that it is in fact the same object and thus count it only once.
Algorithm Description. In order to track and count fruits along a video, we
implemented the algorithm proposed in [8] which works by reconstructing a 3D
scene from the video and localising the fruits in 3D space. While the authors in
[8] used a SfM pipeline to obtain the camera extrinsics and dense depth maps for
the scene, which is a computationally demanding task, we used the ZED SDK
instead to retrieve this data in real time from the ZED 2i stereo camera. As a
result, we avoid several hours of computation and obtain a system that is less
demanding in terms of computer resources and suitable to be setup and used in
ﬁeld, either manually operated or mounted on an agricultural vehicle. Moreover,
we also implemented an algorithm similar to [8] but, instead of re-projecting the
whole set of 3D points, it re-projects only the 3D centroid of each fruit. This
algorithm is summarised in the block diagram shown in Fig. 1, and detailed in
the pseudo-code of Algorithm 1.
For each video frame, we ﬁrst run YOLO on that frame to detect fruits. Then,
for each detected fruit, the pixels contained in its bounding box are projected
onto the 3D global coordinate system using the frame’s depth map. Since some
of these points belong to the fruit, but others do not – usually some background
pixels are contained in the bounding box –, we compute the median of the coor-
dinates of these points to obtain a centroid representing the fruit. The centroid
is used to describe each encountered fruit throughout the video.

into the 2D image plane of the current video frame by using Eq. 1:

of that centroid, which means we are tracking a fruit that has been seen before,
and so we assign it the same ID. If, however, there is no 2D projection falling
inside a given bounding box, then we assume it corresponds to a new fruit and
a new ID is assigned to it. We follow this procedure until the last video frame.
In the end, the number of unique IDs is, in theory, the total number of fruits,
without repetition.
Due to the error associated with the computation of the camera poses and
the fruit centroids, the centroids sometimes lie closer to the surface of the fruit
than to its center of mass. Therefore, when the camera sees the same fruit in
a diﬀerent angle, in a subsequent frame, the centroid projection may end up
outside of the bounding box of that fruit by a small margin, originating a new
detection with a brand new ID, making the algorithm wrongly count this fruit
twice. To minimise this problem and improve the tracking stability, we update
the coordinates of each centroid with each new frame where that same fruit is
detected, by averaging over the centroids previously calculated for that fruit.
Another obstacle to this method is created by YOLO, which sometimes pro-
duces false positive detections. In order to mitigate these, in the end, when we
are counting the unique IDs of the detected fruits, we ignore fruits that were
detected in less than 5 frames in the whole video, as proposed in [8].
The algorithm that we propose here has the potential to be particularly
suitable for real-time detection since, in each step, it only needs the current
video frame, its depth map and the previously calculated centroid coordinates,
instead of having to handle all the 3D points, like in [8]. The depth camera
used is capable of calculating the depth map in real-time, and the rest of the
computational resources are mostly spent on the 2D detection. Thus, given our
practical choice of the YOLO model, the combination of our setup, algorithm,
and model is ideal for in-ﬁeld fruit detection.

Experimental Results
3.1
Test Procedure
We prepared three diﬀerent scenarios to match as closely as possible the one
in which our system is intended to be used, each corresponding to a diﬀerent
clone of the most important apple variety planted all over the world (Schninga
Schnico, Schnico Red, Galafab). In the same orchard used for training the models
(Sect. 2.1), we chose 3 portions of tree lines that were not used during training.
For each portion, we chose 3 to 6 representative trees to evaluate our algorithm
on, which were ﬁlmed on one side of the line only, and whose fruits were manually
counted in the ﬁeld and on the video. The selected trees are not necessarily
consecutive, so the video frames were manually edited to mask all other trees
before we passed them to the algorithm.
Fruit counts manually obtained in ﬁeld are not a suitable ground truth to
directly evaluate our system’s performance, because the number of apples visible
on the video captured from one side of the trees is less than the whole number
of fruits present on the trees, and is higher than the number of fruits counted

Overall, the results suggest that the system that we propose, either with
algorithm proposed in this paper or the one proposed by [8], is able to successfully
track fruits with high precision and provide acceptable errors for the total fruit
count. Comparing the algorithm that we propose in this paper with that of

[8], we realise that re-projecting all the points, rather than only the centroids,
makes the system more robust to errors generated by the object detector and
the depth estimated by stereo. This conclusion stems from the higher MOTA
values reported in Table 3, which indicate more stable tracks.
Our algorithm (Table 2) achieves slightly better results in terms of total fruit
count on the ﬁrst two datasets, but produces a much worse count in the third
dataset due to over-counting, compared to the algorithm proposed by [8]. We
believe this is due to noise in the camera extrinsics estimated in real-time, prob-
ably because the images in this dataset are more diﬃcult to track from a visual
odometry perspective. The increased robustness shown by the algorithm pro-
posed in [8] comes from re-projecting all the points from each fruit and com-
bining that with a Hungarian assignment. That approach reinforces the correct
matches frame after frame, such that even if errors or noise occur later, the mass
of points previously matched avoids a wrong match of new detections to fruits.
On the other hand, when we project only the fruit centroids, since the tracking is
less robust, the algorithm tends to overestimate the number of fruits, because it
tends to lose track of a fruit more easily and thus a new tracking “object” is
created for the same fruit, which ends up being counted several times.
The advantage of working with centroids is a less computationally demanding
algorithm (less points to process), which makes it more suitable for real time
usage on less powerful IoT devices, at the cost of a decreased accuracy. Moreover,
the usage of a stereo camera instead of COLMAP (the SfM component used in
[8]) allowed us to reduce the total computational time drastically. Overall, with
the solution here proposed, we could reduce the total processing time for each
dataset from 2–3 h to 1.5–2.5 min.
4
Conclusions
This work’s strong suit is its robustness when tracking fruits with intermittent
occlusions (as in [8]) while keeping its applicability to devices with lower com-
putational resources. Compared to the algorithm proposed in [8], the approach
that we propose, based on 3D centroids, is computationally less demanding and
thus more suitable to online settings, e.g. where a system with a stereo camera
is used to obtain fruit counts in real time. For use cases where accuracy is more
important than speed, our system can still be paired with the more robust algo-
rithm proposed in [8] either with a stereo camera, such as the ZED 2i used in
this work, or even with a SfM pipeline as described in [8].
The results shown in this paper were obtained with a ZED camera operated
by hand, and the video processed oﬄine. In the near future, we want to test our
algorithm in an online setting, where the camera is attached to an agricultural
vehicle and the depth maps for each frame are fetched in real-time from the ZED
camera, as this would be the most realistic and scalable setup, capable of easily
covering large ﬁeld portions.
Besides the promising results shown here, we must look for the opportuni-
ties that a system like this can add to the precision fruit farming, compared

to the information obtained by the manual counting of a few trees, currently
applied in modern fruit farms. In fact, even with errors, the overview of the
entire orchard, resulting from the installation of the camera in a tractor makes
real-time mapping possible, supporting technical decisions with major economi-
cal impact, such as supplementary fruit thinning, water or nutrient adjustments
or harvest management. Historical data inside each orchard also allows diﬀer-
ent approaches in pruning strategies, adjusting the number of ﬂower buds, as
well as in the deﬁnition of an optimised fruit setting strategy. Combined with
other solutions provided by remote sensing, it can be also a powerful tool for the
application of variable rate technologies, such as precision fertilisation.
The algorithmic approach presented in this work, along with its future
improvements, was done during the development of a software solution which is
currently being built by SISCOG1 . This solution seeks to integrate and process
data from diﬀerent sensors (remote or not), and display the results in a user-
friendly manner in order to support farmer’s decision making. We believe that,
by presenting the results of this research to farmers in this way, we are able to
obtain valuable feedback that will allow us to deﬁne future directions for this
work.
