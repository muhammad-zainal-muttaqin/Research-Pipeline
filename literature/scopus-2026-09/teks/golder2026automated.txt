Automated detection, shape classification, and counting of 
leaves and fruits from bunch leaf and fruit image using deep 
learning models for smart agriculture and forestry

Abstract
Fruit detection, shape measurement, and counting are integral to yield estimation. Detec­
tion and classification of individual partial and full leaves from the bunch of leaves are 
fundamental parameters for various measurements and research in agriculture, forestry, and 
environmental science. In this paper, we propose deep learning models to detect and clas­
sify the shape (partial, full) of individual leaves from bunch leaf images and to count indi­
vidual fruits from bunch fruit images as they are used in various measurements in agricul­
ture and forestry. Previous studies have yet to experiment simultaneously on highly dense 
leaf and fruit datasets with different lighting settings for smart agricultural applications. 
We deploy two light-weighted object detection CNN models (YOlOv8, EfficientDet-D0) 
and a popular object detection CNN model (Faster R-CNN) for leaf detection and yield 
estimation. Moreover, no real-time suitable dataset containing highly dense leaves or fruits 
is available. Therefore, we developed two real-time datasets: one consisting of bunches 
leaves of nine species (612 images) and the other of papaya fruits (1840 images), collected 
from Bangladeshi agricultural fields. The datasets contain exceedingly overlapped objects 
with diverse backgrounds (ordinary and complex) and lighting conditions (sunny, cloudy, 
and gloomy). From the experimental results, we see that YOLOv8 outperforms other mod­
els, achieving 93.88%, 91.59%, and 96.78% precision, recall, and mAP, respectively on the 
leaf dataset for detecting and classifying individual partial and full leaves from the bunch 
of leaves, and 99.19%, 98.92%, and 99.43% precision, recall and mAP, respectively on the 
papaya dataset for detecting individual papaya fruit from the bunch.

1  Introduction
We are steadily progressing towards the era of automated harvesting in agriculture, driven 
by the evolving collaboration and advancement of Image Processing, Machine Learning 
(ML), Internet of Things (IoT), and Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies. The necessity

Multimedia Tools and Applications           (2026) 85:71 
for efficient use of limited natural resources, the growing desire for increased crop yields, 
and the expansion of the global population all contribute to the evolving significance of 
agriculture automation or smart agriculture. In-field agricultural automation relies heavily 
on vision-based fruit and leaf identification and classification [1, 2]. Detection is the primary 
task for any classification in image processing and computer vision. But precise detection of 
objects that are densely grouped and highly overlapped, edges of the objects are unclear, and 
there are unnecessary extra edges that share the same colors as their background are more 
challenging than any other type of detection. We see these problems in many leaves or fruits 
where a single leaf or fruit detection is complex [3, 4].
Accurate and successful detection, shape classification and measurement, and counting 
of leaves and fruits from overlapped contexts are necessary for many downstream tasks of 
agriculture and forestry fields. Detecting partial and full leaves is pre-essential for the classi­
fication or identification of leaves from bunch leaves as partial leaves do not contain a com­
plete set of features. Detection of partial and full leaves, measuring their areas, and counting 
them from the bunch are fundamental factors for measuring the Leaf Area Index (LAI). The 
LAI is a dimensionless measure that quantifies the amount of leaf area in a plant canopy 
relative to the ground area it covers [5]. The LAI is a crucial parameter for numerous down­
stream tasks across various fields, including agriculture (such as crop growth monitoring, 
yield prediction, irrigation management, fertilization planning, tree or crop identification, 
and pest and disease detection), forestry (like forest health assessment, carbon sequestration 
estimation, and biodiversity and habitat monitoring), and environmental science [6]. As LAI 
serves as an elementary measurement for various estimations, it is essential to ensure its pre­
cise calculation. LAI measurement is time-consuming without automation. If we accurately 
detect both partial and full leaves and measure their areas, the LAI can be automatically 
and precisely calculated from the detected leaves. Therefore, the detection of both full and 
partial leaves allows for the automation of all LAI-based applications, contributing to the 
advancement of smart agriculture. Additionally, even without using LAI, partial and full 
leaf detection and counting enable us to accurately assess leaf overlap and determine leaf 
density, which contributes to yield estimation. Evaluating the ratio of full and half leaves 
within overlapped images helps identify the growth stage of plants. Various growth stages 
are associated with distinct leaf shapes and dimensions, providing valuable insights into 
plant progress and enabling informed decisions for cultivation strategies. Besides leaves, 
accurate fruit detection is important for many downstream tasks in smart agriculture. Har­
vesters can use fruit counting to fruit yield estimation to optimize management and choices 
such as labor allocation, storage, packing, and harvest timing. Leaf and fruit datasets col­
lected from specific regional agriculture can be used to make that region’s agriculture smart 
and advanced by providing different insights through our research. While shape classifica­
tion and counting are typically performed on leaf and fruit datasets respectively, reversing 
these tasks could offer more detailed information. This approach could provide enhanced 
information for estimating fertilizer and pesticide needs, gauging fruit maturity, and analyz­
ing fruit shape characteristics. All these factors motivate us to contribute to smart agricul­
ture. In this research, we have classified leaf shapes into two distinct types (full and partial) 
on nine kinds of leaves from the field. For fruit, we have detected and counted the papaya 
from the bunch papaya image. Our objective is to select a fine-tuned lightweight deep learn­
ing (DL) model runnable at low-configured smartphone devices that gives better results for 
detection, shape classification, and counting of leaves and fruits.

There exist several papers on leaves and fruit detection, classification, and counting from 
overlapped images. Most researchers utilize deep learning (DL) based models [7–12]; some 
combine traditional machine learning and non-machine learning techniques with color, tex­
ture, and shape features [13–16] to detect, classify and count leaves and fruits. Most of 
them use non-highly overlapped, non-dense, slightly dense, or non-complicated background 
datasets. The background in the photographs was generally basic, such as soil, sky, dif­
ferent shades, and so on. A few recent researchers have experimented on overlapped and 
complicated background datasets of leaves or fruits [3, 17]. No research has been done on 
both leaf and fruit datasets which are highly dense and overlapped with different lighting 
settings and complex background settings. Consequently, the approaches could be better for 
recognizing densely dispersed items with occlusion. Fluctuating lighting, a complex back­
ground, overlapping objects, the viewing angle of the camera, distance, and other variables 
can all affect object recognition. Evaluating the findings from past studies reveals many 
scopes to enhance the effectiveness of their systems. The speed and accuracy still need to be 
improved. The investigation to find the most suitable research model for mobile devices in 
real-time smart agriculture in this context is still pending. However, no complete dataset of 
fruit and leaf with complicated backgrounds, dense objects, and varying lighting conditions 
is available for Bangladeshi agriculture, and no comprehensive experimental evaluation of 
multiple deep learning models on such kind of dataset has been done before.
In this paper, we have first made two datasets regarding Bangladeshi geography, as there 
are no benchmark datasets for our problems. One contains 612 bunch leaf images from 
nine types of regional trees, bushes, and vines. The other includes 1,840 bunch papaya fruit 
images. Secondly, we apply three state-of-the-art deep learning object detection models 
(Faster R-CNN, YOLOv8, EfficientDet-D0) for retrieving leaf and fruit information (shape, 
count) from bunch leaf and fruit images. We prefer deep learning-based computer vision 
methods over traditional machine learning-based computer vision methods because deep 
learning has the capability of extracting high-level complex features over categorical hand­
crafted feature extraction [18]. The high-level features can easily distinguish overlapped 
complex objects [19]. Nevertheless, when the Faster R-CNN detects multiple or duplicate 
instances of the same object, we utilize the non-maximum suppression (NMS) technique to 
rectify this problem. The NMS method helps in accurately determining the precise shape 
of the object by eliminating redundant detections for the Faster R-CNN model. The NMS 
method is not required for YOLOv8 and EfficientDet-D0. Thirdly, we study the effects of 
some direct mapping and image enhancement approaches on both datasets while detecting, 
shape classifying and counting using deep learning models. We apply Gaussian Blur, RGB 
to LAB, Harris Corner, Adapting Thresholding, Gabor filter, and Wavelet Transformations 
for enlarging datasets and High Boost Filter for preprocessing the data. The YOLOv8 model 
outperforms the other two models in terms of performance metrics (precision, recall, mAP) 
on both leaf and papaya datasets. The experimental results also show that the proposed 
models do not perform better results on enlarged datasets, generated by direct mapping and 
image enhancement approaches, than that of the original dataset. The precision, recall, and 
mean average precision (mAP) are 93.88%, 91.59%, and 96.78%, respectively for detecting 
and classifying individual partial and full leaves from bunch leaves obtained by YOLOv8 
model on the original leaf dataset which are the highest performance scores. The YOLOv8 
model exhibits the highest performance with precision, recall, and mAP scores of 99.19%, 
98.92%, and 99.43% respectively for detecting individual papaya fruits from the bunch

I.	
We have developed two real-time datasets focused on Bangladeshi geography. One 
dataset contains 612 images of bunch leaves from nine different regional trees, bushes, 
and vines. The other dataset comprises 1,840 images of bunch papaya fruits.

2  Related works
Recently, many researchers have been working on leaf and fruit detection, classification, and 
counting from overlapped images using different methods. However, they cannot overcome 
all existing flaws and use all settings for the dataset. This section reviews some methods that 
many researchers experimented with on leaf and fruit information (detection, classification, 
count) retrieval.
Xu et al. [20] proposed a semi-supervised learning approach for counting maize seedling 
leaves using UAV RGB images. They employed the SOLOv2 model for segmentation and 
YOLOv5x for leaf counting, achieving an average precision of 89.6% for fully unfolded 
leaves and 57.4% for newly appearing leaves, with counting accuracy rates of 69.4% and 
72.9%, respectively. Challenges included varying color contrast between maize leaves and 
the background, and they did not address complex, highly overlapped images. Buzzy et al. 
[21] focused on real-time leaf counting with Tiny-YOLOv3 on an annotated Arabidopsis 
1 3
   71

plant dataset. The model showed improved inference time (under 0.01s), a high F1 score 
(over 0.94), and a low false-positive rate (around 24%) compared to Faster R-CNN. How­
ever, the simple dataset background made detection easier and did not represent more com­
plex natural scenarios. Abeyrathna et al. [22] investigated apple recognition and counting 
for robotic harvesting using a 3D camera and several YOLO models (YOLOv4, YOLOv5, 
YOLOv7) and EfficientDet. With data augmentation, they expanded their dataset to 6511 
images. YOLOv7 achieved a stable mAP_0.5 of 90.5% after 20 epochs, while EfficientDet 
achieved 77.5% after 60,000 steps. YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 showed better counting accuracy 
(86.6%) outdoors, with limitations in recognizing ripe apples against a green background. 
Li et al. [23] developed a lightweight Bayberry detection system using YOLOv7. Using 
8990 images, they improved the detection of overlapping and occluded objects with the 
Wise-IoU function. YOLOv7-CS achieved significant mAP 0.5 improvements over other 
models. However, the dataset featured clear-edged Bayberries with distinct backgrounds, 
limiting the approach. Padiyar et al. [24] presented a methodology for fruit classification 
and counting using the YOLO architecture, achieving 85% accuracy on clear background 
images.
Ubbens & Stavness [25] introduced Deep Plant Phenomics, a tool for mutant classifica­
tion, age regression, and leaf counting using deep CNNs on plant rosette images. Giuffrida 
et al. [26] deployed a Pheno-Deep Counter, a multi-input deep network, to calculate the 
number of leaves in rosette-shaped plants from various image sources. Tao & Zhou [27] 
proposed an automated apple detection method integrating color and 3D characteristics for 
robotic fruit grabbing, achieving 80.84% accuracy in leaf counting. Most of these methods 
used small datasets and did not achieve state-of-the-art performance. The literature is sum­
marized in the following Table 1.
3  Proposed methodology
The proposed research focuses on collecting and building overlapped leaves and fruit datas­
ets and developing efficient automated models for detection, shape classification, and count­
ing individuals from the bunch. This section will go through several of the methodological 
techniques used in this study. We can divide our research methodology into seven system­
atic steps or work procedures. The first step is the collection processes of two types of data. 
Preprocessing processes are applied to the dataset in the following step, where we scale the 
photographs and then apply an image enhancement technique to improve the image quality. 
The third step addresses the topic of dataset splitting. Next, the data annotation process is 
performed. The fifth step is data augmentation for enlarging the dataset. The remaining is- 
Model Training and Result Analysis. Figure 1 depicts our entire process and it is discussed 
as follows.

3.1.2  BDPapaya dataset
We captured 1,840 papaya fruit bunch images for this dataset. Features of the dataset are 
shown in Table 2. These images were also captured in the Khulna region of Bangladesh. 
This image data collection aims to explore the effects of augmentation on data that lacks the 
vein pattern found in leaves and the effectiveness of our system for fruit counting. We also 
took the previously mentioned constraints: complex backgrounds, overlapping, and differ­
ent lighting settings during data collection.
3.2  Preprocessing
All the images have been harmonized to a consistent size of 800 × 600. The aspect ratio 
is adjusted by cropping one direction of the mapped image. We need data annotation for 
model development. Thus constant size of each image is necessary. Otherwise, bounding 
boxes of annotated objects vary according to image sizes, and unrealistic bounding boxes 
are obtained.
We have applied High Boost Filter to enhance the image quality. However, we have 
trained the models with or without applying the High Boost Filter. The method is briefly 
described below.
3.2.1  High boost filter
High Boost filtering is an image enhancement method that improves the image quality over 
the original image, especially by sharpening the image. The method sharpens objects’ edges 
on images by emphasizing the high-frequency components in the image without eliminating 
the low-frequency components. The theory behind it is straightforward. At first, we blur the 
input image using a filter. Then we subtract the blurred image from the input image with 
the added significance of A = 1. The following equation defines a High Boost filtered image.
	
fhb (x, y) = Af (x, y) −f ′ (x, y)

f′ (x, y) is the blurred version of f(x, y). The boosting kernel has 9 elements with the 
central value as A + 8 and all other neighbour values as −1. The High Boosted output image

3.3  Data splitting
Data splitting is a technique that involves dividing a data set into two or more subsets for 
different purposes. Data splitting is essential for evaluating a model’s performance and gen­
eralization ability and avoiding overfitting or underfitting. In this study, the leaf dataset is 
divided into approximately 66.66:16.67:16.67 ratio, with 66.66% allocated for model train­
ing, 16.67% for validation, and 16.67% for testing. So, for the harmonized BDLeaf dataset, 
408 images are for model training, 102 for model validation, and 102 for model testing. 
The ratio is approximately 78.26:10.87:10.87 for the BDPapaya dataset and there are 1440 
images for training, 200 for validation, and 200 for testing the model. Due to the imbalanced 
nature of the labeled data, we carefully split the training-validation-test set to ensure that 
each set contained a proportional representation of each class.
3.4  Data annotation
Data annotation is required in supervised object segmentation and detection using deep 
learning techniques. Image annotation aims to identify objects in an image and label their 
positions and classes. We annotate both datasets in PASCAL VOC format using Labeling 
software, which creates an XML file for each annotated image providing information on 
the coordinate values of each bounding box and its ground truth class. We annotate the 
train images on both datasets and do not annotate the test images because we will manually 
evaluate the test results. The labeling procedure involves only two annotators to reduce 
observer inconsistency, and each annotated image is subjected to an inspection procedure. 
Figure 3 depicts pictures of our annotated images.
3.4.1  Annotation of BDLeaf dataset
In the annotation process, we consider three categories of objects in the leaf images: fully 
visible, partially visible, and non-leaf (e.g., background). We have annotated 612 leaf 
images for fully and partially visible leaves; the background is not included in the annota­
tion process. An annotated leaf image is shown in Fig. 3(a).
3.4.2  Annotation of BDPapaya dataset
We have annotated the papaya fruits into three groups: fully visible, partially visible, and 
non-fruit (e.g., background). During this phase, all 1,840 papaya images are annotated. Here 
also, the annotating procedure does not incorporate the background. Figure 3(b) depicts an 
annotated papaya image.
3.5  Data augmentation
In general, CNN needs massive labeled data for every class to perform well and support 
the learning process because it expands to a network with millions of parameters [28]. So, 
for better results from CNN models, the more data we integrate into it, the better accuracy 
we will get from it, and the overfitting problem can be overcome. To maximize the training 
data, we apply various direct mapping and image enhancement approaches.

Most existing researchers enlarge the dataset by rotating every image at different 
angles, horizontal mirroring, color balance processing, brightness transformation, 
blur processing, etc [29–31]. Rotation, mirroring, and brightness-based augmentation 
techniques are not applicable to our problem as leaves and fruits are already rotated 
or flipped in the dense dataset, and various lighting conditions are maintained during 
data collection. We have applied six effective direct mapping techniques to enhance 
the information of our collected data. As general leaf images contain noise and do 
not provide enough morphological details to the CNN classifier, these direct mapping 
approaches are chosen to remove noise from the dataset and to enrich it dimensionally 
and morphologically. Our applied direct mapping and image enhancement techniques 
are: (1) Gaussian Blur Filter, (2) RGB to LAB color Transformation, (3) Harris Corner 
Detector, (4) Adaptive Thresholding, 4) Gabor Filter, and 6) Wavelet Transformation. 
The transformed images are used as training data and test data. For the augmented 
dataset we apply the same splitting ratio that it is used in its original dataset. Figure 4 
shows the resulting revamped images of a sample input image. Each approach with its 
purpose is detailed below.

We applied the Gaussian Blur Filter as a data augmentation technique because, in scenarios

moving while the objects remain stationary, the captured images may appear slightly blurred. 
The filter reduces noise in images captured in low light by filtering out higher frequency or 
high-contrast pixels. Figure 4(b) illustrates the Gaussian Blur Filter applied output image.

3.5.2  RGB to LAB color transformation
In LAB color space, colors are defined uniformly, irrespective of how they are produced 
or the device used to display them. Unlike RGB, which can vary based on the device, 
LAB color space is device-independent. This characteristic enables our recognition model 
to learn deep features that remain consistent across different platforms or devices, as data 
is collected from multiple sources [32]. Figure 4(c) shows the visual representation of the 
original image from RGB to LAB color space.

3.5.4  Adaptive thresholding
Adaptive Thresholding [34] employs a method that computes the threshold for a small por­
tion of an image, allowing us to obtain various thresholds for different sections of the same 
picture, as seen in Fig. 4(e). It produces superior outcomes for photographs taken under 
different lighting situations [35]. The lighting on an image always confuses object classifi­
cation models, causing them to misunderstand a brighter region of an image area. Hence, 
Adaptive Thresholding aids in making each pixel carry consistent information. Using this 
strategy, we obtain a binary image with many efficient and equally distributed characteris­
tics in each pixel. We transform the thresholded one-channel picture into a three-channel 
image for further processing.

3.5.6  Wavelet transform
The Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) is used to examine or analyze digital pictures at 
different resolutions. It gives tremendous insight into the spatial and frequency character­
istics of an image [37]. When we apply DWT to an image, we receive an estimate of the 
picture’s horizontal, vertical, and diagonal detail coefficients, which we then combine. As 
Fig. 4(g) demonstrates, this forward and inverse DWT in a 2D picture produces an image 
with additional information about its horizontal, vertical, and diagonal detail coefficients. 
We have employed Haar wavelets with a threshold value of level 1 for our model, which 
helps to decrease picture noise and redundant information.

3.6  Object detection and classification by CNN models
Three state-of-the-art object detection models: Faster R-CNN, YOLOv8, and EfficientDet-
D0 are employed in our research. A short description of these models is illustrated below.
3.6.1  Faster R-CNN: an overview
The Faster R-CNN [40] model is the most extensively utilized state-of-the-art variant of the 
R-CNN family. It outperforms other network models in terms of accuracy in various object 
recognition tasks based on candidate areas [41, 42]. So, to achieve excellent accuracy, the 
Faster-RCNN model is selected in this work. The network usually consists of four main 
building boxes: CNN Backbone, Region Proposal Network (RPN), Region of Interest (RoI) 
Pooling, and Region Classification & Bounding Box Regression. The first component of 
Faster R-CNN is the CNN backbone, typically a pre-trained deep convolutional neural net­
work such as VGG, ResNet, or MobileNet. We take ResNet50V2 as the backbone. The RPN 
produces “bounding boxes,” or locations of potential objects in an image as shown in Fig. 
5. The ROI pooling layer takes the proposed regions (bounding boxes) from the RPN and 
performs spatial pooling or aligning to obtain fixed-sized feature maps for each region. The 
final part of Faster R-CNN involves feeding the RoI-pooled features into two sibling fully 
connected layers. These layers are responsible for region classification and bounding box 
regression [43]. The region classification layer predicts the probability distribution over dif­
ferent object classes for each RoI. The bounding box regression layer refines the coordinates 
of the proposed bounding boxes to localize the objects more accurately.
Faster R-CNN sometimes generates multiple or duplicate detections for a single object 
in our experiment. To address this issue, we utilize Non-maximum Suppression (NMS) on 
the Faster R-CNN detections. Figure 6 illustrates the results of applying NMS to our datas­

3.6.2  YOLOv8: an overview
Object detection, a pivotal task in computer vision, involves the precise identification and 
localization of objects within images or videos. YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once), an evo­
lutionary extension of YOLO [44] introduced in May 2023, stands out for its exceptional 
speed and accuracy, making it a preferred choice for real-time applications. The basic four 
building blocks of YOLO are residual blocks, bounding box regression, IOU evaluation, 
and NMS. Instead of the traditional two-step process of first identifying regions of interest 
and then classifying them, YOLO performs both tasks in a single pass. YOLO is widely 
used in various applications, including autonomous vehicles, surveillance, and general 
object recognition tasks.
3.6.3  EfficientDet-D0: an overview
EfficientDet, an advanced object detection approach, prioritizes both accuracy and effi­
ciency, mitigating the resource and time costs associated with existing networks [45]. 
The model is rooted in Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN), specifically designed for 
building feature pyramids within convolutional networks. FPN involves a dual process- 
bottom-up and top-down. The bottom-up path generates a hierarchy of features through 
1 × 1 convolutions, while the top-down path combines spatially distant yet semantically 
robust features. The EfficientDet model inherits its architecture from EfficientNet, a 
convolutional neural network employing a compound coefficient to uniformly scale 
dimensions- width, depth, and resolution. The EfficientDet category offers various net­
work options, and in this case, the D0 512 × 512 variant is selected. EfficientDet-D0 is 
chosen for its ability to balance accuracy and computational efficiency, making it suit­
able for real-time applications.
In our previous study in [46], we applied different versions of YOLO (n, s, l, m, x) and 
F-RCNN on a fraction (1210 samples) of the papaya dataset. In this case, the YOLOv8 
models performs better than other models. In this paper, we have increased the papaya data­
set, applied augmentation rules, and utilized the YOLOv8 model along with other learning 
models including EfficientDet and F-RCNN.

5  Conclusions
Detection and classification of individual partial and full leaves from the bunch of leaves 
can be the fundamental parameters for forest management and various harvest automation 
applications, such as crop growth monitoring, density of plants prediction, yield prediction, 
and irrigation management, etc. Fruit detection and counting from the bunch of fruits are 
also fundamental for estimating fruit production. In this paper, we develop two real-time 
image datasets: one on bunches of leaves from nine species and the other on bunches of 
papaya fruit. We fine-tuned three models (Faster R-CNN, YOLOv8, and EfficientDet-D0) 
on our collected leaf dataset for detecting and classifying individual partial and full leaves 
from the bunch of leaves, and on our collected papaya dataset for detecting individual 
papaya fruits from the bunch of papayas. From experimental results, we see that YOLOv8 
outperforms all other models on both the leaf and fruit datasets (original and augmented) 
and achieves 96.78% and 99.43% mAP, respectively for the detection of individuals from 
the bunch of leaves and fruits. Considering computational complexity and accuracy rate, 
YOLOv8 is more robust and viable model for both cases. Though the High Boost Filter 
slightly improves the performance of the YOLOv8 model for the original leaf dataset, it 
reduces the performances of the models for other datasets. Moreover, the original data fit 
the model more accurately than that of the augmented data. By taking pictures from papaya 
fields, farmers can estimate their papaya production automatically with our proposed papaya 
fruit detection model.
In future studies, we will enlarge the leaf dataset. We will apply automated individual 
leaf (partial and full) measures in various LAI-related applications.
