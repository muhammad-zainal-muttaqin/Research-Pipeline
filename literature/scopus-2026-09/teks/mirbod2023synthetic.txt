Synthetic Data-Driven AI Using Mixture of Rendered and 
Real Imaging Data for Strawberry Yield Estimation 
Omeed Mirbod, Daeun Choi 
mirbodo@ufl.edu, dana.choi@ufl.edu 
Written for presentation at the 
2023 ASABE Annual International Meeting 
Sponsored by ASABE 
Omaha, Nebraska 
July 9-12, 2023 
ABSTRACT. The use of synthetic data in agriculture can benefit researchers and agricultural business innovators by reducing 
the amount of time, effort, and cost required to collect field data. Current approaches for collecting synthetic image data in 
agriculture applications rely on sample images found on the web or attempting to recreate an agricultural scene that does 
not accurately reflect the targeted environment. In this study, images were collected from a strawberry farm using cameras 
mounted on a ground vehicle which traversed over plasticulture strawberry bed rows. The strawberry farm was then 
recreated in simulation with 3D models of strawberry plants to be used for capturing synthetic 2D image data. These datasets 
were used to train three Faster R-CNN neural networks for fruit detection and applied to the strawberry farm field images 
for inference. The synthetic images, despite having less structural variability than the real images, were still effective in 
identifying fruit in test images from the field. Strawberry fruit detection accuracy was 68% (F1-score 0.80) using only 
synthetic data and 90% (F1-score 0.93) using a mixture of synthetic and real images, showing that synthetic data can be 
used to reduce the number of real images needed for training an object detection neural network model. The study 
demonstrates the potential for synthetic data in agricultural technology development, particularly for fruit detection. 
However, future work is necessary to further investigate features needed to refine synthetic crop models for improved 
detection accuracy in field conditions. 
Keywords. Strawberry detection, yield estimation, synthetic agricultural data, machine vision, digital twin 
1. Introduction 
With the increasing demand for automation and precision management of crops, there is a greater need for agricultural 
field data to develop and test new equipment and algorithms. New Ag-Tech companies are increasingly entering the market 
to provide these technological needs with one venture capital firm report indicating 695 deals that were closed globally, 
totaling $4.70 billion, with most investment in the U.S. (AgFunder 2020). Collection of agricultural field data for research 
and development of new tools and services involves labor, cost, and risk. In certain applications, such as automated 
harvesting and pruning, there is only a limited window of opportunity to collect relevant data and test equipment prototypes 
before needing to wait for the next growing season. Engineers are limited by time, the number of trials they can test their 
equipment, and must be careful not to damage the crop or any supporting infrastructure during test trials. Unforeseen 
circumstances such as new disease pressures or weather events, such as a Spring frost, can also impact crop availability,

Previous studies on using synthetic agricultural data to train neural network models have shown mixed results on 
detection accuracy. Goondram et al. (2020) trained a YOLOv3 model on real, synthetic, and mixture of real and synthetic 
strawberry images for inference on real strawberry images. Detection on real images, and mixture of real and synthetic 
images resulted in an F1-score of 97.8% and 98.3% respectively, while training only on synthetic data resulted in 7.7%. The 
study showed that simulated data could serve as partial substitute for an image dataset but not a complete replacement due 
to the reality gap between real and synthetic images. Similarly, Fei et al. (2021) compared the use of synthetic 3D models 
and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for fruit detection on grape clusters. Models trained only on the 3D synthetic 
data resulted in poor detection compared to mixing a small sample of labeled images from the target domain. A much 
different approach by Rahnemoonfar and Sheppard (2017) on tomato fruit count focused less on creating photorealistic 
images. Their synthetic data was generated by creating a green and brown blurred background image with randomly sized 
red circles to simulate a tomato plant. Trained on 24,000 of these synthetic images of size 128x128 pixels, the accuracy on 
fruit detection for 100 real images was 91%. 
Previous studies have trained detection models on synthetic data and applied them to real images, but the images are less 
representative of crops found in field conditions. In the case of strawberries, fruit size and color can vary depending on the 
growth stage, and a plant can hold multiple fruits at different growth stages which all need to be detected. This study focuses 
on using a ground platform mounted with cameras to collect images from a strawberry field that can be used for the task of 
yield estimation. The strawberry field is then recreated in simulation to represent the real-world scene more accurately. The 
goal of the study was to generate 3D models of strawberry plants in commercial 3D simulation software so that 2D images 
could be captured to train a neural network model for fruit detection on a strawberry farm. Specific objectives of this study 
were to: (1) Develop a real-world image dataset of strawberry plants using a machine vision system mounted on a ground 
vehicle traversing over strawberry beds, (2): Develop a synthetic image dataset using simulation software to capture 2D 
images of 3D strawberry plant models, and (3): Train three different neural network models to test the effectiveness of using 
synthetic data for inference on strawberry field images. 
 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1 Machine Vision System for Image Data Collection 
Images of strawberry plants were collected at the University of Florida Gulf Coast Research and Education Center in 
Wimauma, FL during March and April 2023. The strawberry variety included ‘Florida Brilliance’ planted in rows of raised 
beds under plastic mulch with trickle irrigation. A custom-made ground platform was developed to roll over the strawberry 
beds with three pairs of stereo cameras (Model: Alvium 1800 U-240C, Allied Vision, Stadtroda, Germany) mounted on the 
platform to image the sides and top surface of the beds. To reduce the impact of lighting variability in images from the sun, 
the ground vehicle was covered with a black tarp and artificial lighting from LEDs (model CMA3090, Cree Inc, Durham, 
NC) were mounted next to the cameras to produce a more uniform foreground lighting in the images. Each stereo pair 
camera was interfaced to a Raspberry Pi 4 (Model B with 8GB RAM) running Ubuntu 18.04, and the Robot Operating 
System (ROS) was used to record images. Image acquisition from all six cameras was synchronized with an external trigger 
from an Arduino Mega 2560 microcontroller board (Arduino LLC, Somerville, MA, USA). The ground platform traversed 
at a rate of 1 meter per second collecting images at 3Hz. 
2.2 Simulated Strawberry Farm and Synthetic Strawberry Plant Image Data Collection 
A simulated open strawberry field environment was created using the NVIDIA Isaac Sim platform (NVIDIA Co, Santa 
Clara, CA, USA). The environment consisted of a 3D model representation of the beds which approximated the size of the 
strawberry beds at the farm where data would be collected (Figure 1). Two different 3D strawberry plant models were used 
to populate the beds, the first model (M1) was downloaded from TurboSquid (TurboSquid, New Orleans, Louisiana, USA) 
which included five different plant variations, and the second model (M2) was custom-made with higher resolution and 
details to reflect a strawberry plant more closely at different growth stages (Figure 2). They were used to populate the 
strawberry beds to closely mimic the placement of plants in the real strawberry field.

2.3 Neural Network Model Training and Yield Estimation Pipeline 
To train an object detection model, images from two rows of the ‘Florida Brilliance’ variety in a plot were used for 
training, validation, and testing. The rows were sectioned into north and south segments with the south side being used for 
collecting training and validation images and the north side for collecting test images. In both simulation and field 
environment, images could be captured from the side and top views of the strawberry bed. The TensorFlow 2 Object 
Detection API with model name “Faster R-CNN Inception ResNet V2 1024x1024” provided by the TensorFlow 2 Detection 
Model Zoo (Huang et al., 2017) was used to train a Faster R-CNN model (Ren et al., 2015) on the images. Four different

3. Results and Discussion 
3.1 Fruit Detection Performance Using Synthetic, Real, and Mixed Datasets 
Training of models converged quickly; therefore, the training was stopped at 3200 steps to prevent overfitting of data 
(Figure 4). The resulting fruit detection on test images for each model is listed in Table 2. Percent accuracy is the predicted 
count by the model over the actual count. Even though simulated strawberry plant images from the M1 model were simple 
in texture, uniform in color, and the synthetic plants had less structural variability than plants in real images, the trained 
model was able to identify more than one-third of the fruit in the test images. Decreasing the number of training images 
from 75 to 25 increased the detection rate on the M1 model from 40% to 50%. While it might seem counterintuitive, the 
increase in detection rate with fewer synthetic training images might be attributable to the model's ability to generalize better. 
Using many synthetic images that too closely resemble each other might lead the model to overfit on these specific images. 
With 75 synthetic images, the model may have formed an overly specific expectation of what the fruit looks like based on 
the synthetic data whereas only using 25 images created a more vague representation of the fruits. This is an interesting 
observation, suggesting that fewer training images could sometimes lead to better generalization, especially when the 
training images are synthetic and not perfectly representative of the real-world objects. However, further study is needed to 
fully validate this hypothesis. Fruit detection by both M1 and M2 models on test images seemed invariant to size, color, or 
orientation of fruit, but the detection on those features was also not consistent.

4. Conclusion 
A method for training a neural network model using synthetic data for fruit detection in a strawberry farm was developed 
and tested. A camera system mounted on a ground vehicle captured images of strawberry plants while traversing over 
strawberry beds. The same setup was developed in simulation to mimic the environment of the field where a simulated 
camera would traverse over a strawberry bed collecting images. Training a model on only synthetic image data resulted in 
68% (F1-score 0.80) detection accuracy of fruit in field images. Training on only real image data resulted in the highest 
accuracy of 91% (F1-score 0.95), while using a mixture of the two resulted in 90% accuracy. Fruit traits in field images that 
were consistently missed by the detection model, such as immature fruit, were recreated in the synthetic domain using a 
custom-built strawberry plant model, which increased the detection count by 29 percentage points compared to a simplified 
plant model. Strawberry fruit features such as seed texture and color did not appear to hinder model performance. By starting 
with a simple 3D model of a crop and adding additional complexities to the model as needed to increase accuracy, this study 
seems to suggest that more effort should be placed on finding relevant features to accurately represent a crop in the synthetic 
domain rather than attempting to make a digital twin model look as realistic as possible to the original target. Future work 
will further investigate those features needed to tune a 3D synthetic crop model for greater detection accuracy in a field 
environment.
