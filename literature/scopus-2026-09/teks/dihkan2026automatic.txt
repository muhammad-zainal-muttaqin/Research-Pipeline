Automatic Kiwifruit Segmentation From Terrestrial LiDAR 
Point Clouds for Precision Agriculture
Mustafa Dihkan
  |  Feride Secil Yildirim  |  Fevzi Karsli  |  Gultekin Erbas
Department of Geomatics Engineering, Engineering Faculty, Karadeniz Technical University, Trabzon, Turkey
Correspondence: Mustafa Dihkan (mdihkan@ktu.edu.tr)

Keywords: fruit segmentation | LiDAR point cloud | precision agriculture | Random Forest | support vector machine
ABSTRACT
With the advancement of automation in agriculture, the determination of the health status of fruits, yield estimation, and the 
realization of automated harvesting have become critical applications in precision agriculture. In this context, accurate extrac­
tion of the spatial information of fruits plays a vital role. This study aims to segment kiwi and non-­kiwi points belonging to a 
kiwi tree using Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) algorithms based on terrestrial LiDAR point cloud data. 
Accordingly, the use of machine learning methods is proposed to contribute to yield estimation in the agricultural sector. In this 
study, a single kiwi tree point cloud dataset was selected from a kiwi orchard scanned with a terrestrial LiDAR system and man­
ually labeled. The point cloud was divided into two subsets to prepare training and testing datasets for the SVM and RF models. 
From the LiDAR data of the kiwi tree, red, green, and blue (RGB) color values were extracted along with geometric features 
representing the spatial structure of the point cloud. For each point, the following features were computed: linearity, planarity, 
sphericity, omnivariance, anisotropy, curvature, local density, verticality, k-­nearest neighbors (kNN) mean, kNN standard de­
viation, and normal vector components (Normal X, Normal Y, Normal Z). The computed geometric features were provided to 
a LinearSVC-­based Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) algorithm to select the most relevant parameters for the dataset and 
classification models. Using the selected features, SVM and RF models were trained and tested, and their performances in kiwi 
fruit segmentation were evaluated and compared using Overall Accuracy (OA), Precision (Pr), Recall (Rc), and F1-­score (F1) met­
rics. Based on the evaluation conducted on the labeled test dataset, the RF algorithm outperformed the SVM across all accuracy 
metrics. The most notable difference was observed in the Precision metric, where RF achieved approximately 10% higher per­
formance. Additionally, to assess the generalization capability of the models in kiwi fruit segmentation, prediction experiments 
were carried out on unlabelled point cloud data acquired from the same kiwi orchard. Considering both quantitative accuracy 
metrics and qualitative visual results, it was concluded that the RF algorithm produces more reliable and balanced outcomes in 
distinguishing kiwi and non-­kiwi points, whereas the SVM algorithm provides an advantageous structure in terms of preserving 
fruit integrity.

techniques caused by the growing population and declining 
natural resources worldwide, along with the rapid development 
of automation, artificial intelligence, and remote sensing tech­
nologies (Suman et al. 2021; López-­Quílez 2025). Precision ag­
riculture technologies are increasingly being used to increase 
agricultural productivity, optimize resource use, and make crop 
management more efficient. In this context, precision agricul­
ture applications automate agricultural processes using various

aforementioned requirements (Getahun et  al. 2024). With ad­
vancements in agricultural technologies, the use of LiDAR 
(Light Detection and Ranging) systems in applications such as 
fruit detection and yield estimation has become widespread in 
areas such as the identification, classification, and yield estima­
tion of agricultural products (Rivera et al. 2023).
In the literature, there are various studies conducted using image 
and point cloud data for fruit detection, classification, and yield 
estimation on trees. These studies include classical, deep learn­
ing, and machine learning–based methods (Xiao et  al.  2023; 
Oh 2024). In their study, Ulutaş and Altın (2023) compared the 
Mask R-­CNN and Faster R-­CNN models for detecting kiwifruit 
on trees using image data. This study demonstrates that deep 
learning–based segmentation is an effective method for kiwi fruit 
detection. Wang et al. (2009) aimed to improve recognition ac­
curacy and efficiency by applying SVM in the robot vision sys­
tem of an apple harvesting robot to identify apples and determine 
their location. Experimental results show that the classification 
performance of SVM is better than that of neural networks. It 
was determined that the apple recognition rate achieved by the 
SVM classifier using a combination of color and shape features 
was higher than the rate obtained using only color or only shape 
features individually. Wijethunga et al. (2008) developed an au­
tomated kiwifruit counting approach based on digital image 
analysis to improve yield estimation and harvest planning. In 
this framework, kiwifruits were segmented from orchard images 
using image processing techniques, and the number of fruits was 
automatically determined through object detection and counting 
algorithms. Kang et al. (2022) aimed to develop a high-­precision 
fruit detection and localization system for use in fruit harvest­
ing. By fusing LiDAR and RGB camera data, they identified fruit 
objects using instance segmentation methods. Shao et al. (2024) 
investigated the use of high-­ resolution 101-­ channel hyperspec­
tral LiDAR (HSL) data in estimating fruit ripeness, number, and 
size. The study demonstrates the high potential of HSL data in 
estimating fruit parameters compared to traditional methods. 
Nejati et al. (2020) conducted a study on the accurate detection 
of kiwi fruit using image data under challenging conditions such 
as variable lighting and fruit overlaps. Yang et al. (2024) proposed 
a new kiwifruit detection algorithm based on a lightweight deep 
learning network (improved YOLOv4-­tiny) using image data. Fu 
et al. (2018) investigated kiwifruit detection in field images using 
the Faster R-­ CNN deep learning model. Sapkota et  al.  (2024) 
developed a method for detecting and sizing immature green 
apples in commercial orchards using a YOLOv8-­ based model 
combined with shape-­fitting techniques on RGB-­D image data. 
He et al. (2019) proposed a double-­layer Pulse-­Coupled Neural 
Network (PCNN)–based image segmentation algorithm to accu­
rately segment kiwifruit under varying illumination and complex 
background conditions. By integrating frequency-­tuned saliency, 
optimal RGB color-­difference features, and enhanced hue infor­
mation, the method achieved superior visual and quantitative seg­
mentation performance compared to conventional approaches. 
Fu et  al.  (2015) focused on night-­ time kiwifruit detection and 
developed a machine vision–based system using artificial illu­
mination. Employing an RGB camera, an R–G color model, and 
classical image processing techniques, the method reduced back­
ground noise and fruit overlap, achieving a detection accuracy 
of approximately 88.3% under optimal lighting conditions. Zhou 
et al. (2024) introduced Kiwi-­YOLO, an enhanced YOLOv8-­based 
detection framework designed for complex orchard environ­
ments with variable lighting, occlusion, and dense fruit clusters. 
Through lightweight network design, improved feature fusion, 
and an optimized loss function, the model improved detection 
performance while significantly reducing computational cost, 
demonstrating strong robustness for practical intelligent harvest­
ing applications. Ren et al. (2025) proposed a lightweight object 
detection model, YOLOv10-­Kiwi, based on YOLOv10, to enable 
fast and accurate detection of kiwifruits in trellised orchards. The 
model was trained on images collected from kiwifruit orchards 
and optimized to enhance performance under complex back­
ground conditions, such as dense foliage, branches, shadows, and 
partially occluded small fruits. Li et al. (2025) aimed to achieve 
accurate segmentation of kiwifruit in orchard environments and 
to determine the optimal picking point on the fruit stem, which 
is critical for harvesting. For this purpose, image processing and 
deep learning–based methods (DS-­UNet) were employed to sep­
arate the fruit from the background, followed by analysis of the 
fruit–stem connection region to identify the most suitable detach­
ment point. The study is particularly intended to provide essen­
tial visual information for automated robotic harvesting systems. 
Miao et al. (2026) employed image-­based multi-­feature fusion in 
combination with the ResTNet model to achieve more accurate 
and reliable kiwifruit quality grading. In their approach, multiple 
features, including color, texture, and shape, were extracted from 
kiwifruit images and fused, and classification was performed 
using the deep learning–based ResTNet model. Ge et al. (2022) 
proposed a method for classifying 3D apple tree organs and es­
timating yield using Color and Shape Multi-­ Feature Fusion 
and SVM-­based methods. They extracted 196-­dimensional fea­
ture vectors consisting of RGB (Red, Green, Blue), HSV (Hue, 
Saturation, Value), Curvatures, Fast Point Feature Histogram 
(FPFH), and Spin Image features. The SVM was then trained and 
used to classify apple tree organs. Zhang et al. (2020) proposed a 
pomegranate tree organ classification and fruit counting method 
based on multi-­feature fusion and SVM in their study. In their 
method, three-­ dimensional point clouds of pomegranate trees 
were obtained using an RGB-­D camera. The 3D point clouds were 
pre-­processed to extract color and shape features for training the 
SVM classifier. A merged-­ split hierarchical clustering method 
was used to count pomegranate fruits. The results obtained 
demonstrate that the proposed method is effective and applica­
ble for organ classification and yield estimation in pomegranate 
trees. Dorj et al. (2017) stated that the aim of their study was to 
develop a computer vision algorithm that can detect and count 
citrus fruits on trees using image processing techniques and es­
timate yield. The new citrus fruit recognition and counting algo­
rithm used color characteristics to estimate citrus yield, and the 
relevant models were developed to provide early yield estimates. 
The proposed algorithm demonstrated significant potential for 
early yield estimation of individual citrus trees and offered the 
possibility of adaptation to other fruit crops.

•	 This study aims to contribute to precision agriculture ap­
plications by enabling the effective separation of kiwifruit 
from leaves and branches.
•	 By selecting and utilizing the most suitable parameters for 
kiwifruit segmentation, the study seeks to achieve high seg­
mentation accuracy while reducing computational time.
•	 For kiwifruit segmentation based on LiDAR point cloud 
data, the R, G, and B color values of each point, together

Random Forest algorithms, and a comparative performance 
analysis of both classifiers was conducted

stead, a novel 3D dataset of a kiwifruit tree was generated 
using a terrestrial LiDAR scanner, and ground truth data 
were created through manual labeling. In addition, to eval­
uate the generalizability of the trained models, prediction 
experiments were performed on unlabelled point cloud data 
acquired from a kiwifruit orchard.

3.2   |   Feature Extraction and Feature Selection

designed to enable the accurate and efficient separation of kiwi­
fruit from branches, trunks, and leaves within point cloud data. 
To this end, not only the positional and spectral information of 
each point but also its local geometric structure, spatial density 
characteristics, and orientation properties were taken into ac­
count. First, normalized (scaled) RGB color information was 
computed for each point.

In addition to the geometric features, distance-­based attributes 
namely local density, kNN mean distance, and kNN distance 
standard deviation were also computed to better represent the 
spatial context of each point.
Given the relatively large number of features obtained during 
the feature extraction stage and the potential high correlation 
among certain features, a geometric feature selection process 
was applied to improve classification performance and reduce 
model complexity. For this purpose, the Recursive Feature 
Elimination (RFE) method was employed, using a linear-­kernel 
Support Vector Machine (LinearSVC) as the base estimator. 
During the RFE process, the 13 geometric features were evalu­
ated, while the RGB color features were excluded from the selec­
tion procedure and directly incorporated into the final feature 
matrix.
As a result of the RFE process, seven features exhibiting the 
highest discriminative power between classes were selected: 
Linearity, Planarity, Sphericity, Anisotropy, Curvature, Local 
Density, and kNN Mean Distance. These selected features were 
combined with the normalized RGB information to construct 
the final feature matrix, which was subsequently used in the 
classification stage.
Three-­dimensional (3D) point cloud data of kiwifruit trees were 
used in *.las format. The point clouds contain, for each point, X, 
Y, and Z coordinates, color information (R, G, B), and manually 
assigned class labels. The class labels represent kiwifruit points 
with the value 1 and background points with the value 2. Since 
the color information is stored in a 16-­bit format (0–65,535) de­
pending on the sensor output, the color channels were scaled 
and normalized to the range [0–1].
To compute discriminative geometric features for each point, 
a point-­wise k-­nearest neighbor (kNN) approach was adopted. 
For this purpose, a KD-­tree structure was constructed over the 
entire point cloud, and the k nearest neighbors of each point 
were identified (the value of k was optimized in a later stage of 
the study). To characterize the local geometric structure of each 
point, a covariance matrix (Equation  1) was computed using 
the coordinates of the points within each neighborhood (Lin 
et al. 2014; Qin et al. 2018).

selected k parameter directly determines the spatial scale at 
which each point is represented. Since the value of k defines 
the size of the neighborhood used to compute the covariance 
matrix, it substantially influences the sensitivity and discrim­
inative capability of all derived geometric, normal-­based, and 
distance-­ based features. Lower k values characterize only 
the immediate local vicinity of a point, allowing fine-­ scale 
geometric details to be captured; however, this may increase 
sensitivity to noise. In contrast, higher k values encompass 
a broader neighborhood, yielding more stable statistical 
measurements, but potentially suppressing local geometric 
variations, such as subtle structural differences between fruit 
and branch elements. Consequently, the appropriate selection 
of the k parameter is a critical step in point cloud–based clas­
sification studies.
In this study, an experimental optimization procedure 
was conducted to determine the most suitable k value 
for kNN-­ based feature extraction. To this end, a set of 
k 
values 
representing 
different 
neighborhood 
scales, 
k = {80,90,100,110,120,130,135,140,145}, was evaluated. For 
each k value, the following features were computed across the en­
tire point cloud: Linearity, Planarity, Sphericity, Omnivariance, 
Anisotropy, Curvature, Verticality, three-­ dimensional surface 
normal vectors, local density, kNN mean distance, and kNN dis­
tance standard deviation. These features were then combined 
with RGB color values, scaled using the StandardScaler, and 
classified using LinearSVC.
During the optimization process, the performance evaluation 
focused particularly on minimizing false positives for the kiwi­
fruit class, that is, reducing the misclassification of branches as 
kiwifruit. Accordingly, model performance was assessed using 
precision, recall, and F1-­score metrics, with primary emphasis 
placed on the precision value of the kiwifruit class. This strategy 
aligns with the main objective of the study, which is the reli­
able detection of fruit points while minimizing noise-­induced 
misclassifications.
The experimental results indicated that a k value of 140 yielded 
a higher precision for the kiwifruit class compared to the other 
tested values. Therefore, this value was selected as the fixed 
neighborhood parameter for all subsequent feature extraction and 
classification stages of the study. This choice ensured that local

tree point cloud were evaluated on one labeled test point cloud 
and three unlabelled point cloud subsets randomly selected from

homogeneous and reliable classification output.

4   |   Result and Discussion
This study aimed to segment kiwifruit from a kiwifruit tree ac­
quired using a terrestrial laser scanner in a kiwifruit orchard 
located in Ordu, Türkiye. To achieve this objective, a machine 
learning–based framework was developed using SVM and RF 
algorithms.
Initially, point-­ level annotations of kiwifruit were manually 
generated for a selected tree in the orchard to serve as reference 
data for training and testing. The manually labeled point cloud 
of the kiwifruit tree was partitioned into training and testing 
subsets. In addition to the labeled test dataset, three unlabelled 
point cloud subsets randomly extracted from different trees in 
the orchard were included in the testing phase to assess the gen­
eralizability of the trained models.

Image-­ based methods generally rely on the two-­ dimensional 
projection of the scene and mostly focus on detecting fruits lo­
cated on the outer surface of the tree. Due to dense foliage and 
branch structures, fruits located in the inner parts of the tree are 
often occluded or completely invisible in such images. In con­
trast, TLS data, especially when acquired from multiple view­
points, represent the three-­ dimensional structure of the tree 
more comprehensively through distinctive geometric features 
such as linearity, planarity, sphericity, curvature, local density, 
and kNN-­ based distance metrics, which are difficult to learn 
using image-­based methods, thereby increasing the potential for 
detecting partially occluded or internally located fruits.
Nevertheless, TLS-­ based data acquisition has certain limita­
tions, such as the requirement for close-­range scanning and the 
generation of large data volumes. However, these limitations 
can be significantly mitigated through advanced point cloud 
processing techniques to improve data quality and by integrat­
ing TLS data with complementary data sources such as RGB im­
agery or UAV systems. In this regard, it is concluded that this 
TLS-­based machine learning approach, with its robustness to 
illumination conditions, low training data requirements, high 
geometric interpretability, and low computational cost, can be 
used not only as a standalone solution for precision agriculture 
applications such as automatic fruit detection, yield estimation, 
robotic harvesting, and orchard management, but also as a 
strong and sustainable complementary approach that enhances 
performance by providing additional geometric and spatial in­
formation, particularly in complex orchard environments where 
color-­based discrimination is insufficient.
5   |   Conclusion
This study aims to automatically segment kiwifruit using terres­
trial laser scanning (TLS) point cloud data and to contribute to 
precision agriculture applications. Within this context, the kiwi­
fruit–non-­kiwifruit point discrimination problem was addressed 
using a machine learning–based framework, and a comparative 
analysis was conducted employing SVM and RF algorithms. 
During the classification process, RGB color information ex­
tracted from the point cloud was integrated with geometric, 
normal-­based, and distance-­based features computed using the 
k-­nearest neighbors (kNN) approach, and the Recursive Feature 
Elimination (RFE) method was applied to identify the most dis­
criminative and optimized subset of features for classification. 
Discriminative geometric features that are difficult to learn in 
image-­ based methods, such as linearity, planarity, sphericity, 
curvature, local density, and kNN-­based distance measures, are 
explicitly represented, providing a physically interpretable and 
controllable framework.
Despite using a limited dataset obtained from a single tree, the 
SVM and RF models achieved an overall accuracy of approxi­
mately 90% and an F1-­score ranging from 88% to 91%, with the 
RF algorithm achieving approximately 10% higher precision 
than SVM, resulting in more balanced and reliable differenti­
ation between kiwifruit and non-­ kiwifruit points. These re­
sults demonstrate that, in terms of the data–accuracy ratio, the 
proposed method is highly efficient compared to deep learn­
ing–based approaches that require large-­scale image datasets, 
extensive data augmentation, and long training times. When

is the first in the literature to address point cloud-­based kiwi

fruit from leaves and branches. In this study, a novel 3D dataset

real data was obtained through manual labeling. Furthermore, 
prediction experiments were conducted on unlabelled point 
cloud data from the kiwi orchard to evaluate the generalizability 
of the trained models. In conclusion, this TLS-­based machine 
learning approach, with its robustness to illumination, low 
training data requirement, high interpretability, and low com­
putational cost, represents a strong, sustainable, and practical 
alternative for precision agriculture applications, including au­
