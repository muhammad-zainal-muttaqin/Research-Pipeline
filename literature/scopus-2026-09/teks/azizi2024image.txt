A B S T R A C T
This review provides an overview of apple detection and localization using image analysis and artificial intel­
ligence techniques for enabling robotic fruit harvesting in orchard environments. Classic methods for detecting 
and localizing infield apples are discussed along with more advanced approaches using deep learning algorithms 
that have emerged in the past few years. Challenges faced in apple detection and localization such as occlusions, 
varying illumination conditions, and clustered apples are highlighted, as well as the impact of environmental 
factors such as light changes on the performance of these algorithms. Potential future research perspectives are 
identified through a comprehensive literature analysis. These include combining cutting-edge deep learning and 
multi-vision and multi-modal sensors to potentially apply them in real-time for apple harvesting robots. Addi­
tionally, utilizing 3D vision for a thorough analysis of complex and dynamic orchard environments, and precise 
determination of fruit locations using point cloud data and depth information are presented. The outcome of this 
review paper will assist researchers and engineers in the development of advanced detection and localization 
mechanisms for infield apples. The anticipated result is the facilitation of progress toward commercial apple 
harvest robots.
1. Introduction
Picking apples manually can be a labor-intensive and expensive 
process, especially when orchards have a large number of trees to har­
vest. This issue has been exacerbated by the rising labor cost and the 
decreasing availability of skilled workers [1]. In addition to the risk of 
operator falls from ladders, hand harvesting can also lead to repetitive 
strain injuries in the arms and wrists, as well as potential cuts and 
punctures from contact with sharp stems or branches [2,3]. However, 
the mechanization of apple picking would address these issues. There­
fore, the implementation of robotic apple harvesting systems can 
significantly decrease the requirement for human labor, improve worker 
safety in orchards, and offer cost-efficient solutions that ultimately 
benefit both apple growers and buyers.
To overcome the problems of manual harvesting of fruits, mechani­
cal harvesting was developed [4,5,6]. In general, while mechanical 
apple harvesting can be more efficient than manual, it is not without its 
limitations and challenges [7]. Mechanical harvesters can damage the 
fruit during the picking process, which can lower its quality and reduce 
its shelf life [8,9,10]. The inability to accurately distinguish ripe fruit is 
another problem with mechanical apple harvesting, while human 
pickers can distinguish between ripe and unripe fruit easily, mechanical

harvesters have difficulty navigating uneven terrain or steep slopes, 
which limits their effectiveness under challenging topography [13]. 
High capital costs and system reliability are other problems associated

harvesters can increase efficiency and reduce the need for manual labor,

The most modern system of apple picking employs robots specifically 
trained for the task. By analyzing images obtained from onboard cam­

localize fruits for harvesting. Despite the importance of the detection 
and localization of apples by modern apple harvesting by robots, there is 
an information and research gap in this area. The current research fo­

developing detection and localization methods [15,16,17,5]. However, 
there is limited research on other aspects of apple harvesting detection 
and localization, such as optimizing the process in complex orchard 
environments or improving real-time performance [18,19,20]. This in­
formation and research gap presents an opportunity for further studies 
and innovations in the field of apple harvesting robot detection and 
localization. Therefore, the objective of this review study is to present 
comprehensively new methods and achievements in apple detection and 
localization based on vision methods and AI. Additionally, the chal­
lenges and possible solutions within this field will also be discussed.
1.1. Study organization
This review paper is organized into the following sections. Section 2
gives a Brief account of the history of fruit harvesting and the challenges 
of modern apple picking robots. Section 3 presents the image sources 
and classic methods used for apple detection tasks, along with the 
accompanying limitations that are associated with them. Sections 4 and 
5 provide a summary of the studies conducted on innovative methods for 
apple detection and localization, respectively. Section 6 addresses the 
challenges encountered in applying apple detection and localization 
techniques for apple harvesting, along with the upcoming prospects for

overall conclusions are presented in Section 7.
2. History, nature, and challenges of fruit harvesting
2.1. Evolution of fruit harvesting
In the past, people utilized gardening shears, ladders, and baskets for 
apple harvest. Over time, manual tools were gradually replaced by 
motorized mechanical systems, leading to the mechanization of agri­
culture and horticulture. The main challenges of user-based methods 
included inefficiency at large scales, rising labor costs, the risk of injuries 
during harvesting, and the tedious nature of the work, prompting a shift 
to mechanical methods. The inability to distinguish between ripe and 
unripe fruits, possible damage to fruit and tree, and high energy usage 
combined with low maneuverability and navigation challenges in dense 
orchards, are significant technical obstacles for mechanical fruit har­
vesting systems, particularly for apples. These considerations paved the 
way for the modern era of automation in agriculture, driven by 
advanced technologies and sciences such as artificial intelligence and 
robotics. In the past two decades, digital-based automation technology 
has been utilized to automate diagnosis and decision-making, which has 
increased agricultural productivity. Fruit harvesting is also not isolated 
from the course of this evolution, and Fig. 1 presents a representation of 
this process.
2.2. Multidisciplinary nature of fruit harvesting
Robotic fruit harvesting is a vast field closely intertwined with 
several other areas of research and technology. It encompasses disci­
plines such as computer vision, mechanics, data science, energy, and 
environmental issues (Fig. 2). Several techniques of computer vision 
with the use or not of artificial intelligence (AI), are utilized to recognize 
and locate ripe apples amidst the foliage. Robots can be made to identify 
color, shape, and other visual features to determine the ripeness of the 
fruits while localizing them. Data science can be utilized to analyze 
historical and real-time data collected by the robots, which can include 
information about apple varieties, growth stages, weather conditions, 
and soil quality. By analyzing the data, patterns, and correlations can be 
discovered, enabling better decision-making for satisfactory harvesting 
processes. Mechanics and energy in apple harvesting robots encompass 
the design and optimization of the robotic arms and end effectors, 
power, and energy management systems, mobility and locomotion ca­
pabilities, and harvesting mechanisms, which contribute to the overall 
efficiency, productivity, and sustainability of the apple harvesting 
process.

3. Overview of classic apple detection and localization methods
3.1. Image sources for apple detection
For decades, most object detection methods have relied on vision, 
and identifying fruits is not an exception to this rule. Therefore, in the 
first step, digital images are needed so that targets (e.g., fruits) can be 
pursued by processing them. These images can be captured using 
monochrome, color, multispectral/hyperspectral, and thermal cameras 
(Fig. 3). These cameras provide images from which a wide range of 
features can be extracted. Multispectral and hyperspectral cameras are 
widely used in the agricultural sector and hold potential for tasks such as 
apple detection, particularly in evaluating product quality [22]. The 
distinctive feature of multispectral cameras is their ability to capture 
data across specific wavelength bands beyond the visible spectrum, 
along with their affordability. In contrast, hyperspectral devices excel at 
providing more detailed information about the internal chemical char­
acteristics of products. More applications are expected to enhance the 
use of these technologies in agriculture and promote sustainable apple 
production.
3.1.1. Monochrome images
Prior to the development and wide adoption of color imagery, re­
searchers had no other choice but to rely on monochrome technology 
(also called black and white). One of the initial studies to utilize 
monochrome cameras for fruit detection was conducted by Parrish and 
Goksel [23]. The monochrome camera they used was specifically 
equipped with a red filter, which was designed to capture the red 
wavelengths present in the environment. Sites and Delwiche [24] 
incorporated the use of additional filters in conjunction with mono­
chrome cameras to enhance the detection of apples. They conducted 
data collection at night to minimize occurrences of incorrect detections. 
During the day, changes in the brightness of both leaves and apples often 
led to increased erroneous results. For this reason, they suggested 
installing a barrier to eliminate background interference and shield 
against sunlight. Among the pioneering studies that employed mono­
chrome cameras for fruit detection were Dobrusin et al. [25], Pla et al. 
[26], and Edan et al. [27]. The detection accuracy ranged from 75 % to 
88 %. In monochrome images, typically represented in grayscale images, 
more emphasis is placed on the textural, geometrical, and shape aspects 
for object recognition [28]. In this context, in one of the relatively new 
studies, researchers developed a method referred to as convexity for 
identifying apples, essentially by analyzing the shape of objects [29]. In 
this method, the edges of black and white images are identified and 
merged. By analyzing the brightness profiles obtained from each edge,

3.1.2. Full color images
Color is a crucial factor in detecting fruits, but monochrome cameras 
lack this information, and thus the performance of these cameras is 
usually unsatisfactory as discussed in the above section. As a result, the 
need for color images in the field of agriculture and horticulture was 
considered several decades ago. In other words, as color is a critical 
indicator for most fruits due to distinct color exhibition, color cameras 
provide a suitable opportunity to detect fruits in addition to identifying 
their ripeness ([14]). Color cameras closely replicate human vision, and 
hence, they provide information that matches human expectations and 
standards in apple detection tasks. Color cameras provide useful capa­
bilities, including detection and segmentation of apples based on color, 
as well as extraction of geometric and textural information. These are 
some of the common uses of color images acquired through color cam­
eras. In this regard, Ji et al., [30] undertook the identification of apples 
on trees by extracting color and texture features from color images and 
employing the Support Vector Machine (SVM) classification model. 
They reported achieving an accuracy of 89 %, demonstrating that color 
features extracted from color images have significant potential in the 
task of apple recognition, and can be utilized in apple-harvesting robots. 
In another study, researchers utilized color information extracted from a 
dataset of color apple images with two intentions: segmenting the apples 
themselves and segmenting the edge regions of the apples to enhance 
recognition performance [31]. Numerous similar studies have been 
conducted on the use of color camera images for the identification of 
various types of orchard products, including apples and other fruits ([32,
26]; Jimenez et al., 2000; [33,34,35,36,37,38]). An example of a com­
mon image processing technique as color segmentation of an apple 
image along with the binary image is shown in Fig. 4. Traditional apple 
detection methods, which involve manual processes like color space 
conversion and thresholding, are less automated and often less accurate 
in complex conditions. In contrast, deep learning approaches provide 
higher accuracy with minimal manual intervention after training, as 
they can learn intricate patterns in the data. Although traditional 
methods are computationally efficient and quick on standard hardware, 
they struggle to scale with larger datasets, while deep learning requires 
substantial computational resources during training but can achieve 
real-time segmentation once optimized.
In the domain of image processing, regardless of whether traditional 
techniques or more advanced methods such as deep learning (DL) are 
used to analyze and understand image content, it consistently engages 
with feature extraction, which is associated with apple identification. In 
fruit identification, the most commonly employed features include 
color, texture, and shape, complemented by image segmentation [39]. 
The features, along with the techniques used to extract them, are listed 
in Table 1. Using this comprehensive compilation, the foundational 
principles for an approach to apple identification are further elucidated.
While color cameras are still being used in the field of intelligent 
apple identification, as mentioned earlier, their main weakness lies in 
their sensitivity to changes in ambient light conditions. When the 
brightness of a scene varies, it directly impacts the brightness of both the 
target pixels (e.g., apples and the background). This remains a constant 
challenge when working with visible color images. Therefore, other 
types of sensors that are either less sensitive or completely insensitive to 
light conditions have been considered.
3.1.3. Thermal images
In conventional visible light imaging, the light source is required to 
form an image. If the scene is dark or even poorly lit, identifying objects 
can be significantly hampered. However, in thermal imaging, the pro­
cess doesn’t rely on reflected light for illumination. Instead, it uses the 
principle that all objects emit electromagnetic radiation as a tempera­
ture signature. Thus, based on the differences in emission or absorption 
values between fruits and non-fruits (e.g., foliage and branch), thermal 
sensors can be effectively utilized for fruit detection [48,49,50]. Stajnko 
et al. [49] investigated the utilization of thermal imaging to detect fruits 
and determine fruit yield in orchards. The study demonstrated that fruit 
detection using thermal images can be relatively accurate, especially for 
mature fruit exposed to sunlight. However, the accuracy is declined for 
fruit in shadow or deep within the tree canopy due to reduced temper­
ature differences. Moreover, a correlation was observed between 
manually counted and thermal-image-detected fruits, indicating that 
thermal imaging could be a useful tool for yield prediction.
Despite the valuable information thermal images provide based on 
the thermal properties of objects, they remain sensitive to temperature 
fluctuations throughout the day and night [39]. Hence, in certain 
studies, the fusion of thermal and visible images is utilized for fruit 
detection. In a study by Wachs et al. [36], a multimodal approach was 
used to recognize occluded green apples in natural scenes, employing a 
combination of full-color RGB and infrared images. They utilized the 
Mutual Information (MI) index to register both RGB and thermal images, 
reporting that MI proved to be the most effective registration method. 
Then, Haar classifiers were applied to detect apples after arranging them 
in a cascade structure. They achieved a hit rate (true positive) of 0.995 
and a recognition accuracy of 74 %. In another study, Chandel et al. [51] 
introduced a system that integrates thermal with RGB color images in 
the natural environment, aiming to tackle the challenges arising from 
the exclusive use of thermal sensors. They reached a standard error 
within the ranges of 0.12–0.23 ◦C. The benefit of the fusion approach in 
this study was that the accuracy of fruit surface temperature estimations 
was improved due to enhancing the segmentation method. This method

3.1.4. Stereo images
Stereo vision involves capturing more than one image of a scene 
simultaneously. This technique poses advantages, such as the ability to 
extract 3D information [52]. Indeed, this becomes feasible by supplying 
depth information and spatial data, which can be utilized to both 
identify and precisely locate fruits. Furthermore, the depth information 
facilitates understanding the gap separating the fruits from the robot 
arm in smart harvesting, thereby making it possible to harvest without 
causing damage to the fruits. The practice of utilizing stereo vision for 
fruit detection has a history dating back approximately three decades 
[53]. Takahashi et al. [54] conducted a pioneering study in the field of 
fruit recognition, specifically focusing on apple robotic harvesting using 
stereo vision techniques. This study served as the foundation for several 
subsequent research endeavors in this domain. The reported fruit 
recognition accuracy values for different apple varieties ranged between 
65 % and 90 %. Binocular stereo vision was used in a study for apple 
detection in actual scenes, taking into account the complex and crowded 
tree environment in orchards [55]. They used the color indexing tech­
nique as a chromaticity model for separating apples from the back­
ground. Then, by employing a matching algorithm, they identified the 
centers of occluded apples, resulting in a maximum picking error rate of 
9.4 %. In another study, Si et al. [56] utilized stereoscopic vision for 
locating apples in an orchard. For this purpose, color indexing, shape 
features, and a matching algorithm derived from epipolar geometry 
were employed on stereo pairs of apple images. They stated that 89.5 % 
of the apples could be accurately recognized with this procedure. The 
use of stereo vision in various agricultural products has been the subject 
of numerous studies [57,58,59,60].
Although the classic stereo vision method partially fulfills the fruit 
harvesting robot’s needs, it exhibits several shortcomings. In an or­
chard’s complex and dynamic environment, the greatest challenges for 
apple detection tasks include occlusions caused by foliage, tree limbs, 
shade, and variable illumination. Hence, under these conditions, the rate 
of correctly recognized fruits is low [58]. One of the existing solutions 
proposed to overcome occlusion in varying lighting conditions involves 
using disparity to ascertain the state of occlusion [61]. In this method, 
the process of reaching the target object is done hierarchically. Initially, 
the target candidates are identified based on 3D information gathered 
from disparity. Subsequently, color features and location data are uti­
lized to distinguish the identified candidates from other targets. Finally, 
the spatial relationship between these two groups is analyzed to identify 
the state of occlusion and to determine the final object. Another notable 
challenge arises from the significant time required for stereo matching, 
which can present problems in practical applications, especially in un­
structured and ill-posed regions [62]. Traditional methods are often 
time-consuming and not accurate enough. Hence, researchers are 
developing new methods, such as techniques equipped with AI algo­
rithms, to improve the accuracy of stereo matching and reduce pro­
cessing time [63,64,65]. An example of images related to different 
image sources is shown in Fig. 5.
It is worth mentioning that, aside from the cameras covered here, 
there are other kinds of photographs that employ alternative light 
sources, such as time-of-flight (ToF) and structured light cameras. These 
cameras are mostly used to create depth images [66,67,68]. Although 
these cameras can access depth information quickly, their accuracy and 
resolution are insufficient. Furthermore, their high cost limits their use 
in the agricultural sector.

consist of several components including feature extraction, region pro­

localization (Fig. 6). Depending on the type of objects and the purpose of 
detection, pre- and post-processing may also be performed. In feature 
extraction, traditional approaches often employ feature extractors such 
as Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), Scale Invariant Feature 
Transform (SIFT), Speed Up Robust Features (SURF), and Dense Pose 
Mapping (DPM), for the object detection task. Since the features 
extracted by these methods are manual, they are known as hand-crafted 
features which are widely used [70]. They capture valuable data such as 
edges, patterns, color, and shape, which can distinguish objects from 
each other and their backgrounds. Region proposals or candidate re­
gions involve generating potential regions in an image that probably 
contains the target objects. These areas are proposed initially for sub­
sequent object classification and localization [71,72]. Afterward, the 
algorithm proceeds to conduct object classification for each proposed 
region and ascertain whether it contains an object or not. This phase 
involves training a classifier using traditional machine learning (ML) 
techniques such as SVM or learning unique traits of the object. Due to 
the possibility of identifying multiple region proposals for a single ob­
ject, a non-maximum suppression algorithm is utilized to remove 
redundant or overlapping proposals. This algorithm selects the most 
confident proposal for each object while discarding the remaining ones 
[73]. In the last step, the algorithm detects and determines the precise 
location of the object in the image by estimating the object’s bounding 
box.
3.2.1. Artificial neural network
In the development of robot-based apple harvesting systems, it’s 
imperative to accurately identify and detect the fruits at the appropriate 
time. The methods where features are obtained manually through trial 
and error, do not significantly advance development of robotic har­
vesting. For instance, one study used a basic color model, which iden­
tified apples in natural images through the color difference between 
apples and background [74]. This research resulted in an 18 % error, 
and similar studies that use simple methods to model the tree’s complex 
structure encounter limitations in practical applications. Considering 
the inherent complexities associated with apple cultivation environ­
ments, there is a need for tools and models capable of adequately 
deciphering the relationships between these complexities (canopy) and 
the target object. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) have been proven 
capable of modeling complex patterns (because of their non-linear 
structure) and solving prediction problems [75].
Changyi et al. [76] proposed a relatively robust algorithm using a 
back propagation neural network (BPNN) for apple detection and 
counting. In this study, the distinct features of apples, specifically 
focusing on their color and shape were considered. Initially, a neural 
network was applied to the input image to identify the colors of the 
apples. The topology of this BPNN encompassed three layers, with the 
hidden layer consisting of 16 neurons. The transfer functions employed 
for the hidden and output layers were Tansig and Purelin, respectively. 
The result of this process was the removal of the background, leaving 
only the pixels corresponding to the apples. Next, morphological oper­
ations were used to reveal the edges of the apples. Finally, the images 
resulting from the previous steps were subject to a Circular Hough 
Transformation to identify the apples, which appeared as circles. The 
number of these circles, identified by their clear centers, corresponded 
to the number of apples in each image. They reached the coefficient 
correlation value of 0.98 between the counting performed by the pro­
posed method and the visual counting. In other research, a multi-scale 
multilayered perceptron (ms-MLP) with a watershed segmentation was 
used for extracting apples from the orchard images [77]. Patches of 
pixels from the raw input images were transformed into contextual 
windows at different scales to feed the three-layer ms-MLP for the 
classification task (Fig. 7). The detection probability for ms-MLP 
reached 83 %, compared to the 92 % that was achieved with a con­
volutional neural network (CNN).
3.2.2. Support vector machine
Support Vector Machines (SVMs) are one of the most popular ML 
algorithms that have been widely used in various scientific domains. An 
SVM model performs its role by finding hyperplanes in N-dimensional 
space, which can then be used to accurately classify previously unseen 
examples [78]. This classification can be performed either linearly or 
non-linearly, depending on the used kernels (Fig. 8). One of the reasons 
for the widespread use of SVMs is their ability in fast training speed and 
guaranteed convergence [79,80,81].
In a study, researchers utilized an SVM classifier to identify various 
fruits [30]. The inputs to this network were the shape and color features 
of the apples, which were prepared and extracted from the images using 
a vector median filter and region growing segmentation. This also serves 
to remove noise from the color information. In the subsequent step, color 
features and the shapes of apple borders were extracted. Following this, 
the SVM classification model was trained using three different types of 
kernel functions - the poly kernel, radial basis kernel, and sigmoid kernel 
- to improve apple recognition accuracy. The method was evaluated 
using a harvesting robot in the orchard, which attained a recognition 
rate and processing time of 89 % and 352 ms per image, respectively. 
Although the results of their research were relatively responsive to the 
needs of the apple-picking robot, the error rate of 11 % was found to be 
high due to occlusion. SVMs have also demonstrated their application in 
fruit classification in various studies. In a study, different fruits were 
classified using a series of multi-class kernel support vector machines 
[83]. The fruits were automatically categorized by removing the back­
ground using the split-and-merge method, then extracting the histogram 
features for color, texture, and shape, and finally inputting these fea­
tures into three types of SVMs. The highest accuracy obtained by this 
method was 88.2 %. To streamline the problem’s complexity and reduce 
the dimensions of the feature space, they employed principal component 
analysis before the classification process so that they successfully 
addressed the challenge of selecting effective features. However, the 
issue with multi-features persists, suggesting a need for advanced 
methods like DL in the task of fruit classification [84]. In addition to 
apples, SVMs have been used for detecting various fruits, such as 
strawberries and pineapples with satisfactory results [85,86,87,88,89,
90].

The application of the K-Means and AdaBoost algorithms as unsu­

The images used in their research were multi-modal, incorporating color

blueness-yellowness (Fig. 9a). In this study, the detection of apples was 
conducted using two approaches: high and low-level features. For the 
first approach, the overall shape of the apples was identified by 
extracting color and geometric features. Subsequently, this method 
attained a recognition accuracy of 53.1 %. In the second approach, 
low-level features derived from the Haar transformation and AdaBoost 
algorithm were utilized. After applying a voting scheme, the overall 
accuracy of this method was found to be 74.3 %. They reported that 
unconstrained illumination has a direct negative effect on the accuracy

In a study aimed at enhancing the performance of an apple-picking 
robot, Tao and Zhou [95] implemented several ML algorithms, 
including the K-NN, specifically for the classification of target data such 
as apples, branches, and leaves. For this purpose, point clouds were 
processed as reference data. Initially, redundant information was elim­

threshold for the depth image. Subsequently, the region growing seg­

extract data from three classes: apple, branch, and leaf. Then, 3D fea­

3D descriptor known as the Color Fast Point Feature Histogram (Col­

classifiers: K-NN, SVM, Random Forest (RF), and a combination of Ge­

these four models, the K-NN yielded the lowest classification accuracy 
for the apple class at 80.3 %. Conversely, the combination of the genetic 
algorithm and GA-SVM model delivered the highest classification ac­
curacy, scoring 92.3 %. Despite its simplicity, the K-NN algorithm re­

presents a serious challenge when dealing with high-dimensional data, 
such as the numerous features obtained in this study. Fig. 10 presents the

4. Modern methods for apple detection
Research on applying robotics in handling horticultural products 
spans over two decades [100]. Traditional ML methods are simple and 
offer advantages such as speed in fruit detection tasks. However, despite 
these benefits, they face serious challenges which prevent their accuracy 
from exceeding a certain limit. A challenge highlighted in the referenced 
materials is occlusion, which seems nearly insurmountable using 
traditional methods. The issue of optimally accurate detection persists 
for fruits like apples, which exist in completely unstructured and com­
plex environments with unstable lighting conditions [101]. Indeed, the 
performance of traditional methods cannot meet the requirements of 
automated apple harvesting. On the other hand, the high cost of labor 
has increased the demand for robotic apple harvesting systems among 
farmers, gardeners, stakeholders, and researchers. These systems are 
expected to outperform traditional ML methods in fruit detection ac­
curacy. Consequently, fruit recognition tasks are increasingly being 
tackled using DL-based methods. Fig. 12 illustrates the trend in apple 
detection research conducted over the past two decades. Year by year, 
the graph witnesses the steady ascent of apple detection publications, 
serving as a testament to the pursuit of knowledge within the scientific 
community. It is noteworthy that the rise of deep learning and the 
development of convolutional neural networks, which excelled in image 
classification task in 2012, caused a great attention for applications 
beyond the computer science. The availability of powerful processing 
devices, like Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), aided this achievement. 
However, it took some time for this approach to be applied in other 
sectors, including agriculture, as more datasets were needed to train 
these networks. By 2018, we began to see the application of deep 
learning in various agricultural tasks, with a significant increase in 
research studies focused on fruit detection compared to previous years. 
As a result, a larger portion of the studies conducted on apple detection 
over the past two decades is related to the emergence of deep learning,

The DL approach has emerged to fully automate operations, enabling 
the computer to process signals (such as images and videos) and perform 
feature extraction and classification without the need for any manual 
intervention, which is the main distinction between ML and DL [102]. 
Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that the brightest 
place where DL has shown its success is in the vision domain where it 
deals with images [103,104,105]. These high-performance results are 
achieved by extracting low to high-level features across multiple layers 
within deep neural networks. In other words, different features are 
extracted at various layers of the network. For instance, in the initial 
layers (surface layers) of the network, low-level features like edges, 
corners, edges, and colors are recognized. As we move to the middle 
layers, more relatively complex patterns start to emerge, and in the 
deeper layers, the network detects the overall shape of objects. Fig. 13
shows these representations. Consequently, the main characteristic that 
distinguishes DL algorithms from traditional approaches is their ability 
to learn features in a layer-by-layer fashion. In the meantime, image 
recognition and object detection have emerged as the primary areas of 
research that extensively employ DL algorithms. This is especially 
evident in scenarios involving complex scenes and unstable lighting 
conditions within the scene and environment. Detecting objects such as 
apples from trees in a highly unstructured and complex garden envi­
ronment necessitates the utilization of novel and advanced methods, 
such as DL-based models. Hence, this section comprehensively reviews 
various DL methods and algorithms employed in the identification and 
detection of apples.
The initiation and evolution of DL-based models relied on networks 
called CNNs, renowned for their remarkable success in object recogni­
tion with fruits detection included. CNNs are considered the core of DL 
models as they perform the essential task of feature extraction. The work 
is performed through a specific process involving blocks such as 
convolution, pooling, dropout, and specialized activation functions, 
each assigned a specific task. Fig. 14 shows the representation of a CNN 
process.
4.1. Pre-processing and image segmentation
In many detection tasks, pre-processing and segmentation steps are 
often required before object detection can be performed effectively. 
Indeed, removing the background paves the way to detect the objects, 
particularly when dealing with image datasets captured in complex and 
dynamic environments. Due to this close relationship between seg­
mentation and background removal, some segmentation models such as 
U-Net, Faster R-CNN, and SegNet have been developed in conjunction 
with detection models to detect fruits. For instance, background sub­
traction using depth information as an initial binary segmentation was 
performed in an apple detection task [106]. The effect of removing the 
background trees and using the Fast R-CNN deep model improved the 
detection accuracy by 2.5 %. According to their report, the additional 
operation did not significantly affect the processing speed. Therefore, it 
is recommended for an apple-picking robot. Numerous studies have 
been conducted in connection with data pre- and post-processing, uti­
lizing various operations such as adjusting brightness levels, random 
erasing, mixup data augmentation, increasing contrast, and normalizing 
the histogram. The effectiveness of DL-based models has significantly 
improved thanks to these methods [107,108,20].

divided into three categories: two-stage, one-stage, and hybrid detection 
models. Two-stage object detection models, also called two-stage

4.2.1. Two-stage apple detectors
4.2.1.1. RCNN and SPPNet. RCNN and SPPNet were among the first DL- 
based object detection models that were developed based on stage-wise 
training. The RoIs proposed by a region proposal network (RPN) 
represent a trainable task, in contrast to traditional search methods such 
as window sliding or selective search [112]. This feature is the most 
critical aspect of R-CNNs, as it contributes to their computational effi­
ciency [113,114]. Studies have shown that RCNN has improved mean 
Average Precision (mAP) by 30 % compared to traditional object 
detection algorithms, becoming the basis for future two-stage object 
detection models such as Fast-RCNN, Faster RCNN, and Mask RCNN 
[115]. Despite significant advancements, RCNN’s detection speed is 
slow because of redundant feature computations on a high number of 
overlapped proposals. SPPNeT was introduced to overcome this chal­
lenge by adding a spatial pyramid pooling (SPP) layer to the network 
which reduced the time for generating region proposals and made them 
ready for classification in the second part of the network [116]. Despite 
the relative improvement in detection speed, the accuracy of the model 
is limited because the convolutional layer in SPPNet can only be 
fine-tuned by adjusting its fully connected layer. The structure of RCNNs 
and SPPNet is similar, and due to their multi-stage training (no 
end-to-end training), which leads to slow speed; limited extensive 
research has been conducted in the apple detection task with these 
networks.
4.2.1.2. Fast/faster RCNN. Fast RCNN was developed to overcome the 
limitations of RCNN [117]. By allowing the adjustment of the con­
volutional layers and using the effective RPN, the power of this model 
for object detection has been increased, making it a more effective object 
detection algorithm. These changes in the structure of the Fast RCNN 
model led to its overall improvement, but despite this, according to the 
method of finding region proposals, which are 2000 in number, it is still 
the same as the previous two models (RCNN and SPPNet) and this is an 
obstacle to reducing the overall speed of the Fast RCNN [112]. These 
changes in the structure of the Fast RCNN model resulted in overall 
improvement; however, based on the method of generating 2000 region 
proposals, it remains unchanged from the previous two models (RCNN 
and SPPNet), which hinders the overall speed reduction of the Fast 
RCNN. Most of the time needed to detect objects is spent generating 
region proposals because of the selection search method, which searches 
through all of the region proposals in the image and maps them into the 
feature maps [118]. Fig. 16 shows RCCN-based object detection models 
as two-stage object detectors.
In 2015, researchers introduced the Faster RCNN model, which 
replaced the selection search method with the RPN to directly generate 
region proposals [120]. The task of RPN is assigning the region pro­
posals to the deep neural networks, sharing the extracted features with 
the backbone, and delivering it to the detector part of the model. By 
embedding RPN, the speed and accuracy of the Faster RCNN model 
improved significantly, and it became the first two-stage network to 
achieve real-time applications.
Faster-RCNN algorithm was used to detect and harvest apples from 
vertical-fruiting-wall trees [106]. The dataset they used included RGB 
and depth images captured with the Kinect V2 camera, allowing access 
to point cloud information. Due to the dense and complex environment 
and background in the garden, the researchers removed the background 
and any unwanted information, aiming to enhance the performance of 
their model. To achieve this, depth images and a thresholding method 
were applied to remove the background and non-target trees. Hence, 
two sets of image datasets were processed: original and foreground RGB 
images. The RCNN model with two pre-trained networks as backbones 
(ZFNet and VGG16), was employed for apple detection. When consid­
ering the average precision as the evaluation metric, the RCNN model 
achieved a value of 89.3 % with the VGG16 network, surpassing the 
performance of the RCNN model with the ZFNet network. They 
emphasized that their results on background-free images were signifi­
cantly better than those on original images with foreground, suggesting 
their suitability for apple picking robots with near real-time data pro­
cessing requirements. Although the authors in this study provided 
valuable insights into the application of DL for automatic apple detec­
tion, they only considered one class (apples) without taking into account 
the possibility of multiple classes, such as clustered and occluded apples. 
The other limitation was that the research included a small dataset of

instance segmentation of objects was also feasible by adding a branch for 
generating segmentation masks. Hence, it has widespread applications 
in different sectors such as medical imaging, and agriculture [131,132,

4.2.2. One-stage apple detectors
Two-stage detectors prioritize increasing accuracy due to their 
processing paradigm, which in turn reduces their performance speed. 
Hence, they do not apply to real-time applications because the speed 
parameter is a major concern for fruit-harvesting robots. Therefore, one 
of the main motivations for the development of one-stage detector 
models has been to reduce the computational complexity to improve 
their speed to be more efficient, despite the lower accuracy of their 
initial models [69]. However, later versions of basic YOLO (You Only 
Look Once) were created, resulting in a significant improvement in 
accuracy.
4.2.2.1. YOLO. The YOLO model [129] was the first one-stage model 
for the object detection. The overall operation of YOLO is to patchify the 
input image into a grid of patches and investigate the presence of objects 
in each patch. The size and aspect ratio of objects are effectively taken 
into consideration by YOLOv1. The loss function used in YOLO is the 
sum of squared errors, which differs from two-stage object detectors that 
calculate separate losses for bounding box regression and classification. 
The evolution process of the Yolo model since its inception can be seen 
in Fig. 17. The performance of the models is evaluated using criteria 
such as precision, recall, and mean average precision at Intersection over 
Union (IoU) thresholds of 0.50 and 0.5–95, referred to as mAP50 and 
mAP50–95, respectively. In addition, the evolution from YOLOv1 to the 
latest iterations demonstrates how each version builds on the strengths 
and weaknesses of its predecessors to achieve significant improvements 
in accuracy, speed, and usability. These advancements reinforce the 
superiority of newer models in both academic research and practical 
applications, making the YOLO family as a leading choice for object 
detection tasks across various fields. The problem with the basic YOLO is 
that when the size of the objects inside the images is smaller than a 
certain limit or when they are densely packed together, the original 
YOLO algorithm faces a significant challenge so that its performance is 
lowered [136]. Therefore, newer versions were presented to address 
these problems.

4.2.2.2. YOLOv3. The performance of YOLOv3 was significantly 
improved by replacing the Darknet-19 backbone feature detector (used 
in basic YOLO and YOLOv2) with Darknet-53, introducing better 
methods for handling objects of various sizes, and adding residual blocks 
[137]. This enhanced version of YOLOv3 has been used by researchers in 
fruit detection studies.
Researchers investigated the task of detecting apples in images taken 
during three stages of growth: young, expanding, and ripe apples [16]. 
To this end, an improved YOLOv3 model was proposed using the 
DensNet method to optimize feature layers with low resolution in the 
original YOLOv3 and enhance their propagation. The study compared 
the results of the improved YOLOv3 model with state-of-the-art object 
detectors including YOLOv2, original YOLO, and Faster R-CNN. Ac­
cording to the authors’ report, their model is more efficient than all the 
models that were compared so that it reached the highest values of 
F1-score and IoU. Considering the huge number of augmented datasets, 
the model’s performance could probably be improved by optimizing the 
method of data augmentation technique. A similar study was conducted 
to detect apples, except that the standard YOLO model was used without 
changing its architecture. Kuznetsova et al. [107] also studied the 
impact of pre- and post-processing operations on the overall perfor­
mance level. Their conclusion demonstrated the positive impact of 
pre-processing operations, such as contrast increasing, applying slight 
blur, and thickening the borders, as well as post-processing operations 
like removing uneven-sized apples (beyond a specific threshold) and 
replacing specific pixels to remove the negative effects of leaf shadows, 
glare, and damaged apples and enhance the performance of the YOLOv3 
model. However, this research did not discuss computational resources 
for implementing the proposed approach.
4.2.2.3. YOLOv4. Real-time applications became increasingly vital in 
the YOLO evolutionary process. A relatively major performance 
improvement has been achieved by replacing Darknet-53 with 
CSPDarknet-53 in the backbone layer of the YOLOv4 model. Addition­
ally, in the model’s neck component, a PANet has been incorporated to 
gather and consolidate feature maps [138]. However, the head part of 
the model which is responsible for final detection and classification, 
remains unchanged as YOLOv3.
Zhang et al. [139] presented a lightweight variation of YOLOv4, 
designed specifically for apple detection tasks in the challenging orchard 
environment. This improved version addresses the complexities associ­
ated with detecting small, medium, and large-sized apples. GhostNet, a 
lightweight feature extractor, was utilized for the backbone of the 
network. In the case of the neck and head of YOLOv4, the authors 
introduced depth-wise separable convolution to reconstruct them. Also, 
to increase the accuracy of the model in detecting small and medium 
targets, an attention module was added to the GhostNet. Finally, the 
improved YOLOv4 developed by the authors indicated an increased 3.5 
% mAP. In this study, there is insufficient discussion regarding the 
computational requirements necessary for accurately assessing its 
applicability in real-world conditions. Another research with a similar 
structure entitled G-YOLO based on lightweight YOLOv4 was carried out 
for apple detection [140]. The results of this study indicate the effec­
tiveness of the method used and its suggestion for embedding in apple 
picking robots. A study similar to these two was conducted by Wu et al. 
[20], which obtained comparable results to the studies mentioned 
above.
4.2.2.4. YOLOv5. By integrating the anchor box selection process, the 
model YOLOv4 was modified and evolved into a new version called 
YOLOv5 within the YOLO family. Consequently, YOLOv5 can auto­
matically determine which anchor boxes are suitable for each dataset it 
is considering and use them during training (GitHub repository). Studies 
have shown the success of this new model in different object detection 
tasks [141].
The use of lightweight networks is interesting due to resource limi­
tations in fruit detection by robots. Therefore, a lightweight apple 
detection model named YOLOv5-PRE was designed for rapid fruit 
detection in the complex orchard environment [142]. To minimize 
model size, ShuffleNet and GhostNet networks were incorporated into 
the feature extractor section (backbone) of the model. To improve 
detection accuracy in the head section, attention mechanisms such as 
Coordinate Attention (CA) and Convolutional Block Attention Module 
(CBAM) were implemented. According to the findings of this study, the 
mAP was 94 %. Additionally, the average detection time for one image 
on GPU and CPU was recorded as 27.0 ms and 172.3 ms, respectively.

was conducted to overcome these challenges and improve the results of

F1-score, respectively. In another study, a YOLOv7 structure with an 
attention mechanism for detecting and counting apples in the complex 
environment of an orchard was introduced [147]. These attention 
modules increased the accuracy of the model in detection. On the other 
hand, a multi-objective tracking technique using a Kalman filter and 
motion trajectory prediction was effective in the final counting of ap­
ples. According to the author’s findings, the Yolov7-CA model out­
performed Yolov7 alone by achieving 91.3 % mAP and 0.85 F1-score. 
Valuable results have been obtained in this study; however, computa­
tional considerations have not been discussed. In some other studies, the 
improved model of YOLO7 has been used in the apple detection task 
[148,149].
The latest state-of-the-art model in the YOLO algorithm series, which 
is well-known for its object detection and classification capabilities is 
YOLOv8. However, few studies were found for the apple detection task 
at the time of writing this review study. Likely, studies utilizing this 
model for the detection and localization of apples will be published in 
2024.
4.2.2.6. SSD. Single-shot Multibox (SSD) is a strong target detection 
model in the DL domain [150]. SSD focuses on applying a series of small 
convolutional kernels to the feature maps to predict the category scores 
in each anchor box [151]. By utilizing feature maps of different reso­
lutions at different scales, separating predictions by aspect ratio 
(width-to-height ratio), and making the procedure an end-to-end 
training, high detection accuracy is achieved. The main difference be­
tween SSD and YOLO (as well as other one-stage detectors) is that the 
SSD model performs the task of detecting objects with different scales in 
various layers of the network, whereas, in other models, this is only done 
in the top layers [69].
Liang et al. [152] utilized SSD to detect apples in a dataset collected 
in a complex orchard. In addition to the SSD, the Faster R-CNN model 
was also used to compare the results and determine which framework is 
more suitable for apple detection. Two variants of SSD including SSD300 
and SDD500 (input size of 300 × 300 and 500 × 500 pixels respectively) 
were applied to the dataset. For evaluation purposes, two metrics were 
taken into consideration - precision and average time. The evaluation 
results revealed that the SSD500 model obtained the highest precision 
value (94.6 %). In terms of speed, it was recognized as the fastest al­
gorithm, requiring only 0.023 s to process each SSD300 image. These 
values were 91.1 % and 0.07 s for the Faster R-CNN model. They 
concluded that the SSD model algorithm outperformed Faster R-CNN in 
detecting apples.

decrease in the performance of detector models. Therefore, researchers 
introduced the RetinaNet model in which the focal loss function 
replaced the conventional cross-entropy loss function [153]. This loss 
function prioritizes hard examples (misclassified samples) from minor­
ity classes to prevent the predominance of easy samples during training.

4.2.3. Hybrid apple detectors
4.2.3.1. Multi-stage. While introducing two-stage and one-stage object 
detection models, certain models were developed using a combined 
approach. These models, multi-stage models, harness the advantages of 
both groups and have been developed to enhance efficiency and 
generalizability [111]. In a study, researchers proposed a Fruit-MTCNN 
(F-MTCNN) model that combines elements of both one and two-stage 
object detection models to detect different fruits such as apples, or­
anges, and strawberries [156]. This model consisted of three stages: 
object classification and bounding box regression, non-maximum sup­
pression, and refinement of candidate bounding boxes using a Refine 
Network (RNet) and an Output Network (ONet). Each stage has been 
responsible for a part of the task of detection. The evaluation results of 
the F-MTCNN have shown that it is satisfactory. From the computational 
point of view, the average time for detection was reported to be 80 s for 
100 images, which is nearly real-time. One of the limitations of this 
study is the dataset used. Part of the images were taken from the 
ImageNet dataset, and their number increased through data augmen­
tation techniques. However, it should be noted that this approach differs 
somewhat from real-world conditions. Additionally, another limitation 
is the absence of a comparison with state-of-the-art models, despite its 
achievement of satisfactory results.
Wang and He [157] used the structure of Mask RCNN and added a 
layer as an attention mechanism for the apple instance segmentation 
task. The objective of using the attention module was to improve the 
extracted features in a separate stage. To this end, they used the fusion of 
deformable convolution and the transformer attention module to allow 
the neural network to focus on relevant elements while suppressing 
irrelevant elements in the input images. They achieved values above 95 
% for precision, recall, and F1-score measures. Additionally, a run time 
of 0.25 s per image was obtained. The authors reported that their

4.2.3.2. Hybrid YOLO. Some versions of YOLO model (e.g. YOLOv3) 
can interact with different algorithms to improve their performance. For 
instance, in a study, researchers incorporated a clustering method based 
on the Rao-1 algorithm and ResNet50 to enhance the performance of 
YOLOv3 for detecting damaged apples [160]. The output of the Rao-1 
algorithm was optimized anchor box sizes used in YOLOv3. Addition­
ally, the necessary features were derived from the input images using a 
pre-trained ResNet50 network. By comparing the results of the 
improved YOLOv3 model with the two renowned YOLO and Fast R-CNN 
models, it was discovered that the mean mAP value is higher than the 
aforementioned models. Accordingly, the authors recommended its 
utilization for smart agriculture. Despite the good results of this 
research, one limitation is that the dataset used for training and evalu­
ation was collected from the Internet (normal and damaged apples) and 
did not include damage-level information and images of different types 
of damage, which limits the generalizability of the suggested technique 
to identify different types of damage in apples. Studies show that hybrid 
models are increasingly preferred for object detection tasks [161]. In 
addition, models that can fuse features have great potential in object 
detection. A good example of these models is the Feature Pyramid 
Network (FPN), which has indicated satisfactory outputs [162,163]. 
However, it is worth noting that the relatively small number of studies 
on hybrid object detection methods specifically for apple detection can 
be attributed to several factors, including the specificity of the task, the 
complexity of hybrid models, the demand for real-time applications, and 
a focus on cutting-edge approaches like transformers or attention 
mechanisms.
5. Apple localization
In computer vision, object localization involves detecting instances 
of a given category within images and drawing a bounding box around 
them [164]. Paying attention to the definitions and differences of the 
three concepts of classification, localization, and detection is important 
in understanding vision tasks and their goals. In classification, a sample 
image is given a label by referring to existing training data that already 
has labeled images. Localization refers to the process of identifying the 
location of a particular object category in an image so that the image 
contains one object (or instances of that category) as a target object. To 
enclose the target object and draw a bounding box, the location of that 
object must be determined. While, object detection encompasses a 
broader and more complex concept than localization, identifying the 
locations of multiple objects, even with different categories within an 
image rather than a specific object. This task includes classifying and 
assigning a class label to each object and drawing a bounding box 
around them. Fig. 18 visualizes these concepts in line with the current 
study. It is worth noting that fruit sizing and load estimation, yield 
estimation and forecasting, and robotic harvesting are the main appli­
cations of apple localization [29]. The approaches used for apple 
localization are reviewed in the following subsections.
Over the past decade, numerous studies have been conducted on the 
apple localization task [165,166,167,168,169]. These studies often 
utilize different image sources to accurately locate the target objects. 
However, these approaches often encountered challenges in practice 
due to reasons such as low performance, expensive equipment costs, and 
application complexity. In general, apple localization tasks are per­
formed using three overall methods including depth data, point cloud 
data, and other advanced methods.
5.1. Localization using depth data
Earlier in the discussion of stereo vision, it was mentioned that one of 
the primary applications of image depth information is to calculate the 
distance from the target to the camera location. This allows for esti­
mating the center point of objects to grab target objects (apple fruit in 
our case study) by the end-effector of a picking robot. This depth in­
formation is either directly provided by the imaging camera sensor (i.e., 
RGB-D cameras) or obtained later through methods like geometric 
transformations. By calculating the 3D coordinates of the objects, their 
positions can be determined. Researchers used depth images taken by 
Kinect v2 to localize apple fruit [170]. They extracted gradient field 
information from the depth images to create an isobath map and 
gradient projection. Then, the gradient information, which was initially 
in 3D, was projected onto a 2D plane. Finally, the 2D gradient was 
rotated clockwise by 90◦, and the vorticity centers were estimated as the 
centers of apples. Their study did not focus on apple detection but 
instead on the positioning of apples. They achieved a recognition rate of 
96.61 %, which is a promising result in fruit localization. In another 
study, an RGB-D camera was used to capture both RGB color images and 
depth images of apples on trees [18]. The authors utilized depth images 
to calculate the Z component of the targets. In other words, they com­
bined the 2D coordinates from the RGB image with depth information to 
obtain the 3D coordinates of the apple harvesting points. Fig. 19 illus­
trates the process of connecting and converting point coordinates

In a 3D space, point clouds represent an object’s external surface as a

identifiers and localizers of objects. This is achieved by analyzing the 
arrangement, distribution, and unique properties of each point within 
the point cloud. Point clouds are often obtained either through different 
sensing devices such as LIDAR, stereo cameras, and RGB-D cameras or 
by computations using geometric methods, stereo matching, etc. An 
instance of a point cloud generated from RGB-D images by Li et al.,

removing the background with appropriate quality. Nguyen et al. [172]

6. Challenges, possible solutions, and future perspectives
The reviewed studies all indicate that the substantial obstacles in 
apple detection and localization are occlusion, extreme variations in 
light conditions, and clustering. These factors significantly impede the 
adoption of intelligent fruit harvesting systems, such as robots, and pose 
serious limitations in practical and commercial applications. Currently, 
the performance, generalizability, and reliability of existing methods are 
not at an acceptable level for real-world applications. For this reason, 
most apple fruit harvesting tasks are still dependent on human labor. 
Hence, ongoing research is being conducted to further improve the 
robust algorithms used for detecting and identifying fruits.
6.1. Apple detection
6.1.1. Occlusion
The challenge of occlusion is a fundamental one not only in outdoor 
images but also in indoor images, which are probably not as unstruc­
tured and complex as the natural environments that have affected the 
performance of all state-of-the-art detection algorithms. Occlusion oc­
curs in various ratios, scales, and locations making it difficult to handle 
[179]. In this regard, the occlusion phenomenon can generally be 
reduced or overcome by acting on the physical and algorithm sides. 
Physical and hardware operations as proactive approaches can be 
implemented in different ways. As a first step, a few human-based ac­
tions can be taken in the orchards. Human assistance is possible in two 
ways: the first involves orchard maintenance, where operations such as 
pruning and thinning tree foliage are performed to minimize occlusion. 
In other words, by removing the excess canopy of trees, apples are more 
visible to imaging devices and can be detected more efficiently [180,
181]. This is one area where training can play an important role [182]. 
Indeed, by teaching orchard workers how to properly prune and main­
tain trees in orchards, a way to minimize occlusions is possible.
Another possible challenge that directly affects the occlusion in apple 
trees is the attention to the architecture of the orchard. [183,184]. When 
the layout and design of the orchard are not appropriate in terms of 
distance between trees, crowding, apple variety, and growth pattern, 
occlusion will intensify and make it difficult to handle. Therefore, the 
second way to reduce obstruction is to design orchards purposefully and 
innovatively. However, planting trees too sparse is not economically 
beneficial for growers. These human-based solutions can improve fruit 
detection accuracy and efficiency even with a level of partial occlusion. 
As a result of these operations, the orchard is prepared for robotic 
harvesting in the future such that all apple fruits are detected and har­
vested efficiently. It is worth mentioning that despite the design of 
machine-friendly modern orchards, there is still a possibility that some 
fruits are hidden behind the leaves and branches or located in the 
highest part of tree canopies, which makes it difficult to detect by 
ground-based robots. In such scenarios, drones equipped with cameras 
and end-effectors can be utilized because of their flexibility.
In the algorithm sector, efforts should be directed to strengthen de­
tector models, especially for small objects such as apple fruits that are 
occluded. To develop models that are sufficiently robust against occlu­
sion, it is first necessary to determine the degree and nature of the oc­
clusion. For this purpose, large-scale datasets for occluded apples are 
required. In the absence of large and rich benchmarks, apple detection 
models are less generalizable with artificial or augmented data. 
Furthermore, algorithms such as contextual object detection [185] and 
vision transformers [19] are improving their ability to detect complex 
occlusion patterns around objects. In natural environments such as or­
chards, these advanced methods can be used to identify and detect target 
objects.
6.1.2. Unstable illumination
In apple orchards, the environment is naturally complex, and light 
conditions change, which makes accurate and robust detections chal­
lenging. Natural and environmental factors such as weather, the length 
of the day, and foliage occlusion cause these unstable light conditions. 
Shadow, variable contrast, and local reflection and highlights of target 
objects are among the results of unstable illumination that negatively 
affect detector models [186]. One of the solutions offered by researchers 
to mitigate the impact of light change and compensate for its uniformity 
is to use artificial light or a special imaging source during image col­
lecting [187]. As a result, the even distribution of light ensures that 
apple detection algorithms perform better with these lighting devices. 
However, the cost of installing and maintaining these lighting systems 
should be considered. In addition, environmental factors should also be 
considered when assessing energy consumption, power supply, and 
optimal light location in orchards. It was discussed earlier how some 
image sources (such as thermal image data) are not sensitive to ambient 
light, which allows sensor fusion to be used to compensate for the lim­
itations of each sensor. It is worth noting that if access to datasets with 
varying brightness and contrast conditions is available, they should be 
utilized for training DL models; otherwise, resorting to generating syn­
thetic data or applying random brightness changes during the learning 
process becomes inevitable. The most effective approach is often to 
combine these methods since it allows the strengths of one technique to 
counterbalance the weaknesses of another, leading to an overall more 
efficient apple detection algorithm. To implement these methods suc­
cessfully in real-world orchards, it is necessary to conduct numerous 
tests and resolve any potential bugs. As a result, the detection system 
will perform well under all expected lighting conditions.

cluster of apples as neighboring occurs when they grow very close to 
each other for various reasons, and this makes detector models 
encounter challenges in distinguishing between individual apples [188,

two previous challenges of occlusion and changing light conditions, so

generate complex light and shadow patterns, which may make algo­
rithms difficult to handle. Ensuring the collection of high-quality 
training data, including accurately labeled images of clustered apples, 
to facilitate the development of robust detection algorithms.
A precise investigation of the outputs of the models reveals that 
clustered samples reduce the performance of the apple detector models 
by adversely affecting their precision and recall as the key metrics. 
Because clustered apples lead to missing apples (false negatives) and 
multiple apples being counted as one apple (false positives), precision 
and recall are reduced, resulting in inaccurate yield estimation. In 
addition, significant errors in counting apples can lead to the allocation 
of resources for harvesting and packing being inefficient because of 
incorrect yield estimation.
One of the solutions that can be implemented to reduce the challenge 
of clustered apples in apple detection algorithms is to improve data 
annotation and data augmentation. In this regard, preparing detailed 
annotations of each fruit in a cluster can aid in training models that can 
identify individual apples. Also, to make the models robust, various 
scenarios of clustered apples can be simulated by applying data 
augmentation techniques on the train image datasets. The depth infor­
mation in RGB-D images can also help identify apples placed at different 
depths in terms of distance. It should be noted, however, that natural 
and uncontrolled environments, such as orchards, can sometimes pro­
duce severe noise in depth images. A more practical approach can be the 
3D reconstruction of apples by advanced models to separate individual 
apples according to the calculated volume for each fruit. Managing the 
cluster challenge can also be helped by monitoring the growth pattern of 
cluster apples over time. Finally, to deal with clusters, harvesting robots 
with two end-effectors to grab multiple apples instead of a single apple is 
a possible solution.
6.2. Apple localization
It is not unusual for the challenges associated with detecting apples 
to also be present in apple localization because of their close relation­
ship. This implies that the challenges of occlusion, illumination changes, 
and clustering apples apply also to the task of apple localization. When 
the apples are occluded, for instance, the 3D coordinates cannot be 
accurately determined because of occlusion. Other aspects of apple 
localization challenges are discussed. The biggest challenge with depth 
images as one of the apple localization methods is their sensitivity to 
sunlight and reflective surfaces which leads to noisy images. To address 
this problem, the use of high-resolution depth sensors, high-quality 
cameras, and advanced scanning techniques can enhance the ability to 
detect small objects such as apples, particularly from a distance [189]. If 
necessary, the quality of depth and point cloud data can be enhanced by 
implementing robust pre-processing operations, such as noise filtering. 
It is also important to ensure that the algorithms are highly efficient, 
allowing them to be implemented on lower-power hardware and utilized 
in real-time conditions by the harvesting robots. This is particularly true 
for point cloud data, as it requires comparatively more processing time. 
The final point to note is that point clouds may be sparse and there may 
be gaps in which no data is captured, especially when close far distances 
and occlusions are present. In such scenarios, to reduce the effects of 
sparsity, techniques such as up-sampling, interpolation, and using 
additional sensor data such as RGB information can be useful.
6.3. Future perspectives
Examining current research and considering the route of future 
research in the area of apple detection and localization using vision- 
based approaches shows several promising perspectives for apple fruit 
robotic harvesting. In the era of active artificial intelligence and the 
development of imaging sensors, integrating advanced DL algorithms 
and multi-channel images such as RGB-D holds significant potential for 
enhancing the performance and robustness of apple detection technol­
ogies. This approach exploits the benefits of both 2D and 3D information 
that are crucial for robotic harvesting systems, enabling accurate 
detection and localization of apples even in complex and dynamic en­
vironments. The fruit detection and localization can be strengthened by 
using multiple cameras to take images of fruit trees to gain more in­
formation on occluded apples. Consequently, the location of an occluded 
apple can be determined from every RGB-D image frame in the multi- 
view sensing platform. Researchers could focus on the development of 
state-of-the-art object detection models specifically for apple fruit 
detection tasks based on RGB-D images or even multispectral images in 
orchards. To ensure robustness and generalizability, the models should 
be trained with enough annotated datasets, including data on growth 
stages and apple varieties. It is crucial to note that future studies should 
integrate these vision approaches with robotic platforms for real-time 
decision-making and other related operations. As a part of this, chal­
lenges such as sensor fusion, motion planning, and human-robot inter­
action need to be addressed to ensure harmonious integration of apple 
detection and localization systems with autonomous robotic harvesters. 
Despite the advances in hardware (e.g., NVIDIA) for real-time image and 
video processing using AI, the challenges mentioned in real-time 
detection of apples persist.
To address the challenge of balancing high-precision apple detection 
with economic constraints faced by small-scale growers, it is essential to 
explore cost-effective strategies and alternatives. One potential 
approach is to optimize existing sensor systems through software ad­
vancements, such as machine learning algorithms, which can enhance 
detection accuracy without requiring expensive hardware upgrades. 
Additionally, leveraging edge computing and IoT technologies can aid in 
processing data locally to reduce the need for high-end centralized 
computing resources. Another strategy involves adopting lightweight, 
modular sensor systems that can be customized to specific needs, 
allowing growers to make incremental investments as their operations 
expand. Collaborating or partnering with technology providers to 
customize and subsidize solutions can further enhance the accessibility 
of advanced systems for small-scale growers.
It is noteworthy that human-robot collaboration in apple harvesting 
is being integrated to enhance research by addressing concerns related 
to labor displacement. This approach allows for robots to assist rather 
than replace human workers, improving efficiency by handling repeti­
tive tasks while skilled activities are managed by humans. As a result, 
labor shortages are alleviated, and opportunities for skill development 
arise. Proposed case studies of successful collaborations should be 
included, along with suggestions for future research on optimizing 
human-robot interactions. This will present a comprehensive perspec­
tive on the future of agricultural labor and the potential for a synergistic 
relationship between humans and robots.
Regarding apple localization, the 3D approach and the incorporation 
of spatial awareness into robotic systems offer a promising opportunity 
for enhancing the localization and harvesting of apples. On one hand, 
the technologies of point clouds, binocular localization, and stereo 
vision are crucial for reconstructing 3D scenes and calculating the 3D 
coordinates of target objects, which is crucial for accurate apple locali­
zation and effective robot arm manipulation. The widespread adoption 
of these technologies is expected to be facilitated by the improvement of 
hardware capabilities and affordability, as well as the evolution of DL 
models. On the other hand, future research could lead to the fusion of 3D 
spatial data with rich feature information obtained from other imaging

sensors. In this way, researchers can develop comprehensive orchard 
models that significantly enhance the robot’s ability to navigate, detect,

occluded or clustered. The progress in edge computing and the creation

mediate processing tasks on-board, rather than relying on cloud

for the implementation of robotic harvesting systems that are more

7. Summary
During the past two decades, the machine vision approach and the 
implementation of artificial intelligence technologies, especially ML and 
DL, in agriculture have received significant attention. This article pre­
sented a comprehensive review of image analysis, computer vision, and 
artificial intelligence algorithms for the task of detecting and locating 
apples. First, various image sources used in vision-based methods were 
reviewed. Following that, algorithms based on DL methods were dis­
cussed and their advantages and disadvantages in apple detection tasks 
were examined. These algorithms include one-stage, two-stage, and 
hybrid methods. The issue of apple localization was then examined and a 
review of studies in this area was conducted. The apple localization 
methods employed depth information and point cloud processing. The 
strengths and weaknesses of each method were discussed in detail.
A discussion of the fundamental challenges for apple detection and 
localization in the orchard environment, such as the occlusion of apples, 
light changes, difficulty in detecting clustered fruits, and existing solu­
tions to these challenges was presented. Finally, future directions of 
research were discussed for overcoming these challenges, including the 
integration of advanced DL and multi-vision techniques, considering the 
possibility of implementing them in real-time for apple-picking robots. 
Furthermore, the application of 3D vision for comprehensive under­
standing of the complex orchard environments, and accurate calculation 
of fruit coordinates through point cloud and depth information, are 
suggested as future research fields. Finally, the ethical, environmental, 
and economic issues should be considered too in the development of 
robotic apple harvesters.
