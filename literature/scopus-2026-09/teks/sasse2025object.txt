Abstract—In precision viticulture, deep learning and computer
vision have been increasingly employed to automate grape cluster
counting, a crucial task for yield estimation and farm man-
agement. However, existing methods fail to address the unique
challenges posed by the pergola vine training system — a widely
used but understudied method, particularly in Brazil’s Northeast,
where viticulture plays a key role in economic and social
development. To address these limitations, we propose a novel,
lightweight approach that combines object detection (YOLOv8)
and tracking (ByteTrack) to count grape clusters in handheld
smartphone videos captured under diverse environmental con-
ditions. Unlike previous works, our technique is applied to the
unique layout of the pergola system, where occlusions, perspective
variations, and irregular cluster distributions complicate auto-
mated counting. We evaluate our method on real-world vineyard
footage, achieving 75.1% mAP@50 and a 69.9% F1-score for
cluster detection, with a final cluster count only 27% above the
actual value—establishing the first baseline for this challenging
task. By enabling cost-effective, accessible yield estimation, our
work contributes to improving efficiency and equity in viticul-
ture. We publicly release our implementation to encourage fur-
ther research and practical adoption (https://github.com/artsasse/
pergola-grape-count/blob/main/grape cluster count.ipynb).

I. INTRODUCTION
The integration of computer vision and deep learning
is revolutionizing various fields. In the agricultural sector,
these innovations have boosted productivity by leveraging
data collected through sensors and vision-based systems to
optimize decision-making. The fusion of these data sources
enables a more precise analysis of farm activities, integrating
information from different sources to enhance monitoring and
agricultural management [1].
In grape cultivation, these new technologies have been
widely employed to predict yield, detect pests, automate
processes with robots, map vineyard areas, among other tasks.

Yield prediction, in particular, is a key aspect of precision
viticulture and can involve several subtasks, such as grape clus-
ter and berry counting, grape classification based on quality
and ripeness, and crop weight estimation. Grape cluster and
berry counting are essential for estimating vineyard produc-
tivity and optimizing harvest planning. Traditional methods,
based on manual sampling, are labor-intensive and prone to
errors, making computer vision and deep learning approaches
promising alternatives. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
[2], and multiple object trackers (MOTs) [3] have been used to
identify and count fruits in images and videos, often surpassing
conventional methods in accuracy and scalability [4].
In addition to the inherent inaccuracies of manual counting,
the limited frequency of data collection can undermine the
reliability of yield estimates, particularly given the dynamic
nature of agricultural environments. For instance, factors such
as early ripening, extreme weather conditions, or damage
caused by pests and strong winds can lead to the premature
loss of grape clusters. Without data freshness [5], farmers may
rely on outdated estimates, resulting in suboptimal decisions
regarding irrigation, harvesting, and commercial distribution.
Automating cluster counting through efficient computational
methods based on computer vision and deep learning would
enable more frequent monitoring of vineyard production, al-
lowing real-time strategic adjustments.
In this context, we propose, implement, and validate a
novel technique for automatically counting grape clusters,
specifically designed for the pergola vine training system. This
system, predominantly used in vineyards across Brazil’s South
and Northeast regions [6], [7], presents a unique structural
layout that introduces challenges in image analysis not ad-
dressed in previous studies. Given that grape production in
Northeastern Brazil has been linked to more equitable income
distribution [8], and that the pergola system demands greater
labor and costs compared to alternative training methods [7],
bridging this research gap represents a valuable step toward
fostering social and economic benefits in the region. The main
contributions of this research are:
• A technique to automatically count grape clusters in RGB
videos of pergola system vineyards, a previously unex-

II. PROBLEM DEFINITION AND HYPOTHESES
Several computational methods for grape counting have
been proposed in the literature, but many of them exhibit
significant limitations. Some techniques estimate only the
number of individual berries rather than entire grape clusters,
which may not be suitable for certain agricultural applications.
Others are designed exclusively for static images, making
them unsuitable for full-video analysis, or require specialized
recording equipment, limiting accessibility for many farmers.
Drone-based counting methods, for example, also face chal-
lenges in vineyards with dense foliage, where cluster visibility
is obstructed, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
Besides these technical challenges, none of these methods
have been specifically tested in vineyards using the pergola
system [9], which features an arched canopy structure, as
illustrated in Fig. 2. The pergola system is the most widely
used vine training method in vineyards across Brazil’s South
and Northeast regions [6], [7]. Unlike traditional row-based
vineyards, where grape clusters form a relatively uniform
”wall” at a consistent distance from the camera, pergola
vineyards create a tunnel-like structure where clusters are dis-
tributed at varying depths. This results in significant variations
in their apparent size and the occurrence of occlusion, due to
perspective, making analysis more challenging compared to
previous studies, where cameras could be positioned perpen-
dicularly to the clusters for a more uniform view. Moreover,
some of the previous techniques are only described in research
papers, with no publicly available implementations, making it
difficult to reproduce results and adapt them to different data.
In the studied vineyard, the grape cluster counting is cur-
rently performed manually, by workers who walk along the
vineyard rows, annotating the quantity of clusters in each of
them. We decided to proceed with a counting technique based
on processing videos captured in a similar way, by someone
traversing these vineyard rows. This way, we expect our work
to be easily applied to real-world farms and extended to be
used as an application in a smartphone, for example, without
disrupting much of the traditional process, but ensuring a
higher efficiency.

A. Approach Selection
Based on Farjon et al.’s survey about counting methods in
agriculture using deep learning [4], we highlight four possible
approaches for this task:
1) Direct counting and weakly supervised methods, which
estimate the number of fruits without requiring individ-
ual identification;
2) Object detection combined with 2D tracking, where a
detector identifies fruits in each video frame, and a
tracker associates these detections over time to prevent
duplicate counting;
3) Object detection combined with 3D mapping, which
reconstructs a three-dimensional scene to enhance count-
ing accuracy;
4) Density estimation, which generates a heat map of the
target objects in each image and try to regress the
number of objects from that representation.
The density estimation approach, despite bringing a good
performance and demanding less in terms of image annotation
efforts, has been mainly applied to static images, especially
those from Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), which can not
be used in our case, since the dense foliage at the top of the
pergola vineyards prevents the capture of useful images for
counting. Actually this protection against the sunlight is one

of the main reasons the pergola training system is used in the
Northeast of Brazil [6].
The direct counting and weakly supervised methods suffer
from a similar problem, since they are direct towards the
analysis of static images, not keeping track of repeated fruits
along successive frames.
The object detection methods are best suited for our case,
since we are using videos instead of static images. Between the
2D and the 3D tracking approaches, we chose to implement
the former since it is computationally cheaper than the latter
in general [10] and we found more detailed examples of its
use in counting tasks, which could serve as inspiration for
our own technique. Many 3D mapping techniques also require
the usage of specific hardware, like stereo cameras or depth
sensors, demanding more than just RGB videos, which are our
only resources in this study.
More specifically, our selected approach first employs a
convolutional deep learning-based detection method to learn
and predict bounding boxes for grape clusters in the frames for
each captured video. Then based on the predicted bounding
boxes for the successive frames, a tracking algorithm assigns
identifiers to unique grape clusters, keeping track of their
trajectories. The effective counting is based on the total
number of identifiers assigned during the video reproduction.
Even though the pergola system videos differ considerably
in layout, perspective and occlusion, to the data captured
in other studies, as the the camera moves deeper into the
vineyard, each grape cluster still define a trajectory, which can
be uniquely tracked. To evaluate our technique with pergola-
specific data, we collected video footage from a table grape
farm located in the S˜ao Francisco Valley, a key hub for
irrigated fruit production in Brazil’s Northeast. The technique’s
implementation and evaluation and the dataset captured are
described in detail in Section IV.
Following Dasarathy’s characterization [11], we can identify
three main fusion steps in our technique:
1) Object Detection: Data Input - Feature Output (DaI
- FeO), through the feature extraction from pixels by
CNNs, and Feature Input - Decision Output (FeI - DeO),
through the prediction of object locations in each video
frame.
2) Object Tracking: Temporal Decision Input - Decision
Output (DeI - DeO), through the association of trajecto-
ries between detected objects in successive video frames.
3) Object Counting: Decision Input - Decision Output
(DeI - DeO), through the processing of the raw number
of tracks to compute the quantity of grape clusters.
B. Research Questions and Metrics
Since our objective is to propose a cluster counting tech-
nique adequate to pergola vineyards, we defined two research
questions to guide our evaluation, one focused on each com-
ponent of our method (detection and tracking):
• RQ1: How well does the technique detect clusters in
static images?
• RQ2: How well does the technique count clusters in
videos?
Farjon et al.’s survey [4] highlights the importance of
measuring the performance of object detection and counting
separately, whose metrics are used to evaluate each of our re-
search questions, respectively. For RQ1, we use the following
metrics:
• Mean Average Precision (mAP): since the number of
grape clusters (positives) is much smaller than the number
of other objects (negatives) in an image, the model
accuracy would not be a balanced metric for evaluation.
Precision and Recall are metrics that take this imbalance
into account, since the former measures the fraction of
detections that correspond to true grape clusters, and the
latter measures the fraction of true grape clusters correctly
detected. However, each of them only indicate the level of
overestimation and underestimation respectively. Average
Precision (AP) helps measuring both problems since it
averages the Precision over all possible levels of Recall.
Different levels of Recall are achieved by varying the
confidence threshold of the detections [12]. In practice,
this threshold should be optimized for each application,
considering which error needs to be avoided the most
(grape clusters going undetected or mistaken detections)
to optimize counting, but the average of possible preci-
sions gives a balanced perspective. mAP measures the
average of APs for each class, but in our case we only
have one class (grape clusters). We specifically use mAP
for an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 50%
(mAP@50), which is a standard value [4]. IoU is defined
by the fraction of I (area of intersection between the
predicted bounding box and the ground-truth bounding
box) in relation to U (union between the bounding
boxes’ areas). Whenever the IoU is above the determined
threshold we consider the predicted bounding box as a
correct detection. The AP (which is the mAP in our
case) also directly corresponds to the area under the curve
(AUC) of the recall-precision curve.
• F1-Score: Similarly to mAP, F1-Score also takes both
Precision and Recall into account by calculating their
harmonic mean [13]. The difference is that the F1-Score
is calculated for an individual confidence threshold and
typically, in practice, we aim at the confidence threshold
which gives us the best F1-Score. We fix the confidence
threshold in the default value used by YOLOv8, the tool
we used for object detection, which will be described in
detail in Section IV. Since different values of precision
and recall can yield the same score, we also report these
two metrics as a complement to the F1-Score.
For RQ2, regarding counting performance, we use the
following metric:
• Mean Absolute Error (MAE): it is the absolute dif-
ference between the predicted number of grape clusters
and the ground-truth quantity [14]. Since we only have
one class, we don’t need to average that error over

neural networks, we chose the absolute value instead of

III. RELATED WORK
Shen et al. [10] proposed a grape cluster counting approach
using YOLOv5, a 2D tracking algorithm, and a counting
line. However, their study was conducted in vineyards with
a conventional training system, where the camera moves
perpendicularly to the vine rows. Additionally, their source
code and dataset were not publicly released, limiting repro-
ducibility and direct comparison. Despite these differences,
their methodology is the most similar to ours.
Santos et al. [15] introduced the Wine Grape Instance
Segmentation Dataset (WGISD) and explored YOLO-based
detection. They employed a 3D reconstruction technique to
estimate cluster positions from motion across multiple images.
While they demonstrated the integration of detection and 3D
mapping in videos, their study does not quantitatively evaluate
counting accuracy.
Jaramillo et al. [16] also applied a 2D tracking-by-detection
approach for grape cluster counting using smartphone cameras.
Their method relied on artificial illumination for nighttime
image capture, and the camera was stabilized by a gimbal and
mounted on an all-terrain vehicle (ATV) moving at a constant
speed. Instead of YOLO, they used Faster R-CNN [17] for
detection.
Ciarfuglia et al. [18] combined detection, segmentation, and
both 2D and 3D tracking for grape cluster counting. They
worked in a tendone vineyard, a trellis system similar to
pergola, and used a semi-supervised annotation approach to
improve counting accuracy.
Beyond grapes, tracking-by-detection strategies have been
used for other fruits with similar growth patterns, such as
passion fruit [19], [20] and tomatoes [21]. However, these
studies were conducted under controlled conditions with cam-
eras positioned perpendicularly to the crop rows.
Unlike previous works, our study specifically addresses
pergola-trained vineyards, where grape clusters are distributed
along a tunnel-like canopy, making conventional perpendicu-
lar imaging impractical. This results in significant variations
in cluster distance and scale, increasing the complexity of
detection and tracking. Furthermore, while many studies use
fixed camera setups on drones or vehicles, our videos were
recorded by people under variable daytime conditions, adding
further real-world challenges. Our approach also stands out for
its accessibility, as it relies solely on standard RGB images
without the need for specialized sensors. Lastly, we ensure
reproducibility by providing our code and model configura-
tions, while leveraging state-of-the-art detection (YOLOv8)
and tracking (ByteTrack) techniques to address this underex-
plored scenario.

IV. METHODOLOGY
First we present the data used for training and evaluation,
details of the technique implementation and then we delve into
how the technique was tested to answer the research questions.
A. Dataset
Our original dataset consisted of 445 manually annotated
frames on the Roboflow platform, extracted from four videos
recorded in the vineyard under study. Three of these videos
were captured in the late afternoon and one in the morning. All
recordings were made using the 26 mm camera of an iPhone
XR, by the same research team member, ensuring some level
of consistency in capture conditions.
To enhance dataset diversity and improve the model’s gen-
eralization ability, we applied data augmentation techniques,
including rotation, horizontal flipping, brightness and contrast
adjustments, and slight saturation modifications. This process
expanded the dataset to 1,069 frames. The data was split into
training, validation, and test sets following a 88%, 8%, and
4% ratio, respectively.
It is important to note that the original images were captured
at 1080p resolution with a 16:9 aspect ratio, but were resized

B. Implementation
Based on our literature review, we decided to implement a
technique comprised of two components: object detection and
object tracking. For the detection step, we selected YOLOv8
[22], which had not yet been explored in previous grape
cluster counting studies. The model was trained and assessed
using manually annotated frames extracted from video footage
collected in the vineyard. For the tracking step, we employed
ByteTrack [23], an out-of-the-box tracking algorithm that
builds upon the widely used SORT [24] and DeepSORT [25].
YOLOv8 was selected for this project due to its advanced
architecture, which provides high accuracy, speed, and ro-
bustness in complex scenarios, with even greater inference
efficiency compared to previous versions [22]. YOLO [26] was
the first deep learning one-stage detector, being characterized
by its speed, with each version bringing new improvements
in accuracy and efficiency [12]. One of the key advantages
of YOLOv8 specifically is its enhanced feature pyramid, a
mechanism that leverages multiple levels of abstraction during
processing. This improvement enables the model to detect
objects of various sizes with greater accuracy, which is essen-
tial for counting grape clusters that can vary significantly in
scale and position within the video. Additionally, this approach
minimizes information loss and enhances the model’s ability
to handle variations in lighting, occlusions, and perspective
changes. In this study, we specifically used YOLOv8s, an
optimized version that maintains detection quality while sig-
nificantly reducing model size. This choice was driven by the
intent to run inference on low-power edge devices, ensuring
a balance between performance and computational efficiency
for real-world field applications. We use the detection-oriented
version of YOLOv8s, which was pre-trained on the MS-COCO
dataset [27] to speed up convergence of the model.
ByteTrack [23] is a multi-object tracking tool that employs
the Kalman Filter [28] and the Hungarian Algorithm [29]
to associate detected objects and predicted trajectories in a
video. Its unique feature is how it leverages low-probability
detections, instead of focusing only on high-probability ones,
like previous techniques. First, high-confidence detections are
assigned to existing trajectories, ensuring accurate matching.
Then, low-confidence detections are considered and linked
to previously unmatched trajectories, preserving object tracks
that might otherwise be discarded due to temporary detec-
tion failures. This strategy significantly improves tracking
robustness and continuity, particularly in challenging scenar-
ios where occlusion and object appearance variations impact
detection accuracy. We chose ByteTrack because it offers
competitive performance on Multiple Object Tracking (MOT)
tasks, while keeping state-of-the-art or similar efficiency, mea-
sured by frames per second (FPS), in many of these tasks,
even against more recent techniques [30]–[32]. Moreover,
ByteTrack can be used as an out-of-the-box general purpose
tool through the Supervision1 library, making it easy to apply
to new contexts, without needing to adapt code from specific
experiments or benchmarks.
Our development pipeline was based on the Roboflow
tutorial2 on object counting in videos. In addition to the official
YOLOv8 version provided by Ultralytics, we extensively used
the Roboflow Supervision library, which streamlined both the
integration with ByteTrack and the generation of annotated
videos with object counts.
C. Experiment
In this study, our focus is to propose a grape cluster
counting technique based on object detection and tracking and
to evaluate its performance on data collected from vineyards
that follow the pergola training system. To synthesize our
objective, we define the following research questions:
• RQ1: How well does the technique detect clusters in static
images?
• RQ2: How well does the technique count clusters in
videos?
To answer these research questions, we will rely on metrics
discussed in the survey by [4]:
• For object detection performance (RQ1), we will use
Mean Average Precision (mAP) and F1-Score. Especially
we use mAP@50, which evaluates detection performance
at a 50% Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold. The
IoU measures the overlap between the predicted bounding
boxes and the ground truth, and mAP@50 specifically
considers detections where IoU exceeds 0.5. This en-
sures that we account for both localization accuracy and
detection confidence in our evaluation. The precision is
averaged over the precision values for all confidence
levels in the detections. The F1-Score is computed for the

We trained YOLOv8s for 25 epochs, using the default
hyperparameters provided by the package. We also trained the
same model again for 75 epochs, a YOLOv5s [33] model for

a preliminary exploration with public but unofficial datasets

data to achieve better results. Similarly, we adopted the default
settings for ByteTrack.
For the line-based counting method, we arbitrarily defined
the position of the counting line based on the heatmap of
detections in the training images, as shown in Fig. 6. This
is certainly a hyperparameter that can be optimized in future
studies to improve accuracy and robustness.
The detection stage was evaluated using a validation set of
88 images. The counting stage was assessed using a 5-second
video from a separate recording, which was not part of the
training set. The ground-truth cluster count was determined
through detailed video annotation using the CVAT3 software.
Due to the high cost of training and annotation, only one
execution of the experiment was performed. However, future
studies should incorporate multiple repetitions to ensure the
statistical significance of the results. The experiment was
conducted in an environment with a dedicated GPU (NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 4060).
V. RESULTS
Regarding RQ1 (detection performance), our technique
achieved the following results for the evaluated metrics:
• Precision: 71%
• Recall: 68.9%
• F1-Score: 69.9%
• mAP@50: 75.1%
A direct comparison with previous works is not possible,
as these studies focused on vineyard systems and camera
perspectives very different from ours. Still, we expected to
face a challenging scenario for detection due to the image
perspective, which causes grape clusters in the background to
appear smaller, increasing the likelihood of missed detections.
Additionally, in our dataset, dense foliage, combined with
perspective variations and the presence of people in the videos,
contributes to partial occlusions of grape clusters, further
complicating the detection task.

Relying solely on the number of unique identifiers associ-
ated with tracks leads to an overestimation of grape clusters,
indicating the need for a fusion mechanism to improve accu-
racy. On the other hand, the counting line—a technique used
in previous works [10] to prevent double counting—results in
a severe underestimation, as many grape clusters never cross
the line due to the vineyard’s perspective. This highlights the
necessity of a more adaptive counting strategy that accounts
for the spatial distribution of clusters within the video. We
hypothesize that using multiple counting lines and merging
their results could yield a more precise estimate. For now,
despite its overestimation, counting unique identifiers appears
to be the most practical approach given our specific camera
position.

VI. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Regarding data, we have additional videos available from
the studied farm, which could be used to further train and eval-
uate the proposed technique. Additionally, we could leverage
publicly available datasets, such as the one by Santos et al.
(2020) [15], to enhance detection performance.

Concerning the models, we could explore more thoroughly
the hyperparameter optimization for both detection and count-
ing models. Also some studies incorporate auxiliary mecha-
nisms such as attention [36] or pruning [10], which could be
tested in combination with our approach.
For better counting evaluation, it would be ideal to have
more videos with reliable ground-truth counts and to find a
more precise method for manual counting. We could leverage
the expertise of field workers who perform manual grape
cluster counting as part of their daily tasks.
As stated in [3], multi-source sensor data fusion is the
next frontier for tracking techniques, which could also be
explored in our case. Since the videos were captured manually
in an irregular terrain there are non-uniform movements of
the camera which could be taken into account to avoid track
association mistakes. A related problem for YOLO-based
detection in drone-captured aerial images has been addressed
in [37]. In other fusion level, in [38], the authors estimate 3D
positions from 2D RGB images to enhance pedestrian tracking,
which could be adapted to our case. Finally, with the presence
of multiple sensors in modern smartphones, we could use the
same equipment and resources to employ stereo or RGB-D
imaging, which has already been done in other contexts [39].
VII. CONCLUSION
In this study, we proposed and evaluated a grape cluster
counting technique for videos of vineyards using the pergola
training system. Our method is based on modern object de-
tection and tracking techniques and was trained and evaluated
on video data collected from a grape farm in the Brazilian
Semi-Arid region.
As expected, the pergola system layout, the environmental
conditions and the video perspective created a challenging
context for detection and counting. Nonetheless, our technique
achieved significant results for both tasks, representing the first
result of its kind for pergola system vineyards.
Finally, this study represents a promising step toward the
application of computer vision techniques, particularly object
detection and tracking methods, for automated grape cluster
counting in vineyards where the pergola system is predominant
— both in the Brazilian Northeast and South regions and in
other parts of the world.
