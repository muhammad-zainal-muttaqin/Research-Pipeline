Abstract 
Crop a>ribute detection, as a key component of intelligent agricultural harvesting ma-
chinery, plays a crucial role in harvesting eﬃciency, loss reduction, and autonomous op-
eration control. Compared with existing reviews on artiﬁcial intelligence and sensing 
technologies in agriculture, this review focuses on crop a>ribute detection scenarios ori-
ented toward the intelligent decision-making and control requirements of agricultural 
harvesting machinery. It mainly analyzes crop a>ributes that aﬀect harvesting operations, 
as well as the sensors and algorithms involved in detecting these a>ributes, and further 
clariﬁes the relationship between detection methods and control decisions in agricultural 
harvesting machinery. For grain crops, the key a>ributes relevant to harvesting operations 
include plant height, plant density, spike number, crop lodging, canopy structure, and 
crop position. For fruit and vegetable crops, the key a>ributes relevant to harvesting op-
erations include maturity, position, and quality. From the perspectives of multi-source 
data acquisition, data analysis, and a>ribute detection algorithms, the key technologies in 
the ﬁeld of crop a>ribute detection are systematically summarized and analyzed, includ-
ing sensors used in crop a>ribute detection, such as RGB, spectral, near-infrared, and Li-
DAR sensors, as well as data analysis and recognition approaches, such as image classiﬁ-
cation, object detection, and point cloud analysis. The complexity of ﬁeld environments 
and the dynamics of machine operation are analyzed, highlighting the technical bo>le-
necks of current detection systems in environmental adaptability, real-time responsive-
ness, and resistance to interference. To address these challenges, feasible optimization di-
rections were proposed, including multi-sensor fusion, weakly supervised learning, and 
few-shot learning. This review aims to provide systematic references and theoretical sup-
port for the coordinated development of crop detection and control decision-making in 
intelligent agricultural harvesting systems.

studies are important for promoting intelligent, unmanned, and precise harvesting oper-

Research on crop detection in agricultural harvesting has developed multiple tech-
nical routes, mainly including visible-light image-based feature analysis, multi-source 
sensor perception, and data-driven intelligent recognition methods[5]. Early methods 
based on RGB imaging and handcrafted features were easily aﬀected by changes in envi-
ronmental conditions[6]. The application of sensors such as LiDAR, near-infrared, and 
hyperspectral imaging has enabled the perception of crop three-dimensional structures 
and physiological characteristics[7]. Combined with deep learning methods such as object 
detection (e.g., YOLO) and instance segmentation (e.g., Mask R-CNN), detection accuracy 
and robustness have been signiﬁcantly improved, and detection tasks have expanded to 
include spike counting, lodging detection, disease localization, and quality grading[8]. For 
example, a Mask R-CNN-based system achieved 77.76% spike recognition, 98.81% disease 
detection accuracy, and 77.19% disease severity prediction accuracy in wheat[9]. These 
detection results can provide quantitative information for harvesting control, such as ad-
justing feed rate based on crop density and spike distribution estimation, conducting 
header height adjustment and path correction according to lodging-area identiﬁcation, 
and performing picking-trajectory planning and end-eﬀector control based on fruit target 
localization. In addition, detection accuracy and inference speed jointly determine 
whether perception results can be converted into control actions in time under continuous 
harvesting or robotic picking rhythms. To address challenges such as complex ﬁeld con-
ditions, real-time response requirements, and limited onboard computing resources, more 
researchers have begun to introduce methods such as multi-source data fusion, weakly 
supervised learning, and edge computing. These methods improve the robustness of crop 
a>ribute detection and the feasibility of ﬁeld deployment through multimodal infor-
mation complementarity, reduced annotation costs, and edge-side real-time inference[10]. 
Although current crop a>ribute detection technologies for agricultural harvesting 
scenarios have achieved promising results in some applications, their use for practical 
harvesting decision-making and control still faces multiple challenges, including complex 
weather conditions, variable illumination, dust, vibration, occlusion, and real-time re-
sponse requirements, as summarized below: (1) factors such as variable ﬁeld lighting, 
plant occlusion, and terrain undulation can easily introduce data noise and reduce detec-
tion accuracy[11]. Beyond general environmental complexity, extreme ﬁeld conditions 
(such as rain, nigh>ime operation, and dusty environments) can further degrade sensor 
data quality, thereby signiﬁcantly aﬀecting the stability of crop a>ribute detection. (2) me-
chanical vibrations and posture changes during high-speed operations interfere with sen-
sor data acquisition, causing errors in recognition and positioning. In addition, strict real-
time requirements in harvesting limit the application of high-precision methods due to 
inference delays[12]. In response to these challenges, more researchers have begun to fo-
cus on adaptive multi-sensor fusion, lightweight models, and motion compensation, 
which may provide potential solutions for improving the robustness, adaptability, and 
real-time performance of detection systems in the future. 
Based on the above background, this paper conducts a systematic review of key is-
sues in crop a>ribute detection during agricultural harvesting operations. Unlike existing 
reviews that mainly focus on general agricultural monitoring or single detection tasks, 
this study constructs an application-oriented analytical framework tailored to harvesting 
scenarios by linking crop a>ribute requirements, sensing technologies, and detection 
methods under the dynamic and real-time constraints of harvesting operations, highlight-
ing the relationship between perception and agricultural machinery control. Within this 
framework, crop a>ributes are regarded as perception targets, sensors provide the basis 
for data acquisition, and detection methods serve as tools for information interpretation.

2. Methodology 
This review adopted a structured literature review framework to improve the com-
prehensiveness and transparency of the collected studies. The literature search mainly 
covered relevant studies published between 2015 and 2025, and the ﬁnal retrieval was 
completed in April 2026. This time range was selected to cover recent advances in agricul-
tural harvesting machinery, crop a>ribute detection, sensor applications, and intelligent 
perception methods, while ensuring the timeliness and representativeness of the reviewed 
literature. Relevant publications were retrieved from multiple mainstream academic da-
tabases, including Web of Science, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, and 
Google Scholar, supplemented by forward and backward tracing of key references. Dur-
ing retrieval, Web of Science and Scopus were mainly used to identify peer-reviewed jour-
nal articles and conference papers, IEEE Xplore was used to obtain engineering and tech-
nical studies, ScienceDirect and SpringerLink were used to retrieve full-text journal arti-
cles, and Google Scholar and reference tracing were used to identify additional relevant 
studies. Because some publications were indexed in more than one database, duplicate 
records were removed during screening. Both Chinese and English publications were con-
sidered, with a focus on representative studies related to agricultural harvesting scenarios, 
crop a>ribute detection tasks, sensing technologies, and practical application value.

cluding “crop a>ribute detection”, “agricultural harvesting”, “harvesting machinery”, 
“machine vision”, “LiDAR”, “multispectral imaging”, “near-infrared”, “hyperspectral 
imaging”, “object detection”, “image segmentation”, “crop quality detection”, “crop mon-
itoring”, “plant phenotyping”, “yield estimation”, “precision agriculture”, “smart

3. Crop Aributes for Agricultural Harvesting Machinery 
In crop harvesting, a>ribute detection directly aﬀects operational strategies and op-
erational outcomes. The key factors inﬂuencing harvesting operations diﬀer across sce-
narios. For ﬁeld grain crops, factors such as crop density, lodging status, canopy distribu-
tion, and row structure can aﬀect header height, forward speed, feed rate, path alignment, 
and machine load. For fruit and vegetable crops, factors such as maturity status, fruit po-
sition, external quality, canopy occlusion, and branch distribution can inﬂuence harvest 
timing, target selection, picking trajectory, end-eﬀector motion, and grading decisions. 
Therefore, in this review, crop a>ributes are not discussed as independent biological in-
dicators, but are regarded as perception variables directly corresponding to speciﬁc har-
vesting decisions. Based on these harvesting operation requirements, this chapter focuses 
on a>ributes of grain crops, including plant height, lodging status, density, spike number, 
canopy structure, and crop position, as well as a>ributes of fruit and vegetable crops, in-
cluding maturity, spatial position, and quality, and analyzes their application value in 
improving harvesting eﬃciency, reducing losses, maintaining operational stability, and 
supporting machinery decision-making. 
3.1. Aributes of Grain Crops 
Grain crops such as rice, wheat, rapeseed, and maize are usually continuously and 
densely distributed in the ﬁeld, and their a>ribute detection diﬀers from perception tasks 
targeting individual objects in fruit and vegetable harvesting. Under conditions such as 
high-density planting and lodging, real-time perception of a>ributes such as plant height, 
density, spike number, lodging status, canopy structure, and spatial position is important 
for the control of agricultural harvesting machinery. This section focuses on the main 
grain crop a>ributes related to harvesting operations and their corresponding detection 
methods. 
3.1.1. Plant Height 
Plant height is a basic morphological indicator describing the vertical dimension of 
crop growth. In accordance with relevant national standards, it is measured from the base 
of the main stem at ground level to the upper end of the spike, with awns excluded from 
the measurement. This parameter is commonly used to characterize crop development,

biomass accumulation, and population structure[38]. Plant height varies with cultivar 
characteristics, growth stage, planting density, and environmental conditions, resulting in 
noticeable diﬀerences among crop species and growth periods. 
In combine harvesting, crop height information is closely related to the se>ing of 
working components, especially the vertical position of the header. If the header is raised 
beyond the eﬀective cu>ing range, part of the spike layer may remain unharvested, re-
sulting in direct grain loss. Conversely, an overly low cu>ing position increases the 
amount of straw entering the threshing and cleaning units, which may raise the machine 
load and reduce threshing performance[39]. Therefore, continuous height sensing in ﬁelds 
with non-uniform growth or lodging conditions can provide a basis for coordinated reg-
ulation of forward speed, drum rotation speed, and feeding amount. Such parameter ad-
justment helps maintain a stable material ﬂow through the harvester and reduces losses 
and grain damage during operation. In practical combine harvesting operations, plant 
height information needs to be updated fast enough to follow local changes in crop height 
along the travel path, so that the header can be adjusted before the cu>er reaches the cor-
responding crop region. When height estimation is unstable under uneven terrain or 
lodged canopy conditions, delayed or inaccurate control may lead to missed cu>ing, ex-
cessive straw intake, or increased feeding load. 
Plant height detection mainly relies on non-contact 3D sensing technologies, includ-
ing LiDAR, RGB-D systems, structured light cameras, and UAV-based imaging. These 
devices provide spatial crop information for height estimation and modeling. Common 
methods include maximum height diﬀerence, average height within local windows, and 
main stem ﬁ>ing for structured crops such as corn and sorghum[40]. For example, stereo 
3D imaging combined with tilt compensation reduced measurement error from 15.6 to 3.9 
cm through coordinate transformation, as illustrated in Figure 3[41]. Centimeter-level 
measurement accuracy is generally suﬃcient to support header height adjustment and 
basic operational control during harvesting. However, under complex uneven terrain, its 
eﬀectiveness in preventing header damage is still inﬂuenced by terrain variation and 
equipment tolerance. Therefore, adaptive control strategies are required to ensure opera-
tional safety.

In ﬁeld applications, crop height estimation often requires preprocessing to reduce 
terrain-related noise and improve measurement stability. A common strategy is to derive 
crop height maps (CHMs) by comparing the crop surface represented by digital surface 
models (DSMs) with the ground reference described by digital terrain models (DTMs). 
Point cloud denoising, ﬁltering, and local surface reconstruction can then be used to ob-
tain more reliable height information. In addition, temporal image sequences and seman-
tic crop masks can be integrated to maintain spatial–temporal consistency and to distin-
guish crop pixels from background regions during height estimation. For example, Li 
combined UAV-based multisource sensing, 3D reconstruction, and machine learning to 
estimate winter wheat biomass with high accuracy[42]. 
In summary, plant height is an important basis for adjusting harvesting machinery 
parameters, directly aﬀecting path planning and control strategies. Methods based on 3D 
reconstruction and structural feature extraction have become important perception ap-
proaches in intelligent harvesting systems. By providing height information directly re-
lated to header height adjustment and feed rate control, these sensing and analysis meth-
ods help transform plant height from a crop growth indicator into an operational param-
eter for harvesting control. However, terrain variation, canopy occlusion, and mechanical 
vibration still introduce measurement uncertainty and reduce system robustness, while 
trade-oﬀs among accuracy, cost, and real-time performance across sensing modalities 
limit stable application. 
3.1.2. Plant Density 
Plant density describes the population size of crops within a given ﬁeld area and is 
generally quantiﬁed by the number of plants per square meter. In agricultural standards, 
this a>ribute is usually reported using the unit “plants·m−2”. It provides a basic indicator 
for characterizing planting pa>erns, crop population development, and yield-related ﬁeld 
conditions. In practice, it can be measured using ﬁxed quadrat counting or row and plant 
spacing estimation methods[43]. Accurate knowledge of ﬁeld crop density distribution is 
fundamental for operation path planning, feed control, and harvesting strategy formula-
tion in mechanical harvesting. Operation eﬃciency, machinery load management, and 
yield estimation are greatly inﬂuenced by such information. 
Plant density information provides an important basis for regulating harvester oper-
ating parameters. When crop stands are dense, the amount of material entering the ma-
chine increases, which may cause feeding congestion, insuﬃcient threshing, and greater 
grain damage. In areas with lower plant density, the forward speed can be increased 
within an appropriate range to maintain harvesting eﬃciency and reduce unnecessary 
fuel consumption. Moreover, density distribution is closely associated with lodging ten-
dency, spike spatial arrangement, and nutrient utilization, and can therefore support pre-

3.1.3. Spike Number 
Spike number, referring to the number of spikes per unit area or sample region in 
crops such as wheat and rice, serves as a key indicator for assessing yield potential and 
harvesting complexity. According to agricultural standards, spike number is obtained by 
manually counting within a designated sample area (e.g., 0.5 m × 0.5 m), and the result is 
expressed as “spikes·m−2”[38]. Spike number not only determines the upper limit for yield 
estimation but also provides guidance for controlling the feed rate of combine harvesters, 
adjusting header load, and conﬁguring the threshing system. 
During the harvesting stage, accurately identifying spike number helps intelligent 
agricultural machinery dynamically adjust travel speed and threshing intensity. Accurate 
identiﬁcation of spike number prevents clogging, damage, or grain loss caused by over-
feeding spikes. Additionally, spike distribution aﬀects header path optimization and the 
integrity of harvesting operations. Missing spikes directly result in yield loss, while over-
lapping areas may lead to crop re-harvesting and increased energy consumption. There-
fore, precise perception of spike number is a crucial foundation for ensuring harvesting 
eﬃciency and quality control[49]. In practical operations, robust spike counting under oc-
clusion, overlap, and machine-induced image blur helps identify locally sparse or dense 
grain-bearing regions. This information can support header coverage adjustment and re-
duce missed harvesting or repeated cu>ing in areas with uneven spike distribution. 
Spike detection mainly uses high-resolution imaging (RGB, UAV, LiDAR-based 3D) 
combined with deep-learning detectors (e.g., YOLOv5, Faster R-CNN, and SSD) for auto-
matic recognition and counting. These methods can distinguish spikes from leaves, weeds, 
and other non-target objects in complex backgrounds. An example of wheat spike detec-
tion results is shown in Figure 5, demonstrating good generalizability and accuracy. For 
instance, Zhang proposed an improved YOLOv5s-T method, which eﬃciently detects 
wheat spikes based on a multi-growth-stage dataset. The R2 values for three periods were 
0.97, 0.85, and 0.78, with an overall R2 of 0.87. The model size was reduced by 36.8%, and 
inference eﬃciency was improved[50]. 
Due to occlusion, overlap, and pose variation, 2D image-based spike detection may 
lead to missed or duplicate counts. To address this, methods such as density regression 
(e.g., CSRNet), multi-scale feature extraction, and instance segmentation (e.g., Mask R-

depth information (e.g., RGB-D or point clouds) enables 3D spike localization and multi-

3.1.4. Crop Lodging

degree of crop lodging is typically characterized by the angle between the stem and the 
ground[53]. Lodging not only aﬀects the normal maturation of crops and their ventilation 
and light conditions, but also poses a serious threat to the operational stability and har-
vesting eﬃciency of machinery. 
Crop lodging can cause spikes to be covered by stems and leaves or distributed close

vesting machinery. In lodged ﬁelds, the robustness of lodging-area and lodging-direction 
detection is particularly important because crop posture can change rapidly with local

3.1.6. Crop Position 
Crop position refers to the spatial coordinates of individual crops or crop populations, 
typically including two-dimensional (X, Y) and, in some cases, vertical (Z) information. It 
is a fundamental a>ribute for precision agricultural operations, particularly in combine 
harvesting, autonomous navigation, and zoned management[63]. Crop position detection 
supports spatial mapping as well as tasks such as path planning, header control, row 
alignment, and localized operation adjustment. 
Crop position detection is implemented on two levels in practical applications: At the 
macro scale, “distribution map” modeling analyzes the spatial distribution of crops in the 
ﬁeld to generate density heatmaps, identify vacant areas, and perform regional variability 
analysis. At the micro scale, “row detection” identiﬁes the centerline and boundaries of 
planted rows in crops such as corn, wheat, and rice, thereby supporting autonomous nav-
igation, header alignment, and inter-plant positioning. For harvesting machinery, position 
information needs to be updated with suﬃcient real-time performance to correct naviga-
tion deviations during machine movement and maintain header alignment with crop 
rows. Meanwhile, the robustness of crop-row or crop-position detection under missing 
plants, irregular planting, temporary GNSS instability, or visual occlusion is also im-
portant, as it determines whether perception results can be eﬀectively integrated with 
steering control and local operation planning. 
Distribution maps are generated by fusing GNSS positioning with multi-source re-
mote sensing data. Crop images or point clouds are aligned with geographic coordinates 
to produce heatmaps or vegetation index maps representing crop density, coverage, and 
health. These methods can identify empty sowing, missing seedlings, or re-sowing areas, 
supporting adaptive feed rate control and zonal harvesting strategies[64]. 
Row detection is used in path control for combine harvesters and autonomous plat-
forms, with methods based on images or LiDAR point clouds. Image-based approaches 
use edge detection (e.g., Canny and Hough) to extract row gaps and centerlines, while 
LiDAR methods model crop rows using point cloud density and spatial features, adapting 
to complex lighting and occlusion. For example, a LiDAR-based row centerline detection

3.2.1. Maturity 
For fruit and vegetable crops, maturity is closely associated with the appropriate har-
vest window, postharvest storability, transport tolerance, and commercial grading. In ag-
ricultural standards, maturity is generally described from several perspectives, including 
external appearance, physiological development, and harvest suitability. Its evaluation 
usually involves both external and internal indicators, such as peel color, surface texture, 
water status, soluble solids, starch accumulation, protein content, and acidity[70]. Because 
the ripening process diﬀers among crop species, the criteria used for maturity assessment 
are also crop-speciﬁc. For example, citrus maturity is commonly judged by color develop-
ment and the sugar–acid balance, whereas banana maturity is more closely related to the 
conversion of starch into sugars. 
In automated harvesting, maturity information directly aﬀects harvest timing, target 
selection, and batch operation scheduling. Fruits or vegetables that have not reached the 
required maturity may have low commercial value or poor postharvest quality, whereas 
overmature targets are more susceptible to mechanical damage during picking and trans-
portation. Therefore, maturity detection can provide decision support for selective har-
vesting, picking sequence planning, and pre- or post-harvest grading. In practical deploy-
ment, maturity recognition should meet the real-time requirements of online target selec-
tion, so that the picking system can determine whether a target should be harvested before 
the end-eﬀector approaches it. Meanwhile, the robustness of maturity assessment under 
illumination variation, fruit occlusion, and cultivar diﬀerences is also important, because 
unstable classiﬁcation results may lead to missed harvesting, incorrect picking decisions, 
or inconsistent grading. In addition, maturity detection results need to be integrated with 
picking-path planning, end-eﬀector control, and grading systems to support continuous 
and selective harvesting operations. 
Maturity assessment of fruits and vegetables usually requires the combined use of 
multiple sensing modalities to characterize both surface appearance and internal quality 
changes. Among external traits, peel color provides a direct and easily observable cue for 
ripeness evaluation. During ripening, changes in pigment composition alter the visual ap-
pearance and spectral reﬂectance of the fruit surface. These variations can be described 
using diﬀerent color representations, including RGB, HSV, and Lab spaces. For instance, 
banana ripening can be reﬂected by changes in the Lab b channel during the green-to-
yellow transition, whereas tomato maturity is often evaluated using redness-related indi-
ces. To obtain more detailed spectral information, multispectral and hyperspectral imag-
ing systems are used to record reﬂectance responses over a broad wavelength range from 
the near-ultraviolet to the near-infrared region. Based on these data, indices such as NDVI, 
GCI, and RVI can be calculated to improve the description of ripening-related physiolog-
ical changes[71].

stituents, including water, soluble sugars, starch, organic acids, proteins, and volatile 
compounds. Spectral sensing techniques, especially near-infrared spectroscopy (NIR) and

changes. These methods infer maturity status by capturing wavelength-dependent re-
sponses associated with water, sugars, lipids, and other biochemical components. For ex-

3.2.3. Quality 
Crop quality is one of the key a>ributes that need to be detected during fruit and 
vegetable harvesting, directly determining the market value and storage performance of 
agricultural products. In fruit and vegetable crops, quality mainly refers to whether the 
fruit is healthy and intact, and whether there is any infestation by pests and diseases, me-
chanical damage, or softening and ro>ing. Quality issues typically exhibit diversity, local-
ity, and irregularity, with uneven distribution and visual signs such as subtle color spots, 
deformation, or texture changes. Therefore, the precision and robustness of the perception 
system in automated harvesting systems are subject to higher demands. 
Crop quality is crucial because damaged or diseased fruits reduce market value and 
can promote rot during transportation and storage, causing greater losses. In automated 
harvesting, if these issues are not identiﬁed in time, it will increase sorting pressure and 
interfere with harvesting strategies (such as misjudging the picking points). Furthermore, 
pest and disease distribution often exhibits certain spatiotemporal correlations, and timely 
monitoring can provide feedback for orchard pest and disease control strategies[78]. For 
fruit and vegetable crops, quality a>ributes usually involve multiple aspects, including 
sugar content, ﬁrmness, moisture content, and internal tissue structure, which are often 
indirectly characterized using spectral or near-infrared technologies[79,80]. Signiﬁcant 
diﬀerences exist among crops in terms of quality evaluation criteria and detection meth-
ods. For example, fruit quality detection typically focuses on internal maturity and soluble 
solids content, whereas vegetable quality assessment emphasizes external appearance 
and freshness[81-83]. Therefore, in practical applications, quality detection methods need 
to be speciﬁcally designed according to the crop type, which further increases the com-
plexity of related research. For online harvesting decisions, quality assessment needs to 
provide suﬃciently timely results so that defective, diseased, or unsuitable targets can be 
avoided or separately handled before picking. Meanwhile, the robustness of quality de-
tection under uneven illumination, partial occlusion, and subtle early-stage defects is also 
important, as it determines whether perception results can be eﬀectively integrated with 
selective picking, quality grading, and post-harvest sorting systems. 
Quality perception typically relies on multimodal visual features, with color changes, 
such as dark or discolored areas, and rough edges serving as direct indicators of lesions 
or damage, making RGB images commonly used. However, visible light information can 
sometimes fail to detect early-stage diseases, so Near Infrared (NIR), thermal imaging, 
and hyperspectral imaging (HSI) sensors are gradually being introduced. NIR analyzes 
sugar and moisture changes, thermal imaging detects temperature anomalies, and hyper-
spectral imaging captures lesion reﬂectance across multiple bands, enabling early and 
comprehensive quality assessment[84].

achieving high-precision detection of surface defects, including lesions, rot, and damage.

4.1.1. Optical Camera 
The optical camera is one of the earliest and most widely used sensor types in agri-
cultural crop detection. Reﬂection signals in the red, green, and blue bands are collected 
and analyzed to generate high-resolution color images. These images characterize basic 
crop a>ributes such as appearance, color variation, and morphological structure. As one 
of the core components of intelligent agricultural vision perception systems, optical cam-
eras serve a wide range of applications, including crop identiﬁcation, maturity judgment, 
pest and disease screening, and damage detection. Data acquired from optical cameras 
provides an important basis for harvesting decisions and operational control[90]. 
The most common visible light imaging system is the monocular RGB camera, which 
captures images with a single lens. The simple structure and low cost make the device 
suitable for most image analysis tasks under standard conditions. Image feature extraction 
algorithms such as color histograms, texture directions, and edge shapes, combined with 
classiﬁcation methods including SVM, K-means, and shallow neural networks, are com-
monly applied in crop detection. These methods enable preliminary identiﬁcation of crop 
types, maturity stages, and surface lesions. In fruit and vegetable sorting lines, a monoc-
ular RGB camera can measure fruit size and color for screening, helping the grading sys-
tem eliminate fruits with skin damage or rot[91]. However, this system has limitations 

Agronomy 2026, 16, 973 
26 of 51 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy16100973 
when facing complex tasks like detecting three-dimensional structures and handling oc-
clusions, as it lacks depth information and suﬃcient perception dimensions. 
To enhance spatial perception capabilities, binocular cameras have been applied in 
agriculture. Three-dimensional structural information of crops is reconstructed through 
disparity calculation[92]. Compared to LiDAR, binocular cameras have advantages such 
as smaller size, lower power consumption, and lower cost, making them suitable for crop 
height estimation, fruit localization, and path guidance. Studies have shown that combin-
ing algorithms like SGBM (Semi-Global Matching) and ELAS (Eﬃcient Large-scale Stereo 
Matching) can achieve centimeter-level depth estimation, eﬀectively reconstructing the 
canopy height and spike distribution of crops like corn and wheat. 
In recent years, stereo vision cameras have been gradually integrated with traditional 
monocular systems in agricultural machinery applications. The stereo vision system com-
bines image texture with disparity estimation to generate three-dimensional point cloud 
data with higher resolution, making the system suitable for fruit recognition and target 
grasping tasks. For example, in tomato-picking robots, a stereo camera on the end eﬀector 
enables real-time identiﬁcation of fruit position, orientation, and maturity, while support-
ing path planning to improve picking eﬃciency[93]. In such applications, image acquisi-
tion, disparity calculation, and target localization need to be completed before the end-
eﬀector reaches the fruit; otherwise, delayed visual feedback may lead to inaccurate grasp-
ing points or unnecessary robotic arm adjustments. Therefore, when optical vision sys-
tems are used for online harvesting operations, they need to be closely synchronized with 
robotic motion control. 
In summary, optical cameras, as fundamental sensors for crop appearance percep-
tion, have evolved from early monocular static imaging to multi-camera, stereo, and dy-
namic perception systems[94,95]. Their integration with image recognition algorithms 
and 3D modeling technologies improves the accuracy, adaptability, and real-time perfor-
mance of agricultural harvesting detection[96]. From an engineering perspective, visible-
light vision systems have low cost and convenient integration, making them suitable for 
large-scale deployment[97,98]. However, they are sensitive to illumination changes and 
occlusion, and their stability in complex environments still requires further improvement.

spectral information, have demonstrated advantages in tasks such as crop health moni-

band reduction is important because spectral decisions related to maturity or disease need 
to be completed within the operation cycle of fruit selection, grading, or machinery pa-
rameter adjustment. 
In summary, multispectral and hyperspectral imaging technologies can provide de-
tailed characterization of crop a>ributes by capturing rich spectral information, support-
ing improved detection accuracy and operational decision-making in harvesting tasks 
such as maturity assessment, disease detection, and damage identiﬁcation[103-108]. How-
ever, spectral imaging systems are associated with high equipment cost and data pro-
cessing complexity, requiring trade-oﬀs among detection accuracy, system cost, and pro-
cessing eﬃciency in practical applications[109-112].

4.1.4. LiDAR 
In agricultural harvesting, three-dimensional crop features such as plant height, can-
opy density, and spatial distribution are important for path planning and harvest timing. 
Traditional 2D imaging is limited by occlusion and scale variation, making it diﬃcult to 
capture full spatial information. LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging), an active laser-

in modern intelligent agricultural machinery. 
The working principle of LiDAR is based on emi>ing laser pulses and measuring 
their return time to precisely calculate the distance between the sensor and the target sur-
face. By scanning the crop canopy, LiDAR reconstructs a high-precision 3D point cloud 
model of crop spatial distribution. Structural information such as plant height, canopy 
thickness, and lodging status can be extracted from this model, providing fundamental 
data support for optimizing harvesting strategies[121]. 
In ﬁeld applications, LiDAR-based height measurements can eﬀectively estimate 
crop growth progress and assist in determining the optimal harvesting window. For ex-

precise target positioning for picking robots, improving harvesting success rates[126]. In 
such applications, LiDAR point clouds need to be processed and registered in time, so that 
canopy height, obstacle position, or row-structure information can be converted into 
header adjustment, navigation correction, or picking-path commands during machine 
movement. Therefore, maintaining robustness under vibration, dust, and sparse point-
cloud conditions is critical for reliable system integration.

4.1.5. Other Sensors 
In agricultural harvesting, besides mainstream optical, spectral, near-infrared cam-
eras, and LiDAR, some less common sensors oﬀer unique advantages in speciﬁc environ-
ments or for particular functions. These sensors are an important supplement to the con-
struction of multi-source perception systems. Representative sensors include infrared 
thermography, millimeter-wave radar, and structured light cameras. 
Infrared thermal imagers are passive sensors that form images by detecting heat 
emi>ed by objects, oﬀering advantages such as independence from external light sources 
and usability at night. The surface temperature of crops varies during diﬀerent growth 
stages or health conditions, and thermal imaging can reveal temperature changes related 
to water stress, pests, or the ripening process[128]. For example, the respiration of mature 
fruits and vegetables increases, causing localized heating, while diseased areas often show 
abnormal cold or hot spots. Non-contact and real-time imaging allow deployment on har-
vesting robots and machinery, enabling eﬃcient online monitoring[129]. 
Millimeter-wave radar is an active sensor that measures distance and velocity based 
on millimeter-wave signal reﬂection. Its strong penetration and environmental robustness 
make it suitable for complex ﬁeld conditions such as low light, dust, rain, and fog. During 
high-speed harvesting operations, it supports terrain recognition, obstacle detection, and 
crop boundary identiﬁcation. For example, when a combine harvester turns or approaches 
ﬁeld edges, it can detect large obstacles such as tree stumps or ridges, supporting path 
planning and obstacle avoidance. In addition, its high ranging accuracy and velocity de-
tection enable dynamic monitoring of targets on fruit and vegetable conveyors, improving 
response speed and safety[130]. 
Structured light cameras are active 3D imaging devices that project coded light pat-
terns and analyze their deformation to obtain depth information. Compared to LiDAR, 
they are suitable for mid- to short-range, high-density 3D modeling and provide higher 
resolution and data stability[131]. In fruit and vegetable harvesting, they are used for spa-
tial separation of fruits and branches, and for reconstructing fruit contours and volume to 
support robotic path planning and gripper positioning[132]. They are relatively robust to 
occlusion and irregular shapes, reducing misjudgment during harvesting. Compared to 
stereo vision, they are less sensitive to lighting and do not require complex calibration, 
making them suitable for edge deployment on mobile platforms. 
In summary, infrared thermal cameras, millimeter-wave radar, and structured light 
cameras oﬀer distinct advantages in temperature, distance, and 3D shape perception, 
helping to complement the perceptual limitations of mainstream sensors and expand the 
sensing dimensions of agricultural harvesting systems. However, their practical applica-
tion is still constrained by environmental sensitivity, spatial resolution, measurement 
range, and system integration complexity, while multi-source heterogeneous data align-

4.3.1. Image Segmentation 
Image segmentation is used to partition agricultural images into meaningful regions 
and to delineate crop-related areas at the pixel level. Through this process, detailed spatial 
features, including lesion regions, leaf edges, lodging boundaries, and harvestable areas, 
can be obtained from complex ﬁeld backgrounds. Unlike object detection or a>ribute re-
gression, which mainly provide bounding boxes or numerical estimates, segmentation 
produces ﬁne-grained masks that preserve the shape and boundary information of target 
regions. Therefore, it has been widely applied to tasks such as crop disease analysis, ma-
turity-region delineation, spike or ear contour extraction, and labeling of harvestable re-
gions[13]. 
Traditional segmentation in agricultural images was usually implemented through 
intensity- or color-based threshold selection, together with edge detection operators. 
These methods identify crop-related regions by separating pixels according to predeﬁned 
or automatically determined grayscale and color criteria. Representative approaches in-
clude Otsu thresholding and Canny edge detection, which can produce acceptable results 
when illumination and background conditions are relatively stable[14]. However, their 
performance often deteriorates in ﬁeld scenes because variable light, cast shadows, and 
complex backgrounds can change pixel distributions and weaken object boundaries. To 
improve segmentation robustness, later studies incorporated color-space conversion, such 
as HSV and Lab representations[15], together with morphological post-processing, in-
cluding erosion, dilation, and closing, to reﬁne segmented regions and reduce noise. 
With the increasing availability of pixel-level annotations, supervised semantic seg-
mentation networks have become important tools for crop image analysis. FCN intro-
duced a fully convolutional prediction framework that allows dense category assignment 
for each image pixel, providing an early basis for deep-learning-based agricultural seg-
mentation. On this foundation, U-Net further improved feature recovery by combining 
encoder-derived semantic information with decoder-stage spatial details through skip 
connections. This structure has therefore been widely adopted in tasks such as leaf delin-
eation and lesion-area extraction[16]. In applications involving wheat rust and apple black 
spot, U-Net-derived models have often achieved segmentation accuracies above 90%. 
Instance segmentation extends semantic segmentation by distinguishing diﬀerent 
object instances while also generating their corresponding masks. This capability is useful

https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy16100973 
in dense crop scenes where neighboring plants, fruits, or ears may overlap. Mask R-CNN 
is a representative method of this type and has been applied to tasks such as fruit locali-
zation and ear separation. In corn ear segmentation, for example, its region proposal fol-
lowed by mask prediction strategy can separate adjacent ears more eﬀectively, thereby 
supporting subsequent counting and robotic path planning[17]. However, the region-pro-
posal-based two-stage design also increases computational burden and inference latency, 
which makes real-time deployment on embedded harvesting platforms more diﬃcult. 
Based on typical convolutional neural network methods such as U-Net and Mask R-
CNN, segmentation models have been further developed in terms of architecture design 
and feature modeling. For example, the DeepLab series (e.g., DeepLabv3+) enhances 
multi-scale feature representation by introducing atrous convolution and atrous spatial 
pyramid pooling (ASPP), showing good adaptability in scenarios with signiﬁcant crop 
scale variation. When applied to harvesting machinery, such segmentation results need to 
be generated before the machine reaches the detected lodging or harvestable region, so 
that path correction or header adjustment can be performed in time. However, these meth-
ods still mainly rely on local convolutional features, which may be aﬀected by missing 
local information in cases of severe occlusion. 
In recent years, Transformer-based segmentation models have become an important 
complement to traditional convolutional approaches. Unlike convolution-based methods, 
Transformers establish global feature relationships through self-a>ention mechanisms, 
enabling be>er modeling of long-range dependencies and improved performance in oc-
cluded scenarios. For example, models such as Segmenter and Mask2Former have demon-
strated strong segmentation performance in complex environments. However, these mod-
els generally involve higher computational complexity, and their deployment in real-time 
agricultural harvesting scenarios requires balancing segmentation accuracy and compu-
tational eﬃciency. 
For disease spot detection, image segmentation combined with a>ention mechanisms 
(e.g., SE or CBAM in U-Net) improves recognition of small targets and irregular bounda-
ries under complex conditions. To reduce annotation costs, weakly and semi-supervised 
segmentation methods use image-level labels, point annotations, or pseudo-labels. This 
reduces annotation burden and improves practicality for large-scale disease surveys[18]. 
In harvesting, image segmentation is used for disease detection, lodging segmenta-
tion, ear contour extraction, and fruit integrity assessment. For example, lodging segmen-
tation supports statistical analysis and path adjustment for autonomous machines. In fruit 
picking, instance segmentation separates occluded fruits from background structures, im-
proving robotic positioning and stability[19]. 
In summary, image segmentation has evolved from rule-based methods to high-pre-
cision perception approaches integrating deep semantic modeling, a>ention mechanisms, 
and weakly supervised learning, signiﬁcantly improving the spatial resolution capability 
of intelligent harvesting systems. By performing pixel-level delineation of crop organs, 
lodging areas, disease regions, or fruit boundaries, image segmentation methods can sup-
port local operation decisions such as cu>ing area identiﬁcation, target separation, and 
quality screening. However, these methods still depend on high-quality annotations, are 
sensitive to complex backgrounds and illumination variation, and may degrade when de-
tecting small or irregular targets. Meanwhile, high-resolution segmentation models often 
involve high computational cost, limiting their real-time application in harvesting systems.

multi-stage perception systems, provide basic decision-making information for maturity 
judgment, quality grading, and harvestability assessment. 
4.3.3. Object Detection 
Unlike image classiﬁcation, which assigns a global label to an input image, object 
detection provides both semantic and geometric information for individual targets. It can 
identify the target class and estimate its location and size within the image, making it 
suitable for multi-object perception in harvesting scenes. Accordingly, object detection is 
widely used for fruit counting, ear or spike tracking, lesion localization, and harvest-route 
planning. In systems such as combine harvesters and robotic picking platforms, this ca-
pability supports ﬁeld target recognition and real-time localization under dynamic oper-
ating conditions. 
Before deep-learning-based detectors became dominant, object detection in agricul-
tural images was mainly implemented through window scanning and handcrafted

putational cost. As a result, they were diﬃcult to adapt to harvesting machinery applica-

Deep-learning-based detectors have greatly improved the ability of agricultural vi-

harvesting platforms, providing a practical balance between detection accuracy and pro-
cessing speed[25]. Compared with two-stage detectors, YOLO-type models generally re-
quire fewer computational steps and are more suitable for embedded harvesting devices, 
although their performance may decline under small-target, dense, or heavily occluded 
conditions[26-28]. As localization tasks increasingly require depth and structural infor-
mation, object detection has also been extended from two-dimensional RGB images to 
RGB-D fusion and point-cloud-based perception[29]. Depth cameras, LiDAR, and struc-
tured-light sensors provide geometric cues in addition to color and texture features, sup-
porting more accurate three-dimensional target localization in tasks such as maize ear po-
sitioning and fruit coordinate extraction[30]. For example, RGB-D-based detection can im-

thereby enhancing the stability of multi-target detection in complex harvesting scenes[31].

the results can be directly used for grasping-point selection and collision avoidance dur-

occlusion and reliable synchronization between visual perception and robot control. 
In harvesting scenarios with varying lighting, complex poses, and motion blur, target

through strategies such as data augmentation, a>ention mechanisms, and multi-scale fea-
ture fusion[32]. 
In summary, object detection methods provide important support for real-time per-
ception in crop harvesting and have evolved from traditional approaches to deep learn-
ing-based techniques for multi-target localization, fruit tracking, and spatial coordinate 
extraction. The detected targets and their spatial position information can provide direct 
information for fruit picking, spike counting, obstacle avoidance, and actuator path plan-
ning. However, in complex harvesting environments, occlusion, scale variation, and mo-
tion blur still aﬀect detection accuracy and stability. Meanwhile, one-stage methods oﬀer 
higher eﬃciency but may be limited in dense or small-target scenarios, whereas two-stage 
methods provide higher accuracy but involve greater computational cost, restricting real-
time application.

4.3.4. Point Cloud Analysis 
Point cloud analysis represents crops as sets of spatially distributed three-dimen-
sional points and is used to describe their geometric morphology and ﬁeld distribution. 
Each point records its position in 3D space through X, Y, and Z coordinates and may also 
carry additional information, such as intensity, reﬂectance, or color, depending on the 
sensor type. Such data are commonly acquired using LiDAR, structured-light cameras, or 
RGB-D sensors. Unlike conventional 2D image analysis, point cloud analysis preserves 
depth and spatial relationships, making it more suitable for reconstructing crop structures 
and describing a>ributes such as plant height, canopy architecture, lodging form, popu-
lation density, and crop location[33]. 
Point cloud technology in agriculture has evolved from static 3D reconstruction and 
height estimation to geometric feature extraction and learning-based analysis. Traditional 
methods apply spatial ﬁltering, normal estimation, and curvature analysis to build crop 
models and extract features such as canopy hierarchy, porosity, and vertical variation, 
supporting path optimization and parameter adjustment; lodging detection further uses 
surface normal variation and tilt trends to evaluate crop uprightness for machine control. 
Recent deep learning approaches, including PointNet, PointNet++, and PointConv, di-
rectly model raw point clouds for classiﬁcation, segmentation, and key point extraction, 
enabling applications such as branch and leaf recognition, corn ear counting, and fruit 
segmentation. For example, a 3D phenotyping system for apple trees achieved an R2 of 
0.987 in height prediction and 93.4% accuracy in branch counting[34]. For robotic harvest-
ing, such structural information can support manipulator workspace judgment and pick-
ing-trajectory planning. However, when branch overlap, leaf occlusion, and viewpoint 
changes cause incomplete point-cloud reconstruction, the robustness of the analysis re-
sults still needs to be ensured. 
To enhance operational reliability and facilitate ﬁeld deployment, recent studies have 
combined image data with point cloud information in crop perception systems. In such 
frameworks, RGB images contribute appearance cues, including color and surface texture, 
whereas point clouds provide geometric information related to depth, shape, and spatial 
arrangement. The complementary use of these two data types enables a more complete 
representation of crop targets and improves perception robustness under occlusion, back-
ground clu>er, and sensor interference in ﬁeld environments. Moreover, SLAM has been 
incorporated into harvesting perception platforms to support simultaneous environment 
mapping and machine localization. When coupled with real-time point cloud processing, 
SLAM-based perception can assist agricultural machinery in maintaining navigation ac-
curacy and tracking crop targets continuously during ﬁeld operation[35]. 
In summary, point cloud analysis methods have evolved from simple geometric ﬁt-
ting to 3D perception frameworks integrating deep learning, spatiotemporal modeling, 
and multi-modal fusion. Their high spatial resolution and ability to represent complex 
structures make them valuable for harvesting regulation tasks that rely on 3D infor-
mation. The extracted 3D structural parameters, such as plant height, canopy geometry, 
lodging angle, and target position, can support header adjustment, navigation correction, 
picking trajectory planning, and operation parameter optimization. However, these meth-
ods are sensitive to data quality, as point cloud noise, sparsity, and missing data can aﬀect 
structural reconstruction and feature extraction. Meanwhile, large-scale point cloud pro-
cessing involves high computational cost, and multi-modal fusion requires accurate cali-
bration and registration, increasing system complexity.

image deformation, reducing the accuracy of spatial reconstruction and object detection. 
In most existing studies on crop a>ribute detection, calibration is performed under static 
conditions, while real-time compensation for posture changes and motion disturbances 
remains insuﬃcient, which can reduce system adaptability in complex harvesting envi-
ronments. Therefore, some researchers have begun to adopt methods such as image 
deblurring, motion compensation, and multi-sensor calibration for improvement. For ex-
ample, CNN-based deblurring models can improve image quality to some extent under 
motion blur, while visual–inertial systems combining IMUs and cameras can also be used 
for dynamic posture compensation to improve system stability and positioning accu-
racy[144]. In addition, multi-view acquisition and image fusion can provide complemen-
tary information when single-frame data are unreliable. However, under high-speed op-
eration, severe vibration, and complex terrain conditions, real-time compensation accu-
racy, cross-sensor synchronization, and model inference eﬃciency still face limitations. 
Therefore, stable perception and real-time compensation under motion interference still 
need to be further strengthened.

interfere with feature extraction and perception consistency. Improved sensor robustness, 
image stabilization, and multi-source fusion can partially mitigate these problems, but 
their eﬀectiveness is still constrained by dynamic operating environments and multi-sen-

mulate during continuous operations, further aﬀecting detection stability. 
6. Future Development Trends 
With the advancement of agricultural intelligence, crop a>ribute detection is evolv-
ing from single sensing to multi-dimensional integration and dynamic decision-making. 
Despite progress in sensing and algorithms, challenges remain in generalization, collabo-
ration, and environmental adaptability. Future work should focus on ﬂexible perception 
frameworks and improvements in learning mechanisms, system architectures, and re-
source scheduling. At the same time, the application of artiﬁcial intelligence in agricul-
tural decision-making introduces challenges related to reliability and interpretability. En-
suring the trustworthiness of decision-making results is therefore a key prerequisite for 
practical deployment. This chapter focuses on future directions such as weakly supervised 
learning, few-shot learning, and multi-agent collaborative architectures, with emphasis 
on their roles in improving data utilization eﬃciency, enhancing system generalization, 
and supporting collaborative perception and decision-making. 
6.1. Weak Supervision and Few-Shot Learning 
In agricultural harvesting, crop a>ribute detection is challenged by limited annotated 
data, high labeling costs, and ambiguous class diﬀerences. Complex ﬁeld conditions, in-
cluding seasonal variation, crop diversity, and weather changes, further hinder the collec-
tion of large-scale, high-quality datasets. Traditional supervised deep learning methods 
often suﬀer from overﬁ>ing and performance degradation under data scarcity. Weak su-
pervision and few-shot learning therefore represent key directions for improving agricul-
tural perception systems. 
Weakly supervised learning trains models using incomplete or imprecise labels. In 
agricultural detection tasks, image-level tags, bounding boxes, or sparse point annotations 
can replace dense pixel-level labels. Methods such as multiple instance learning, pseudo-
labeling, and self-supervised learning improve model generalization. In addition, spatial 
priors from remote sensing data and historical operational data can serve as auxiliary su-
pervision, reducing reliance on manual annotation[145]. 
Few-shot learning enables rapid generalization with limited samples and is suitable 
for scenarios such as new crop identiﬁcation, rare condition recognition, and novel disease 
detection. Meta-learning methods such as MAML and Prototypical Networks optimize 
models through few-shot task simulation, enabling fast convergence with minimal 
data[146]. Incorporating domain-speciﬁc priors, such as agronomic knowledge or crop 
growth models, further enhances performance. Data augmentation also plays a key role, 
with techniques such as GAN-based image synthesis and geometric or illumination trans-
formations improving robustness in real-world deployment[147]. 
In summary, weakly supervised and few-shot learning can alleviate data scarcity and 
improve the adaptability and generalization of agricultural vision systems, making them 
suitable for rapid deployment on new crops, emergency detection in extreme scenarios, 
and cross-regional model transfer. However, these methods usually rely on limited or 
noisy annotations, which may aﬀect model stability and accuracy in complex ﬁeld envi-
ronments. Meanwhile, few-shot methods have limited adaptability to intra-class

variations under diﬀerent growth stages and environmental conditions, while weakly su-
pervised methods may be aﬀected by label uncertainty in ﬁne-grained recognition. 
6.2. Multi-Agent Collaborative Perception System 
As agricultural operations expand and tasks become more complex, single-platform 
detection systems can no longer meet large-scale, heterogeneous perception demands. A 
key direction is the development of multi-agent collaborative systems involving drones, 
agricultural machinery, ground robots, and edge nodes. Through coordinated sensing 
and information sharing, these systems enable large-scale, high-precision, and robust crop 
perception and harvest control. 
The key advantage of multi-agent systems lies in spatial and functional complemen-
tarity. Aerial drones provide large-scale, high-mobility global observation for mapping 
crop distribution and detecting lodging, while ground machinery and robots oﬀer higher 
local resolution for tasks such as fruit recognition and quality assessment. Their coordina-
tion enables a macro–micro perception framework that improves eﬃciency and adapta-
bility. Eﬀective collaboration requires distributed perception and decision-making, allow-
ing agents to operate autonomously while sharing information for global coordination. In 
addition, reinforcement learning and graph neural networks can optimize task allocation 
and coordination, improving resource utilization and system eﬃciency. 
In summary, multi-agent systems are suitable for complex agricultural environments 
with limited single-platform coverage, such as orchards, mixed-crop ﬁelds, and moun-
tainous farmlands. With advances in 5G, satellite–terrestrial communication, and robotic 
platforms, they can enable collaborative perception and large-scale deployment, support-
ing continuous crop monitoring and automated operations. However, communication la-
tency, packet loss, asynchronous data acquisition, and time-alignment errors may aﬀect 
real-time coordination and perception consistency. Meanwhile, diﬀerences in sensing ca-
pabilities among platforms increase the diﬃculty of data fusion and task allocation, while 
larger system scales place higher demands on bandwidth and computational resources. 
7. Conclusions 
This review addresses crop a>ribute detection in the context of intelligent agricul-
tural harvesting machinery. This review focuses on the a>ributes of grain, fruit, and veg-
etable crops from the perspectives of harvesting eﬃciency, loss rate, operating speed, and 
failure rate. It also analyzes the sensors and algorithms involved in detecting these a>rib-
utes and clariﬁes the relationship between detection methods and control decisions in ag-
ricultural harvesting machinery. Key a>ributes such as plant height, canopy structure, 
density, spike number, spatial positioning, maturity, and quality are discussed, along 
with their roles in operational decision-making. This review further aims to systematically 
summarize crop a>ribute detection requirements, analyze key sensing technologies and 
data analysis methods, and identify current challenges and future development directions 
in intelligent harvesting systems. The application characteristics of mainstream sensors 
and the evolution of core methods, including image classiﬁcation, object detection, seman-
tic segmentation, and point cloud analysis, are summarized. 
Recent advances in sensing and data analysis have improved detection accuracy and 
promoted more precise and automated harvesting. However, crop detection in real har-
vesting environments still faces challenges from complex ﬁeld conditions, real-time con-
straints, and motion interference. Factors such as illumination variation, occlusion, une-
ven terrain, and high-speed operation increase sensor noise and reduce system reliability. 
Future research should focus on improving multi-sensor fusion adaptability, devel-
oping lightweight and eﬃcient models for real-time deployment, and integrating crop 
detection with agricultural big data for intelligent decision-making. Crop detection is
