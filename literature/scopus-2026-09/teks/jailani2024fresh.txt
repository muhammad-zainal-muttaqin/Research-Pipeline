Abstract— In this study, we evaluate the impact of different 
training methodologies on the performance of YOLOv5s, a 
popular object detection model, by comparing training time, 
GPU memory usage, and detection accuracy. We explore four 
distinct approaches: single batch training, multiple batch 
training, single batch training with concatenated images, and 
multiple batch training with concatenated images. The objective 
is to determine whether using concatenated images can reduce 
training time while assessing its effects on GPU utilization and 
detection accuracy, all while using the same dataset. 
Our experiments reveal that training with concatenated 
images significantly reduces the overall training time. 
Interestingly, GPU memory usage remains consistent with that 
of traditional training methods without concatenation. 
However, this approach leads to a slight reduction in detection 
accuracy compared to the native methods that do not involve 
image concatenation. 
In conclusion, for applications where reducing training time 
is crucial and a marginal decrease in accuracy is acceptable, 
training with concatenated images presents a more efficient 
alternative to conventional methods. 
Keywords—YOLOv5, Object Detection, Image Concatenation, 
Training Time Reduction, Detection Accuracy, Machine 
Learning. 
I. INTRODUCTION  
The rapid advancements in deep learning and artificial 
intelligence (AI) have significantly impacted various 
domains, with object detection emerging as a critical 
application in areas such as autonomous vehicles, 
surveillance, and robotics. Among the various object detection 
models, YOLO (You Only Look Once) has gained 
considerable attention for its balance between speed and 
accuracy, with YOLOv5 being the latest iteration known for 
its optimized architecture and performance improvements. 
In object detection tasks, model training is often resource-
intensive, requiring substantial computational power and time. 
As datasets grow larger and more complex, optimizing 
training processes becomes increasingly important for both 
academic research and real-world applications. Traditional 
training methods, which involve processing images in single 
or multiple batches, can be time-consuming and demand high 
GPU memory usage. To address these challenges, researchers 
have explored various strategies to enhance training 
efficiency, including the use of advanced data augmentation 
techniques, multi-scale training, and image concatenation. 
This paper presents a comparative study of four distinct 
training methodologies using the YOLOv5s model: (1) single 
batch training, (2) multiple batch training, (3) single batch 
training with concatenated images, and (4) multiple batch 
training with concatenated images. Image concatenation, in 
particular, has been proposed as a technique to reduce the 
number of training iterations by combining multiple images 
into a single input, thus potentially decreasing the overall 
training time. However, the impact of this approach on GPU 
memory usage and detection accuracy remains a topic of 
investigation. 
The primary objective of this research is to evaluate 
whether using concatenated images during training can 
effectively reduce training time while maintaining acceptable 
levels of GPU utilization and detection accuracy. By 
conducting experiments across the aforementioned training 
methodologies using a consistent dataset, we aim to provide 
insights 
into 
the 
trade-offs 
associated 
with 
image 
concatenation in the context of object detection. 
The results of our study indicate that training with 
concatenated images significantly reduces the overall time 
required for data processing without increasing GPU memory 
consumption. However, this approach does introduce a slight 
degradation in detection accuracy compared to traditional 
training methods. These findings suggest that image 
concatenation can be a valuable technique for scenarios where 
training efficiency is prioritized over marginal losses in 
detection performance. 
In the following Section II, we discuss related work, 
followed by the methodology of the experiment in Section III. 
Finally, Section IV presents the results and discussion derived 
from this study. 
II. RELATED WORK 
Object detection has been extensively studied in computer 
vision, with models such as YOLO (You Only Look Once) [1] 
and SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) [2] leading to 
significant advancements in both speed and accuracy. 
YOLOv5, the latest iteration in the YOLO series, further 
optimizes the balance between computational efficiency and 
detection performance, making it a popular choice for real-
time applications [3]. 
Training optimization techniques, particularly in deep 
learning, have focused on improving training efficiency and 
reducing computational costs. For instance, techniques such 
as multi-scale training [4] and advanced data augmentation [5]

III. METHODOLOGY 
This study aims to evaluate the effects of different training 
methodologies on the performance of the YOLOv5s object 
detection model, specifically focusing on training time, GPU 
memory usage, and detection accuracy. The experiments were 
conducted using a machine equipped with an Nvidia RTX 
2070 GPU with 8GB VRAM, running on Ubuntu 20.04 
operating system. 
A. Dataset Preparation 
The dataset used in this study consists of 5,042 images of 
oil palm fruits in plantation environment, as shown in Fig. 1, 
which were selected as the target objects for detection. Of 
these, 3,900 images were allocated for training, 900 images 
were designated as the validation set, and 242 images were 
reserved for the test dataset. The validation dataset was kept 
consistent across all training methods to ensure that the results 
were not influenced by variations in the validation set, thereby 
maintaining the integrity and comparability of the evaluation 
metrics. 
 
Fig. 1. Example of training image datasets 
B. Training Methods 
The training process was carried out using the YOLOv5s 
model, a well-known architecture for real-time object 
detection. The study explored four distinct training 
methodologies: 
 
• Single Batch Training: In this method, the standard 
YOLOv5 training process was employed, where the 
dataset was trained using a batch size of 1. This method 
serves as the baseline for comparing the effects of 
batch size on training performance. 
• Multiple Batch Training: This method also followed 
the standard YOLOv5 training process, with the 
primary difference being the use of a batch size of 32. 
The purpose of this comparison was to evaluate the 
impact of batch size on training efficiency and resource 
utilization. 
• Concatenated Image Training - Single Batch: In this 
method, images from the training dataset were 
concatenated into 3x3 grids, with every nine images 
combined into a single image, as illustrated in Fig. 2. 
The resulting 433 concatenated images were then used 
for training with a batch size of 1. The purpose of this 
approach was to assess whether image concatenation 
could reduce training time while maintaining detection 
accuracy. 
• Concatenated Image Training - Multiple Batch: 
Similar to the previous method, the training dataset 
was first concatenated into 3x3 grids, as illustrated in 
Fig. 2. The 433 concatenated images were then used 
for training with a batch size of 32. This method aimed 
to combine the potential benefits of larger batch sizes 
and image concatenation. 
 
Fig. 2. Example of training concatenated image datasets 
For the validation dataset, the original, non-concatenated 
images were used to calculate the actual mean Average 
Precision (mAP) to ensure accurate and consistent 
performance measurements across all methods. 
C. Training Parameters 
To ensure a reliable comparison across the different 
training methodologies, the following parameters were kept 
constant: 
• 
Epochs: All training processes were run for 30 
epochs. This constant allows for the evaluation of the 
training time, GPU memory usage, and mAP across 
methods without variations due to different epoch 
counts.

D. Performances Metrics 
The performance of each training methodology was 
evaluated based on the following metrics: 
• Training Time: The total time required to complete the 
training process for each method was recorded. This 
metric provides insight into the efficiency of each 
approach. 
• GPU Memory Usage: The GPU memory consumption 
during training was monitored and recorded for each 
method. 
This 
metric 
indicates 
the 
resource 
requirements and scalability of each training approach. 
• F1 Score Confidence: The F1 Score was calculated 
using the validation dataset to measure the accuracy 
and reliability of object detection. The F1 Score 
combines precision (the ratio of correctly predicted 
positive observations to the total predicted positives) 
and recall (the ratio of correctly predicted positive 
observations to all observations in the actual class) into

This metric balances the trade-offs between precision 
and recall, providing a comprehensive view of the 
model’s effectiveness, particularly in situations with 
class imbalances or where the costs of false positives 
and false negatives differ. 
E. Experimental Setup 
Each training experiment was conducted in a controlled 
environment to minimize variability. The GPU memory usage 
was recorded in real-time during the training process, while 
training time and F1 Score were measured upon completion 
of each experiment. The results were then compared to 
evaluate the trade-offs between training time, GPU utilization, 
and detection performance for the different training methods. 
IV. RESULT AND DISCUSSION 
This section presents the evaluation of the four training 
methods—Single Batch Training, Multiple Batch Training, 
Concatenated Image Single Batch, and Concatenated Image 
Multiple Batch—based on two critical performance metrics: 
training time efficiency and detection accuracy, as measured 
by the F1 Score. The findings highlight the trade-offs between 
training speed and accuracy, offering insights into the 
strengths and limitations of each approach. 
A. Training Time and GPU Memory Usage 
The results from the detection test dataset reveal 
significant differences in training time efficiency among the 
four training methods examined. The Concatenated Image 
Single Batch method demonstrated a marked reduction in 
training time, completing 30 epochs in just 0.652 hours. This 
is significantly faster compared to the Single Batch Training 
method, which required 2.936 hours to complete the same 
number of epochs. Both methods showed similar GPU 
memory usage, with the Concatenated Image Single Batch 
and Single Batch Training methods consuming 1387 MiB and 
1487 MiB, respectively. 
Similarly, the Concatenated Image Multiple Batch method 
further reduced training time, completing the training process 
in only 0.097 hours, outperforming the Multiple Batch 
Training method, which took 0.387 hours to reach the same 
milestone. The GPU memory usage for these methods was 
nearly identical, with the Concatenated Image Multiple Batch 
and Multiple Batch Training methods utilizing 6945 MiB and 
6947 MiB, respectively. 
These findings suggest that the concatenated image 
training approach is highly effective in reducing training time 
without imposing additional GPU memory demands. The 
observed reduction in training time is attributable to the 
reduction in the number of training images, as the 
concatenation process combines every nine images into a 
single composite image. This reduction in the number of 
images effectively decreases the number of iterations required 
per epoch, thereby accelerating the overall training process 
compared to traditional training methods that use individual 
images.

B. F1 Score Confidence 
Although the Concatenated Image method reduced 
training time, this efficiency gain came at the cost of decreased 
detection accuracy, as reflected in the F1 Score. The model 
trained using the Concatenated Image Single Batch method 
achieved a peak F1 Score of 0.57 at a confidence level of 0.593 
(Fig. 4), which is significantly lower than the peak F1 Score 
of 0.88 at a confidence level of 0.245 obtained with the Single 
Batch Training method (Fig. 3). Similarly, the Concatenated 
Image Multiple Batch method reached a peak F1 Score of 0.66 
at a confidence level of 0.486 (Fig. 6), which is also lower than 
the peak F1 Score of 0.85 at a confidence level of 0.324 
achieved by the Multiple Batch Training method (Fig. 5). 
These results suggest that although the concatenated 
image training approach enhances efficiency by reducing 
training time, it may have a detrimental effect on the model's 
detection accuracy. The decrease in F1 Scores indicates that 
the model’s ability to balance precision and recall is 
compromised when using concatenated images, possibly due 
to the altered distribution and characteristics of the training 
data. The trade-off between training time and detection 
accuracy is visually depicted in the F1-confidence curves for 
all training methods, which are presented below.

C. Future Works 
Although the F1 Score indicates that detection accuracy 
for both single and multiple batch sizes is lower with the 
Concatenated Image training method compared to the 
standard training approach, this method offers several 
noteworthy advantages beyond the reduction in training time. 
One of the key benefits of the Concatenated Image method is 
its enhanced ability to detect smaller or slightly blurred objects 
within an image. This capability is particularly important in 
scenarios where object size is reduced due to distance from the 
camera or where image resolution is suboptimal. 
This advantage was observed in one of the test images, 
where multiple oil palm fruits were arranged at varying 
distances from the camera. The model trained using the 
standard method was able to detect only the two nearest oil 
palm fruits, as shown in Fig. 7. In contrast, the model trained 
with the Concatenated Image method successfully detected 
four oil palm fruits but failed to detect the fruit closest to the 
camera, as illustrated in Fig. 8. This result suggests that the 
Concatenated Image method can improve the detection of 
smaller or less distinct objects, even if it sacrifices some 
overall detection accuracy. 
These findings indicate that a hybrid approach, combining 
both standard and concatenated image training methods, could 
potentially yield superior results. Such an approach could 
capitalize on the faster training times and enhanced small-
object detection of the Concatenated Image method while 
retaining the higher detection accuracy of the standard 
method. Below is the test image, accompanied by the 
detection results from both training models, illustrating these 
observations.

In summary, this study underscores the trade-offs between 
training efficiency and detection accuracy in object detection 
tasks. While the Concatenated Image method offers a 
promising avenue for reducing training times, it is crucial to 
carefully consider the implications for model performance, 
particularly in applications where high detection accuracy is 
critical. Future work could explore hybrid training approaches 
that integrate the benefits of both methodologies to optimize 
both efficiency and accuracy.
