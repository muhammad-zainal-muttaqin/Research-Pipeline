A B S T R A C T
Grape-cluster detection, grape-berry counting, and berry-per-grape-cluster counting are the three major tasks
in digital viticulture. Although the detection of clusters and berries was treated as separate tasks in previous
studies, due to the clustered nature of the grape phenotype and the close hierarchical spatial distribution
relationship between grape clusters and berries, those three tasks should be considered at the same time in
our view; further, we define these tasks as subtasks of the grape detection and counting task. The major
challenge of combining the three subtasks of grape detection and counting together is how to obtain the
detection results of those subtasks from the output of a single neural network. To address this issue, we
propose a Probability Map-based Grape Detection and Counting framework that first detects two intermediate
probability maps through a neural network and utilizes three postprocessing stages to finish the three grape
detection and counting subtasks sequentially. After being trained and validated on the trainval set of WGISD,
combining 100 extra images collected in Chengdu by our team, the proposed framework is tested on the test set
of WGISD to verify its performance. The proposed framework achieves a localization performance of average
precision (AP)(iou0.5) 0.851 and a counting performance of MAE 1.845, RMSE 2.142 for grape clusters, a
counting performance of MAE 23.414, RMSE 31.391 for grape berries, and a counting performance of MRD
0.142, 1-FVU 0.865 for berries per grape cluster on the test set of WGISD. Our source code will be publicly
available at https://github.com/volcanoYcc/Probability-Map-based-Grape-Detection-and-Counting.
1. Introduction
Digital viticulture, which combines computer technology with grape
production and deploys various sensors in the vineyard to monitor the
environmental information and growth conditions of grapes in real
time, can provide a series of reference bases for grape production
management activities (Mohimont et al., 2022). Many previous studies
have focused on using computer vision technology to address practical
issues in vineyards, and the main research tasks include predicting
grape yield (Parr et al., 2023), analyzing grape maturity (Rahman and
Hellicar, 2014), detecting grape diseases and pests (Bömer et al., 2020),
determining grape picking point locations (Zhao et al., 2022), and
judging whether pruning is needed (Buayai et al., 2023). In particular,
the detection and counting of grape clusters and berries is the most
basic and common computer vision task in digital viticulture.
The fruit detection and counting task is clear for common non-
clustered fruits such as mangoes (Liu et al., 2019), strawberries (Yu
et al., 2019), apples (Kang and Chen, 2020), and blueberries (Ni et al.,
2020). However, since grapes are clustered fruits, they have grape
clusters, grape berries, and berries per grape cluster as phenotype

Therefore, how to correctly utilize these three phenotype characteristics
to describe the grape phenotype more comprehensively and accurately
achieve the specific grape detection task is the key issue that needs
to be addressed in digital viticulture. Previous studies divided the
detection and counting of grapes into three independent tasks: (1) the
grape-cluster detection task; (2) the grape-berry counting task; and
(3) the berry-per-grape-cluster counting task. Furthermore, the studies
proposed many methods for achieving them separately.
We notice that since grape clusters are composed of multiple grape
berries, the process of detecting the bounding boxes of each cluster
can also be equivalent to detecting all the grape berries contained in
each cluster. Moreover, after locating all the berries in a cluster, the
bounding box of that cluster can be easily determined using the location
of these berries. In addition, without producing duplicate detection,
missed detection, and false detection, the location and counting results
of grape berries in the whole image, after removing the interference of
discrete berries, are equivalent to the union of berry detection results
in all grape clusters. Considering those relevance and existing studies

In the present study, we define the grape-cluster detection task,
the grape-berry counting task, and the berry-per-grape-cluster counting
task as subtasks of the grape detection and counting task, as shown in
Fig. 1. Although the grape-cluster detection task and the grape-berry
counting task can easily be achieved with a general neural network,
due to the difficulty of simultaneously expressing the two uncertain
quantities of ‘‘clusters’’ and ‘‘berries contained in each cluster’’, the
berry-per-grape-cluster counting task is challenging to directly abstract
as output for end-to-end neural networks. Thus, to combine the three
subtasks of the grape detection and counting task together and improve
the interpretability of the framework, we decouple the grape detection
and counting task into three postprocessing stages of a neural network
to propose a comprehensive detection framework named Probability
Map-based Grape Detection and Counting (PMGDC). PMGDC first uti-
lizes a neural network to predict intermediate probability maps of grape
clusters and grape berries. Subsequently, in three postprocessing stages,
PMGDC uses the local peak method and the newly proposed Up-Hill and
Down-Hill methods to extract the detection results of grape clusters,
grape berries, and grape berries contained in each cluster from the
probability maps, completing the grape detection and counting task
with only one detection.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
1. For the first time, a systematic analysis of the detection and
counting of grapes is conducted, and the grape detection and
counting task is defined as the aggregation of the grape-cluster
detection task, the grape-berry counting task, and the berry-per-
grape-cluster counting task.
2. For the first time, while representing the location of grape
clusters and grape berries, probability maps are used to represent
the spatial containment correlation between grape clusters and
grape berries.
3. Two postprocessing algorithms for the probability maps of grape
clusters and grape berries are proposed to address the issue of
completing multiple grape detection tasks with a single neural
network. These methods obtain the grape berries contained in
each grape cluster and the bounding boxes of grape clusters by
analyzing the gradient of the grape probability maps.
4. A grape detection and counting framework named PMGDC is
designed to complete three subtasks of the grape detection and
counting task simultaneously. This framework achieves a local-
ization performance of average precision (AP) (iou0.5) 0.851
and a counting performance of MAE 1.845, RMSE 2.142 for
grape clusters, a counting performance of MAE 23.414, RMSE
31.391 for grape berries, and a counting performance of MRD
0.142, 1-FVU 0.865 for berries per grape cluster on the test set
of WGISD.
2. Related studies
Grape-cluster detection task, that is, detecting grape clusters in
the image and counting their number. Grape-cluster detection has
multiple applications, such as combining the counting results with the
average weight of grape clusters obtained from historical sampling
surveys to predict the total grape production in a specified area. By
determining the location of grape clusters, it can help picking robots
infer the picking point of each grape cluster and improve the efficiency
of grape picking. The detection of grape clusters started with machine
learning methods that distinguish grape clusters from the background
based on the difference in light and color between grape clusters and
the background (Luo et al., 2016). However, these detection methods
are limited by color changes caused by different grape varieties and
differences in light conditions at different times. After the rapid devel-
opment of deep learning technology, many researchers have migrated
object detection (Akai et al., 2021) and instance segmentation (Santos
et al., 2020) techniques to the field of grape-cluster detection, obtaining
more accurate detection and counting results. Other researchers have
combined the semantic segmentation technique with 3D reconstruction
of grape clusters (Hacking et al., 2019) to model the vineyard scene
while detecting grape clusters in a three-dimensional space.
Grape-berry counting task, which is to detect the number of grape
berries in the image. Compared with grape clusters, grape berries are
smaller in volume and are more dense targets. Since the volume and
weight differences between each grape berry are often significantly
smaller than those between different grape clusters, the grape-berry
count can be used to obtain a more accurate grape yield estimation
after combining the average weight of grape berries obtained from
historical sampling surveys. Furthermore, some studies have shown
that grape berries contribute approximately 60% to the estimation
of grape yield, while grape clusters contribute only approximately
30% (Nuske et al., 2014). Studies using machine learning methods to
detect grape berries utilize Hough circle detection and other methods
to identify the ‘‘circular’’ shape of grape berries and locate them (Nuske
et al., 2014). The performance of the method is affected by occlusion
between berries and cannot recognize nonspherical grape varieties. On
the one hand, studies of detecting grape berries with deep learning
methods regard grape berries as instance objects, similar to the stud-
ies of detecting grape clusters, and use object detection (Śkrabánek,
2018) and instance segmentation (Aquino et al., 2017; Zabawa et al.,
2020) methods to identify them. On the other hand, many studies
also regard grape berries as dense objects, ignoring their shape and
migrating crowd counting methods to the detection and counting of
them (Coviello et al., 2020). Since the berries of most grape varieties
are approximately spherical, some studies also model grape berries as
spheres and complete berry counting through 3D reconstruction (Liu
et al., 2020).
Berry-per-grape-cluster counting task, which is to count grape
berries in each grape cluster in the image, is a relatively novel research
topic. In the actual grape production process, the excessive number of
grape berries growing in a cluster not only increases grape yield but

3. Materials and methods
In this section, we first introduce the method of using probability
maps to represent grape phenotype characteristics and then introduce
the proposed Probability Map-based Grape Detection Framework and
its process of completing three subtasks of the grape detection and
counting task in detail. Finally, we introduce the acquisition of datasets
used in our experiments.
3.1. Probability map for grape phenotyping
3.1.1. What is a probability map?
Probability maps represent objects using two-dimensional Gaussian
kernels with values between 0 and 1, in which the higher value of
a point has a higher probability of being the center point of the
object (Baek et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2021). Points in Gaussian kernels
obey the probability density function shown in Eq. (1) as follows:

of the Gaussian kernels are the locations of the objects, probability
maps can better express the spatial features of objects than density
maps (Li et al., 2018), which express the count of objects with the sum
of elements in matrices and blur the position of them. Therefore, in
previous studies, probability maps have been widely used to address
issues related to object localization, such as pose estimation, object
detection, and dense object counting.
3.1.2. How do we generate probability maps?
Generate probability maps for grape clusters. Since existing
grape-cluster annotations in datasets (see Section 3.3.1 for details)
contain the shape information of clusters, we can directly use these data
to generate two-dimensional Gaussian kernels with the same length
and width as grape clusters in cluster probability maps. Notably, since
mosaic data augmentation is used in model training, if probability
maps of grape clusters are pregenerated before training, Gaussian
kernels will become incomplete due to crop operations in mosaic data
augmentation. To address this issue, we dynamically generate cluster
probability maps in training based on the grape-cluster bounding boxes
after data augmentation. Considering that the process of generating
two-dimensional rectangular Gaussian kernels may consume a lot of
time, we adopt the strategy proposed in Baek et al. (2020), which is
to first generate square Gaussian kernels whose side length is equal
to the length of rectangular grape-cluster bounding boxes, then use
perspective transformation to switch them to the same size as grape
clusters.
Generate probability maps for grape berries. Existing point-level
grape-berry annotations in datasets (see Section 3.3.1 for details) are
not sufficient for determining the size of two-dimensional Gaussian ker-
nels. To address this issue, we use the method described in Cheng et al.
(2021), which determines the size of Gaussian kernels in probability
maps based on the distance between point-level annotations. Specifi-
cally, for each grape berry, we search for the three closest berries to it
and calculate their distance to that berry. Subsequently, we manually
set the size of the Gaussian kernel representing that berry based on
the sum of those distances. Since images in datasets contain hundreds
of grape berries, generating grape-berry probability maps in training
will be substantially time consuming. Moreover, the impact of crop
operations on the Gaussian kernels of grape berries is far less than that
of grape clusters. Thus, different from the strategy of generating the
probability maps of grape clusters, we pregenerate all the probability
maps of grape berries before training.
3.1.3. How do we extract grape phenotype characteristics from probability
maps?
Locating grape clusters and berries. Probability maps represent
objects with Gaussian kernels. For the probability maps of grape clus-
ters and berries predicted by the neural network, the detected grape
locations can be obtained in reverse by finding local peaks composed
of predicted Gaussian kernels in the probability maps. The coordinates
of the peaks in each Gaussian kernel are the localization results of the
network for the grape clusters and berries. For the probability maps of
grape berries, these peak points directly represent the position of grape
berries. For the probability maps of grape clusters, on the one hand, the
peaks of the Gaussian kernels represent the center points of the clusters.
On the other hand, the sizes of the Gaussian kernels can also reflect the
sizes of the grape clusters.
Determining containment relationship between grape clusters
and berries. Probability maps have been widely used in previous
studies, addressing issues related to object localization. We also use
probability maps predicted by the neural network to localize grape
berries and the center points of grape clusters. However, we believe that
probability maps can express richer object features while represent-
ing their locations. Probability maps represent objects with Gaussian
kernels; further, a basic characteristic of Gaussian kernels is that their
values gradually decrease from the center to the periphery. Concur-
rently, when generating probability maps for grape clusters, we use
Gaussian kernels with the same length and width as the grape clusters.
Therefore, in grape-cluster probability maps, for any point located in
any Gaussian kernel representing a grape cluster, we can detect the
difference in values between that point and its surrounding points, look-
ing for points with greater values around the given point to determine
the orientation of the grape-cluster center to that point. Based on this
idea, we can use the gradient in the grape-cluster probability maps
to uniquely determine the containment relationship between all grape
berries and grape clusters without repetition.
Detecting bounding boxes of grape clusters. After extracting the
location and containment relationships of grape clusters and grape
berries from the grape probability maps, we hope to further obtain
the edges of grape clusters, that is, the bounding boxes of grape
clusters. Previous research (Baek et al., 2020) obtained the locations of
objects from the object probability maps with the watershed algorithm.
However, since the values at the periphery of Gaussian kernels are
relatively low, the accuracy of determining the bounding boxes of
grape clusters with the watershed algorithm will be greatly affected
by the neural network’s incorrect prediction of the Gaussian kernel
boundaries. To address this issue, we propose locating bounding boxes
of grape clusters with the grape berries contained in each cluster. As
considerably smaller objects compared with grape clusters, the proba-
bility maps of grape berries are less likely to be affected by the neural
network’s incorrect predictions than those of grape clusters. Therefore,
after locating the areas of grape berries contained in each grape cluster,
we combine the probability maps of grape berries to determine more
accurate bounding box boundaries for grape clusters.

of extracting grape phenotype characteristics from probability maps to
finish the three subtasks of the grape detection and counting task are

3.2. PMGDC
3.2.1. Framework overview
The architecture of the proposed PMGDC framework is shown in
Fig. 3, which can be divided into the upstream stage and the down-
stream stage. In the upstream stage, the grape image is inputted into
a neural network, which detects the intermediate probability maps of
grape clusters and berries separately. While in the downstream stage,
three postprocessing modules are applied to obtain the detection results
of three subtasks of the grape detection and counting task from the
intermediate probability maps.
In the downstream stage, the local peak method is first applied
to the grape-berry probability map, obtaining grape-berry locations
to complete the grape-berry counting task. Subsequently, the Up-Hill
method is applied to the grape-cluster probability maps and the grape-
berry locations, obtaining grape berries contained in each cluster to
complete the berry-per-grape-cluster counting task. Finally, the Down-
Hill method is applied to the grape berries contained in each cluster
and the grape-berry probability map, obtaining grape-cluster bounding
boxes to finish the grape-cluster detection task.
The detailed architecture and detection process of PMGDC are
introduced in the remaining content of Section 3.2.
3.2.2. Generate intermediate grape probability maps with a neural network
The architecture of the neural network used to detect the probability
maps of grape clusters and berries is shown in Fig. 4. Compared with
networks used in previous studies, we utilize a simple but effective U-

berries and clusters in grape images. This neural network first extracts

3.2.3. Obtain grape detection and counting results from grape probability
maps
Grape-berry counting:
To solve the grape-berry counting task, we use the local peak
method to detect grape-berry locations from grape-berry probability
maps, as shown in Fig. 5. We adopt the local peak postprocessing
method for probability maps from Cheng et al. (2021). First, we per-
form a 3 × 3 average pooling operation with a stride of 1 on the
grape-berry probability map to reduce noise. Subsequently, we perform
a 3 × 3 max pooling operation with a stride of 1 on the average pooled
probability map. The points in the max pooled probability map that
have the same value as those in the averaged pooled probability map
are local peaks, which are the candidate points of grape berries. The
value of each candidate point represents its probability of being a grape
berry. We keep the points with a probability greater than 0.5 as the
detected grape berries.
Berry-per-grape-cluster counting:
After obtaining the grape-berry locations, to solve the berry-per-
grape-cluster counting task, we use the Up-Hill method to detect the
grape berries contained in each cluster from the grape-berry locations
and the grape-cluster probability map, as shown in Fig. 6. First, we
perform a 3 × 3 average pooling operation with a stride of 1 on the
probability map of grape clusters to reduce noise. Subsequently, we
use the proposed Up-Hill method to divide the grape berries contained
in each cluster. For each grape berry, we find the point in the grape-
cluster probability map at the same coordinate as that berry, regarding
that point as the start point and the value of that point as the start
value. Subsequently, find the point with the largest value within a
certain range around the start point in the grape-cluster probability
map, regarding that point as the end point and the value of that point
as the end value. Using the newly found point as the start point,
we iterate through the previous search process until the end point
is exactly the same as the start point. Finally, we divide the grape
berries with the same final end point into the same cluster to solve
the berry-per-grape-cluster counting task.
As for the selection of search ranges in the Up-Hill method, we
take into account two aspects of factors. First, considering the possible
incorrect predictions of the neural network in grape-cluster probability

Notably, although the Up-Hill method can also be replaced by
the traditional floodfill and watershed algorithms, it is more suitable
for detecting the grape berries contained in each cluster. On the one
hand, traditional methods are not available to accurately predict grape
clusters when the neural network incorrectly predicts a single peak as
multiple peaks in grape-cluster probability maps, where the false start
points of the floodfill algorithm can lead to deviation in locating grape
clusters. On the other hand, the uneven gradients at different edges of a
single Gaussian kernel in predicted grape-cluster probability maps also
make it hard to correctly classify grape berries located at the edges of
grape clusters through traditional threshold-based methods.
Grape-cluster detection:
Finally, to solve the grape-cluster detection task, we use the Down-
Hill method to detect grape-cluster bounding boxes from the grape-
berry probability map and the grape berries contained in each cluster,
as shown in Fig. 8. In the proposed Down-Hill method, we first perform
a 3 × 3 average pooling operation with a stride of 1 on the probability
map of grape berries to reduce noise. Subsequently, for the grape
berries contained in the same cluster, we find the four grape berries
with the maximum and minimum x-coordinates and y-coordinates,
which are the four Gaussian kernel peaks in the grape-berry proba-
bility map. After recording the values of the topmost, bottommost,
leftmost, and rightmost peaks, we then perform an iterative search
process for those four peak points along the directions of decreasing
y-coordinate, increasing y-coordinate, decreasing x-coordinate, and in-
creasing x-coordinate, respectively, in the grape-berry probability map.
When we find a point whose value is less than 0.4 times the peak
value or whose value starts to increase compared with the point in
the previous iteration, we determine that point as the boundary of the
grape-cluster bounding box.
Notably, compared with determining grape-cluster bounding boxes
using 0.4 times the peak value of grape-berry Gaussian kernels as
thresholds, another feasible strategy is using a fixed threshold to stop
the search iterations. However, we notice in experiments that, although
the peak value of a Gaussian kernel in a grape-berry probability map
should simply represent the probability of that Gaussian kernel being
a berry, when using a fixed threshold to determine the bounding box

3.3. Datasets
3.3.1. Dataset acquisition
We use three different datasets to train and validate the proposed
PMGDC framework, including WGISD, the Chengdu dataset, and the
BpGC dataset, as shown in Fig. 9.
WGISD. WGISD (Santos et al., 2020) was captured between 2017
and 2018 in the vineyards of the Guaspari Winery in Brazil, containing
a total of 300 RGB images of five different grape varieties. All images
have grape-cluster bounding box annotations, and 137 images addi-
tionally provide grape-cluster instance-level mask annotations. For this
dataset, we supplemented point-level location annotations for a total
of 187,306 grape berries in all the images in our previous study (Deng
et al., 2020).
Chengdu dataset. The Chengdu dataset was captured by us in a
vineyard in Longquanyi District, Chengdu, China, in July 2023. The
dataset contains a total of 100 RGB images of two grape varieties: Shine
Muscat and Summer Black. We add bounding box annotations for 772
grape clusters and point-level location annotations for 30,601 grape
berries in these images. We use this dataset together with the trainval
set of WGISD to train the neural network to compensate for the lower
data volume of WGISD.
BpGC dataset (berry-per-grape-cluster dataset). To validate and test
the performance of PMGDC for the berry-per-grape-cluster counting
task, we combine the grape-cluster mask annotations in WGISD with
our supplementary grape-berry location annotations to obtain the BpGC
dataset. For the grape-cluster annotations in 137 images (110 trainval,
27 tests) in WGISD, we find the grape-berry annotations contained in
each grape cluster, that is, covered by the mask of each grape cluster,
and regard those berries and that cluster as an integral berry-per-
grape-cluster annotation. We use 110 images from the trainval set of
WGISD as the validation data for the berry-per-grape-cluster counting
task in training and 27 images from the test set of WGISD to test the
performance of PMGDC after training.

3.3.2. Data augmentation
In model training, we adopt the mosaic data augmentation method
proposed in Yolov4 (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020), which augments the

4. Experiments and results
4.1. Experimental settings
All the experiments in this study are conducted on the same server
with the following configuration: 80-core Intel Xeon Processor (Sky-
lake, IBRS) 3.0 GHz CPU, one NVIDIA Tesla V100 PCIe 32 GB GPU,
Ubuntu 20.04 operating system, and CUDA 11.3. All the network mod-
els are compiled using PyTorch 1.10 in the Python 3.8 environment.
In the training of PMGDC, the batch size is set to 1, and the number
of workers is set to 8. AdamW with eps of 1e−8 and weight decay of
1e−2 is used as the optimizer, and the learning rate is adjusted using
the cosine annealing strategy with the initial learning rate of 2e−4 and
the end learning rate of 1e−6. MSE loss is used to calculate the loss
between the predicted probability maps and the ground truth.
The framework proposed in this study and all the compared pre-
vious methods are trained for 200 epochs in each training process;
further, after each training epoch of those methods on the train set,
we evaluate their performance on the val set, saving the model weight
files with the optimal performance among all the training epochs. After
training, all the models are tested on the test set, obtaining their final
grape detection and counting performance.
Notably, it is difficult to define a joint evaluation metric for the
three subtasks of the grape detection and counting task completed by
our proposed PMGDC framework. Thus, we evaluate the performance
of PMGDC for those tasks after each training epoch and save three
model weight files with the optimal grape-cluster detection, grape-
berry counting, and berry-per-grape-cluster counting performances sep-
arately, denoted as BGC, BGB, and BGBC in the experimental results.
While other methods compared in our experiments are available to
solve only one subtask of the grape detection and counting task, we
save one optimal model weight file in each training process.

4.3.1. Parameter selection of the Up-Hill method
In Section 3.2.3 (2), we introduce the Up-Hill method for detecting
grape berries contained in each cluster and the strategy of gradually
expanding the search range of the point with the largest value around
the start point in each iteration. In the current study, we compare
the grape detection performance of PMGDC when utilizing the Up-Hill
method with different maximum search ranges of the largest end point.
Experimental results are shown in Table 2. Notably, since the grape-
berry counting task is not affected by the parameters of the Up-Hill
method, it is not compared in this section.
It can be observed that, when the maximum search range is 3 × 3,
PMGDC does not perform well in grape-cluster detection and berry-
per-grape-cluster counting tasks. As the maximum largest end point
search range increases, the performance of PMGDC first shows an
increasing trend and achieves optimal grape detection and counting
performance around the 17 × 17 search range, then starts to get worse
after the maximum search range exceeds 25 × 25. This is because, as the
maximum search range increases, the incorrect detection of Gaussian
kernels such as false detected peaks and detecting single peak as
multiple peaks in grape-cluster probability maps will first be gradually
offset by the larger peak search range. As the maximum search range
becomes even larger, it starts to extend from the current grape cluster
into adjacent grape clusters, resulting in the issue of detecting more
than one grape cluster as one cluster. To maximize the grape detection
and counting performance of PMGDC, in subsequent experiments, the
maximum search range side length in the Up-Hill method is fixed as
12×𝑙
1280 × 2 + 1, where 𝑙is the length of the long side of the input image.
4.3.2. Types of Gaussian kernels in grape-cluster probability maps
We use rectangular Gaussian kernels of the same size as grape
clusters to generate ground-truth grape-cluster probability maps in the
training of PMGDC, which actually takes more time than generating
square Gaussian kernels. To prove the necessity of generating Gaussian
kernels of the same size as grape clusters, we compare the detection
performance of PMGDC when the grape-cluster probability maps are
generated with rectangular Gaussian kernels or square Gaussian kernels
whose side length is equal to the average length and width of grape
clusters. Experimental results are shown in Table 3.
It is apparent that, although the two strategies for generating grape-
cluster probability maps have no significant difference in the perfor-
mance of grape-berry counting and berry-per-grape-cluster counting,
training PMGDC with grape-cluster probability maps generated with
rectangular Gaussian kernels of the same size as grape clusters can
greatly improve the accuracy of grape-cluster detection. Experimental
results in Table 3 can verify the necessity of generating Gaussian
kernels of the same size as grape clusters in the training of PMGDC.

4.3.3. Influence of image size on grape detection performance
While many previous dense object counting methods conducted
model training and testing without image scaling (Li et al., 2018;
Cheng et al., 2021), most object detection methods rescaled images to
a fixed and relatively small size before inputting them into a neural
network (Ren et al., 2015; Duan et al., 2019; Bochkovskiy et al., 2020).
Since PMGDC combines object detection of grape clusters and dense
object counting of grape berries in a single framework, to test the
grape detection and counting performance of this framework under
different image sizes, we resize the input grape images to multiple
sizes in training and testing, and the experimental results are shown
in Tables 4 and 5.
Overall, for the grape images with an original length of 2,048, the
detection performance of PMGDC for three subtasks of the grape de-
tection and counting task is better when the rescaled input images are
relatively larger. When the input image size is smaller than 1024 × 768,
the grape detection and counting performance of PMGDC drops off
a cliff. Since PMGDC detects grape clusters based on the localized
grape berries and the small image size makes it difficult for the neural
network to identify dense and small grape berries, the grape detection
and counting performance of PMGDC becomes worse when the input
image size is small. Experimental results in Tables 4 and 5 prove
the necessity of utilizing input images with relatively larger sizes in
PMGDC’s training and testing stages, while the detection performance
and time cost of PMGDC when detecting images with different sizes can
provide parameter setting references for the actual deployment and the
subsequent dataset migration of the proposed PMGDC framework.

as shown in Fig. 13. Gaussian kernels in grape-cluster probability maps

5. Discussion
In the current study, grape detection and counting are systemati-
cally analyzed, and the grape detection and counting task is defined as
the aggregation of grape-cluster detection tasks, grape-berry counting
tasks, and berry-per-grape-cluster counting tasks. We propose that the
three subtasks of grape detection and counting be performed simulta-
neously, which can fully utilize the close spatial distribution correlation
between grape clusters and grape berries. Based on this idea, to simulta-
neously accomplish the three subtasks of the grape detection and count-
ing task, we propose a method that utilizes probability maps to repre-
sent the spatial correlation between grape clusters and grape berries.
Based on this novel representation method, we design a PMGDC frame-
work that can simultaneously accomplish the tasks of grape-cluster
detection, grape-berry counting, and berry-per-grape-cluster counting.
Previous methods first detected grape clusters and then detected
grape berries within cluster regions to associate cluster and berry
features (Deng et al., 2020; Khoroshevsky et al., 2021). However, when
handling adjacent grape clusters, such methods detect grape berries
simultaneously contained within the bounding boxes of multiple grape
clusters multiple times, causing duplicate detection. Additionally, grape
berries not contained within any grape-cluster bounding box cannot
be detected. In contrast, the proposed PMGDC framework first detects
grape berries and then identifies which specific grape cluster each grape
berry uniquely belongs to. Subsequently, the framework further deter-
mines the region of each grape cluster, which can effectively eliminate
the issue of duplicate detection for grape berries. Furthermore, PMGDC
detects grape clusters based on grape-berry localization results, and
previous research has shown that computerized sensing methods often
perform better in predicting grape berries than in predicting grape
clusters (Nuske et al., 2014). Therefore, even without complex attention
and transformer modules, PMGDC’s performance in detecting grape
clusters is still not inferior to state-of-the-art methods.
While PMGDC can simultaneously complete three grape detection
tasks, it is not inferior to widely used object detection and dense

a localization performance of AP(iou0.5) 0.851 and a counting per-

counting performance of MRD 0.142, 1-FVU 0.865 for berries per grape
clusters on the test set of WGISD. The test results in open-world scenes
also corroborate the detection accuracy of PMGDC, demonstrating its
effectiveness in three subtasks of the grape detection and counting task
and the feasibility of integrating grape phenotype analysis from the
original three independent tasks into the unified grape detection and
counting task.
However, we have also noticed some issues with PMGDC. For
some adjacent grape clusters, PMGDC cannot accurately classify the
berries at the edges of the grape clusters into their actual clusters.
This is because WGISD does not provide instance segmentation anno-
tations for all grape clusters. Therefore, during the training process
of PMGDC, Gaussian kernels in ground-truth grape-cluster probability
maps used to represent the containment relationship between grape
clusters and grape berries are generated based on the bounding boxes
of the grape clusters. However, those ground-truth probability maps
cannot perfectly represent the containment relationship between grape
clusters and berries. In subsequent research, completing the grape-
cluster instance segmentation annotations in WGISD and generating
grape-cluster ground-truth probability maps based on those mask an-
notations, providing a more accurate representation of the containment
relationship between clusters and berries, may help alleviate such an
issue.
6. Conclusion
Previous studies treated the detection of grape clusters, grape berr-
ies, and berries contained in each grape cluster as separate tasks. How-
ever, considering the clustered phenotype feature of grapes, there is a
close spatial distribution correlation between grape clusters and grape
berries. In the present study, we propose integrating the grape-cluster
detection task, the grape-berry counting task, and the berry-per-grape-
cluster counting task as subtasks of the grape detection and counting
task, considering them simultaneously in grape detection. Based on this
idea, we propose a PMGDC framework. Experimental results demon-
strate that this framework can effectively complete the three subtasks
of the grape detection and counting task simultaneously.
However, this study also has certain limitations. First, our method
detects only visible grape clusters and grape berries, while occluded,
invisible grape berries are not discussed. Second, our research only
focuses on grape detection and counting, which are upstream tasks in
the field of digital viticulture. However, we do not apply the grape
detection results to downstream tasks such as predicting grape pick-
ing points and actual grape productions, which are more relevant to
practical production activities.
In future research, we will attempt to construct a larger grape
image dataset that can better meet the training requirements of the
grape-cluster detection task, the grape-berry counting task, and the
berry-per-grape-cluster counting task. Additionally, we will explore the
possibility of using a single neural network to simultaneously detect
three subtasks of the grape detection and counting tasks and to achieve
end-to-end simultaneous detection for those three tasks. Finally, we will
attempt to integrate the proposed PMGDC method with more practical
production tasks in the vineyard, truly achieving the goal of improving
grape production efficiency.
