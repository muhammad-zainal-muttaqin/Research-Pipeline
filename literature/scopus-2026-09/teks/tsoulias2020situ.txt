Abstract— The development of reliable fruit detection and 
localization systems has been approached with remote sensing 
methods aimed at optimizing the orchard management to 
obtain high crop quality and economically improved 
harvesting practice. This work presents a new technique that 
uses a light detection and ranging (LiDAR) laser scanner to 
detect and localize apple fruits in the orchard. In a 1 ha apple 
orchard  (Malus x domestica 'Gala') two trees were defoliated 
before harvest period. A LiDAR scanner emitting at 905 nm, 
with a real time kinematic global navigation satellite system to 
geo-reference the data and an inertial measurement unit to 
acquire orientation data were mounted on a tractor (0.2 km/h) 
to produce the 3D tree point cloud before and after defoliation. 
Subsequently, the apples of each tree were harvested and 
classified in four size classes according to height (Hmanual) and 
diameter (Dmanual).  
An intensity analysis of tree elements was performed, 
obtaining mean intensity values of 28.9%, 29.1%, and 44.3% 
for leaves, branches and trunks, and apples, respectively. 
These results suggested that the intensity parameter can be 
useful to detect apples.  A four-step fruit detection algorithm 
was developed to localize and estimate the height (HLiDAR) and 
diameter (DLiDAR) of fruits. A mean detection success of 92.5% 
was obtained in relation to the total amount of fruits on the 
defoliated trees during the stages of fruit development. A mean 
correlation of R2 = 0.83 was obtained for Hmanual and HLiDAR, 
whereas a less pronounced relation was observed between 
DLiDAR and Dmanual (R2 = 0.62) during fruit development. The 
mean detection success was decreased to 70.5% when the fruit 
detection algorithm was applied in the foliated trees. From the 
experimental results, it can be concluded that LiDAR-based 
technology and, particularly, its intensity information has 
potential for remote apple detection and 3D localisation. 
Keywords— Reflectance, point cloud, horticulture, laser 
scanner . 
I. INTRODUCTION  
The yield estimation in apple production is based on 
manual counting and weighting of fruit in tonnes. However, 
this method is labour intensive, while lacking spatial 
information [1]. Recently, development of fruit detection 
algorithms was approached either utilizing photogrammetry 
[2] or the spectral information [3]. Nevertheless, the two-
dimensional data lacks information, since the canopy 
development of the fruit tree extents in three dimensions 
(3D). Thus, it is challenging to overcome issues of occlusion 
and structure of the object, especially under field conditions.  
During the last decade, 3D remote sensing technologies 
have been emerged, allowing a more detailed monitoring of 
tree canopy geometry [4]. The surface reconstruction from 
3D point clouds provides objectivity and enhanced spatial 
resolution, while recording the object from different 
perspectives improves the accuracy in fruit localization on 
the tree. In some cases, combination of both colour and 
depth information, using RGB-D cameras, can enhance the 
segmentation results, enabling the discrimination of similar 
colour and depth to localize the fruit [5], or even fruit 
surface reconstruction [6], [7].  Wang et al., [8] used depth 
information to estimate the size of mango fruit, achieving 
root mean square error (RMSE) of 4.9 mm and 4.3 mm 
considering fruit length and width, respectively. However, 
RGB-D cameras are prone to noise, because they are 
influenced by daylight variation and their accuracy falls 
greatly with distance [1], [6]. 
Systems with active light source are light detection and 
ranging (LiDAR) laser scanners providing an alternative 
technology which can generate 3D data. Laser scanner 
produces high density 3D point clouds providing detailed 
information of tree geometry by means of the laser hits and 
additionally their backscattered intensity. This intensity of 
the detected hit represents the apparent backscattered 
reflectance (R) of the scanned object. The combination of 
geometric and intensity features are used broadly in forestry 
to discriminate leaves from woody parts and estimate the 
biomass [9], [10]. Gené-Mola et al., [7], used the R from a 
LiDAR 
laser 
scanner 
to 
distinguish 
and 
localize 
apples (Malus 
domestica Borkh. 
cv.

from 
the 
remaining elements of the tree, proposing a localization 
success of 87.5%, an identification success of 82.4%, and an 
F1-score of 0.858 in relation to the total amount of fruit. 
Moreover, the combination of intensity and point density in 
3D was used to improve the wheat biomass, filtering out the 
soil points [12]. This information can be utilized in crop 
phenotyping, segmenting organs traits such as cotton balls 
[13] or even sorghum panicles [14]. 
 The main objectives of this study were (i) to investigate 
if the apparent reflectance obtained by a LiDAR laser

II. MATERIAL AND METHODS 
A. Site description 
The 
experiment 
was 
conducted 
in 
a 
commercialorchard(65 ha) of Malus x domestica 'Gala' 
located in fruit production area near Brandenburg (Latitude: 
52.608° N, Longitude: 13.817 ° E), Germany, during 2019. 
The subject trees (n=2) were pruned in the commercially 
relevant formation as slender spindle on 4 horizontal parallel 
wires spaced 3.5x1.0 m, with maximum height of 2.8 m. 
The two analyzed trees were between 85-87 BBCH growth 
stage [15]. A drip irrigation system was installed along the 
tree rows. 
 
B. Fruit measurements 
The trees were defoliated at 85-87 BBCH growth stage 
in order to isolate the apples on the plant.Τhe wires of the 
construction were used as border to discriminate the fruit 
between (i) the ground and the first wire (WG-1), (ii) the first 
and the second wire (W1-2), (ii) the second and the third wire 
(W2-3) and the (ii) the third and the forth wire (W3-4). 
Subsequently, the total number of apples for each category 
were counted and harvested, while their height (HManual) was 
measured manually in the lab.  
 
C. LiDAR measurements 
A LiDAR system was mounted on a tractor and driven 
parallel to the defoliated trees of the orchard at constant 
speed (0.2 km h-1) and at 2.5 m distance from the row axis. 
The 2D-LiDAR (LMS511 pro model, Sick, Düsseldorf, 
Germany) sensor in this study had an angular resolution of 
0.1667°, scanning frequency of 100 Hz, scanning range of 
80 m and scanning angle of 270°. The height of the laser 
sensor (1.7 m) was approximately half the maximum height 
of the trees. A real time kinematic global navigation satellite 
system (AgGPS 542, Trimble, Sunnyvale, USA) with ± 25 
mm + 2 ppm horizontal and ± 37 mm + 2 ppm vertical 
accuracy was placed vertically next to the LiDAR to 
georeference 
each 
scan. 
Furthermore, 
an 
inertial 
measurement unit IMU (MTi-G-710, XSens, Ans Enschede, 
The Netherlands) was used to provide roll, pitch, and yaw 
information needed for deviation compensation of the 
sensor readings, necessary due to the movement of the 
tractor. The IMU worked at the same frequency with 
LiDAR, whilst the RTK-GNSS worked at 5 Hz. All data 
was firstly synchronised based on time stamped and 
secondly saved as a txt file. During the measurement, the 
scanner was pointed at a right angle to the forward direction 
of the tractor. Therefore, each tree was scanned from both 
sides.   
D. Data analysis 
Each side of tree point cloud was aligned and registered 
using the iterative closest point algorithm, while a random 
sample consensus algorithm was applied to remove the 
signals originating from the soil [11]. Empirical R 
thresholds were applied to segment the fruit points from the 
points obtained from woody parts such as stem and 
branches. The bivariate point density histograms of x-z and 
y-z axis were generated for the segmented fruit to localize 
its position (Matlab Version R2017a, Mathworks Inc., 
Natick, MA, USA) and classify the local fruit maxima. 
Consistently, the assumption was followed that the area 
closer to the apple position would appear with enhanced 
frequency characterizing a local maxima [11, 12]. The y-z 
bivariate histogram was used to analyse the height of each 
classified apple. The points in each histogram were initially 
binned based on the mean height of apples (72 mm) for each 
tree. Consistently, the assumption was followed that the area 
closer to the apple position would appear with enhanced 
frequency characterizing a local maxima [16, 17]. The 
binned points of lower frequency but connected with the 
local maxima were categorized in the same class. Thus, the 
height of each fruit was estimated by calculating the 
difference between the maximum and the minimum point in 
the z-axis (HLiDAR). Descriptive statistics capturing the mean 
absolute error (MAE), the bias (MBE) and the RMSE 
considering the actual and the estimated height of the apples 
were calculated in Matlab (Version R2017a, Mathworks 
Inc., Natick, MA, USA) [17][17]. 
 
III. RESULTS 
 
The edges of the objects where the LiDAR laser beam 
was scattered, including the metal wires (WE) resulted in an 
R between 0 to 1% (Figure 1). Most R values for the 
branches and the stem were indicated between 1.1 - 47.9 % 
and 48.6 – 78.0 %, respectively; the apple points ranged 
between 78.6 - 88.0 %. The values above 90% were mostly 
noise and therefore removed. The R thresholds allowed 
distinguishing apple points from the remaining tree parts of 
the 3D point cloud (Figure 2b). The segmented points of 
WE, branches, stem and apple occupied 7.01%, 27.07%, 
44.44% and 17.67% of the total amount of points in both 
trees, respectively.

Applying the bivariate method each individual apple was 
identified in W2-3 and W3-4, where the apples were 
distributed more sparsely. More specifically, the HManual was 
correlated with HLiDAR (R2adj = 0.72) in W2-3 with 3.31 mm 
RMSE and 2.33 mm MBE. Whereas the apples in W3-4 area 
revealed a lower correlation (R2adj= 0.65), with 41.23 % 
RMSE and 1.33 MBE.  Malambo et al., [14] utilized the 
point density to estimate the height of sorghum panicles, 
reporting a correlation coefficient of r = 0.86 with RMSE = 
11.4 cm. Despite the 62.5 % and 66.7 % localization 
success, no correlation was indicated between Hmanual and 
HLiDAR in WG-1 (R2adj =0.45) and W1-2 (R2adj =0.45) areas, 
respectively. Tsoulias et al., [18] estimated the diameter of 
apples, at different growth stages, in foliated and defoliated 
tress combining  the backscattered reflectance from a 
LiDAR and geometrical features. The LiDAR estimated 
diameter was related with the reference values, revealing an 
adjusted coefficient of determination (R2adj) of 0.95 and 
RMSE of 9.5% at harvest stage. When comparing the results 
obtained on foliated and defoliated tree’s data, the 
maximum of 88.2% precision, 91.0% recall, and 89.5 F1 
score were observed at harvest stage, where the fruit was 
fully developed.  
IV. CONCLUSION 
 
 A LiDAR system was used to scan defoliated apple 
trees and extract their apparent backscattered reflectance. 
The derived R achieved efficiently discrimination and 
segmention of the apple points from the woody parts. The 
local maxima of each fruit was determined utilizing the 
bivariate histograms in x-z and y-z axis to classify the 
apples points. The HLiDAR was correlated with HManual 
(R2adj=0.72) in W2-3 region, where the apples seem to be 
more distinct from coinciding points of the branches. 
However, the limitation of this methodology was illustrated 
in regions where the density of apples was increased. The 
proposed methodology needs to be validated in real-world 
canopies having varying pruning systems.
