A B S T R A C T
Fruit detection and 3D localization have been shown to be critical for precision agriculture applications, such 
as yield estimation and robot harvesting tasks. However, real-world agricultural settings can be associated with 
high density of fruits, strong occlusions, and variable illumination, leading to difficulty in accurately detecting 
and localizing fruits. Although there exist 2D approaches dedicated for fruit counting, these approaches may 
easily merge adjacent fruits due to projection effects in dense occlusions, thus limiting their effectiveness. 
Motivated by this, in this paper, we explore the problem of 3D fruit detection and localization with vision 
foundation model adaptation and 3D Gaussian-based scene representation. Particularly, we use low-rank 
adaptation to leverage SAM3 to generate high-quality instance masks with different crops and illuminations. 
Meanwhile, to address noisy pseudo label issue, we adopt cross-view contrastive learning with uncertainty 
weighting for enhanced 3D feature representation. Moreover, we devise a Hough voting procedure in the 
3D Gaussian space to integrate multi-view evidence of occluded fruits, followed by clustering to estimate 
the number of fruits as well as their 3D locations. The proposed approach has been extensively tested with 
thousands of fruits from synthetic orchard scenes, real apple orchard datasets, as well as greenhouse sweet 
pepper scenes. Experiment results show significant performance gains for densely occluded cases, with the 
F1-scores reaching 0.971 and 0.977 for plum and mango, respectively. Meanwhile, our approach can achieve 
more than a twofold boost for 3D instance segmentation over FruitNeRF. More notably, the adapted weights 
and the post-processing settings transfer from the open-field apple orchard to greenhouse sweet pepper scenes, 
where only the SAM3 text prompt is changed and the 3D Gaussian representation is still optimized for each 
new scene.
1. Introduction
Modern precision agriculture heavily utilizes automated systems 
to facilitate the observation and analysis of crop traits across differ-
ent field environments (Tian et al., 2020). Fruit counting is crucial, 
directly impacting yield estimation, harvest planning, and robotic op-
erations (Tu et al., 2025). In actual production, manual counting is not 
only time-consuming but also often unreliable in large-scale orchard 
scenarios. Therefore, vision-based methods have been widely adopted, 
primarily due to the rapid development of deep learning in object 
detection tasks.

models (VFMs), agricultural perception tasks have become increasingly

Early research primarily relied on handcrafted features or depth 
detectors to achieve 2D image detection from a single RGB image 
(Sapkota et al., 2024; Kang and Chen, 2020). These methods performed 
well under controlled conditions, but their performance significantly 
degraded in real orchard or greenhouse environments. In real-world 
scenes, fruits are typically densely distributed and often occlude one 
another, while short-term illumination variations can be drastic, with 
adjacent fruits sometimes separated by only a few centimeters or even 
overlapping (Gené-Mola et al., 2020). Representative examples include 
plum scenes in FruitNeRF (Meyer et al., 2024) and apple scenes in 
Fuji-SfM (Gené-Mola et al., 2020).
Recent studies on fruit counting and yield estimation based on the 
YOLO family of detectors have achieved substantial progress in tasks 
such as young apple fruit detection (Z. Liu et al., 2024), grape picking-
point localization (Wang et al., 2025), passion fruit yield estimation (Tu 
et al., 2025), cotton boll number prediction (Tan et al., 2025), and 
lightweight detection networks for orchard deployment (Song et al., 
2025; Zhang et al., 2025). However, most existing approaches still rely 
on single-view detection or video-tracking paradigms, which exhibit 
limited robustness under severe occlusion among adjacent fruits (Liu 
et al., 2025). As highlighted by Z. Li et al. (2025) in a recent review 
of fruit 3D reconstruction, the transition from 2D detection to 3D 
perception is becoming an important trend in precision agriculture for 
addressing densely occluded fruit scenes.
Multi-view geometry offers a potential solution to this problem by 
improving reliability through the fusion of observations from differ-
ent perspectives. Structure from Motion (SfM) can reconstruct cam-
era poses and sparse scene geometry, thereby enabling the alignment 
of detection results across multiple views (Gené-Mola et al., 2020). 
Neural scene representation methods further extend this approach. 
FruitNeRF (Meyer et al., 2024), for example, combines segmentation 
with volumetric rendering to reduce duplicate counting to some extent. 
However, its high computational cost and lengthy optimization process 
limit its applicability in practical agricultural workflows (Z. Li et al., 
2025).
In recent years, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 2023) 
has gained increasing attention as a more efficient scene representation. 
It uses explicit Gaussian primitives to describe the scene, each carrying 
information on position, shape, opacity, and color. Compared to the im-
plicit model NeRF, this representation renders faster and facilitates geo-
metric inference. In agricultural applications, these Gaussian primitives 
can be filtered or grouped to identify individual fruits without relying 
on expensive volume queries (Jiang et al., 2025). Existing research has 
applied this technology to plant phenotypic analysis, such as cotton 
boll localization and strawberry reconstruction (Jiang et al., 2025; 
J. Li et al., 2025). Regarding vision foundation models, the Segment 
Anything Model (SAM) (Kirillov et al., 2023) has demonstrated stable 
zero-shot segmentation capability, while its successor SAM3 (Carion 
et al., 2025) further supports text-driven open-vocabulary instance 
segmentation, allowing segmentation tasks to be guided by natural lan-
guage descriptions. Early studies adapting vision foundation models to 
agricultural applications have shown promising potential. For example, 
Devanna et al. (2025) combined SAM with Grounding DINO for zero-
shot annotation of grape bunches; Tan et al. (2025) integrated SAM 
with a density-map model for cotton boll counting; and Li et al. (2026) 
applied SAM to agricultural field parcel extraction through adaptive 
mask refinement. However, directly applying these general-purpose 
foundation models to dense fruit scenes remains challenging. The visual 
distribution of orchards and greenhouses differs substantially from that 
of the natural images used for pre-training: fruits frequently occlude 
each other, shadows and illumination variations blur object bound-
aries, and dense spatial distributions make instance separation more 
difficult. These factors collectively introduce domain bias and reduce 
segmentation accuracy.
Training such models from scratch is computationally expensive 
and requires large-scale annotations, making it impractical for most 
agricultural scenarios. In contrast, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu 
et al., 2022) provides a parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategy that 
updates only a small number of parameters while effectively mitigating 
domain bias. In this work, we adapt SAM3 using LoRA to improve its 
segmentation performance in agricultural scenes while preserving its 
general visual knowledge. Nevertheless, improving 2D segmentation 
alone is insufficient, because the resulting masks are further used for 
3D reconstruction. If the masks contain noise or if instance labels are 
inconsistent across different viewpoints, these errors can propagate into 
the 3D representation and ultimately affect fruit counting. To address 
this issue, we tightly integrate cross-view 3D reasoning with the vision

foundation model. Specifically, we use 3D Gaussian representations to 
aggregate multi-view information, and design an uncertainty-weighted 
cross-view contrastive learning strategy to reduce the influence of noisy

cross-view spatial cues, enabling stable fruit center estimation even 
under occlusion.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. 
First, we propose SAM3-Adapt, a LoRA-based parameter-efficient adap-
tation method for improving fruit instance segmentation with SAM3 
in agricultural scenes. It keeps the general representation ability of 
the vision foundation model while reducing the domain gap caused 
by agricultural images. Second, we introduce an uncertainty-aware 
cross-view contrastive learning module, which reduces the effect of 
noisy pseudo-labels on 3D feature learning and makes fruit instance 
features more consistent across views. Third, for fruit counting and 3D 
localization under occlusion, we add a Hough voting mechanism to 
the 3D Gaussian representation. By combining spatial evidence from 
multiple views, it helps estimate fruit centers and separate instances 
more reliably. In addition, we build a fruit perception dataset covering 
different agricultural scenes and crop types to evaluate the method 
under different environments. Finally, by applying the same adapted 
weights and post-processing settings from open-field orchards to green-
house sweet pepper scenes, with only the text prompt changed, we 
demonstrate its transferability for cross-crop 3D fruit perception.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Dataset construction and preprocessing
We constructed datasets for three types of agricultural scenarios: 
synthetic scenes, open-air apple orchards, and greenhouses. Experi-
ments were conducted using 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 
2023) and the vision foundation model SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025) to 
examine the model’s performance under different lighting conditions, 
occlusion levels, and fruit densities, while also verifying the transfer-
ability of the proposed method across different visual environments.
2.1.1. Multi-source dataset integration
Although computer vision in agriculture has progressed rapidly in 
recent years (Tian et al., 2020), high-quality 3D agricultural datasets 
remain scarce. Existing works mainly address 2D detection (Sapkota 
et al., 2024; Z. Liu et al., 2024) or segmentation (Kang and Chen, 2020), 
and therefore provide limited geometric information (Z. Li et al., 2025). 
Collection of 3D agricultural datasets is expensive, and it requires 
a multi-view collection procedure, precise calibration, and a stable 
reconstruction algorithm. In addition, different datasets may use differ-
ent sensors, making comparisons between datasets challenging. In this 
work, we combine three complementary public agricultural datasets, 
each of which represents various agricultural scenes and difficulties. 
Basic statistical properties of the used datasets are listed in Table 1.
The FruitNeRF (Meyer et al., 2024) dataset contains images of 
synthetic and real-world scenes. Its synthetic part was created using 
Blender, and includes six classes of fruits: apple, plum, lemon, pear, 
peach, and mango, providing 2942 annotated fruit instances, along 
with exact 3D location annotations. Images of 583 real-world fruit 
instances taken using the Nikon D7100 camera at a resolution of 
6000 × 4000 were also included in this dataset. The dataset provides 
relatively simple scenes with clearly defined geometric structure and 
stable camera motion which makes it good for evaluating performance 
of the considered tasks under controlled conditions. However, it has 
low diversity of scenes.
The Fuji-SfM (Gené-Mola et al., 2020) dataset consists of images 
taken from a commercial Fuji apple orchard. In contrast to the Fruit-
NeRF dataset, this one includes a much higher diversity of outdoor 
scenes that have varying lighting conditions, dense foliage and oc-
clusions. It includes images of one outdoor scene taken using a Sony 
Alpha 6000 camera at a resolution of 4000 × 3000 which contain 1455 
annotated fruit instances. Due to the absence of annotations on exact 
3D fruit locations, the dataset becomes challenging for solving tasks of 
fruit instance segmentation, localization, and 3D scene understanding.
The BUP20 (Pan et al., 2023) dataset was acquired using a mobile 
robot with an attached Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D sensor, capturing 
RGB-D images and odometry information in a greenhouse environment 
with sweet peppers. This dataset differs from Fuji-SfM in the presence 
of depth cues and an indoor structured environment, making it a 
good choice for multimodal perception evaluation. However, it lacks 
annotated fruit instances and 3D fruit location labels.
Overall, these three datasets complement each other well. Fruit-
NeRF provides accurate geometric annotations on controlled scenes, 
while Fuji-SfM brings challenging outdoor orchard conditions. In turn, 
BUP20 dataset adds RGB-D information from a greenhouse environ-
ment to our benchmark.
2.1.2. Data standardization and instance annotation
The datasets used in this article are sourced from different sources, 
and the formats of the sensor and dataset vary. To ensure data con-
sistency, we first converted all camera parameters to a COLMAP-
compatible format. For data sets that contain depth, for example 
BUP20, the 3D points were created using back-projection of the depth 
map. However, for RGB data only, as in Fuji-SfM, the cameras were 
first estimated and point clouds were then built using COLMAP.
With respect to the generation of ground truth (GT) for each dataset, 
we took various strategies depending on the annotation conditions of 
each dataset. In the case of FruitNeRF’s synthetic subset, we directly 
utilized the official annotation information in Blender, which includes 
fruit instance’s identity and 3D coordinate. Hence, these annotations do 
not depend on our method and are not generated from SAM3 results.
For real-world datasets where no complete 3D annotations can be 
found, we followed a semi-automatic annotation procedure as depicted 
in Fig. 2. Firstly, we employed SAM3 to get rough candidate instance 
segmentation results, and then manually corrected each image by 
using VGG Image Annotator (Dutta and Zisserman, 2019). The process 
includes excluding false positives, adding missing fruits, splitting stuck 
instances in dense regions, and correcting inaccurate instance masks. 
We would like to note that the predicted results from SAM3 were used 
only for initialization purpose but not directly taken as ground truth.
In addition, we manually refined masks in heavily occluded and 
dense regions by using the combination of multiview information and 
reconstructed 3D structure, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Specifically, we keep 
a fruit when it is occluded in one view but is visible in other views 
and is consistent with the 3D reconstruction result. And, if an instance 
cannot be distinguished at all, then it will be removed from GT for 3D 
localization evaluation.
2.2. SAM3 in agricultural scenes
2.2.1. Limitations of open-set detection for 3D fruit reconstruction
Closed-set detectors are unsuitable for 3D fruit reconstruction, be-
cause they require every target category to be fixed before training and 
degrade sharply once the deployment distribution shifts. Detectors of 
the YOLO family (Wang et al., 2024) perform stably under a known 
data distribution, but their generalization ability decreases markedly in 
unfamiliar environments or for new species unless data are re-collected 
and the model is fine-tuned. Open-vocabulary detection alleviates this 
problem by locating objects of unseen categories from textual cues. 
Grounding DINO (S. Liu et al., 2024) is a representative method: it 
aligns visual and linguistic features so that detection no longer depends 
on a predefined category list, which gives it strong semantic generaliza-
tion ability. However, directly applying such open-vocabulary detection 
methods to 3D reconstruction still has significant limitations. A key 
issue is the relatively coarse-grained output format, primarily providing

2.2.2. Adapting SAM3 to agricultural scenes
Text-prompted segmentation alone is insufficient for agricultural 
imagery, because foundation models pre-trained on generic natural 
images underperform task-specific supervised models when the target 
domain has distinctive visual statistics (Kirillov et al., 2023; Li et al., 
2023). SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025) employs a text-based segmentation 
mechanism, in which a natural-language description drives pixel-level 
localization and segmentation of all matching instances in an image. 
Compared with interactive paradigms that rely on manual clicks or 
bounding boxes, this mechanism generalizes better in open-vocabulary 
settings. Its pre-training data, however, consist mainly of generic nat-
ural images, so zero-shot segmentation in a specialized domain often 
falls short of expectations.
The visual characteristics of agricultural scenes further amplify this 
problem. In orchards and greenhouses, fruits of the same type have 
highly similar colors and textures, resulting in severe mutual occlusion, 
making it difficult to distinguish instance boundaries in dense areas. 
Under these conditions, SAM3’s text-based prompt mechanism strug-
gles to effectively separate neighboring fruits, easily leading to mask 
adhesion or boundary drift, and failing to provide stable and consistent 
pixel-level supervision for subsequent multi-view 3DGS segmentation. 
In addition, the appearance of fruits varies greatly within a class due to 
differences in variety, growth stage and light conditions. General text 
descriptions are difficult to fully cover these fine-grained out-of-domain 
samples, which naturally affects the robustness of segmentation (Gurav 
et al., 2023).
2.2.3. Adaptive vision foundation model based on low-rank fine-tuning
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning offers a practical route to close this 
domain gap, because it adapts a foundation model to a new visual do-
main at a small fraction of the cost of full fine-tuning while preserving 
the pre-trained representation (Hu et al., 2022). We therefore employ 
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) to fine-tune the vision 
foundation model SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025). LoRA does not update all 
parameters of the model; it inserts small learnable matrices and keeps 
the pre-trained weights fixed, so the model can absorb agricultural 
data without losing its general visual capability. For a weight matrix

The LoRA rank ablation demonstrates that parameter-efficient adap-

a small number of trainable parameters. Even at a low rank of 𝑟= 2,

negligible improvement in the overall average mIoU. When the rank is 
further increased to 𝑟= 32, the average mIoU decreases to 0.846, while 
the training time increases by approximately 76%. Therefore, we adopt 
𝑟= 8 as the default LoRA rank in our experiments.
Training is divided into two phases. In the first phase, all pre-
training parameters are fixed, and only the LoRA module is updated. 
This allows the model to learn domain-specific content, such as dense 
regions and different lighting conditions. In the second phase, the last 
and penultimate layers of the image encoder are unfrozen and fine-
tuned together. This allows learning higher-level information without 
altering the lower-level content.

2.2.4. Evaluating adaptation of vision foundation models
After LoRA fine-tuning, SAM3 produces more stable and spatially 
consistent instance masks in both synthetic and real agricultural scenes, 
especially in densely occluded regions, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5. 
We compare our method with two segmentation baselines used for 
agricultural scenes: the original SAM3 and Grounded-SAM2, which 
pairs Grounding DINO with a SAM-family segmenter (Ren et al., 2024). 
For a fair comparison, all methods use the corresponding fruit category 
names as text prompts. The evaluation is carried out on four datasets, 
including FruitNeRF synthetic scenes, FruitNeRF real-world samples, 
the Fuji-SfM orchard dataset, and the BUP20 greenhouse dataset.
From the qualitative results, the adapted model gives more complete 
masks and separates nearby fruits more clearly than the baselines. This 
is especially noticeable when fruits are clustered together or partly oc-
cluded, where instance separation is difficult. By contrast, the original 
SAM3 and Grounded-SAM2 often produce broken masks or overlapping 
instance regions, which makes the segmentation less reliable. These re-
sults suggest that LoRA fine-tuning helps the foundation segmentation 
model better fit agricultural scenes with dense fruit layouts and strong 
occlusion.
Although LoRA adaptation improves the quality of 2D fruit masks, 
the generated pseudo-labels still contain errors. In heavily occluded 
regions, mask boundaries may be inaccurate. Fruits with colors close 
to leaves, branches, or the background may also be missed or wrongly 
classified. In addition, because the appearance of the same fruit changes 
across viewpoints, its pseudo-labels may not always be consistent in dif-
ferent views. These issues make fully reliable 2D supervision difficult to 
obtain in real agricultural scenes. If such noisy masks are directly used 
for 3D representation learning, the supervision may become biased 
and affect feature learning as well as instance separation. To reduce 
this problem, we use two complementary mechanisms. The contrastive 
learning module in Section 2.3.1 weakens the influence of unreliable 
samples and helps learn more discriminative instance-level features. At 
the same time, the multi-view voting strategy in Section 2.3.3 combines 
observations from different views to improve spatial consistency. Al-
though these mechanisms cannot remove all segmentation errors, they 
reduce the effect of noisy 2D masks and provide more stable guidance 
for 3D fruit localization, centroid estimation, and instance prototype 
construction.

after multi-view mask fusion. Even after adaptation, the SAM3 masks 
still show blurred boundaries, missing small fruits, and leaf occlusions.

center through the supervision of projected centroids of 2D masks. 
As a result, the learned voting response is more robust for instance 
grouping rather than accurate geometric center estimation. For fruits 
with irregular or elongated shapes such as sweet peppers, the visible 
centroid position can be further affected by occlusion and viewpoint 
changes. To address this, multi-view voting points are aggregated in 3D 
and used as clustering cues, which reduces the offset caused by single-
view occlusion and shape irregularity. The experiments on the BUP20 
sweet pepper dataset have shown that the voting-refined points can be 
clustered into separate fruit instances.

The complete objective function integrates reconstruction, feature 
learning, voting, and clustering into a single loss function:

in Section 2.3.3, simultaneously producing the fruit count and the 3D 
center location of each fruit, which together provide complete counting 
and spatial information for downstream tasks.

2.4.1. Training hyperparameters

follows. During the SAM3 LoRA adaptation stage, we use the AdamW

is set to 𝑟= 8, with a scaling factor of 𝛼= 16 and a dropout rate of 0.1. 
For 3DGS optimization, the model is trained for 30,000 iterations. The

and 3D reconstruction can take a large amount of time in large orchard 
scenes. Therefore, we also report the running time of each part of the 
full pipeline. For the Tree03 scene with 300 input frames, COLMAP 
sparse reconstruction takes about 38 min, 2D mask inference with the 
adapted SAM3 model takes about 12 min, and 3DGS training for 30k 
iterations takes around 1.5 h. For the larger Fuji-SfM scene with 1455 
fruit instances, COLMAP sparse reconstruction takes nearly 4 h, which 
is about 45% of the total pipeline time. This shows that camera pose 
estimation is still a major time-consuming step in large-scale 3D fruit 
counting.
The SAM3 LoRA adaptation stage takes about 3 h. However, this 
cost is only needed once and is not tied to a specific test scene. After 
training, the LoRA weights can be reused directly for different orchard 
or greenhouse scenes. Therefore, this stage is not counted as repeated 
per-scene cost when reporting the running time of a single scene. The 
overall efficiency comparison of different methods is reported in Table 
4.
End to end, the proposed method completed the full pipeline in 
2.4 h on the 300-frame scene, compared with 3.3 h for FruitNeRF 
and 8.9 h for FruitNeRF++ (Table 4). Each pipeline comprised three 
stages: COLMAP-based sparse reconstruction (SfM), scene optimization, 
and post-processing, including mask inference, point-cloud or feature 
export, clustering, and counting. COLMAP was run independently for 
each method using identical input images and reconstruction parame-
ters. Under this common protocol, SfM and post-processing required 
35–38 min and 13–18 min, respectively, across the three methods. 
Rendering throughput was measured only after scene optimization and 
excluded offline voting and HDBSCAN clustering; it therefore should 
not be interpreted as end-to-end efficiency. Benefiting from the explicit 
3DGS representation, the proposed method achieves approximately 
110 FPS, which is about two orders of magnitude faster than NeRF-
based methods. This substantial improvement in rendering efficiency 
is important for future deployment in real-world agricultural scenar-
ios, where fast visualization and repeated scene inspection are often 
required. Consistent with the timings above, camera pose estimation re-
mains a substantial fraction of the total cost and becomes the dominant 
bottleneck in larger scenes such as Fuji-SfM.
3.2. Fruit counting performance
3.2.1. Synthetic dataset results
On the FruitNeRF synthetic dataset, our method performs better 
than FruitNeRF in all six fruit categories, and the improvement is more 
obvious in dense scenes, as shown in Table 5. In the apple scene, our 
method detects all 283 fruits and obtains an F1-score of 1.000. The 
difference becomes larger when fruits are highly clustered. In the plum 
scene, there are 781 fruit instances, and the distance between nearby 
fruits is smaller than 5 cm. FruitNeRF detects only 315 fruits with 
an F1-score of 0.575, mainly because overlapping projections tend to 
merge several fruits into one detection. By comparison, our method 
detects 741 fruits and reaches an F1-score of 0.971, giving a relative 
improvement of 68.9%. A similar result can be found in the mango 
scene, which contains 1150 heavily occluded instances. FruitNeRF 
detects 807 fruits with an F1-score of 0.816, while our method detects 
1123 fruits and obtains an F1-score of 0.977.
Table 5 additionally reports the absolute counting error and the 3D 
center localization error; for FruitNeRF, both are computed from its 
released predictions under the protocol of Section 2.4.2, so the two 
column groups are directly comparable. Averaged over the six cate-
gories, the mean absolute counting error is 13.67 fruits for the proposed 
method and 139.17 fruits for FruitNeRF. The 3D center localization 
error ranges from 0.21 to 0.56 cm for the proposed method and from 
0.34 to 0.88 cm for FruitNeRF, and the proposed method is closer to 
the ground-truth center in every category, with the largest margin in 
the heavily occluded plum and mango scenes. Precision, Recall, and F1 
are reported alongside the absolute error because a correct count does 
not imply that every instance is matched correctly: in the lemon scene 
the count predicted by FruitNeRF equals the ground truth exactly, yet 
about six instances remain incorrectly matched.
3.2.2. Real-world dataset results
On the four real-world scenes, SAM3-Adapt gives counting results 
close to the ground truth, with errors within four fruits. It also shows 
clear advantages in the densely occluded Tree03 scene and the BUP20 
scene where the annotations are less precise, as reported in Table 6 and 
Fig. 8. For Tree01, our method detects 175 fruits, compared with 173 
from FruitNeRF and 179 ground-truth fruits. For Tree03, our method 
detects 290 fruits, very close to the ground truth of 291, whereas 
FruitNeRF detects only 264 fruits. In the large-scale Fuji-SfM scene with 
1455 fruit instances, our method detects 1450 fruits, which is close to 
FruitNeRF with 1459 detections. In the BUP20 greenhouse scene, our 
method detects 107 sweet peppers, while FruitNeRF detects only 83. As 
shown in Fig. 8, we further compare our method with the Grounded-
SAM2 and SAM3 baselines, whose 2D masks are fed into the same 
3DGS-based voting and clustering pipeline to obtain their 3D counts; 
our method produces counts consistently closer to the ground truth 
across all scenes.

3.2.3. Ablation study
To separately examine the independent contributions of the four 
core components, namely LoRA adaptation, cross-view contrastive 
learning, clustering loss, and Hough voting, we conduct a systematic 
ablation study on the two most challenging scenes, plum and mango, 
from the FruitNeRF synthetic dataset. The baseline configuration al-
ready uses LoRA-adapted SAM3 together with 3DGS and DBSCAN, 
in order to isolate the contribution of the 3D components. Other 
configurations progressively introduce additional components based on 
this baseline. The results are reported in Table 7.
The ablation study confirms that each introduced component con-
tributes positively to the final performance, as reported in Table 7. 
Specifically, contrastive learning, clustering loss, and Hough voting 
improve the average F1-score by +0.063, +0.033, and +0.051, respec-
tively. Hough voting shows a particularly notable effect in the densely 
occluded plum scene, where the F1-score increases by +0.077 from 
configuration (C) to configuration (D). This result highlights the impor-
tance of explicit 3D center voting for reducing the ambiguity caused by 
2D projection overlap. When all three components are combined, the 
full method improves the average F1-score by +0.147 compared with 
the baseline.
3.2.4. 3D instance segmentation performance
To further evaluate the 3D reasoning ability of the proposed method, 
we report explicit 3D instance-level evaluation results in this section. 
The evaluation protocol is described in Section 2.4.2. Specifically, we 
compute the 3D IoU between each predicted 3D fruit instance and the 
corresponding ground-truth instance. TP, FP, and FN are determined 
under two IoU thresholds, 0.25 and 0.50, and five metrics are then 
reported, including mIoU, mAcc, mAP, AP@0.25, and AP@0.50. Since 
real-world scenes lack accurate 3D instance-level ground truth, this 
evaluation is conducted on the most challenging plum scene from the 
FruitNeRF synthetic dataset. The results are shown in Table 8.

4. Discussion
4.1. Counting performance and scene complexity
Experiments on synthetic and real datasets show that the effective-
ness of this method is largely determined by the density and occlusion 
of fruits in the scene. The best improvement is achieved when fruits are 
very close together, with their boundaries almost completely overlap-
ping on two 2D images. For example, in the plum scene, the F1 score 
improved from 0.575 to 0.971. This improvement is mainly related 
to the difference between counting from 2D projections and voting 
for centers in 3D space. Methods such as FruitNeRF (Meyer et al., 
2024) mainly count fruits from rendered 2D masks. When fruits are 
dense or strongly occluded, several nearby fruits may appear as one 
connected region in the 2D projection, which easily leads to under-
counting. By contrast, our method introduces Hough voting into the 
3DGS representation and uses the 3D fruit centers for counting. In 
this way, fruits that overlap in the image can still be separated in 3D 
space. As a result, the method does not fully depend on complete 2D 
object boundaries, but distinguishes instances through their 3D center 
responses. This change from 2D mask aggregation to 3D center voting 
explains the clear improvement in dense and complex scenes.
Compared with recent studies on 3D agricultural perception (Jiang 
et al., 2025; J. Li et al., 2025; Meyer et al., 2025), the proposed method 
combines foundation model adaptation, cross-view contrastive learn-
ing, and 3D voting in one framework. It keeps the real-time rendering 
advantage of 3DGS, with about 110 FPS, while adding instance segmen-
tation and 3D counting ability. Existing methods are often limited by 
slower inference, weaker domain adaptation, or insufficient handling 
of fruit counting under dense occlusion. Compared with recent high-
performance 2D detection methods (Z. Liu et al., 2024; Tu et al., 2025; 
Song et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), our method uses 
a 3D representation as an intermediate counting layer. Therefore, it can 
estimate the fruit number and also provide 3D center coordinates for 
robotic harvesting and spatial analysis.
4.2. Contribution of each loss term
The ablation study gives a closer look at how the three losses work 
during training. Fig. 11 plots the fruit-count curves under three training 
settings on four real-world scenes, namely Tree01, Tree02, Tree03, and 
Fuji-SfM. When only the contrastive loss is used, the count changes 
slowly and is often higher than the ground truth. In most cases, it 
becomes stable only after 25,000 iterations. This shows that cross-
view feature consistency is helpful for matching the same fruit across 
views, but it does not give a clear constraint on the 3D position of 
fruit centers. Adding the clustering loss makes the training process more 
stable. The predicted count usually settles between 15,000 and 20,000 
iterations. This is because features from the same fruit are pulled closer, 
while features from different fruits are pushed apart. Still, when fruits 
are very close or heavily occluded, nearby instances may not be fully 
separated, which can cause small counting errors. After the voting loss 
guided by 2D mask centroids is included, the count becomes stable 
earlier, usually within 10,000–15,000 iterations. The final counting 
error is within 2%. This trend can also be seen from the green curves 
in Fig. 11, where the predicted counts move toward the gray dashed 
ground-truth lines more quickly. This indicates that the voting loss 
provides a clearer cue for learning 3D fruit centers, especially in dense 
scenes. More quantitative results are listed in Table 7.
4.3. Transferability across crop types
To further evaluate the transferability of the proposed framework 
across different crop types and cultivation environments, we apply it 
to the greenhouse sweet pepper dataset BUP20, in addition to the 
two orchard apple datasets. Compared with orchard apples, sweet pep-
pers exhibit substantial differences in plant morphology, fruit appear-
ance, spatial distribution, and imaging conditions. Apples are typically 
grown in open-field orchard environments, whereas sweet peppers are 
cultivated under more controlled greenhouse conditions. Therefore, 
BUP20 provides a representative test case for assessing the cross-crop 
generalizability of the proposed framework.
The experimental results show that the adapted weights and the 
post-processing settings transfer from orchard apples to greenhouse 
sweet peppers: only the SAM3 text prompt is changed, while the LoRA 
weights and the clustering configuration are kept unchanged, and the 
3D Gaussian representation is still optimized for every new scene. This 
indicates that the method is not limited to orchard apple datasets, but 
can be transferred to crop types with different visual characteristics 
and growing environments. From the perspective of crop structure, or-
chard apples and greenhouse sweet peppers still share certain common 
properties: in both cases, fruits are generally located within observable 
canopy regions, and sufficient spatial information can be obtained 
from multi-view images. These shared properties provide necessary 
conditions for cross-view feature learning and 3D voting-based in-
stance localization, which partly explains why the framework can be 
transferred from orchard apple scenes to greenhouse sweet pepper 
scenes.
However, the applicability of the proposed method to other crop 
systems still requires further investigation. In particular, vine crops 
such as grapes and kiwifruits usually have more complex spatial struc-
tures, with fruits densely distributed in clusters and very small dis-
tances between adjacent instances. In addition, intertwined branches, 
leaves, and fruit occlusions may further increase the difficulty of in-
stance separation, center localization, and cross-view consistency mod-
eling. Moreover, trellis structures and constrained acquisition paths 
may limit the available viewpoints, thereby reducing the stability of 
3D reconstruction and fruit localization.
It should be noted that the main components of the proposed 
framework, including LoRA-based vision foundation model adaptation, 
cross-view contrastive learning, and 3D Hough voting, do not rely on 
fixed geometric templates or size priors for a specific crop. Therefore, 
the method has the potential to be extended to other crop types. 
For vine crops, future work may further incorporate local geometric 
constraints, cluster-level instance modeling, or separation strategies 
designed for densely distributed fruits, in order to improve robustness 
under complex canopy structures and severe occlusions. Recent studies 
on grape and kiwifruit detection and yield prediction (Devanna et al., 
2025; Wang et al., 2025) also provide useful references for extending 
the proposed framework to vine crop scenarios.
4.4. Limitations
Quantitative evaluation of 3D localization was restricted to the 
synthetic FruitNeRF scenes because comparable annotations are un-
available for real-world datasets. Neither Fuji-SfM nor BUP20 provides 
reliable 3D fruit centers or instance-level labels; evaluation on these 
datasets therefore focused primarily on counting accuracy. Real-world 
benchmarks containing 3D fruit centers and instance-level annotations 
are needed to assess localization and segmentation under field condi-
tions. Computational scalability also remains a substantial constraint 
for orchard-scale deployment. Processing a medium-sized tree required 
several hours, while COLMAP pose estimation alone required approx-
imately 4 h for the large-scale Fuji-SfM scene. Moreover, each scene 
requires a separately optimized 3DGS model, limiting scalability to 
commercial orchards containing thousands of trees. The framework 
also inherits the static-scene assumption of 3DGS, which may not hold 
under field conditions. Wind-induced motion, illumination changes, 
and specular reflections from irrigation or dew may reduce geometric 
and photometric consistency across views.

More efficient camera-pose estimation may reduce the dominant

and global SfM methods, including DROID-SLAM (Teed and Deng, 
2021), MASt3R-SfM (Duisterhof et al., 2025), and GLOMAP (Pan et al., 
2024), offer potential alternatives to COLMAP. Integrating these ap-
proaches with online 3DGS optimization warrants investigation as a 
route toward deployment on agricultural robotic platforms. Evaluation 
on vine crops such as grapes and kiwifruit would provide a more strin-
gent test of the framework under dense clustering and severe occlusion. 
Such scenes complicate fruit-center definition, instance separation, and 
occlusion modeling across multiple views. Finally, cross-scene pretrain-
ing and more generalizable 3DGS representations may reduce per-scene 
optimization and improve transfer across scenes and crop types.
5. Conclusions
This study addresses fruit counting and 3D localization in orchards 
and greenhouses, where dense clustering, occlusion, and variable illu-
mination limit the reliability of single-view approaches. We propose 
a unified framework that combines domain-adapted SAM3 with an 
explicit 3D Gaussian representation. Low-rank adaptation improves 
instance segmentation in agricultural imagery while updating only a 
small subset of model parameters. Uncertainty-weighted cross-view 
contrastive learning then integrates per-view instance evidence into 
a shared 3D Gaussian feature space. Hough voting aggregates multi-
view geometric cues, followed by clustering to estimate fruit counts 
and three-dimensional locations. By performing instance discrimination 
and counting in 3D, the framework exploits complementary views 
and reduces instance merging, missed detections, and duplicate counts 
caused by projection overlap.
The results demonstrate that integrating an adapted vision founda-
tion model with an explicit 3D scene representation enables reliable 
fruit perception across synthetic, orchard, and greenhouse environ-
ments. The benefits are particularly evident in densely clustered and 
heavily occluded scenes. Because clustering and post-processing require 
no crop-specific shape or size priors, the adapted weights and settings 
transfer from open-field apples to greenhouse sweet peppers. This 
transfer requires only a change to the SAM3 text prompt, although the 
3D Gaussian representation must be re-optimized for each new scene. 
The resulting fruit counts and 3D locations provide valuable spatial 
information for yield estimation and robotic harvesting, with potential 
applications in other plant phenotyping tasks.
