A B S T R A C T
Yield estimation is crucial for growers and agronomists to optimize crop management practices and facilitate
harvest planning. However, traditional manual fruit counting and fruit health assessment activities on large
fields are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to errors. Computer vision-based yield estimation
methods involving fruit counting and health assessment using unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), have gained
significant attention in recent years. This study proposes an automated yield estimation and health assessment
approach through UAV imaging. Our methodology comprises three main components: (1) a robust fruit
detection network based on the ‘‘you only look once - neural architecture search’’ (YOLONAS) model, (2)
a fruit health assessment module to detect diseases in individually identified fruits, and (3) a post-processing
and regression module for yield quantity and quality estimation. YOLONAS is a computationally efficient and
accurate object detection model trained on scale-space augmented datasets. The health assessment module
includes separable multiscale convolution layers with an additive attention module. We evaluated our yield
estimation approach on three publicly available datasets featuring peach, apple, and citrus trees. Results reveal
that YOLONAS, trained with a scale-space augmented dataset, improves detection accuracy by 1.2%. We also
used a custom fruit disease dataset to assess the performance of the disease detection model, where we noticed
that super-resolution of detected fruits with pre-trained models significantly enhances disease detection by up
to 17%, especially in low-resolution fruits. Finally, we demonstrate that the proposed method can serve as a
modular framework for yield quantity and quality assessment through UAVs in challenging field conditions.
1. Introduction
Yield estimation is a crucial aspect of orchard management and
precision agriculture, contributing to optimizing production while re-
ducing costs (He et al., 2022). However, conventional methods for
estimating yield, such as manual counting, sampling, and modeling,
are often labor-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to inaccuracy or
unreliability (Ivošević et al., 2023). Many automated approaches have
been developed that employ field imaging and machine learning to
estimate yield either directly or indirectly. Indirect yield estimation
methods involve using features extracted from plants or trees, such
as vegetation indices and canopy structure, along with environmental
factors like temperature, solar radiation, precipitation, and fertilization
to forecast or estimate yield. Direct methods utilize computer vision

as promising and cost-effective alternatives for yield estimation in
orchard crops. UAVs offer the capability to provide high-resolution,
multispectral, temporal, and geo-tagged data for monitoring fruit crops
and estimating yields. They can overcome certain limitations associated
with other remote sensing platforms, such as satellites and airplanes,
which may be affected by cloud cover, atmospheric interference, low
spatial resolution, and high costs (Li et al., 2022a; Fan et al., 2022;
Amarasingam et al., 2022). Furthermore, UAVs enable flexible and

them to reveal hidden fruits nestled within leaves. These characteris-

candidates for computer vision-based applications in agriculture (Singh

The recent literature on UAV imaging for orchard management
can be categorized into three main areas: sensing system architecture,
data processing techniques, and application scenarios (Boursianis et al.,
2022). Sensing system architecture involves designing and selecting
UAV platforms, sensors, and payloads for capturing orchard data (Jafar-
biglu and Pourreza, 2022). Data processing techniques revolve around
the methods and algorithms used to extract valuable information from
UAV data. This often entails the application of deep learning algorithms
for tasks like detecting fruits, assessing their health, and extracting
other valuable characteristics of the fruit or plants (Wani et al., 2022).
Application scenarios pertain to the specific goals and outcomes of uti-
lizing UAV data for orchard management. Researchers have extensively
explored these areas, leading to significant progress in data acquisition,
processing, and application, ultimately enhancing crop management
practices (Rejeb et al., 2022).
Researchers encounter numerous challenges and limitations when
working with UAV imaging for yield estimation. These include the com-
plexity and diversity of orchard environments, the wide range of fruit
species, and the absence of standardized workflows and methods for
data acquisition. These factors collectively pose difficulties in building
a robust system that can function effectively in every orchard. Further-
more, there remain unresolved issues regarding the delicate balance
between accuracy, efficiency, and scalability of various approaches.
Additionally, there is a notable gap in integrating UAV data with other
sources of information, such as satellite imagery, ground sensors, and
crop models. Addressing these challenges will be pivotal in advancing
the field.
This paper focuses on yield estimation using UAV imaging of peach,
apple, and orange trees. These fruits are among the most signifi-
cant crops globally, producing nearly 200 million metric tons each
year (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2020;
Statista, 2021). Accurately estimating their yield is pivotal for planning
harvesting operations, optimizing marketing strategies, and ensuring
fruit quality and profitability. However, this task remains challenging
due to the variability in fruit sizes, shapes, colors, ripeness, occlusion
caused by leaves, and the variable distribution within and among trees.
Furthermore, UAV-based image capturing introduces motion blur, il-
lumination variations, and noise, adding to the difficulties. Therefore,
developing a robust and efficient method for fruit yield estimation uti-
lizing UAV imaging can substantially benefit farmers. One of the most
dominant bottlenecks in developing such a system is the unavailability
of UAV-captured datasets. It requires much effort and time to capture
images using a UAV, covering different growth stages and viewing
perspectives. To mitigate this problem, we propose to augment existing
datasets by introducing motion blur, illumination and scale variations,
and noise to simulate the effects of UAV-captured images.
The main contributions of this research are as follows: (i) We pro-
pose a novel and modular framework that uses efficient deep learning
models to detect fruits, assess their health, and estimate yield based
on various fruit and tree characteristics. (ii) A highly efficient object
detection model, YOLONAS, has been fine-tuned using a scale-space
augmented dataset of peach, apple, and citrus trees to ensure robust
fruit detection. (iii) A scale space augmentation method is proposed to
enhance the number of samples through label-preserving transforma-
tions. Furthermore, it introduces motion blur at different levels, scale
changes, illumination variations, and noise to simulate a UAV camera.
(iv) A fruit health assessment module based on highly efficient depth-
wise separable convolutions and additive attention is trained using a
fruit diseases dataset to identify healthy and diseased fruits to facilitate
estimating yield and losses. It is worth mentioning that various image
super-resolution methods have been comprehensively evaluated to as-
sess their effectiveness in fruit disease detection, especially at lower
resolutions. Also, canopy area and average fruit density parameters of
trees have been considered to estimate the yield using a regression
model.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 discusses the
relevant literature on yield estimation and the major challenges faced
by researchers in this field. Section 3 introduces the proposed method
and explains its rationale and advantages. The evaluation results on
various public datasets and comparison with existing approaches are
reported in Section 4. Section 5 presents a critical analysis of the results
and identifies the limitations of the proposed method. Conclusions are
drawn in Section 6 along with some possible future research directions.
2. Literature review
Crop yield estimation is crucial for farmers to optimize their man-
agement practices and improve profitability. Smart agricultural pro-
cedures are more feasible than traditional, less efficient methods. To
this end, many researchers have proposed automated methods based on
computer vision and machine learning to estimate fruit yield in various
crops. In this section, we explore recent studies on yield estimation
using UAV imaging for temperate fruits, including apple, peach, and
citrus (Zhang et al., 2021b; Mathivanan and Jayagopal, 2022; Li et al.,
2022b). We also compare their approaches concerning sensing system
architectures, data processing techniques, and performance metrics.
2.1. Challenges in yield estimation
One of the significant challenges in fruit yield estimation is ac-
curately detecting and counting fruits in complex and variable back-
grounds, such as dense leaves, branches, shadows, and reflections.
Furthermore, the availability of annotated datasets is crucial for realiz-
ing these tasks. Many researchers have made significant contributions
regarding data collection and annotation. For instance, Häni et al.
(2020) collected a large dataset of 1000 apple tree images and an-
notated it for detection, counting, and segmentation tasks. Similarly,
James et al. collected the CitDet dataset (James et al., 2023), containing
579 high-resolution images of citrus trees captured from both front
and back to support detection and yield estimation. Their prelimi-
nary results showed that FasterRCNN performed slightly better than
Mask-RCNN in detection.
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been widely employed
for fruit detection and segmentation to address these challenges. For
example, Gao et al. (2020) investigated apple detection under different
occlusion scenarios, including non-occluded, occlusion by branches,
leaves, or other apples. They concluded that FasterRCNN can detect
these classes of apples with an overall mean average precision (mAP)
score of 0.897, where apples occluded by other apples presented the
most significant challenge and were detected with a mAP of 0.848.
Detecting fruits at small scales was explored by Lu et al. (2023), who
developed a novel detection head called SOD to enhance the detection
of fruits at small scales through feature refinement and bounding
box relocation. Their evaluation on MinneApple (Häni et al., 2020)
and Gold pear datasets showed an improvement of more than 0.4%,
irrespective of the backbone used. Similarly, Villacrés et al. (2023)
investigated YOLOv5 and FasterRCNN architectures to detect apples in

to estimate yield in apple trees. They reported a tracking accuracy of
up to 97% for apple detection, with a 20.05% counting error observed

Methods for fruit yield estimation also need to address challenges
related to occlusion and overlapping of fruits, which can significantly
reduce the accuracy of fruit detection and counting approaches. To
tackle this issue, researchers have explored stereo vision or aerial vision
to capture three-dimensional (3D) information about the fruits. For
example, Mirbod et al. (2023) developed a method to estimate the size
and number of on-tree apples using stereo vision with a deep learning-
based occlusion handling technique. They employed Faster R-CNN and
Mask R-CNN to detect fruit candidates and extrapolated occluded fruit
regions to enhance size estimation, achieving an average error of 4.8%
for apple size estimation. Similarly, Vijayakumar et al. (2023) proposed
a method to predict citrus yield at the tree level using ground and aerial
vision combined with machine learning models. They utilized YOLOv3
to detect fruits in ground images and a semantic segmentation model
to segment fruits in aerial images. They achieved a Mean Absolute Per-
centage Error (MAPE) of 23.45% using UAV imagery and ground-based
fruit detection from one side of the tree. Yield estimation methods
may also involve segmenting fruits from the background and foliage.
Maheswari et al. (2021) surveyed various semantic segmentation archi-
tectures, including fully convolutional network (FCN), SegNet, U-Net,
etc., and their applications to different fruits such as apple, orange,
grape, etc. They discussed the challenges and issues in fruit yield
estimation, covering aspects like sampling, collection, annotation, data
augmentation, fruit detection, and counting. The authors reported that
deep learning-based semantic segmentation techniques achieved better
fruit yield estimation performance than earlier techniques, thanks to
incorporating human cognition into the architecture.
Fruit yield estimation can significantly benefit from classifying
fruits based on their maturity stage, quality, or variety, providing
more detailed information for farmers and consumers. Various machine
learning algorithms have been proposed to perform fruit classifica-
tion, extracting image features to address this challenge. For example,
MacEachern et al. (2023) detected the ripeness stage of wild blue-
berries using YOLOv4 and image processing techniques, achieving up
to 88.12% mean average precision (MAP) for detection and 24.1%
mean absolute error (MAE) for yield estimation. Rajalaxmi et al.
(2023) proposed an improved MangoNet architecture using Harris
Hawks optimization for fruit classification with uncertainty estimation.
They utilized a CNN model to classify mangoes into four ripeness
categories and estimated the uncertainty of the classification results
using Bayesian inference, achieving an accuracy of 80%. Similarly,
Ge et al. (2022) introduced a method to classify apple tree organs
into four categories (fruit, leaf, branch, and background) using multi-
feature fusion and support vector machine (SVM). They employed
color, texture, shape, and depth features to represent apple tree organs
and applied SVM for classification, obtaining an F1 score of 93.7% for
apple yield estimation.
Detecting fruits in the background can lead to incorrect yield es-
timation, requiring appropriate mechanisms to avoid counting fruits
more than once. To address this issue, researchers have developed
different strategies. In one such work, Wu et al. (2023) introduced a
method called the normal detection matched fruit counting system.
They reduced normal fruit detection based on a threshold, followed
by fruit counting through trunk tracking and identity document assign-
ment. The detection utilized a YOLOv4-tiny model, and the proposed
method achieved a reported average fruit counting accuracy of 95%,
which is an improvement of 4.2%.
2.2. UAV-based yield estimation
A major advantage of UAVs is their capability to carry various types
of sensors, including RGB cameras, multispectral, hyperspectral, ther-
mal, and even LiDAR, enabling the capture of different aspects of crop
physiology and phenology. Leveraging these capabilities, researchers
proposed different approaches for yield estimation in fruits (Niu et al.,
2022; Wengert et al., 2022) and vegetables (Luo et al., 2022; Shi et al.,
2022) by considering vegetation indices, multi-spectral sensing, and
plant physiology analysis.
UAVs can offer high spatial resolution images for tree-level or plant-
level yield estimation, enabling farmers to identify the spatial vari-
ability of crop yield and optimize management practices accordingly.
Detection-based methods provide a solid foundation for estimation
yield in fruits. For instance, Behera et al. (2021a) modified the inter-
section over union (IoU) in the original Faster R-CNN (FR-CNN) for
on-plant fruit detection. They introduced a modified IoU (MIoU) as a
distance metric with the minimum area containing ground truth and
predicted bounding box, paying extra attention to overlapping areas.
This modification overcame the original FR-CNN’s inefficiency, enhanc-
ing detection accuracy. Their method was evaluated on various fruits,
including mango, pomegranate, tomato, apple, and orange, achieving
high correlation coefficients (R2) and average precisions (APs) for fruit
detection and counting. In another study by Apolo-Apolo et al. (2020),
Mask R-CNN and YOLOv3 were trained and evaluated for citrus fruit
yield estimation. They compared the performance of both methods on
various metrics, such as F1-score, IoU, accuracy, etc., for fruit detection
and counting. Mask R-CNN outperformed YOLOv3 in accuracy and
precision, but YOLOv3 demonstrated greater efficiency. UAV-sensed
data can be integrated with other sources and methods to enhance the
accuracy and reliability of yield estimation approaches. Investigating
this aspect, Aslan et al. (2022) conducted a comprehensive survey of
recent studies on UAVs in precision agriculture in open fields and
greenhouses. They reviewed UAV applications, challenges, and oppor-
tunities in precision agriculture, such as crop monitoring, pest and
disease detection, irrigation management, fertilization management,
and yield estimation. They also discussed the integration of UAVs
with other technologies, such as the Internet of Things (IoT), cloud
computing, artificial intelligence (AI), and blockchain. The potential of
such data integration to significantly enhance the performance of smart
agricultural systems across various applications is promising.
While existing methods have demonstrated promising results in
detection, counting, segmentation, size estimation, and classification
across various crops using UAV imagery, several limitations and chal-
lenges persist. Future research needs to address these issues, including
enhancing the robustness and generalization of methods in diverse en-
vironments and scenarios, reducing computational costs and hardware
requirements, and integrating methods with other smart agricultural
technologies for improved overall performance. Additionally, there
is a need for more exploration into estimating yield with quality
assessment, a gap that this work aims to fill (see Table 1).

3.1. Data collection and pre-processing
We considered three major fruits in this study, including Apple,
Peach, and Citrus, due to their economic significance and public avail-
ability of their datasets (worldatlas, 2024). Furthermore, the framework
developed for these fruits can easily be extended to cover other similar
fruits. The datasets employed in this study exhibit diverse charac-
teristics, presenting various scenarios for fruit detection and yield
estimation. For instance, sample images from the CitDet dataset (James
et al., 2023), Peach dataset (Assunç ao et al., 2022), and MinneApple
dataset (Häni et al., 2020) are illustrated in Fig. 2. It is evident that
trees are fully visible in most of the images from the CitDet dataset,
whereas the Peach and MinneApple datasets predominantly feature
partial tree views. Notably, both the CitDet and MinneApple datasets
include images with fruits lying on the ground, which, although not
contributing to the yield, are counted as losses. Such scenarios involv-
ing both full and partial tree views are common in real-world situations,
especially when captured via a UAV, necessitating appropriate post-
processing techniques as outlined in Wu et al. (2023). Furthermore,
the images in these datasets exhibit no motion blur and lack signif-
icant scale variations typically encountered in UAV-captured images.
To introduce diversity and simulate inherent motion blur for robust
detection in UAV images, we applied scale-space augmentations (SSA)
to the datasets before training, as illustrated in Fig. 3. To address
scale variations in fruit sizes and mitigate severe occlusion caused by
leaves, the images underwent cropping, downscaling, and subsequent
upscaling to construct a multi-scale space representation. Additionally,
the images were augmented through flipping, contrast adjustment,
illumination adjustment, hue shift to simulate slight color shifts in
fruits, and cutmix augmentations. Motion blur was introduced at a rate
of 25% to all datasets, where images were randomly motion blurred
in horizontal, vertical, and diagonal directions. Three blur levels (low,
medium, and high) were introduced through spatial filtering of images.
In addition to motion blur, several other transformations were applied
to images such as contrast, brightness, and saturation adjustments,
hue shift, and cutmix augmentation to adjust fruits density in images.
Consequently, the dataset size was increased by 5 6 times since the
augmentation transformations were applied in groups (crops, flips,
intensity and hue shift, motion blur, and cutmix). These augmentations
aim to enhance the detection of fruits in diverse situations, improving
robustness for real-world applications.
Since the images in these datasets have not been annotated for
diseases, we use a custom dataset of the same fruits to train and
evaluate models for their health assessment. Further details of the
datasets are provided in Section 4.1.

the dataset for training the detection and health assessment models
by subjecting images to various transformations and creating a set of
augmented images. This augmentation strategy enables the model to

DINO are exceptionally capable but computationally expensive models.
The RT-DETR model is efficient, but it struggles with the detection
of smaller objects. YOLONAS models represent a novel approach to
neural architecture search (NAS) for object detection tasks, combining
the strengths of the popular YOLOv5 framework with the advantages
of NAS, thereby achieving robustness, adaptability, and efficiency si-
multaneously. The YOLONAS models are generated by applying a
super-gradient algorithm to a base YOLOv5 model, enabling continuous
and differentiable optimization of the network architecture. The super-
gradient algorithm comprises two steps: a forward pass that computes
the loss and the gradients of the model parameters, and a backward
pass that updates the architecture parameters using the gradients of the
loss concerning the model parameters. This dual-step process optimizes
both the architecture and model parameters, resulting in a customized
and optimal model tailored to a specific dataset and task. Notably, these
models have demonstrated promising results on various benchmarks,
including COCO and PASCAL VOC, outperforming other NAS methods
for object detection (Aharon et al., 2021; Choutri et al., 2023).
Given its efficiency and outstanding overall performance, we em-
ploy YOLONAS for fruit detection across three distinct datasets. Addi-
tionally, we showcase its capability to achieve real-time inference speed
on an edge inference device, rendering it highly suitable for real-time
computer vision applications using UAVs.
3.3. Dynamic upscaling
The detected fruits are further analyzed for disease or damage detec-
tion characterized by the appearance of visual symptoms. UAVs often
capture images from varying altitudes, resulting in varied resolutions,
it is crucial to employ super-resolution techniques to enhance image
quality for reliable disease detection. The lower spatial resolution of
fruits in images captured from a distance can make it very difficult to
detect diseases or damages, particularly at early stages. In this regard,
we investigated super-resolution (SR) to upsample lower-resolution
fruits such that the minute details are recovered. This can help iden-
tify subtle signs of infection, such as spots, lesions, discoloration, or
deformation, that might otherwise be overlooked or misclassified. To
cope with this issue, a dynamic upscaling method is developed as
outlined in Algorithm 1. It aims to improve the performance of disease
detection by enhancing details on the fruit skin, particularly in low-
resolution fruit images. Individual fruits are analyzed, and a dynamic
scaling factor is automatically chosen to upscale all fruits to the same
spatial resolution. The upscale factor 𝑢is computed as the ratio of the
maximum input resolution supported by the DiseaseNet model 𝑟max =
224 and the resolution of the input image 𝑟. The Upscale algorithm is
activated only when 𝑢≥1. Two models are chosen to upscale low
resolution fruit images. If 𝑢is greater than 4, then FSRCNN model is
used to upscale. In cases where 𝑢is less than 4, then EDSR is employed.
A simple downsample algorithm is also employed for higher resolution
fruits. This way, the fruits can be fed to the disease detection network as
a batch, regardless of their original scale. It reduces computational cost
and memory usage of the disease detection network. It can also handle
cases where multiple fruits of different sizes are present in the same
image, which is common in real-world scenarios. Efficient SR methods
investigated in this work include:
• EDSR (Lim et al., 2017): This method uses residual blocks and
skip connections to improve the performance and efficiency of the
super-resolution network. It also removes unnecessary modules,
such as batch normalization and activation functions, to reduce
computational complexity.
• ESPCN (Shi et al., 2016): It uses a sub-pixel convolution layer
to upscale the feature maps to the desired resolution instead of
using a deconvolution or interpolation layer, thereby reducing
the number of parameters and computations, achieving improved
quality output.
• LapSRN (Lai et al., 2017): It uses a Laplacian pyramid structure to
progressively reconstruct the high-resolution images from coarse
to fine scales. It also uses feature extraction and reconstruc-
tion networks to learn the mapping between low-resolution and
high-resolution images.
• FSRCNN (Dong et al., 2016): This approach is based on a very
deep network with small filters followed by a deconvolution layer
to achieve fast and accurate super-resolution. It also includes
shrinking and expansion layers to reduce the feature dimension
and increase the receptive field.

an additive attention module (Yoon et al., 2023) designed to detect
diseases in fruit images. The MobileNetV3 consist multiscale depth-wise
separable convolution layers which are highly efficient implementation
of the standard convolutional layers. They separate the convolution
operation into pointwise and depthwise convolutions. Firstly, in depth-
wise convolution, a separate filter is applied to each channel of the
input. These filters only have one channel themselves so they only look
at a single channel of the input at a time, capturing spatial informa-
tion for each channel independently. This is followed by a pointwise
convolution operation where a 1 × 1 convolution with multiple filters
is applied to the output of the depthwise step, thereby combining
information across channels to create the final output. The model
comprises a series of inverted residual blocks with depthwise separable
convolution layers and squeeze-and-excitation modules, followed by a
global average pooling layer that outputs features of the input image.
These features are then fed into the additive attention module, which
employs a small feed-forward network with a single hidden layer
and a tanh activation to compute attention scores. These scores are
normalized by a softmax function to obtain attention weights. The
output of the additive attention module is a weighted sum of the input
vectors, representing a context vector summarizing the most relevant

3.5. Average fruit density estimation
Estimating the fruit density of orchards can provide helpful informa-
tion for crop management and yield prediction. Our method consists of
three steps: (i) fruit detection, (ii) canopy segmentation, and (iii) den-
sity estimation. First, detection output from YOLONAS in the form of
bounding boxes and confidence scores is obtained. Second, we use an
efficient canopy segmentation method based on image processing to
segment the canopy area of the trees from the background, as detailed
in Algorithm 2. Third, we divide the canopy area into a grid of cells and
calculate the average fruit density for each cell as the ratio of detected
fruits and the cell area. We then averaged the densities of all the cells to
obtain the overall fruit density of the orchard. The regression module
then uses the density map to aggregate and extrapolate the fruit count
in the orchard.

3.6. Canopy area and occlusion estimation
Not all the fruits on a tree are visible from either side of the
tree due to high degrees of occlusion by the tree leaves. Estimating
the correct number of fruits on a tree may be more important than
perfectly detecting all fruits (Koirala et al., 2019). Hence, extrapolation
methods are often employed to estimate it. To this end, we consider
the average fruit density and canopy area to estimate the overall
occlusion. This technique is described in Algorithm 3. We also consider
the low-confidence predictions by the object detection model as often
these correspond to the highly occluded fruits in the background along
with some false positives. The overall occluded area is then estimated
using the Canopy area and the detected fruits in the foreground.
Though deep learning-based canopy segmentation approach can also
be developed, our image processing method offers several advantages.
Firstly, it is computationally less expensive, making it suitable for real-
time processing on resource-constrained hardware typically used for
UAVs. Secondly, existing deep learning methods require large training
datasets specific to the orchard environment, which might not be
readily available.

4. Experiments and results
The proposed framework consists of several individual modules
whose performance is comprehensively assessed in the context of de-
tection, health assessment, and yield estimation. In this regard, several
experiments were carried out to assess the feasibility and performance
of each module. Various evaluation metrics are adopted to quantify the
efficiency and accuracy of individual components and the overall sys-

Several datasets have been used in this study to evaluate the per-
formance of the various aspects of the proposed method. Most prior
works on computer vision-based direct yield estimation methods pri-
marily focus on fruit datasets that exclusively include healthy fruits,
neglecting additional information about fruit health. In this study, we
utilize datasets employed in previous works to evaluate both detection
performance and yield estimation. Additionally, we introduce a novel
dataset that includes labels indicating the health status of each fruit,
categorizing them as either healthy or diseased. Further details of the
datasets used are provided below:

was done in Python, utilizing the PyTorch deep learning library, and
traditional image processing operations were performed using OpenCV.
The efficiency of the proposed system was evaluated by running it in

The performance of the fruit image enhancement module using

The performance of the various SR methods, reported using PSNR,
indicates that the EDSR and FRCNN methods can effectively recover de-

images, EDSR performed better than the rest. Fig. 7 shows the perfor-

input spatial resolutions. Higher PSNR values indicate better quality
of the SR image. The table compares five SR methods: bicubic in-

for 3x and 2x magnification, while FSRCNN achieves the highest PSNR

most effective SR method for enhancing fruit images that require up

low-resolution images. The table also shows that bicubic interpolation
performs poorly compared to the SR methods, especially at higher
magnifications. This indicates that bicubic interpolation is unsuitable
for fruit image enhancement, as it introduces blurring and artifacts that
degrade the quality of the SR image and make disease detection more
difficult.

results depicted in Fig. 9 reveal that the dynamic upscaling method
attains up to a 17% improvement in disease detection at 20 × 20
resolution images. Similarly, 13% and 4% better detection results were
obtained with SR for 40 × 40 and 75 × 75 sizes. This shows the
effectiveness of SR methods in recovering fine details and textures
of the fruit images while enhancing their resolution. These results
demonstrate that our method can effectively enhance the quality of
fruit images using super-resolution, which can facilitate the subsequent
tasks of fruit health assessment.

4.8. Comparison with state-of-the-art
Several recent fruit detection and yield estimation works have been
compared with the proposed method. Both single-stage (e.g., Ma et al.
(2023), Zhang et al. (2021a) and James et al. (2023)) and multi-
stage detectors (e.g., Gao et al. (2020), Lu et al. (2023) and Häni
et al. (2020)) have been considered in this comparison. Multi-stage
detectors often use a backbone network, such as Gao et al. (2020),
which evaluated VGG16 and ZFNet, or James et al. (2023), which used

4.10. Ablation study
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method, we conducted
an ablation study on the three main modules: fruit detection, disease
detection, and yield estimation. Various factors affecting the perfor-
mance of these modules have been studied and reported in Table 7. We
compared the performance of our method with different combinations
of these components on the test datasets. The table shows that by
adding SSA to the training dataset, YOLONAS improves the mAP50
score from 0.763 to 0.788 in fruit detection on the combined dataset of
Peach, MinneApple, and CitDet. Performance on the disease detection
task is reported as an F1-score, which measures the balance between
precision and recall. Results show that using low-resolution images
when upscaled using the DynUp method improves the F1-score of the
DiseaseNet model from 0.806 to 0.860, where significant improvements
come from upscaling low-resolution fruits. Finally, the 𝑅2 score shows
that adding a canopy area and average fruit density correction factors
to the Regression Counting model improves the 𝑅2 score from 0.830 to
0.842 on the combined dataset.
The results demonstrate that adding SSA and DynUp can improve
the accuracy and robustness of fruit detection and disease detection
models, respectively. Similarly, adding a canopy area and average fruit
density correction factor can enhance the yield estimation model, but
the improvement is marginal compared to the other tasks.
4.11. Robustness to field conditions
In this section, we evaluate our fruit detection model in the pres-
ence of motion blur, scale and illumination variations, and noise. We
introduced motion blur at three levels by randomly filtering the images
with horizontal, vertical, and diagonal mean filters of sizes 5 (low),
13 (medium), and 21 (high) pixels. Effects of motion blur with three
levels is shown in Fig. 11. Motion blur largely affects smaller objects,
as can be observed in apple and citrus images, where the model fails
to detect smaller objects even when low motion blur is introduced. As
in the peach dataset, relatively larger appearing fruits are minimally
affected by motion blur. Quantitative results depicted in Fig. 12 reveal
that performance loss with low and medium blur in peach is minimal,
around 3% and 5%, respectively. However, a greater performance hit
of more than 12% is noticed in high-motion blur. Similar performance
degradation was noticed in MinneApple dataset, observing 5%, 8%, and
15% loss in mAP scores. In CitDet dataset, since the fruits appear very
small, motion blur affects the fruit severely, causing a slightly greater
performance loss of 7% for low, 11% for medium, 20% for high motion
blur. Similarly, extreme illumination scenarios were presented before
YOLONAS to assess its detection performance. Results also indicate our
model’s robustness to illumination, scale variations, and medium noise
levels, as minimal performance hits are observed under these settings.

5. Discussion
Our work has several contributions and implications for the field of
precision agriculture. First, we proposed a quality-aware yield estima-
tion framework to detect fruits and determine their quality simultane-
ously. The health status of individual fruits provides valuable insights
into overall yield quality for farmers.
The proposed approach offers a unique advantage in yield estima-
tion that can also be used to estimate losses. The YOLONAS network
for fruit detection and a fruit health assessment module based on
depthwise separable convolution layers and additive attention showed
that our approach outperforms existing methods when it comes to
detection accuracy, computational efficiency, and robustness to scale
variation. We also demonstrated the feasibility and applicability of our
approach for real-world agricultural scenarios using different inference
platforms. Our work contributes to advancing computer vision-based
yield estimation methods and provides a valuable tool for growers and
agronomists to optimize crop management and harvest planning.
We also noticed some limitations and challenges of the proposed
framework, which can lead to potential future research directions. They
are summarized below.
• Occlusion Challenges: Detection-based approaches often assume
clear visibility of fruits against the background and foliage. In-
stances where heavy occlusion occurs due to leaves or branches
may lead to inaccuracies in detection, classification, and sub-
sequent yield estimation. Future research could focus on en-
hancing the framework’s robustness in handling such challenging
scenarios. Exploring additional features like depth, infrared, or
hyperspectral imaging, or integrating advanced techniques such
as semantic or instance segmentation, may offer solutions to
improve fruit separation in occluded environments.
• Generalization to Different Fruits and Crops: The current frame-
work is tailored for orchards with relatively uniform and spher-
ical fruits, such as peaches, apples, and citrus. Extending its
applicability to different types of fruits (e.g., apples, oranges,
grapes) or diverse crops (e.g., wheat, corn, soybean) could be a
valuable avenue for future research. Adapting the framework to
different orchard settings, considering variations in row spacing,
tree height, and canopy density, would contribute to its broader
utility.
• Fine-Grained Health Assessment: The existing framework focuses
on three categories of fruit health: healthy, brown rot, and shot
hole. Recognizing the potential for more fine-grained health lev-
els, such as ripe, unripe, overripe, diseased, or damaged, could
lead to a more nuanced health assessment. Future work may
explore extending the framework to handle these granular health
categories, improving its ability to capture variations in fruit
conditions that impact yield quality and value.

6. Conclusion
This paper introduces a modular framework for UAV-based preci-
sion agriculture systems, addressing yield estimation and health as-
sessment. The proposed approach comprises three key components: a
robust fruit detection network (YOLONAS), a fruit health assessment
module, and a post-processing and regression module. Extensive evalu-
ations on publicly available datasets featuring peach, apple, and citrus
trees showcase the efficacy of our approach in achieving high accuracy
across various tasks, including fruit detection, disease detection, and
yield quantity and quality estimation. Notably, the model demonstrates
resilience in handling challenges like varying illumination, occlusion,
and diverse fruit sizes. The presented framework emerges as a valuable
tool for growers and agronomists, offering insights to optimize crop
management practices and streamline harvest planning. Future endeav-
ors will focus on extending the approach to diverse fruits and crops,
incorporating temporal information, and leveraging additional sensing
sources for enhanced yield prediction.
