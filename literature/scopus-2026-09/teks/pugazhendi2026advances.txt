A B S T R A C T
Artificial intelligence-driven, automated, and real-time fruit detection systems are a continuously evolving area 
of research in agriculture. In recent years, YOLO (You Only Look Once) family of algorithms have gained 
popularity and are widely used in agricultural fruit detection tasks. However, fruit detection research is multi­
disciplinary in nature, and the literature, methods, and algorithms are rapidly evolving, which necessitates 
thorough documentation, synthesis, and evaluation of the literature. Therefore, this study documents, assesses, 
and synthesizes the existing literature on YOLO-based object detection through bibliometric analysis (i.e., 620 
articles) and systematic literature review (i.e., 114 articles). We observed that the volume of published literature 
increased by 750% over the span of five years (i.e., 2020 to 2025). YOLOv5 remains the most widely used model 
(33.3%), while YOLOv8 demonstrated a fast-growing adoption rate (27.2%). Additionally, mean detection 
performance across studies was 91.1%. Moreover, critical limitations and challenges are also discussed. Only 
25.4% of studies reported computational efficiency metrics, while 95% used custom datasets, limiting repro­
ducibility. Major challenges include reduced detection performance for small fruits (5–10% decline), severe 
occlusions, and accuracy-speed trade-offs. Priority research directions include hardware-aware optimization, 
standardized benchmark development, and domain adaptation for cross-orchard generalization. These insights 
will guide the advancement of YOLO-based fruit detection from laboratory studies toward robust and reliable 
agricultural deployment.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background and motivation
Agricultural fruit production systems are heavily dependent on a 
manual workforce for harvesting, sorting, packaging, and processing 
[1]. Increasing wages and labor shortages will require novel and trans­
formative solutions to meet the demands of a continuously growing 
global population [2]. Traditional fruit harvesting methods account for 
approximately 40% of total production costs in fruit cultivation, are 
becoming economically unsustainable due to the declining availability 
of seasonal workers and increasing labor costs [3]. Labor shortages and 
increased production costs have catalyzed intensive research into 
automated harvesting systems as a viable pathway for sustainable and 
economically resilient agricultural practices [4]. In recent years, the 
adoption and applications of Artificial Intelligence (AI) driven computer 
vision systems have increased for automated fruit detection and har­
vesting systems [5,6].
However, the agricultural environment is unstructured and complex, 
which presents unique challenges for computer vision systems, 
including variable illumination, occlusion from foliage and branches, 
complex background clutter, and substantial intra-class variability in 
fruit appearance due to growth stage, variety, and environmental con­
ditions [7].

The deep learning revolution, particularly advances in convolutional 
neural networks (CNN), has fundamentally transformed computer vision 
[8]. Traditional machine learning approaches, which are heavily based 
on hand-crafted features and struggle with environmental variability, 
have been largely replaced by deep learning (DL) architectures capable 
of learning hierarchical feature representations directly from data [9]. 
Among the various object detection frameworks, the YOLO family of 
algorithms has garnered substantial attention in agriculture due to its 
exceptional balance between detection accuracy and computational ef­
ficiency [10]. The single-stage architecture of YOLO, which formulates 
object detection as a unified regression problem rather than a 
multi-stage pipeline, enables real-time inference speeds, critical for ro­
botic systems, while maintaining better accuracy than two-stage de­
tectors [11].
YOLO's dominance in agricultural applications stems from critical 
advantages over alternative frameworks. Compared to two-stage de­
tectors like Faster R-CNN, YOLO processes images in a single forward 
pass rather than separate region proposal and classification stages, 
reducing inference time by 5-10 × while achieving comparable accu­
racy. Unlike SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector), YOLO's anchor-free 
variants (YOLOX, YOLOv6-v9) eliminate complex anchor tuning, 
simplifying deployment across diverse fruit morphologies. Furthermore, 
YOLO's unified architecture enables straightforward customization of 
backbone networks and detection heads, facilitating rapid adaptation to 
resource-constrained edge devices, a critical requirement for mobile 
agricultural robots that two-stage detectors may not efficiently meet due 
to their computational overhead.
1.2. YOLO architecture evolution
YOLO architecture, first introduced by Redmon et al. [12], revolu­
tionized object detection by reformulating the task as a single-stage 
regression problem that directly predicts bounding boxes and class 
probabilities from full images in a single pass (Fig. 1a). This architec­
tural innovation eliminated the region proposal stage required by 
two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN), dramatically reducing 
computational complexity and enabling real-time inference. YOLOv1 
demonstrated the feasibility of achieving processing speeds exceeding 
45 frames per second (FPS) while maintaining reasonable detection 
accuracy, establishing a new paradigm for real-time computer vision 
applications [13]. Subsequent iterations progressively refined the ar­
chitecture through systematic improvements to the backbone networks, 
feature extraction mechanisms, and detection head. YOLOv2 introduced 
anchor boxes and multi-scale training, addressing the limitations of 
detecting objects of varying sizes [14]. YOLOv3 implements a feature 
pyramid network architecture with detection at three different scales, 
substantially improving the performance of small objects [15]. The 
YOLOv4 release integrated numerous contemporary advances, 
including the CSPDarknet53 backbone, spatial pyramid pooling, and 
path aggregation networks, establishing new state-of-the-art perfor­
mance benchmarks [16]. Similarly, YOLOv5 has achieved widespread 
adoption, largely attributed to its extensive documentation, Py-Torch 
implementation, and extensive community support [17]. Likewise, 
YOLOv7 introduced trainable bag-of-freebies and efficient layer aggre­
gation networks, optimizing the efficiency-accuracy trade-off [18]. 
YOLOX adopts anchor-free detection and decoupled classification and 
regression heads, simplifying the architecture while improving perfor­
mance [19]. YOLOv8, the latest widely adopted version, incorporates 
C2f modules for enhanced feature fusion and offers native support for 
multiple tasks, including detection, segmentation, and pose estimation 
[20]. The YOLOv9 introduced programmable gradient information and 
a generalized efficient layer aggregation network, further advancing 
architectural efficiency.
The fundamental advantage of single-stage YOLO detectors over 
two-stage alternatives is the computational efficiency achieved through 
unified processing. Two-stage detectors first generate region proposals 
and then classify and refine each proposal, requiring multiple forward 
passes and substantial computational resources. YOLO processes the 
entire image simultaneously, reducing latency by an order of magnitude 
while achieving comparable accuracy [21]. This efficiency advantage is

1.3. Scope and objectives
In recent years, multiple review studies have examined the appli­
cations of DL in agriculture. Badgujar et al. [21] conducted a systematic 
and bibliometric review of 257 publications on YOLO applications in 
general agricultural domains. Koirala et al. [22] reviewed DL ap­
proaches for fruit detection and yield estimation, summarizing tech­
niques, and benchmarks reported up to 2019. Montoya-Cavero et al. [6] 
systematically examined computer vision systems for harvesting robots, 
focusing on hardware, and sensing configurations, as well as 
system-level considerations. Xiao et al. [23] provided an overview of DL 
based fruit detection and recognition from 2018 onward, assessing 53 
relevant studies. Although these reviews offer valuable perspectives, 
their scopes, methodological approaches, and temporal coverage vary, 
resulting in differences in depth, approach, focus, and emphasis. Table 1
outlines the scope, methodology, and focus areas of these studies, 
providing context for the present work, which combines bibliographic 
assessment with a systematic technical review focused on YOLO-based 
fruit detection systems.
1.4. Literature limitations, study objectives, and contributions
Previous review studies have provided valuable insights into DL 
applications in agriculture, establishing a foundational understanding of 
algorithmic approaches and applications. However, several aspects 
remain underexplored, including temporal coverage that includes the 
recent architectural developments (YOLOv8, YOLOv9, YOLOv10), 
comparative analysis across YOLO versions within the specific context of 
fruit detection, and systematic examination of deployment consider­
ations beyond laboratory settings. This study complements existing 
work by providing a focused investigation of YOLO-based fruit detection 
systems. This study contributes to the field by establishing a quantitative 
performance baseline across YOLO versions, fruit types, and environ­
mental conditions. We systematically characterized deployment con­
siderations through an analysis of edge computing adoption, 
computational efficiency reporting, and hardware-software optimiza­
tion strategies. In addition, we examine the characteristics and evalua­
tion practices of the data set to understand reproducibility challenges 
and benchmarking approaches. Ultimately, we identify research di­
rections based on a systematic analysis of existing gaps, providing in­
sights into areas that may benefit from further investigation.
To structure this analysis, we posed the following four research 
questions to guide our investigation: 
RQ1: What is the current research landscape of YOLO-based fruit 
detection in terms of publication trends, geographical distribution, 
leading researchers and institutions, and thematic evolution?
RQ2: Which YOLO versions and architectural modifications are 
employed for fruit detection across different application scenarios, 
fruit characteristics, and environmental conditions?
RQ3: What performance levels are reported across different fruit 
types, application stages (i.e., pre-harvest, harvest, post-harvest), 
and environmental conditions (i.e., controlled vs. natural orchards)?

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Review framework
A dual methodological approach, including bibliometric analysis 
with a systematic literature review (SLR), is used to provide a compre­
hensive understanding of YOLO-based fruit detection research. The 
bibliometric component quantitatively maps the research landscape by 
analyzing publication patterns, citation networks, author productivity, 
and thematic evolution across 620 publications. The SLR adheres strictly 
to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- 
Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [24], providing an in-depth technical 
analysis of 114 studies that examine YOLO architectures, modifications, 
performance metrics, and deployment contexts. This complementary 
approach offers insights into field structure and dynamics, along with a 
technical understanding of methodologies and outcomes, addressing 
limitations inherent in either approach applied independently.
2.2. Bibliometric analysis
2.2.1. Data source and collection
Bibliometric data were retrieved from the Scopus database on 
February 17, 2025, which was selected for its comprehensive literature 
coverage across multiple disciplines and robust metadata quality. The 
search strategy employed Boolean operators combining YOLO-related 
terms ("YOLO" OR "You Only Look Once") with agricultural applica­
tion descriptors ("fruit" OR "crop" OR "agricultural" OR "harvest*") in the 
title, abstract, or keyword fields. No language or document type re­
strictions were applied during the initial retrieval to ensure compre­
hensive coverage. The search yielded 620 articles spanning January 
2020 to early 2025.
2.3. Systematic literature review protocol
2.3.1. Search strategy
This SLR implemented a multi-database search strategy to maximize 
the coverage of relevant literature across diverse publication venues. 
Seven major academic databases were queried: IEEE Xplore, Scopus, 
Springer, ScienceDirect, MDPI, Wiley, and Taylor & Francis. Search

2.3.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
We established inclusion and exclusion criteria, considering estab­
lished research questions. Studies were included if they satisfied all of 
the following criteria: (1) employed the YOLO architecture (any version) 
as the primary or comparative detection method; (2) focused on fruit 
detection, classification, localization, or related tasks directly support­
ing fruit harvesting or monitoring; (3) reported quantitative perfor­
mance metrics enabling objective assessment; (4) published as peer- 
reviewed journal articles or conference proceedings; and (5) available 
in English language for detailed full-text analysis.
Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) addressed 
plant disease or pest detection without fruit detection components (n =
10); (2) focused on agricultural robotics, automation, or navigation 
without implementing fruit detection systems (n = 6); (3) employed 
non-YOLO detection methods exclusively (n = 163); (4) lacked quanti­
tative performance evaluation or were published in non-peer-reviewed 
venues with insufficient methodological rigor (n = 204); (5) published 
in languages other than English preventing detailed analysis (n = 82); 
and (6) classified as review articles, meta-analyses, or survey papers 
rather than primary research (n = 7). These exclusion criteria were 
applied hierarchically during the screening stages to systematically 
narrow the dataset to studies that directly addressed the review 
questions.
The retrieved records underwent systematic screening following the 
PRISMA workflow (Fig. 3). The screening involved removing duplicate 
records and applying inclusion and exclusion criteria. This process also 
included a full-text assessment, which resulted in 114 articles for a SLR.
2.3.3. Data extraction
A standardized data extraction framework was developed, which is 
described below. For each included study, the following information 
was systematically extracted: bibliographic data (i.e., authors, year, 
publication venue); fruit type and variety investigated; application stage 
(i.e., pre-harvest, harvesting, or post-harvest); task type (i.e., detection, 
classification, segmentation, pose estimation, counting); YOLO version 
employed (v1–v9, YOLOX); network modifications (i.e., backbone 
network, attention mechanisms, neck architecture, detection head, loss 
function variants, and novel convolution operations); quantitative per­
formance metrics (i.e., mAP and FPS); dataset characteristics (i.e., image 
size, custom versus public dataset, and annotation type); deployment 
environment (i.e., orchard, controlled environment, or laboratory); 
hardware platform (i.e., GPU-based, edge device). Data extraction was 
performed independently by two reviewers, with regular cross-checks 
and consensus meetings to ensure consistency. The extracted data

3. Results: bibliometric analysis
3.1. Publication trends and research growth
Bibliometric analysis (n = 620) revealed exponential growth in 
YOLO-based fruit detection research in a five-year period (Fig. 4a). 
Annual publication volumes increased from 28 articles in 2020 to 238 
articles in 2024, representing a 750% increase. This trajectory illustrates 
the emergence of new research fields transitioning from exploratory 
phases to systematic investigations and application development. We 
observed a growth pattern that exhibits three distinct phases: initial 
adoption (2020–2021) characterized by foundational studies establish­
ing YOLO’s feasibility for agricultural applications; rapid expansion 
(2022–2023) marked by network refinements; and acceleration 
(2024–2025), reflecting increasing interest and adoption focusing on 
real-world deployment. Notably, 2024 alone contributed 238 publica­
tions, constituting 38.4% of the entire corpus, indicating sustained 
research momentum rather than saturation.
3.2. Geographical landscape
Geographical distribution suggests that most of the research output is 
from Asian countries (Fig. 4b), with China leading with 294 articles 
(47.4% of the global total). India ranks second with 72 articles (11.6%). 
This is primarily because both China and India are the leading producers 
of major agricultural commodities globally. The United States and Japan 
tied for third place, with 33 publications each (5.3%). Malaysia, Italy, 
and South Korea constitute the next tier with 15–19 publications each, 
indicating emerging regional centers of excellence. European contribu­
tions remain fragmented across multiple countries without a dominant 
single-nation presence, collectively representing approximately 15% of 
the total output.
3.3. Disciplinary distribution
Scopus subject classification reveals a substantial interdisciplinary 
nature of this research area (Fig. 4c), which includes computer science 
(24.7%), engineering (19.1%), agricultural and biological sciences 
(15.7%), mathematics (9.3%), and other fields (31.2%). This distribu­
tion demonstrates the convergence of previously distinct research 
communities around shared technological challenges. The relatively 
balanced representation across computer science, engineering, and 
agricultural sciences contrasts with early YOLO applications, which 
were dominated almost exclusively by computer vision researchers, 
indicating successful knowledge transfer and adoption as well as inter­
disciplinary integration.
3.4. Publication venues and impact
Document type analysis indicates most contributions are in the form 
of journal articles (64.4%) followed by conference proceedings (31.3%), 
with the remaining 4.3% comprising book chapters and early access 
articles (Fig. 4d). Leading journal venues include Computers and 
Electronics in Agriculture, Biosystems Engineering, Agriculture, Sen­
sors, and Agronomy, which represent specialized outlets for agricultural 
technology rather than premier computer vision journals. Conference 
contributions are primarily published in IEEE and ACM proceedings, 
particularly in CVPR workshops on agriculture, ICRA (robotics), and 
specialized agricultural engineering conferences. This venue distribu­
tion suggests that the research community spans agricultural engineer­
ing and computer science, with limited penetration into top-tier 
computer vision venues (CVPR, ICCV, ECCV main tracks), potentially 
indicating the perception of agricultural applications as niche speciali­
zation rather than mainstream computer vision research.
3.5. Research themes and keywords
3.5.1. Major keywords
Co-occurrence analysis of author-supplied keywords identified major 
keywords which included “deep learning” (appearing in 58% of arti­
cles), “object detection” (52%), “YOLO” and variants (48%), “fruit 
detection” (41%), “precision agriculture” (32%), “real-time detection” 
(28%), “computer vision” (26%), and “harvesting robot” (22%). This 
keyword distribution confirms the expected technical and application 
foci while revealing an emphasis on real-time processing, reflecting 
practical deployment considerations.
3.5.2. Thematic clusters
Network visualization as shown in Fig. 5, identified three major 
thematic clusters that collectively define the current research landscape. 
The first cluster, centered on DL methods, is characterized by terms such 
as YOLO, CNN, and object detection, which represent research focused 
on algorithmic development and advancement in model networks. The 
second cluster, pertaining to agricultural applications, includes key­
words such as fruit, orchards, tomatoes, and agricultural robots, 
reflecting the application of vision-based detection systems to practical 
tasks such as fruit detection, crop monitoring, and robotics. The third 
cluster, performance optimization, encompasses terms such as detection 
accuracy, precision, performance, estimation method, and loss function, 
indicating efforts to enhance the model reliability, computational effi­
ciency, and deployment feasibility. The strong inter-cluster linkages 
underscore a well-integrated research domain in which methodological 
innovations are closely aligned with real-world agricultural needs, and 
performance-oriented studies ensure the robustness of proposed 
solutions.
3.6. Key insights from bibliometric analysis
The synthesis of bibliometric findings reveals a rapidly evolving 
research field characterized by an exponential growth trajectory, 
geographic concentration in Asia correlating with agricultural 
modernization priorities, genuine interdisciplinary convergence rather 
than superficial borrowing across computer science and agricultural 
engineering, and emerging research frontiers addressing deployment 
practicalities and advanced capabilities beyond basic detection.

4.2. Fruit types and application diversity
4.2.1. Most studied fruits
Apple emerged as the most extensively studied fruit (Fig. 6), with 15 
studies (13.2%), reflecting its global economic importance, high labor 
requirements, and challenging detection characteristics, including var­
iable occlusion in dense canopies [25–33]. Tomatoes followed closely, 
with 14 studies (12.3%) distributed across greenhouse and open-field 
applications, benefiting from controlled environment research oppor­
tunities and high-value commercial production systems [34–40]. 
Strawberry represents the third most studied fruit with 12 studies 
(10.5%), driven by delicate handling requirements necessitating precise 
detection and gentle robotic manipulation [41–47]. Citrus [48–51] and 
grapes [52] appeared in five studies (4.4%), while peaches [53,54] 
appeared in four studies. Emerging research targets include oil palm [55,
56], passion fruit [57], cherry tomato [58], and camellia oleifera [59] 
with 3–4 studies each, representing regional economic priorities and 
specialty crop opportunities.
4.2.2. Emerging and specialty fruits
Beyond mainstream fruits, the corpus includes investigations of 
specialty and regional crops, including pineapple [60,61], pomegranate 
[62], date fruit [63], pitaya (dragon fruit) [64], chestnut [65], kiwifruit 
[66], olive [67], jujube [68], coffee fruit [69], and chili pepper [70]. 
Notably, camellia oleifera [71] appears exclusively in China, reflecting 
regional agricultural priorities and demonstrating localized technolog­
ical development for specialty crops. This diversity indicates a broad­
ening application scope, extending beyond the major fruit types to 
encompass comprehensive agricultural automation.

4.3. Application stages and tasks
4.3.1. Preharvest applications
Preharvest applications comprised 45 studies (39.5%) focusing on 
fruit detection and counting for yield estimation, maturity and ripeness 
assessment, growth monitoring, and early stage disease detection 
[74–76]. These applications generally focus on real-time processing 
requirements compared to harvesting operations, emphasizing the 
importance of counting accuracy and integration with farm manage­
ment systems [39,77]. The representative performance included 
pomegranate detection, achieving a 92.2% mAP, demonstrating suffi­
cient accuracy for monitoring applications where false positives and 
negatives can be tolerated within operational margins [62].
4.3.2. Harvesting applications
Harvesting operations represent the largest application category, 
with 50 studies (43.9%), addressing fruit localization for robotic pick­
ing, picking point detection, pose estimation for grasping, and real-time 
detection on mobile platforms [78,79]. These applications require strict 
latency, typically below 100 ms, to enable responsive robotic manipu­
lation, precision localization within ±5 mm tolerances, robust occlusion 
handling in complex canopy structures, and edge deployment on mobile 
robotic platforms with limited computational resources. Exemplary 
systems include strawberry picking, achieving 96% mAP at 62.6 FPS, 
and grape stem detection, reaching 98% mAP, demonstrating the 
feasibility of high-accuracy real-time performance for specific applica­
tions [41].
4.3.3. Postharvest applications
Postharvest processing applications constitute 19 studies (16.7%) 
focusing on defect detection, quality inspection, grading, and sorting 
operations. The advantages of a controlled environment, including 
consistent lighting, clean backgrounds, and stationary fruit positioning, 
facilitate superior performance, exemplified by date fruit classification 
achieving 99.4% mAP [80]. Higher-resolution imaging and multi-class 
classification capabilities address quality assessment requirements, 
whereas relaxed real-time constraints relative to harvesting applications 
permit more computationally intensive processing [81].
4.4. YOLO version distribution and evolution
4.4.1. Version adoption patterns
YOLOv5 was the most widely employed algorithm (Figs. 7 and 8), 
used in 38 studies (33.3%), attributed to its excellent documentation, 
extensive community support, balanced accuracy-speed trade-offs, and 
straightforward customization, which facilitates rapid prototyping. 
Secondly, YOLOv8 has been adopted in 31 studies (27.2%), emerging as 
the preferred choice for 2024–2025 research through enhanced archi­
tectural improvements and native multi-task capabilities. YOLOv7 
appeared in 10 studies (8.8%) despite its strong performance charac­
teristics, suggesting that community support and ecosystem factors in­
fluence adoption beyond pure technical merit. YOLOv4 was used in 13 
studies (11.4%), primarily in earlier periods (2020–2022), with reduced 
adoption in recent years. Legacy YOLOv3 was employed in 10 studies 
(8.8%), whereas, YOLOX adoption remained limited to six studies 
(5.3%) despite its anchor-free advantages. YOLOv9 and YOLOv2 
appeared in three studies each, reflecting the very recent release of 
YOLOv9 and the relatively older version of YOLOv2. Fig. 7 shows the 
adoption patterns of the YOLO versions.
4.4.2. Temporal evolution
Version adoption evolution demonstrates (Fig. 8) clear patterns: 
YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 dominance during 2020–2021, a progressive shift 
toward YOLOv5 during 2022–2023, and YOLOv8 emergence during 
2024–2025 concurrent with sustained YOLOv5 usage. This pattern re­
flects the typical behavior of the research community, where proven 
platforms maintain substantial user bases, while newer versions attract 
early adopters, eventually achieving dominance as maturity and com­
munity resources develop.

4.5.4. Loss function optimization
Loss function modifications (28 studies, 24.6%) evolved from early 
GIoU/DIoU/CIoU (2020–2022) to recent EIoU (n=8), SIoU (n=6), and 
WIoU (n=3). Advanced IoU variants incorporate geometric consider­
ations (i.e., distance, aspect ratio, angle) for more informative gradients. 
The observed performance gains included, 15–20% faster convergence, 
1–3% mAP improvement, enhanced bounding box accuracy, with no 
inference overhead. This demonstrates cumulative refinement through 
incremental improvements [88,89].
4.5.5. Novel convolution operations
Standard convolutions exhibit computational redundancy, as many 
learned filters capture similar patterns, consuming excessive memory 
and computing resources unsuitable for edge deployment. Novel 
convolution operations (i.e., depth-wise separable, deformable, and 
ghost convolutions) reduce this redundancy through factorization and 
selective application strategies, achieving 8-9 × parameter reduction 
while preserving the representational capacity for feature learning [52,
90]. Convolution operation replacements appear in 22 studies (19.3%), 
dominated by depth wise separable convolutions (DWConv, 12 studies) 
achieving 8–9 × parameter reductions. Deformable convolutions 
(DCNv2, DCNv3, and four studies) effectively model geometric trans­
formations for irregular fruit shapes. Additional innovations include 
ghost convolution (seven studies), ODConv (three studies), and 
SPD-Conv (three studies), each addressing specific efficiency or capa­
bility requirements.

4.6. Performance analysis
4.6.1. Detection accuracy distribution
In the fruit detection task, detection performance is crucial since it 
can directly impact the overall system’s performance. The overall mAP 
values ranged from 50.05% to 100%, with a mean of 91.1% and a me­
dian of 93.1% (Fig. 9a). High-performing studies (mAP ≥95%) 
accounted for 45 studies (39.5%), moderate-performing studies (85% ≤
mAP < 95%) accounted for 52 studies (45.6%), and lower-performing 
studies (mAP < 85%) accounted for 17 studies (14.9%). Accuracy 
variations correlate with fruit characteristics (i.e., size and color 
contrast), environmental complexity (i.e., occlusion and lighting vari­
ability), dataset quality and size, and model sophistication, suggesting 
that these factors collectively determine achievable performance rather 
than algorithm selection alone. Fig. 9 shows a comprehensive perfor­
mance analysis. The plot only shows the accuracy distribution, which is 
not comparative in nature since each study type differs significantly.
4.6.2. Real-time performance analysis
Real-time model performance is crucial for robotic harvesting, 
monitoring, and processing tasks, where the speed of operation directly 
determines the system’s throughput capacity. However, relatively few 
studies, around 29 studies (25.4%) reported FPS metrics (Fig. 9c). We 
observed that FPS ranged from 11.23–90.91 with 15 studies (51.7% of 
the reporting subset) achieving real-time capability (≥30 FPS). Notable 
performers (YOLOv8s, YOLOv5n, YOLOX-Tiny) included strawberry, 
pear, and pomegranate detection (96% mAP, 62.6 FPS) [97], pear 
detection (94.08% mAP, 90.91 FPS) [93]; and pomegranate detection 
(93.4% mAP, 74.07 FPS [86], respectively), demonstrating the feasi­
bility of achieving high accuracy and real-time performance with 
appropriate optimization strategies.
4.6.3. Performance by YOLO version
YOLO versions are continuously evolving with significant improve­
ments over the previous ones. A version-wise comparison reveals 
(Fig. 9b) that YOLOv8 achieves the highest mAP (91.8%), followed by 
YOLOv5 (90.5%), YOLOv7 (89.2%), and YOLOv4 (88.1%), demon­
strating incremental improvements across versions, albeit with dimin­
ishing marginal returns, which suggests architectural maturation.
4.6.4. Environmental impact
The performance of the detection model is largely dependent on 
environmental conditions. For example, fruit detection in farm condi­
tions will be influenced by environmental factors, whereas lab settings 
allow for controlling these natural conditions. Natural orchard envi­
ronments (75 studies) achieved a mean mAP of 88.7% with substantial 
variance (7%) due to variable lighting, occlusion, and background 
complexity (Fig. 9d). Controlled environments (18 studies) demon­
strated a 93.4% mAP with reduced variance, yielding an approximately 
5% performance advantage attributable to environmental consistency, 
suggesting that substantial deployment challenges remain for unstruc­
tured agricultural settings.
4.7. Dataset characteristics
YOLO is a supervised learning approach, where a model learns from 
large amounts of data to do specific tasks. Therefore, the characteristics 
of the dataset do influence model performance. The study also found 
that the dataset sizes ranged from 112 to 60,059 images, with a median 
of ~2,000 images (Fig. 10c). Small datasets (<1,000 images, 28 studies, 
24.6%) raised concerns about overfitting, whereas large datasets 
(>3,000 images, 35 studies, 30.7%) can enable superior generalization. 
Critically, approximately 95% of studies employ custom datasets with 
minimal public dataset usage (<5%), severely limiting reproducibility 
and cross-study comparison. Data augmentation methods include geo­
metric transformations (>80%), color adjustments (~60%), and 
advanced techniques, including Mosaic, Mixup, and CutMix (~40%).
4.8. Hardware and deployment
Hardware selection determines whether detection systems can 
transition from laboratories to field-deployable robots, with the choice 
between GPU-based systems (i.e., high performance, high power) and 
edge-devices (i.e., moderate performance, low power) representing a 
critical trade-off for mobile agricultural platforms. GPU-based systems 
dominate, with 94 studies (82.4%) focused on research and laboratory 
evaluation, while edge-devices appear in only seven studies (6.1%), 
revealing a substantial research-practice deployment gap requiring 
systematic edge device evaluation and optimization strategies for 
practical agricultural robotics deployment. The limited edge deploy­
ment research demonstrates 91.81% mAP at 18.1 FPS on devices 
consuming <15W, compared to 91.13% mAP at 65.0 FPS on GPUs 
consuming 200-300W, indicating edge systems maintain GPU-level ac­
curacy (~100% relative to GPU baseline) but suffer 3.59× inference 
slowdown [42,84,106,114]
4.9. Advanced applications and multi-task learning
Advanced applications have extended the YOLO network beyond 
conventional object detection to address complex perceptual tasks that 
are critical for autonomous agricultural systems, including instance 
segmentation, key-point detection, temporal tracking, and maturity 
classification. Multi-task learning constitutes an architectural paradigm 
where a single model simultaneously executes multiple related tasks 
through shared feature representations, thereby improving computa­
tional efficiency and generalization compared with task-specific models 
[97]. Instance segmentation (eight studies) enables pixel-level boundary 
detection for precise picking-point identification, exemplified by grape 
pedicel segmentation, achieving 99% mAP. Key point detection and 
pose estimation (five studies) support robotic grasping through stem/­
peduncle localization. Detection-tracking-counting integration (six 
studies) maintains temporal consistency for yield estimation through 
DeepSORT and similar frameworks, demonstrating evolution beyond 
basic detection toward comprehensive perception systems. Table 4

5. Discussion
5.1. Technical achievements and persistent challenges
This review study indicates that YOLO-based fruit detection has 
become a mature technology, since most studies report a detection ac­
curacy over 91.0% (Fig. 10a). Fig. 10 presents the statistical analysis of 
YOLO-based fruit detection performance. This high-confidence perfor­
mance band demonstrates consistency across a diverse range of fruits 
and applications, from apples in dense canopies to strawberries in 
controlled environments [25,41,94,95]. Contrary to the expectation of 
continuous architectural improvement, our statistical analysis revealed 
no significant performance difference between YOLO versions v3–v8 (p 
= 0.925), Fig. 10b indicating a performance plateau for this specific 
domain [33,41,128]. This is a critical finding suggests that the choice of 
YOLO version should be driven by deployment constraints, ecosystem 
support, and real-time requirements, rather than anticipated accuracy 
improvements alone.
These data further challenge the common assumption that larger 
datasets inherently guarantee better performance. We found a negligible 
correlation between the dataset size and mAP (r = −0.048, p = 0.626) 
(Fig. 10d). Instead, the optimal dataset size band was identified as 
1,000–2,000 images, which achieved the highest mean mAP of 93.59% 
with the lowest variability (SD = 4.93%) [62,84]. This underscores that 
beyond a critical threshold, data quality, diversity, and annotation ac­
curacy become far more critical than the sheer quantity.

Despite this overall maturity, two key challenges are identified. First, 
small-object detection continues to underperform, with studies on fruits 
such as blueberries and grapes consistently reporting mAP scores 5–10% 
below the overall mean, indicating that general models are not suffi­
ciently optimized for these cases [87,109,128]. Second, robustness 
under severe occlusions remains a challenge. The wide performance 
range for fruits, such as apples (92–99% mAP), highlights that detection 
reliability remains highly dependent on canopy management and im­
aging conditions, a challenge that vision-only models struggle to 
generalize across without integrated environmental sensing or domain 
adaptation strategies [25,28,30].
5.2. The deployment gap: from laboratory to field
A significant “deployment gap” is evident, with only a small fraction 
of research transitioning into practical and scalable field use. This gap 
can be quantified in three critical dimensions. (Fig. 11)
The first is the fundamental accuracy–speed–efficiency trade-off. Our 
analysis suggests that Edge AI systems maintain accuracy equivalent to 
GPU systems (91.81% vs. 91.13% mAP, p = 0.834) (Fig. 11a), achieving 
accuracy retention [41,84]. However, this parity comes at a substantial 
cost, which is edge devices suffer a significant slowdown in inference 
speed (18.1 vs. 65.0 FPS) (Fig. 11b), making them unsuitable for 
high-speed robotic applications requiring >30 FPS [41,110,114].
The second dimension is environmental robustness. The statistical 
breakdown by application stage reveals that harvesting applications 
(Fig. 11c), often conducted under more standardized conditions, achieve 
a higher mAP (92.10%) with a tighter confidence interval than pre­
harvest (90.66%) or post-harvest (90.27%) applications [41,97,116]. 
This suggests that the variable lighting and unstructured settings of 
pre-harvest monitoring, along with the complex visual tasks of 
post-harvest grading, introduce performance volatility that has not yet 
been fully addressed by the current models.
Finally, the issues of domain generalization and reporting severely 
hinder progress. The overwhelming prevalence of custom datasets (95% 
of studies) and the lack of standardized benchmarks fundamentally limit 
their reproducibility, cross-study comparability, and model scalability 
[72,73]. This problem is compounded by inconsistent reporting; critical 
deployment metrics are underreported, with FPS detailed in only 25% of 
GPU studies and a mere two Edge AI studies, while hardware specifi­
cations are often vague or omitted entirely [41,111,114].
5.3. Methodological limitations impeding progress
Several methodological shortcomings, highlighted by our systematic 
review, hinder the transition from laboratory validation to field-ready 
deployment. The most significant is the prevalence of isolated custom 
datasets, which create research silos and prevent objective bench­
marking. Our data shows a median dataset size of 1,948 images 
(Fig. 10c), but the mean (3,379) is skewed by a few large sets, indicating 
a lack of consensus on optimal data-collection strategies [62,84].
Furthermore, incomplete reporting is a major challenge. The absence 
of consistent data on FPS, detailed hardware specifications, and statis­
tical significance tests makes it difficult to assess the real-world appli­
cability and comparative value of the published results. Fewer than 15% 
of studies evaluate complete perception-to-manipulation pipelines, 
focusing instead on detection accuracy in isolation, which overlooks the 
system-level challenges of agricultural robotics [108,127]. This focus on 
isolated performance metrics, without consideration of integration and 
long-term reliability, widens the deployment gap identified in this study 
(Fig. 12)
These technical priorities directly address real-world deployment 
barriers. Hardware-aware edge optimization enables autonomous ro­

power supplies, reducing operational costs by an estimated 40-60% 
compared to GPU-based systems. Improved small-object detection and

5.4. Failure-mode analysis
Despite achieving a mAP above 91.1%, YOLO-based fruit detectors 
exhibit systematic failures under specific field conditions that warrant 
explicit categorization. Our analysis of underperforming studies 
revealed six recurring failure modes that collectively explain the 
persistent performance gap between controlled and natural environ­
ments (Fig. 13).
Occlusion remains the most prevalent limitation. Fruits obscured by 
50–70% foliage or overlapping with adjacent fruits are frequently 
missed or assigned low-confidence predictions (Fig. 13a). This problem 
persists even for architectures designed with occlusion-aware mecha­
nisms. Multi-class scenarios compound detection complexity, as shown 
in nectarine-pear mixed orchards where overlapping bounding boxes 
and class confusion occur (Fig. 13b) [129].

High-density clusters, common in berries, grapes, and tightly packed

Environmental factors exacerbate these geometric challenges. 
Ripeness-stage variation introduces intra-class appearance diversity that 
challenges single-model detection (Fig. 13b), where ripe and unripe 
tomatoes require simultaneous discrimination under varying illumina­

collapse fruit-foliage contrast [131] (Fig. 13d), where rear-lit apples

canopy becomes overexposed while the fruit surface remains under­

6. Research gaps and future directions
This study identifies the critical gaps between laboratory research 
and field-ready deployment. The following data-backed recommenda­
tions prioritize resources toward the most impactful challenges neces­
sary to bridge the identified deployment gap.
6.1. Critical technical priorities
The foremost technical priority is real-time edge deployment. 
Currently, only 6.1% of studies (n = 7) benchmark performance on edge 
hardware [41,84,114]. Although these studies confirm that Edge AI can 
maintain GPU-level accuracy (91.81% mAP), the associated 3.59 ×
slowdown in inference speed (18.1 FPS) fails to meet the >30 FPS 
requirement for real-time robotic harvesting. Future research must 
develop hardware-aware neural architecture search (NAS) specifically 
for edge devices, advance model-hardware co-design, and optimize 
post-training quantization (e.g., INT8/FP16) to achieve a target of 
>92% mAP with <100 ms latency on low-cost hardware.
Another significant challenge is small-object and occlusion robust­
ness. The performance for small fruits (e.g., berries) and in high- 
occlusion environments consistently trails by 5–10%, a limitation not 
fully resolved by current architectures [52,87]. To address this, we 
suggest the use of specialized modules, such as SPD-Conv [7,38], and 
dedicated small-object detection layers. Furthermore, a shift in focus 
from bounding boxes to instance segmentation (e.g., YOLOv8-seg) is 
critical for separating occluded fruit clusters [106,107,116], and the 
integration of depth sensors (e.g., LiDAR, stereo vision) should be 
explored for robust 3D spatial reasoning [28,112].
Finally, the issue of cross-domain generalization remains unad­
dressed. Models trained on custom datasets (95% of studies) exhibit a 
performance drop of 10–25%, when applied to unseen environments 
[72]. Closing this gap requires significant investment in domain adap­
tation and generalization techniques, such as adversarial training and 
style transfer, as well as synthetic data generation and few-shot learning 
methods to enable rapid adaptation to new orchards, varieties, and 
seasonal variations.
6.2. Methodological priorities
A fundamental methodological limitation is the lack of standardized 
benchmarking. The prevalence of custom, small-scale datasets prevents 
meaningful comparisons and obscures progress. The median dataset size 
was 1,948 images (Fig. 10c), but the mean was skewed higher, indi­
cating a lack of consensus on data collection [30,62]. A critical step 
forward would be the creation of a public, large-scale (>50,000 images) 
benchmark spanning multiple fruits, growth stages, seasons, and envi­
ronmental conditions. Complementing this, the establishment of a hid­
den test server for objective evaluation would ensure reproducible and 
fair model comparisons.
Equally important is the adoption of universally accepted reporting 
standards. The under-reporting of critical deployment metrics with only 
25.4% of studies reporting FPS and hardware details is often vague, 
severely hinders the assessment of real-world applicability [41,114]. 
Thus, future research studies should report mAP with confidence in­
tervals, FPS on specified hardware (both GPU and target edge device), 
and key efficiency metrics such as model parameters, FLOPs, and 
inference latency.
Furthermore, we encourage long-term and real-world field valida­
tion of developed models. Fewer than 15% of studies include multi- 
season or cross-orchard validation, and even fewer evaluate the full 
perception-to-action pipeline [28,108]. Future work should validate 
across more than three growing seasons and multiple geographic loca­
tions, with a concerted effort to move beyond isolated detection accu­
racy and prioritize the evaluation of complete, integrated robotic 
harvesting systems.
6.3. Application-oriented priorities
From an application perspective, research should focus on devel­
oping end-to-end robotic systems. Currently, only 15% of studies have 
evaluated a complete detection-to-harvest pipeline [108,127]. Future 
efforts should focus on the seamless integration of high-precision 
detection (>92% mAP) with real-time grasp planning and manipula­
tion, accompanied by economic feasibility analyses (e.g., ROI and labor 
reduction) to demonstrate commercial viability.
There are also significant opportunities in underexploited applica­
tions or areas. Immediate focus should be directed toward tropical and 
subtropical fruits (e.g., dragon fruit, durian) [64], nighttime operation 
(appearing in <2% of studies despite its potential to standardize light­
ing), and the development of weather-resilient perception systems 
capable of functioning reliably in rain, fog, and dust.
6.4. Emerging technology integration
Looking forward, the integration of emerging technologies presents a 
promising pathway. The rapid release of next-generation YOLO archi­
tectures in 2025 directly addresses several challenges identified in this 
review. YOLOv12 [134] introduces an attention-centric design with 
Area Attention (A2) and Residual Efficient Layer Aggregation Networks 
(R-ELAN), enhancing small-object feature extraction under variable 
illumination, a significant step toward the Vision Transformer capabil­
ities recommended above, while maintaining real-time inference.

7. Limitations
7.1. Review methodology limitations
Bibliometric analysis was employed only on the Scopus database, 
hence, potentially missing relevant publications indexed elsewhere. The 
English-language restriction excludes regional research published in 
local languages, particularly significant, given China’s dominant 
contribution. The February 2025 publication cutoff for bibliometrics 
underrepresents recent work. Gray literature, including technical re­
ports, theses, and industry white papers, was excluded despite it's 
potentially value. Systematic review keyword-based searches may miss 
studies employing non-standard terminology. Manual screening in­
volves inherent reviewer interpretation, introducing selection bias. 
Publication bias favoring positive results means that failed approaches 
remain underreported, limiting the understanding of ineffective 
strategies.
7.2. Field-level limitations
Reporting inconsistencies severely limit cross-study comparisons: 
FPS metrics appear in only 25% of studies; GPU specifications are 
frequently 
unspecified 
or 
vague; 
training 
procedures 
and 
hyperparameters are often incomplete; and 40% of studies lack 
comprehensive ablation studies. The custom dataset prevalence (95%) 
fundamentally constrains reproducibility and objective comparison. 
Small datasets (<1,000 images in 25% of studies) raise overfitting 
concerns with uncertain generalization ability. Single test set validation 
predominates with rare cross-dataset evaluations. Real-world field 
deployment and it's validation remain limited compared to laboratory or 
controlled environment testing. The review scope emphasizes detection 
tasks with limited coverage of complete harvesting systems that inte­
grate planning, grasping, and navigation. Economic analysis and long- 
term multi-season validation studies are nonexistent.
7.3. Acknowledgment of uncertainty
Performance metrics reflect specific experimental conditions with 
uncertain generalization to diverse scenarios in the real world. Rapid 
field evolution means that the current state-of-the-art may quickly 
become outdated. Commercial system performance may differ from 
research prototypes owing to engineering constraints, cost optimization, 
and operational conditions that are not represented in academic studies.
8. Conclusions
This study establishes YOLO-based fruit detection as a rapidly 
evolving research field, demonstrating robust technical capabilities with 
a detection accuracy over 91.0% across all studies. The architectural 
evolution from YOLOv3 to YOLOv8 reflects a consistent trajectory of 
refinement, with the widespread adoption of modifications, such as 
attention mechanisms and advanced loss functions, yielding reliable 
incremental performance gains. This technology has proven particularly

Despite these advancements, the transition from laboratory valida­
tion to reliable field deployment has encountered significant barriers. 
The principal challenge is the deployment gap, which is the inability of 
most current systems to simultaneously meet the stringent requirements 
of high accuracy, real-time inference speed, and efficient operation on 
resource-constrained edge hardware. This is compounded by persistent 
technical hurdles in small-object detection, robust handling of occlu­
sions and dense clusters, and a critical lack of generalization across 
different orchards, varieties, and environmental conditions.
The fruit detection progress is further hampered by methodological 
limitations, most notably the overwhelming reliance on custom datasets, 
which stifles reproducibility, obstructs objective benchmarking, and 
obscures the true generalizability of the proposed models.
Therefore, the path to widespread commercial viability hinges on 
addressing the following four critical priorities: 
• Hardware-Aware Edge Optimization: Developing models co- 
designed with edge processors to bridge the accuracy-efficiency 
gap for mobile robotic platforms.
• Standardized Benchmark Development: Establishing large-scale 
public datasets with diverse annotations to enable reproducible 
research and fair comparisons.
• Robustness through Domain Adaptation: Advancing techniques that 
enhance model generalization across varying conditions with mini­
mal retraining.
• End-to-End System Integration: Shifting focus from isolated detec­
tion tasks to fully integrated perception-manipulation pipelines 
validated in real-world settings.
Addressing these priorities is essential for translating the consider­
able promise of YOLO-based fruit detection into practical and econom­
ically viable agricultural automation systems.
