Abstract
The on-tree counting of chestnut fruit (bur) is essential for yield estimation and mon-
itoring of fruit load as well as tree health, which is important in determining the
management strategy for orchards, markets, and tree health monitoring. However, the
practice is still conducted by manual count or farmers’ intuition. Precise and effective
counting method is yet to be established. This study attempted to count chestnut fruits
with the object detection algorithm, You Only Look Once (YOLO), using the RGB
imagery collected with an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV). The model trained with
500 images (7866 burs labeled with bounding boxes) in YOLO version 4 (v4) could
count the number of burs with a R2 value of 0.98 and a root mean square error of 6.3
(8.3% of the mean) relative to the manual count in the data set for each whole tree
(n = 53). The R2 of linear regression between the number of burs obtained with the
YOLOv4 model and the total yield was 0.76, with a standard error of 1.08 kg tree−1
(26.4% of coefficient of variation), which was equivalent to the in situ burs count
by an expert technician. In addition, the number of burs counted with the YOLOv4
model after the adjustment of trunk circumference was negatively correlated with nut
weight. Overall, the study revealed that YOLO algorithm coupled with UAV-based
RGB imagery is a precise and efficient method for on-tree burs detection, which could
be used as an indicator of yield and fruit load.

widely cultivated fruit tree in Europe and Asia (Beccaro
et al., 2019). Castanea trees contribute to forest ecosystem
and landscape (Martin et al., 2012); thus, monitoring the

Machine learning techniques have been used for on-tree
fruit detection for yield estimation and machine harvesting in
fruit and nut tree, including citrus (Citrus sinensis L.), mango
(Mangifera indica L.), apple, grape (Vitis vinifera L.), and
kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis Planch.) (Apolo-Apolo, Pérez-
Ruiz, et al., 2020; Apolo-Apolo, Martínez-guanter, et al.,
2020; Fu et al., 2018; Koirala et al., 2019; Santos et al.,
2020). Moreover, deep learning, the subsection of machine
learning, recently became popular owing to the development
of many state-of-the-art frameworks for object detection and
their robustness in a background environment (e.g., color and
illumination) (Maheswari et al., 2021). In mango, a modified
You Only Look Once (YOLO) algorithm using ground vehicle
detected fruits at 0.988 of a coefficient of determination (R2)
and 5.0 of with a root mean square error (RMSE) per image
compared with the manual count in the imagery obtained
from a mango orchard of the Calypso variety, and an over-
all 62.3% of the harvest was recorded with 18.0 fruit per tree
of RMSE in video sequences by this algorithm coupled with
Kalman filter and Hungarian algorithm (Wang et al., 2019).
Zhang et al. (2022) employed their self-developed field rover
for taking video sequences and proposed a method consisting
of the fruit detection algorithm “OrangeYolo” (modified for
small-scale target detection) and the fruit tracking algorithm
“OrangeSort,” whose mean absolute error (MAE) was 0.081
compared with the manual count based on the video. Most
previous reports have solely focused on the detection preci-
sion; however, few studies have counted the fruit number at
the tree level to examine its relationship with the actual fruit
yield (Anderson et al., 2021). In addition, the applicability of
the estimated count data to tree management has not been con-
sidered. Although Adão et al. (2019) reported the detection
of the on-tree chestnut burs using convolutional neural net-
works (CNNs), its precision is relatively low (0.70 of a Dice
coefficient) due to a limited number of training data sets, and
they did not consider the efficiency of gathering imagery (i.e.,
just taking imagery with their own hands using a digital cam-
era). To the best of our knowledge, an accurate and efficient
method for bur detection, in which the applicability of state-
of-the-art frameworks for object detection should be tested,
was unreported.
High-throughput measurement of field and crop growth
conditions using unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)-based
remote sensing technologies has contributed to precise crop
management practices in fruit and nut tree, such as resource

The objective of this study was to establish a prediction
model of fruit yield and load using deep learning techniques.
We used the YOLO algorithm because of the high accuracy
of its on-tree fruit detection in other fruit trees (Vijayakumar
et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022). The per-
formance of on-tree burs detection with YOLO using RGB
imagery taken by UAV was initially evaluated. Subsequently,
the correlations between the observed yield and algorithm
count data were assessed using regression analysis to establish
the yield prediction model. Finally, the relationship between
the estimated fruit load and nut weight was examined in a
chestnut cultivar to confirm whether the proposed prediction
approach for burs counting could be used to assess the effect
of different degrees of pruning on nut weight.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1
Image acquisition using UAV
The study was conducted in 0.4-ha chestnut field at the Gifu
Prefectural Research Institute for Agricultural Technology
(GRIAT; Nakatsugawa, Gifu, Japan; 35˚29′ N, 137˚28′ E; Cfa

in Köppen–Geiger classification) in 2021. The cultivars of
Japanese chestnut (Castanea crenata Sieb. et Zucc.) and its
hybrid with Chinese chestnut (Castanea mollissima Blume)
were planted in different rows at a spacing of 5 × 5 m (400
trees ha−1). For stable production, pruning practices were
conducted every winter according to the commercial practices
by farmers in the Gifu prefecture. The UAV was flown man-
ually above the trees at 12–15 m altitude from the ground to
take picture of six trees at a vertically downward angle. The
shooting date was on a cloudy day to avoid the influence of
sunlight on July 29, 2021, approximately 1–2 months before
the start of harvesting under the natural condition. A quad-
copter Mavic Mini (DJI Technology, Shenzhen, China), with
an onboard sensor of 1/2.3″ CMOS camera with a field-of-
view of lensof 83˚, a focal length of 24 mm, a focal ration of
f/2.8f, and a focus of infinity, was used. The image resolution
of the camera was 2250 × 4000 pixels (JPG format).
2.2
Burs detection from UAV imagery using
YOLO algorithm
To develop an automatic burs detection model, the YOLO,
which is a deep learning model for object detection, was
used. Transfer learning on all layers of pretrained YOLO
weights was implemented to reduce the effort of labeling pro-
cess. We compared the performance of two YOLO versions,
including YOLOv3 (Redmon & Farhadi, 2018) and YOLOv4
(Bochkovskiy et al., 2020), which outperformed the previous
versions in both speed and accuracy. As square images of a
certain size (e.g., 416 × 416) were used by YOLO, the raw
images were split into 50 images (5 × 10), then the pixel res-
olutions of each split image resized to 416 × 416 to maintain
the resolution and the aspect ratio of imagery. The annotation
software, LabelImg (Tzutalin, 2015), was used for manual
labeling to identify the location of burs by enclosing with
bounding rectangles in all the 500 images taken. The total data
set was randomly split into training (80%, n = 400) and val-
idation (20%, n = 100). The latter was used to maintain the
generalization performance of the models during training to
prevent overfitting.
The model and labels were configured in the numeri-
cal computation library TensorFlow (https://www.tensorflow.
org/) and Google Colaboratory, a cloud service offering a free
for use single 16GB NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU in open-source
(Apolo-Apolo, Pérez-Ruiz, et al., 2020). The training model
was optimized with a stochastic gradient descent method
and a mini-batch momentum factor value set to 0.9, with a
0.0005 decay at a learning rate of 0.001. Data augmenta-
tions with varying hue, saturation, brightness, image sizes,
cropping, and horizontal flipping were conducted with default
setting.

To evaluate the association between the number of burs
counted by algorithm and ground truth (manual/in situ count-
ing), the raw images of the 53 grafting clones of 9 cultivars

acquired images were resized into 608 × 608 pixels, and
then each processed with trained models to obtain the num-

were conducted with the “cell counter” plug-in in the ImageJ
(https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). The in situ burs counting in each
whole tree was done by an expert technician in July 26–30,
2021.
With the data set for each whole tree mentioned earlier
(n = 53), the most accurate model for detecting on-tree burs
was set a confidence threshold of 0.3 and 0.1 for YOLOv4
and YOLOv3, respectively (Table S2), which were deter-
mined by varying confidence thresholds in object detection.
For validation of burs counting, the relationships between the
number of burs obtained by YOLO and manual/in situ count-
ing were assessed using linear regression analysis, and the
model performance was compared based on R2, MAE, and
RMSE values.

2.4
Yield and nut weight evaluation
To evaluate the relationship between yield and the number of
burs counted by YOLOv4 with UAV imagery, the numbers of
nuts, total yield (total weight of nuts before selecting intact
nuts in each tree), and yield of intact nuts from trees used in
the model performance evaluation (n = 53) were recorded.
Mature nuts in each tree were harvested, and we selected intact
nuts (i.e., without damage by split and insect infestation) each
day for about 2 months (from late August to early October).
Nuts were then counted and weighed in gram with a digital
scale.
The newly released “Ena hougetsu” chestnut cultivar by
GRIAT (Kamio et al., 2020b) was used to check the associa-
tion between nut weight and the burs number in the data using
the YOLO algorithm. “Ena hougetsu” has high fruit quality
(sweet taste and fine yellow kernel color) and low rate of nut
damage caused by splitting or insect infestation. These natures
make the cultivar highly rated by breeders despite its rela-
tively small nut weight compared to that of Japanese chestnut
(21.2 g) (Arakawa et al., 2022; Kamio et al., 2020b).
The nut weight was calculated by dividing the gross weight
of intact nuts by their total number in each tree (Nishio
et al., 2014). The numbers of burs were counted by YOLOv4
using UAV-based imagery as described earlier. The trunk cir-
cumference was measured at 20 cm above ground level on
November 25, 2020. As trunk circumference reflects tree size
(Forshey & Elfving, 1979), it was used to normalize data (the
number of burs and the pruning degree) to each tree size. The
number of burs and fruit-bearing branches divided by trunk
circumference was calculated (hereafter the former was called
the normalized burs number). In this metrics, 4, 8, and 12
fruit-bearing branches per trunk circumference (in 10 cm) cor-
responded to heavy, middle, and light pruning, respectively,
using 13 seven-year-old “Ena hougetsu” trees.
3
RESULTS
3.1
Model performance for counting the
number of burs of chestnut tree
The YOLO algorithm was trained for the automated on-tree
counting of burs from UAV imagery data consisting of 500
images and 7866 labeled burs. Compared to the burs number
counted manually, the values of R2 and RMSE (burs per tree)
were 0.976 and 6.3 in YOLOv4, respectively, and YOLOv4
outperformed YOLOv3 in the imagery data set for each whole
tree (Figure 1a; Figure S1A; n = 53). In addition, metrics
of the YOLOv4 model performance were better than that of
YOLOv3. F1-scores of 0.84 and 0.77 and AP with 50% IoU
of 84.27 and 75.05 were observed in the validation data set for
YOLOv4 and YOLOv3, respectively (Table 1).
The undercounting (i.e., false negative detection) was
mainly due to occlusion by foliage and clustering of burs
on parts of a tree, which tend to occur in the rich fruit-
bearing cultivars, such as “Ena hourai” (Figure 2) (Kamio
et al., 2020b), although bias in the undercounting occurrence
was insignificant (p = 0.086, Fisher’s exact test; Table S3),
whereas overcounting was caused by low resolution, which
caused false positive detection by turning young shoots or
leaves into burs-like shape and color (Figure S2). Given that
some burs were covered with foliage, the count data of YOLO
and observation in situ by technical specialist (hereafter in situ
counting) were compared. The RMSE values were 60.7 and
67.7 (burs per tree) in YOLOv4 and YOLOv3, respectively,
whereas the R2 in YOLOv4 (0.828) was slightly lower than
that of YOLOv3 (0.846) (Figure 1b; Figure S1B). The num-
ber of burs counted by YOLOv4 ranged from 42.9% to 103.6%
(65.5% in average) for in situ count, which indicated that about
one third of the burs were hidden when observed directly
from above. Overall, the results indicated that YOLOv4 was
the best model with a confidence threshold of 0.3 and was
subsequently applied for the UAV imagery.
3.2
Regression analysis between yield and
the number of YOLOv4-counted burs
The strength of correlation between yield and the number of
burs counted by YOLO algorithm evaluated by linear regres-
sion was compared to that of in situ counting. The “Ena
hougetsu” cultivar was only added for yield of intact nuts as an
explanatory variable for a multiple regression because of its
superior nut qualities, such as low damaged nuts frequency
(Kamio et al., 2020a). Adjusted R2 values for the number
of nuts, total yield, and yield of intact nuts versus the algo-
rithm count were 0.75, 0.76, and 0.73, respectively (Table 2).
The standard error of total yield was 1.08 kg (26.4% of coef-
ficient of variation). Significant effect (p = 0.003) of “Ena
hougetsu” in the regression for the yield of intact nuts was
detected (Table S4). Interestingly, adjusted R2 for the algo-
rithm count was equivalent to or slightly higher than that of
in situ count, with total yield and yield of intact nuts values of
0.77 and 0.69, respectively (Table 2).

3.3
Relationship between nut weight and
the number of algorithm-counted burs with
adjustment of trunk circumference
Thirteen trees with three levels of the pruning degree,
including light, middle, and heavy, were used to investigate
the effects of pruning on nut weight. The normalized number
of burs showed a significant negative correlation (R = −0.90,
p = 2.4 × 10−5; Pearson’s correlation test) with nut weight
(Figure 3). Moreover, the nut weight and normalized bur
number under heavy pruning showed the highest and low-
est values, respectively, and were significantly different
(p < 0.05, Dunnett test) from those of light tree pruning. In
contrast, moderate and insignificant values were observed for
nut weight and normalized bur number under middle pruning
(Table 3).

DISCUSSION
YOLO algorithm using UAV RGB imagery used in this study
could count the on-tree burs with high accuracy (0.84 of the
F1-score) and might outperform the previously reported mod-
els (0.70 of the Dice coefficient [equivalent to F1-score];
Adão et al., 2019); however, it is yet to be verified because of
the difference in the evaluation data set. Flying a commercial
low-cost multi-copter at 12–15 m altitude could capture about
six trees at 5 × 5 m of planting spacing, which suggested that
this burs estimation approach is potentially a high-throughput
and cost-effective method, which could efficiently be applied
in the detection of burs for yield, fruit load, and tree health
monitoring.
The RMSE values for YOLOv4-counted burs in young
trees (7-year-old or below) were 6.3 (8.3% of the mean value)
relative to the manual count, which could be attributed to
occlusion and clustering of burs and low resolution. Over-
all, 30% of burs were covered with foliage, and chestnut
can grow into tall trees in the absence of pruning (Beccaro
et al., 2019); therefore, in older trees, higher resolution of
imagery or orchard management practices, such as pruning,
were crucial for burs detection. Proposed method can easily
be applied in some commercial cultivation regions of Japan
where chestnut trees are pruned and maintained at a height of
3.5 m (Araki, 2004). Different conditions, such as sunlight and
flight altitude, can affect burs detection; thus, trial with more
training data under the varied condition, including weather,
tree age, and field, is necessary to obtain the precision and
robustness of the algorithm count. Note that trained models
in this study could still achieve a level of accuracy in other
fields or conditions. Improvement of model performance for
burs detection can be achieved by modifying the YOLO algo-
rithm (Koirala et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al.,
2022), utilization of other algorithms, such as faster R-CNN
(Apolo-Apolo, Martínez-guanter, et al., 2020), or using video
sequences instead of imagery (Wang et al., 2019; Zhang et al.,
2022). The automatic labeling tool, CycleGAN, which can
transfer an existing domain fruit data set to a new domain
without extra manual labeling (Zhang, Chen, et al., 2021),
might be useful for constructing more precise models.
Despite certain errors in the burs number counted with
YOLOv4 in this study, its correlation with yield was equiv-
alent or superior to the in situ count data. This might be
attributed to shade intolerance of the Castanea spp.; namely,
the burs under the shade from foliage produce low quality nuts
with small sizes, which would not contribute to yield (Araki,

of yield prediction in other fields or years could be influenced

Therefore, more precise estimation of yield and its compo-
nents could be achieved using other statistical or machine
learning models that consider the counted number of burs and
the environmental conditions as explanatory variables. Such
strategies have successfully been used in other studies with
several crop species (Apolo-Apolo, Martínez-guanter, et al.,

struction would be useful in the management production fields
(Apolo-Apolo, Pérez-Ruiz, et al., 2020). Therefore, future
studies using more collected yield data, burs number, and
environmental conditions in different test fields and repeated
over the years are needed to generate yield maps for making
farmers’ recommendation.
Nut weight was associated with the normalized burs num-
ber counted by YOLOv4 in the cultivar “Ena hougetsu”
(Kamio et al., 2020a). The applicability of burs counting
approach for assessing nut weight in other cultivars or at dif-
ferent tree ages (i.e., over 10 years old) should be explored in
future research. Varying the degree of pruning confirmed that
nut weight could be controlled by altering fruit load through
pruning to a certain degree, and the normalized burs number
could be used to estimate nut weight roughly. In citrus, fruit
size was classified by imagery with calibration process using
scale bar after segmentation of on-tree fruits (Apolo-Apolo,
Martínez-guanter, et al., 2020). A chestnut fruit is composed
of several nuts enclosed in a spiny capsule, and the spines vary
in length and intensity among species and cultivars (Beccaro
et al., 2019). Moreover, bur size is determined by the number
of enclosed nuts, which vary with pollination condition, such
as weather, distance, or difference in flowering stage with
adjacent tree in the wind-pollinated and self-incompatible
nature of Castanea species (Clapper, 1954; Seavey & Bawa,
1986; Shimura et al., 1971). These factors suggest that the
precision of the on-tree nut weight estimation by algorithm
would be affected by abiotic and biotic factors, although it
may technically be possible.
5
CONCLUSIONS
This study demonstrated that deep learning techniques using
UAV-based imagery could accurately and efficiently detect
on-tree chestnut fruits, with application potential in estimating
yield and fruit load. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first model development report for precise and effective burs
detection that demonstrates the association between yield and
nut weight. More data to improve the model and train other
models for detection are necessary for enhancing its precision
and robustness. The number of burs counted by YOLO model
was positively correlated with yield, whose strength is equiva-
lent or slightly superior to the laborious in situ count approach.
Further, regression analysis using the count data revealed a
significant cultivar effect on yield, which suggested that cul-
tivar classification could contribute to improved precision of
yield estimation. Assessment of fruit load using the number of
burs counted with YOLO model and tree size provides criti-
cal information that could guide farmers on the recommended
degree of pruning to achieve increased nut weight. The valid-
ity of this approach needs to be assessed with other cultivars
and older trees in future studies.
