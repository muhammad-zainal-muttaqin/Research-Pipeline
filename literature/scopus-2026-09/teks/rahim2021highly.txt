ABSTRACT
Automatic maturity recognition and counting of tomatoes during
different growth stages from images is of great significance for
optimal management in tomato farming, long-term yield prediction
and robotic harvesting. In this study, we present a novel method
that combines deep instance segmentation, data synthesis and color
analysis to accurately recognize and count tomatoes during differ-
ent growth stages. In our approach, we trained the Mask R-CNN
instance segmentation neural network with synthetically gener-
ated dataset to accurately segment all tomato instances in an image,
then color-based thresholding was applied to identify their growth
stage and count the tomato number accordingly. The synthetic
data generation algorithm preserved the physical structure of the
data objects, thus produced photorealistic synthesized cultivation
scenes. The trained model demonstrated substantial performance
with maximum 92.1% average precision and 91.4% recall against
the real-world test datasets for tomato segmentation. The tomato
maturity recognition accuracy of the color-analysis method was
evaluated by comparing estimated count with ground-truth manual
counts. Our experimental results demonstrated high accuracy of
tomato counting during three different growth stages: green, half
ripened and fully ripened.

INTRODUCTION
Deep learning-based computer vision techniques using convolu-
tional neural network (CNN) present a great potential for automatic
recognition of targets in non-structured agricultural scenes as the
networks have the ability to learn complex models that can be in-
variant to different types of variations in the image data. In the past
decade, computer vision methods using deep learning made signifi-
cant progress in image classification, object localization, semantic
and instance segmentation and consecutively was successfully ap-
plied in many domains. However, training the neural networks for
high-end performance requires availability of sufficient number
of labeled datasets. Data collection and annotation is a laborious,
costly and time-consuming process, and represents a major draw-
back in most current deep learning pipeline.
A promising solution to this bottleneck is to learn from synthetic
images since all ground-truth annotations can be automatically
obtained without the need for human labor. Many researchers
have employed several synthetic content generation approaches for
the preparation of training dataset for various image-based plant
phenotyping tasks. Isokane et al. [7] used the artificial plant models
for rendering synthetic images, and for creating dataset to train
deep neural networks for the estimation of branching pattern, while
Ward et al. generated synthetic images of Arabidopsis rendered
from 3D models and used them for the task of leaf segmentation
[15]. In [12], a modified version of the Inception-Resnet architecture
was trained with a synthetic dataset of red circular blobs, and was
able to obtain tomato detection accuracy of 91% on a dataset of
cherry tomato plants images collected from Google images.
Unfortunately, an important performance issue emerges due to
the gaps between the synthetic and real-world domains. To address
the domain gap issue, many studies attempted to generate realistic
images, such as by using generative adversarial networks (GAN)
[5] and introduced synthetic-to-real domain adaptation. Giuffrida
et al. [14] used GAN-generated images to train a neural network
and employed domain adaptation for Arabidopsis leaf counting.
Similarly, Arsenovic et al. used StyleGAN [8] to generate training
images for the plant disease image classification [2].
On the other side of the coin, an advantage of utilizing synthetic
dataset is the capability of creating (nearly) infinite number of train-
ing data. Techniques that are bridging the synthetic and real-world
gaps by leveraging the advantage is domain randomization, which
train the neural networks to learn essential features using large
variations of synthetic images with randomly sampled physical
parameters. In [13], the researchers adopted domain randomization
to synthesize barley seed images which were used to train Mask
R-CNN for the task of phenotyping barley seed morphology of
several cultivars. Collectively, the use of synthesized images has a

The ability to automatically recognize tomato maturity and count
tomato number during different growth stages from images enable
growers to better optimize management in tomato farming such as
irrigation, temperature control in greenhouse according to ripening
stage and harvest decisions based on tomato maturity such as labor
allocation, storage, packaging, and harvest scheduling and to esti-
mate long-term tomato yield [9, 16]. Automatic tomato counting
and maturity recognition during different growth stages in their
life cycle remain a challenge, due to diverse tomato shapes, size and
color variability during growth stages, and also due to unstructured
complex agricultural environment that includes overlapping and
occlusions.
In this study, we propose a novel image analysis pipeline that
combines deep learning, data synthesis and color analysis, by pre-
senting a method of synthetic cultivation scene generation mim-
icking the physical appearance of tomato bunches, and utilizing
the rendered images as training set for MASK R-CNN [6] instance
segmentation network, and finally applying color-based threshold-
ing to network produced outputs to recognize tomato growth stage.
The evaluation results against two real-world datasets demonstrate
significant average precision and recall in tomato segmentation
and high accuracy in tomato counting according to their growth
stage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Figure 1 illustrates the summary of the entire image analysis ap-
proach presented in this study for tomato maturity recognition and
counting during different growth stages.
2.1
Experimental Reference Dataset
The experimental reference dataset was utilized as a source of seg-
mented tomato images and a reference set to create conditions
and scene grammars for generating realistic synthetic dataset. Our
reference dataset consisted of 100 photographic high resolution
RGB images of tomatoes under cultivation in greenhouses, supple-
mented with high quality manual annotations (per pixel class labels)
of most visible tomatoes collected from Laboro Tomato dataset [17].
We carefully selected 60 images of big size tomatoes and another
40 images of small size tomatoes during different growth states in
order to ensure balance in reference set.
2.2
Structural Tomato Cultivation Scene
Generation
2.2.1
Extracting Tomatoes from Reference Set. At first, all tomato
instances were cut from the reference set (described above in section
2.1) images according to their per pixel annotations. The original
set contained ∼1100 extracted tomato images. However, many of
these tomatoes were not suitable for generating realistic synthetic
images, therefore, we selected images where tomato appears fully
(or almost) with minimal occlusion. The reasoning behind removing
occulated tomatoes is that in the agricultural scene it is uncommon
to see cut tomatoes and the appearance of partially occulated toma-
toes is a side effect of the image generation algorithm described
below in section 2.2.3. After removal, rescaling in a random range
between 48 and 200 pixels (determine empirically) was applied on
the selected set in order to increase variability and tomato numbers
until a set of 2000 images was obtained. Then all tomato images

2.2.2
Scene Backgrounds. Like [11], in order to simulate green-
house environment, we used images of tomato plants under culti-
vation in greenhouses as background. 100 images of tomato plants
without any clearly visible tomatoes were photographed in green-
houses owned by Sun Farm Nakayama Co., Inc., Japan. All back-
ground images were resized to 600 × 800 pixels and then mirrored
to increase image numbers.
2.2.3
Synthetic Scene Generation Algorithm. We observed the ge-
ometrical structure and topological distribution of tomatoes in
photographed tomatoes from reference set (described in section
2.1) and developed tomato cultivation scene generation algorithm
consisted of following criteria: i) tomatoes grow on tomato plants in
groups called tomato bunch, ii) tomato size and number of tomatoes
in a bunch are correlated, iii) in a bunch, bigger tomatoes appear at
top while the smaller ones appear at bottom of the bunch, iv) distri-
bution of tomatoes in a bunch follow some patterns. We generated
tomato cultivation images automatically based on the set of rules
such as detailed in Algorithm 1.
2.3
Color-based Maturity Recognition
We introduced post-processing step to the raw outputs obtained
from Mask R-CNN model to identify growth stage of each seg-
mented tomato instance. We aimed to recognize tomato maturity
during three different growth stages: fully ripened, half ripened
and green (Figure 2). The growth stages of tomatoes were defined
based on their color pixel percentage: i) fully ripened: filled with
90% or more red color shade; ii) half ripened: filled with 30 - 89%
red or orange color shade; iii) green: filled with red or orange color
less than 30%.
For each segmented object, pixels corresponding to its mask
were transformed into HSV color space and thresholding based
on color analysis was applied over three sets of six parameters
(two sets for fully ripened category and one set for half ripened
category). The threshold parameters were determined empirically
using OpenCV 3.4 by analyzing the hue, saturation and value his-
togram of 200 samples (collected from segmented tomatoes used
to generate synthetic dataset) for each maturity category. For fully
ripened category the minimum threshold values of hue, saturation
and value components for first set are 0, 140 and 145, respectively
and maximum threshold values are 5, 255 and 255, respectively and
the second set of threshold parameters for fully ripened category
are determined as minimum hue, saturation and value are 174, 120
and 135, respectively and maximum values are 179, 255 and 255,
respectively. The two parameter sets were combined using OR op-
erator for convenience. For half ripened category, the minimum
threshold values of hue, saturation and value components are 3, 144
and 155, respectively and maximum threshold values are 18, 255
and 255, respectively. Detected raw tomato instances that did not
fall within these three sets of parameters are considered as green.
2.4
Metrics for Model Evaluation
To evaluate the performance of segmentation using Mask R-CNN
we used two metrics recall and average precision. A detection is de-
termined as correct when the detection threshold of the intersection-
over-union (IoU) [4] between the ground-truth and predicted mask
is over 0.5. Then, recall (r) can be measured as the ratio of true pos-
itive matches (correct detections) over the total number of ground-
truth objects and precision (p) can be obtained as the ratio of correct
detections (true positive) over the total number object detections.

3
EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND
DISCUSSION
Segmentation was performed with the publicly available tensor-
flow/keras based Matterport implementation [1] of mask R-CNN,
pre-trained with the COCO dataset [10] on a machine with an
NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 Ti 11GB GPU, Intel ®core i7-9700k
3.60 GHz processor and 64GB RAM, running Linux Ubuntu 18.0,
supported by CUDA 9.0.
3.1
Datasets
A dataset of total 6000 synthetic images were generated with 1000
images for each six categories (see Algorithm 1). These images
were used to train Mask R-CNN model for single class tomato seg-
mentation. The trained segmentation model then evaluated against
two real-world test sets; TomatoA, consisted of 160 images col-
lected from Laboro Tomato dataset and TomatoB, consisted of 60
images of tomatoes captured by smartphone camera in commercial
greenhouses. Table 2 presents detail of two test sets.
3.2
Quality of Generated Images and Model
Evaluation
We assessed the quality of the synthetically generated images by
evaluating the tomato instance segmentation performance of the
Mask R-CNN model trained purely on our synthetic dataset. Table
3 presents the average precision and recall metrics on the two real-
world test sets for single class tomato segmentation by Mask R-CNN
models. The tomato_synthetic model which was trained on syn-
thetic tomato images obtained considerably higher values of aver-
age precision and recall compared to the model trained on reference
set containing 100 real images. Figure 4 (top row) shows some ex-
amples of visual results of tomato segmentation on images from our
two test sets. From the visual results it can be observed that the syn-
thetic model produced both few false positives and false negatives. It
can be seen in Table 3 that segmentation performance of the model
on TomatoB test set, which consists of images with tomatoes of
different shape, color and texture than that of the tomatoes used for
generating synthetic training set was lower (average precision and
recall < 90%) than performance on TomatoA test set (average preci-
sion and recall >=91%). These results indicate that shape, color and
texture variability of the target object influenced model’s detection
performance. Another reason of high performance for TomatoA test
set is that it has identical tomato distribution pattern as the synthetic
training set.
3.3
Maturity Recognition and Counting
The tomato segmentations produced by Mask R-CNN model
(tomato_synthetic) were color-analyzed to recognize their matu-
rity stages: fully ripened, half ripened and green. Table 4 presents
the accuracy of estimated count of tomatoes obtained using our
image analysis pipeline for each image in our two test sets during
three different growth stages. The recognition and counting accu-
racy for fully ripened and green tomatoes were higher (R2>0.92
and R2>0.90 for TomatoA and TomatoB test set, respectively) than
that of half ripened tomatoes (R2<0.76), which can be explained
by the ambiguity in defining color cutoff for ground truth and
small number of half ripened tomato instances in test sets as
data size highly influence R2 value. From Figure 3 it can be ob-
served that in most cases, the counts estimated by our approach
was close to ground truth counts. Figure 5 (bottom rows) illus-
trates some examples of maturity recognition on images from our
test sets.

CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we presented a novel approach of fruit maturity
recognition during different growth stages by combining deep in-
stance segmentation, data synthesis and color-based analysis. We
showed that an instance segmentation neural network intent on
accurately segmenting tomato instances, can be effectively trained
with synthetic scenes generated by preserving object’s physical
structure and distribution. Experimental results against two real-
world datasets showed that the segmentation model obtained signif-
icant average precision and recall. We subsequently evaluated the

tomato maturity recognition accuracy of the color-analysis method
by comparing estimated tomato count with manual count. Our
quantitative experiments showed highly accurate tomato counting
during three different growth stages (green, half ripened and fully
ripened). By producing highly accurate identification of tomato
growth states and counting our approach showed a great potential
for low-cost optimal tomato farm management, yield estimation
and robotic harvesting. Moreover, presented synthetic scene gen-
eration method can effectively address the labeled data scarcity
problem encountered when deploying deep learning-based analysis
in agricultural domain.
