Palm Counting by Eliminating Re-counting

Abstract—Accurate counting of oil palm (Elaeis 
guineensis) is critical for effective plantation management 
and yield forecasting, yet conventional ground surveys 
like manual inspection are labor-intensive, costly, and 
prone to significant inaccuracies. While Unmanned 
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) provide an effective data 
acquisition platform, simpler automated methods using 
object detection alone fail by repeatedly counting the 
same tree in overlapping video frames, preventing a 
reliable inventory. This paper proposes an end-to-end 
deep learning framework that resolves this re-counting 
challenge by integrating the lightweight YOLOv8n object 
detector with the robust DeepSORT tracking algorithm. 
To ensure high performance, the detection model was 
trained on a large public dataset of over 18,877 annotated 
aerial images. This pipeline processes UAV video by first 
using YOLOv8n to detect all palm trees within a frame, 
then passing these detections to DeepSORT, which 
assigns a unique and persistent ID to each individual tree. 
The framework’s effectiveness is demonstrated by its high 
performance, achieving a detection accuracy of 98.7% 
mAP. By preserving a unique identity for each tree 
throughout the video sequence, this integrated method 
effectively solves the core issue of redundant counting. 
This work thus presents a scalable, cost-effective, and 
validated model that overcomes the limitations of both 
manual and basic automated systems, delivering the 
reliable data required for modern precision agriculture. 
Keywords— Deep Learning, Object Tracking, Precision 
Agriculture, UAV Imagery, YOLOv8, Counting 
I. INTRODUCTION  
The oil palm (Elaeis guineensis) management is absolutely 
essential for both the socio-economics and the  
environmental sustainability of the industry and the 
subsequent positive changes that will occur. Sustainable 
management with accurate yield predictions can only be 
performed through an accurate inventory of every tree within 
a plantation. Unfortunately, ground-based surveys have a 
reputation for being extremely labour-intensive, expensive 
and the potential for errors can be significant, making them 
impractical for large-scale operations. As a result, researchers 
have instead pivoted to find automated solutions through 
drone imagery and deep learning. While these advancements 
have significantly improved the ability to detect oil palm 
trees, a review of the literature reveals a persistent challenge: 
the failure to produce an accurate inventory from video due 
to redundant counting. For example, the deep transfer 
learning approach in [2] achieved high accuracy in detecting 
trees, but its methodology is limited to static images and fails 
to address the re-counting issue inherent in video. Similarly, 
the work by Kipli et al. [3] presents a model for counting, yet 
this is an aggregation of frame-by-frame detections and does 
not prevent the same tree from being tallied multiple times. 
The object-based deep learning framework in [8] provides 
robust detection but lacks a tracking mechanism to manage 
object identities across frames. Even advanced models 
focused on domain adaptation [11] or enhanced detection [1] 
concentrate on improving performance on static images and 
are therefore susceptible to the same re-counting errors in a 
video stream. These collective limitations highlight the clear 
research gap that our work addresses. 
Earlier methods applied classical image processing methods 
to high-resolution satellite images, including template 
matching and watershed segmentation, but these were usually 
challenged by tree crown overlap and variations in 
illumination [1]. Recent literature has embraced deep 
learning techniques, mainly based on Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CNNs) for detection. For example, models with 
architectures such as Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN have 
been successful in detecting single palm crowns from single 
aerial images [2], [3]. Although these detection-oriented 
models are an improvement, this method does not solve the 
core problem of video-based monitoring from UAVs, where 
a tree is imaged in many consecutive frames. This intrinsic 
weakness results in extreme over-counting, so these 
approaches are not applicable for producing a reliable 
inventory based on a continuous aerial survey. Other research 
has addressed other issues, such as disease detection [4] or 
yield estimation [5], but the essential problem of sound, 
reliable counting through tracking is a key, open challenge. 
The core problem is the fundamental failure of current deep 
learning models, designed for static images, when the 
researchers are applied to continuous UAV video for tree 
enumeration. These methods treat each video frame in 
isolation, meaning their counting is a simple aggregation of 
detections 
without 
any 
temporal 
tracking. 
This 
simultaneously addresses the potential problem of multiple 
counting, where one tree is counted multiple times as it passes

 To address the limitations of existing methods, this paper

The first stage employs the YOLOv8n object detector, a 
lightweight and efficient model chosen for its high speed and 
accuracy in aerial imagery. It is tasked with detecting all 
possible oil palm trees in every video frame Fig.1. As soon as 
detected, the bounding box coordinates of each tree are sent 
to the second stage that uses the DeepSORT (Deep Simple 
Online and Realtime Tracking) algorithm. DeepSORT's main 
task is object identity management between frames by 
assigning a unique, permanent ID to every individual tree 
being tracked. This process converts a basic detection system 
into a complex tracking system, able to recognize distinct 
trees and identify the same tree across time, thus resolving the 
multiple counting problem. The main contribution of this 
work is an efficient and low-cost framework that produces an 
accurate final count by effectively combining object 
detection with strong tracking. Experimental results confirm 
the good performance of this approach. The YOLOv8n model 
detects with an accuracy of 98.7% mAP@0.5. This combined 
system aptly addresses the problematic issue of double 
counting trees in UAV videos to provide a notable 
advancement in contrast to the prohibitively expensive 
manual surveys and less capable automated systems. The 
work thus presents an established model of building advanced 
monitoring solutions in contemporary precision agriculture.

II. RELATED WORKS  
Automated agricultural surveillance via remote sensing is an 
active research field, with this expansive review covering the 
history of such methods from traditional image processing to 
state-of-the-art deep learning models to contextualize this 
work and clearly define the research gap  the system fills. The 
survey 
is 
structured 
thematically, 
addressing 
early 
computational methods, the maturation of deep learning for 
detection, its use in holistic plantation analysis, and finally, 
the critical and often-neglected necessity of object tracking 
for accurate enumeration from video streams. Early work in 
automated oil palm counting relied on traditional computer 
vision and machine learning. These works primarily 
employed high-resolution aerial or satellite images; for 
instance, the study in [1] utilized a watershed segmentation 
algorithm, a technique that casts the intensity gradient of an 
image as a topography to separate objects with closed 
contours, which is effective for well-defined, non-
overlapping palm crowns. Likewise, the work described in 
[6] attempted template matching, where a pre-computed 
template of a palm crown is slid over an image to find 
matches. While groundbreaking, both methods were highly 
susceptible to variations in environmental conditions like 
illumination and scale, leading to poor robustness. Seeking 
more resilience, the work in [7] employed a Support Vector 
Machine (SVM) classifier. This approach, however, required 
hand-engineered features such as color histograms and 
texture descriptors, which involved significant manual effort 
and domain knowledge. The advent of Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CNNs) revolutionized the field by enabling 
automatic feature learning from raw pixel values, leading to 
a new generation of models. The majority of subsequent 
literature focused on deploying deep learning object detectors 
to identify oil palms in static images. Two-stage detectors, 
particularly the region-based family, were a prominent 
method. The work in [2] was one of the first successes using 
Faster R-CNN, employing a Region Proposal Network 
(RPN) to first identify regions of interest and subsequently 
classify them with high accuracy for single palm crown 
localization. The authors of [3] later advanced this with Mask 
R-CNN, an extension that not only detects objects but also 
provides a pixel-level segmentation mask for each instance, 
offering valuable data on crown size. Later work presented in 
[9] further refined and extended these region-based models

for other agricultural environments. While accurate, these 
two-stage detectors are often computationally intensive. 
Simultaneously, other researchers investigated single-shot 
detectors for their speed advantages. The work in [10] 
focused on a YOLO-based model to efficiently process large 
aerial datasets by reducing detection to a single regression 
problem. This focus on real-time performance was also 
pursued in the work of [11] and [13], which utilized 
alternative YOLO variants. These models were designed for 
and validated on predominantly static, non-overlapping 
images. This design fails to account for the nature of UAV 
data, which consists of continuous video streams where the 
same tree is captured repeatedly from multiple viewpoints 
across thousands of frames.  This leads to the critical and 
unavoidable problem of redundant counting, where each new 
detection is treated as a unique instance. Consequently, the 
final count becomes grossly inflated, rendering the 
enumeration process unreliable for producing an accurate 
inventory. This underscores a crucial distinction: while object 
detection is a well-solved problem, a temporal tracking 
process is the essential, often-missing component required 
for a true counting solution. In addition to the specific 
challenge of enumeration, deep learning has been 
successfully applied to other significant areas of oil palm 
management, speaking to its versatility in precision 
agriculture. One major research area has been the automation 
of disease detection. The research in [4] built a CNN-based 
system for the early detection of Ganoderma Basal Stem Rot 
from ground-level trunk images. This was also the focus of 
the work in [14] and [16], which explored the use of 
multispectral and hyperspectral UAV imagery enables the 
early detection of stress indicators in tree canopies, often 
before it becomes visible to the naked eye.. Another 
significant area of research has been yield estimation. The 
papers in [5] and [15] moved beyond tree counting to 
detecting and counting fruit bunches, providing a more direct 
method for production forecasting. Furthermore, other 
research has focused on monitoring the overall health and 
growth stages of palms. For example, the study in [12] used 
deep learning for canopy health assessment, while the 
research in [17] was aimed at distinguishing different growth 
stages. While these applications are critically important, their 
goal is classification or semantic segmentation, not the 
specific challenge of creating a distinct numerical inventory 
of each tree in a plantation through robust object tracking. 
This thorough literature review, therefore, identifies a clear 
and compelling research deficit. The evolution of technology 
from traditional image processing to the point where object 
detection is a largely solved problem for oil palms in static 
images has firmly established detection as a foundational 
step. Yet, literature largely stops there. The challenge is no 
longer just finding the trees, but uniquely identifying and 
counting them in a dynamic video stream. Methodologies that 
can effectively associate detections between frames, handle 
temporary occlusions, and assign a persistent identity to each 
object are urgently needed. This work directly targets this 
deficiency by introducing an end-to-end framework that 
combines a high-performance detector (YOLOv8n) with a 
purpose-built 
tracking 
algorithm 
(DeepSORT). 
By 
concentrating on solving the re-counting issue, the paper 
extends beyond mere detection and offers a practical, 
scalable, and accurate solution for producing the reliable 
inventories that contemporary precision agriculture requires. 
III. DESING AND IMPLEMENTATION 
The paper used Yolov8n [8] Fig.2 due to its outstanding 
efficiency, offering the best possible trade-off between 
having fast inference speed and detection accuracy. Its 
lightness is particularly well-suited for applications that will 
eventually run on computationally constrained platforms, 
such as real-time onboard processing on a UAV. 
A. Data Collection and Preprocessing 
In this approach, the author employed a large, publicly 
available dataset specifically created for agricultural remote 
sensing, comprising a total of 18,877 aerial images of oil 
palm plantations.  First, an auto-orient function was applied 
to each image, which programmatically corrects the 
orientation of the image based on its metadata. All images 
were then resampled to a standard size of 640x640 pixels. 
This resampling, performed via a stretch transformation, is 
not only crucial to enable batch processing efficiently but also 
aligns the input data with the version of the YOLOv8n model. 
In a final preprocessing step, all images were converted from 
the default RGB color space to grayscale. This was selected 
to reduce the computational complexity of the model and to 
encourage it to learn global features in terms of shape, 
texture, and contrast, more typical features of palm crowns in 
aerial imagery than color. The preprocessed dataset was then 
systematically divided into training, validation, and test sets, 
comprising 87% (16,515 images), 8% (1,574 images), and 
4% (788 images) of the data, respectively. This default 
partitioning allows the model to be trained on the majority of 
the data, its performance tuned on a separate validation set to 
prevent bias, and its final effectiveness reported on a 
completely new test set.

B. DeepSORT Tracking Algorithm 
In order to advance the system from a rudimentary frame-by-
frame detector to a robust counting system, the DeepSORT 
(Deep Simple Online and Realtime Tracking) algorithm was 
utilized as the tracking module. DeepSORT adds a deep 
learning-based appearance descriptor to the popular SORT 
(Simple Online and Realtime Tracking) algorithm Fig.3, 
considerably improving its performance in occlusions and its

long-trackability. The method is two-staged. First, it employs 
a Kalman filter, a robust recursive estimator that 
approximates the motion of each tree that is detected. The 
Kalman filter predicts the location of a tracked tree in the next 
frame based on its velocity and previous position, producing 
a smooth trajectory and allowing the system to bridge short 
gaps where a tree may be momentarily occluded. Second, 
DeepSORT employs a pre-trained re-identification (re-ID) 
network. This small convolutional neural network encodes 
the visible appearance of each detected bounding box into a 
high-dimensional feature vector, or an appearance descriptor. 
When matching new detections to current tracks, DeepSORT 
combines the motion information from the Kalman filter with 
the appearance information from the re-ID network. This 
two-stage matching step makes the tracking predominantly 
robust, allowing it to re-identify a tree correctly even after a 
long occlusion. It is this ability to assign a persistent identity 
to each object that directly mitigates the primary multiple 
counting problem.

C. Model Training and Validation 
The YOLOv8n model was trained in the Ultralytics platform 
with the power of a single NVIDIA GPU. The model was 
trained for a total of 30 epochs, a time period found to be 
sufficient for convergence without overfitting. The batch size 
was 16 to balance memory usage and training stability. The 
optimization process was guided by the framework's default 
auto optimizer, which chooses an optimal algorithm 
automatically based on the dataset, and was conFigd with a 
weight decay of 0.0005 for regularization. The learning rate 
was conFigd with 0.01.  The training process also included 
other 
online 
data 
augmentations, 
such 
as 
mosaic 
augmentation, which combines four training images, and 
random horizontal flips. During training, the performance of 
the model was strictly monitored by testing it against the 
independent validation set after every epoch. 
IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 
All experiments were performed on a high-performance 
workstation with an NVIDIA RTX series GPU, using the 
PyTorch deep learning library and the Ultralytics YOLOv8n 
architecture. The most important strength of this approach is 
an end-to-end detection and tracking pipeline specifically 
designed to provide accurate enumeration from UAV video. 
The system first uses the YOLOv8n model, a lightweight but 
robust object detector, to detect all possible oil palm instances 
within each frame. The innovation of this approach seems to 
be in the second stage, where these frame-wise detections are 
passed into the DeepSORT algorithm. DeepSORT assigns a 
persistent identity to each object, utilizing a Kalman filter for 
motion prediction and a deep appearance descriptor for re-
identification. The two-stage approach is important because 
it converts a stream of stateless detections into a stateful 
representation of each individual tree over time. This 
architecture directly addresses the root problem of redundant 
counting that afflicts simpler detection-only approaches, 
enabling the creation of a true inventory. The model was 
trained and tested on a public dataset of 18,877 annotated 
aerial images, split into training (87%), validation (8%), and 
test (4%) sets. To provide a robust performance of the 
detection model, a collection of standards, industry-standard 
metrics were used. The main metric is mean Average 
Precision (mAP), the area under the Precision-Recall curve 
and one, overall measure of model performance. This paper 
reports mAP at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 
50% (mAP@0.5), measuring how well the model detects 
easy objects, and the more stringent mAP@0.5:0.95, 
averaging mAP over ten IoU thresholds 0.5 to 0.95. The 
training process was monitored for 30 epochs, and model 
convergence is illustrated through the loss curves. The 
smooth declining slope in both the training and validation 
loss curves indicates stable learning without significant 
indications of overfitting. The final quantitative evaluation on 
the unseen test set yielded excellent values, confirming the 
high performance of the model. The trained YOLOv8n model 
achieved a mean Average Precision at an IoU threshold of 0.5 
(mAP@0.5) of 98.7%, demonstrating its strong ability to 
accurately predict the location of oil palms in Fig.4. Under 
the more stringent mAP@0.5:0.95 metric, the model 
maintained a solid performance with a score of 79.7% 
[Table.1]. This high detection accuracy is essential for the 
success of the subsequent tracking stage. The correctness of 
the tracking phase is confirmed by a qualitative assessment 
of the output video, depicted in Fig. 5. The video verifies that 
the system generates tight, precise bounding boxes around the 
oil palms. More critically, it shows that the framework 
properly assigns a persistent, persistent ID to every tree as the 
UAV moves through the plantation. The Tree IDs are 
continuously followed through several frames without their 
ID being modified, even while their position and appearance 
change. The outcome also demonstrates the system's 
robustness in a high-density part of the plantation, where 
many distinct IDs are handled at once by the tracker without 
confusion.

V. CONCLUSION AND FUTUREWORKS 
This paper presented an end-to-end deep learning framework 
to tackle the key problem of high accuracy in counting oil 
palm trees from UAV video. Through combining the high-
speed YOLOv8n detector with the robust DeepSORT 
tracking algorithm, the framework effectively breaks through 
the widespread issue of redundant counting that occurs in 
traditional frame-by-frame analysis. The introduced method 
showed outstanding detection performance and achieved 
high final counting accuracy when evaluated with ground-
truth data. This paper outlines a scalable, affordable, and 
robust 
method 
for 
establishing 
accurate 
plantation 
inventories, providing an important contribution to data-
driven 
decision-making 
for 
contemporary 
precision 
agriculture. Future enhancements include geospatial mapping 
of affected trees and improving model accuracy for real-
world applications.
