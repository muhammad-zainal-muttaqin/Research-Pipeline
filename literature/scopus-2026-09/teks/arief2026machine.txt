Abstract.  In the last couple of years, machine vision with object detection 
methods has been applied in agriculture, especially using deep learning with the 
You Only Look Once (YOLO) algorithm. It has gained attention for rapid in-line 
sorting machine vision, where efficiency in time and labor cost is crucial. This 
study designed and built a real-time machine vision system and a YOLOv4 tiny 
algorithm to identify defective oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) moving in a 
roller conveyor. Oil palm FFBs are the source of crude palm oil (CPO), which is 
the export commodity for countries of Indonesia and Malaysia. Sorting FFB is a 
daily process when an oil palm FFB transporting truck arrives at an oil palm 
refinery mill. This process is usually done manually and traditionally by two 
experienced workers who sort for unqualified and defective FFBs, such as long 
stalk, rotten, empty, and thorny or spiky FFBs. We developed a two-class model 
to detect normal and defective FFBs. The model has obtained the average 
precision (AP) of 98% for normal and 96% for defective oil palm FFBs. The 
accuracy validation using the confusion matrix resulted in an mAP of 99.7%. The 
results exhibited the potential of machine vision and YOLOv4 tiny to detect 
defective FFBs. 
 
Keywords: Defect Detection, Machine Vision,  Oil Palm Fresh Bunches, Two Class 
Model, YOLOv4

1. Introduction 
Computer vision and automation are two related terms in agriculture, where efficiency in time, 
labor, and operational costs is crucial. The terms are also related to precision agriculture and 
smart farming. Precision Agriculture has developed rapidly as an essential field nowadays, since 
the swift advancement in computer vision methods, image processing techniques, and camera 
types.   Precision agriculture aims to increase yield, labor, and operational cost efficiency, and 
reduce environmental impact.  The priority aspects of precision agriculture are automatic early 
detection of plant diseases, robotic fruit and vegetable harvesters, post-harvest sorting, and 
grading. Precision agriculture is related to smart farming, which uses computer vision, 
information and communication technology, and artificial intelligence (AI). Computer vision 
uses a computer and a camera to simulate human vision. Image processing and machine 
learning mimic the human brain in making decisions. Information and communication 
technology enables farmers to monitor and evaluate crops remotely, hence easing field and 
production management. Computer vision helps to overcome many problems in industrial 
agriculture [1,2,3]. 
Computer vision (CV) and machine vision (MV) have applications in post-harvest sorting 
and grading of fruits and vegetables. CV and MV are different scopes. Machine vision is often 
used in manufacturing or in-line sorting and grading processes of agricultural products. It 
consists of a computer vision camera and algorithms, equipped with a lighting unit, moving 
conveyors, control units, and separators. Computer vision has a broader scope, not only 
involving cameras such as in visible, hyperspectral, multispectral, and fluorescence imaging but 
also other sensor types such as thermal and Lidar imaging. Post-harvest sorting and grading 
machine vision aims to substitute the traditional and manual practices by experienced graders, 
which are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to subjectivity and error [4].  A machine 
vision system is a rapid, non-destructive, cost-effective, and reliable method for inspecting fruit 
and vegetables. The inspection includes in-line sorting based on standard classification, 
detecting contaminants and defects, and estimating chemical contents. The types of inspections 
rely on the computer vision methods used, which range from colour imaging to spectral 
imaging, from UV to IR wavelength regions [5]. 
Machine vision with machine learning has become a suitable method for rapid, cost-
effective, and non-destructive detection or inspection of fruit defects in the in-line sorting and 
grading process. Fruit defects, especially skin effects, affect the fruit's price and consumer 
preference. The skin defect is more difficult to detect than other external quality attributes such 
as colour, size, and shape. Challenges still exist for available real-time defect detection 
technologies, especially in reducing processing time and integrating many image types [6]. 
There are many types of imaging methods used to acquire and process the defective fruit 
images, including color imaging and hyperspectral imaging. Machine vision with color imaging 
is a simple and economical technique for detecting fruit defects. The color imaging has been able 
to detect the calyx and stalk scar of tomatoes by an RBF-SVM classifier [7]. Hyperspectral 
imaging has also enabled blueberry internal bruising detection using hyperspectral 
transmittance images and fully convolutional networks (FCN) [8]. Another technique of 
machine vision was to use a depth camera to predict dimensions, mass, and defects of potatoes 
[9]. 
Machine vision with object detection methods has attracted more attention for defect 
detection, due to advances in deep learning (DL) techniques. The development has facilitated 
many machine vision applications in the sorting and grading of fruits and vegetables. Machine

Region-based CNN (R-CNN) is one of the CNN series that is related to object detection. Key 
performance parameters of real–time object detection techniques are frame per second (FPS) 
and Mean Average Precision (mAP). Developments of CNN algorithms towards higher FPS and 
mAP led to Fast R-CNN and Faster R-CNN series. However, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) 
algorithm is the best of the CNN family, which has revolutionized object detection techniques 
[12]. 
The YOLO algorithm has become one of the accepted object detection algorithms. It is due 
to its simplicity and low cost if used with color imaging in video format. The early applications 
of the YOLO algorithm are fruit detection, tracking, and counting using video frames. YOLO 
based apple yield estimation was one of the applications that aimed to substitute manual yield 
estimation. The estimation has succeeded in detecting and counting the apple fruits in vertical 
fruiting-wall architecture using a detection model of apples and tree trunks with a YOLOv4-tiny 
network [13].  Another YOLO application was using two-stage image processing for fruit 
counting based on video frames [14]. The YOLO algorithm types used for ripeness level 
detection, which use four convolutional neural networks (YOLOv3, YOLOv3-Tiny, YOLOv3-SPP, 
and YOLOv4). The algorithm models were trained in the Darknet deep learning framework and 
compared [15]. 
Palm oil industries are the key export commodities for some countries, such as Indonesia 
and Malaysia.  The quality of crude palm oil (CPO) depends on the quality of its raw materials, 
which are oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs). The sustainability of these industries needs 
optimistic efforts. The Industry 4.0 concept is one of the efforts that promotes Artificial 
Intelligence (AI), Internet of Things (IoT), and cybersecurity. Automatic processes in many 
stages of palm oil mills are crucial to ensure the palm oil quality [16]. The sorting and grading 
process is essential for oil palm FFBs that arrive at the receiving stations of palm oil mills. This 
activity is done manually and traditionally by experienced personnel. The sorting and grading 
process starts as an FFB transporting truck entering the mill sorting or receiving area. It usually 
takes four workers to work for one FFB truck. Two workers unload the oil palm FFBs, and the 
other two workers sort the FFBs according to standard guidelines. Some of the regulations 
include four ripeness stages (unripe, underripe, ripe, overripe), damaged (by animals or during 
harvesting or transporting), long stalk, and small (less than 3 kg) [17]. Other criteria are thorny, 
rotten. Thorny FFBs show more spikelets than fruit (poorly pollinated), while rotten ones show 
a dirty and wet bunch. The workers use axes and crowbars to pull and move the FFBs aside. 
These practices are tedious, laborious, subjective, and prone to error and dispute.  Therefore, 
machine vision with computer vision and deep learning offers an easy way to substitute the 
traditional and manual sorting process. 
There have been many efforts to use non-destructive methods for oil palm FFB quality 
evaluation. Most quality evaluations of oil palm FFBs have used maturity or ripeness levels as 
the quality attributes. Thermal imaging is one of the optical methods to evaluate the maturity 
levels of oil palm FFBs [18]. Another non-destructive method was using a smartphone 
application to calculate harvested oil palm FFBs before they were transported to palm oil mills.

It aimed to reduce the embezzlement of oil palm FFBs during transportation [19]. A

Computer vision with object detection algorithms has received significant attention in the last 
couple of years in the oil palm industry. With the development of the YOLO algorithm series, 
more applications have been proposed for oil palm FFB classification and detection. Many 
automatic detection systems have used the YOLO algorithm for oil palm FFBs, such as an 
optimized YOLOv3 tiny to detect and localize three objects at a palm oil plantation under 
various environmental conditions [22]. Another application was with the YOLOv3 algorithm to 
detect ripeness levels of oil palm FFBs in real time [23]. The next application was using several 
object detection algorithms for FFB ripeness classification based on fruit surface colors [24]. 
Another application was counting of oil palm FFBs on a moving conveyor using a three-step 
algorithm: detection, tracking, and counting [25]. Computer vision and YOLOv4 were the first 
steps toward a real-time robotic FFB harvesting system [26]. A comprehensive annotation and 
labelling process was performed to build a dataset of 6 maturity categories of oil palm FFBs 
[27].  
This study aimed to build a real-time machine vision system to simulate the sorting process 
at the receiving station of palm oil mills. This study makes a new contribution to YOLO-based 
computer vision applications for evaluating oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs), particularly in 
detecting and sorting defective FFBs on a moving conveyor. Most applications of the YOLO 
algorithm were for the FFB harvesting process. Most studies evaluated FFBs based on ripeness 
levels. FFBs received at the palm oil mill area originated from many surrounding plantations. 
The FFB conditions are varied due to long road distances from the plantation to mills, rain, and 
damaged roads that cause damage or defective FFBs when they arrive at mills [17].  In this 
study, the defect categories were based on palm oil mill regulations, which sort four defect 
categories: long stalk, thorny, empty, and rotten bunches. The FFBs from other categories have 
been sorted out before arriving at the receiving stations. The machine vision system consisted 
of a computer vision unit and a 5-meter roller conveyor. The conveyor was equipped with a 
motor, control unit, computer, Arduino microcontroller, user interface, and a separator. We 
utilized a CMOS camera that is integrable into the machine system hardware and software; 
hence, the sorting process is fast and real-time. A confusion matrix was used as the metric to 
evaluate the model's performance. 
2. Material and Methods  
2.1. Machine Vision System 
Detecting defective oil palm FFBs is one of the machine vision tasks that aims to simulate and 
substitute the manual sorting process at the receiving area of palm oil mills. This study has 
developed machine vision hardware and software for detecting defects of oil palm FFBs 
according to the government regulation on FFB buying criteria for small holders as well as the 
private companies, which includes rotten, empty, long stalk, and thorny or undeveloped.  Other 
criteria, such as muddy, wet, damaged spikelets, stacked or partially occluded FFBs, were not 
included in this study, as they were found in fewer numbers in the sorting areas. The system 
uses computer vision and object detection methods with the YOLO algorithm. The machine 
vision system and its components are shown in Figure 1.

The system in Figure 1 consists of a color camera and lens, a light source, a conveyor unit, a 
pneumatic-based arm or separator, a control unit for the motor, and a separator. The system 
also has an Arduino board connected to computer units and actuators. The actuator is 
connected to the control unit to initiate the conveyor and the separator lever.  The computer 
vision unit had white metal shielding that limited the background light entering the camera. The 
shielding has frames to position the camera, lens, and lamps. The lighting came from a pair of 
neon lamps with 5 W of light power. The computer vision unit has some essential components, 
including the camera and lenses. We used DFM 22BUC03-ML USB 2.0 color board camera, 
equipped with a 16 mm 1/3” M-12 lens. Some requirements for a real-time camera are a USB 
connection, a trigger, I/O inputs, and a compatible camera driver. The camera driver should be 
able to be embedded in a written acquisition program.  The CMOS color camera has a resolution 
of 0.4 MP (744 x 480 pixels) and a frame rate of 76 FPS.  The camera and lens were positioned at 
the middle computer vision unit with a 90 cm distance from the lens to the conveyor surface 
(about 60 cm working distance). The conveyor has a white plate floor to increase background 
segmentation efficiency in image processing. Some software used includes Python 3.8, YOLO v4 
(tiny), SORT for object tracking library, OpenCV for Computer Vision Library, and Arduino IDE 
for Microcontroller programming. We also used Flask for a Web Server Library for web-
based/remote detection and monitoring. We used two separate computers: the main computer 
(Intel NUC), which connected to the camera and other sensors, and another computer for real-
time Arduino-based control of the pneumatic control valve. 
The machine vision for defect detection runs in real-time. The logical commands for the 
defective FFB detection model start as follows.  When the camera detects an FFB on a video 
frame, a bounding box is assigned and then tracked. Tracking is in progress until it reaches the 
kill line of kill & trigger. If the object passes the limit line, the kill & trigger protocol is 
performed, which has an abnormal class > 70%. Then the signal “1” is sent to the Arduino input, 
which waits for 6 seconds. Until pin "8" at Arduino receives “digitalWrite 1”.  When pin 8 
receives the signal, the pneumatic valve that controls the separator will move forward, aiming 
to push the defective FFB out of the conveyor line.  After 500 ms, Arduino will send another 
signal to the pneumatic valve to withdraw. If the object bounding box passes through the limit 
line of kill & trigger is a normal object, the Arduino does not receive any signal.

2.2. YOLO model construction 
There are some stages to obtain a YOLO detection model for defective oil palm FFBs. The first 
stage was setting up and characterizing the computer vision unit, as in Figure 1. The second 
stage was to build the detection program. A defective FFB detection program was developed 
using Python language and OpenCV Library. The third stage was to construct a YOLO model. We 
used the YOLO algorithm due to sorting efficiency. In a reception area, it takes about 30-45 
minutes to unload and sort oil palm FFBs from a transporting truck.  The fourth stage was to 
integrate mechatronic circuits and control into the acquisition and YOLO detection program. 
Figure 2 shows the steps for constructing the two-class defective detection model.  First, FFB 
images were acquired to build a dataset [28], which used oil palm FFBs from a PTPN V 
plantation of Riau Province, Indonesia, by bringing the machine vision system to its receiving 
area. Three experienced graders at the mill validated the FFB classes before operators recorded 
FFB images. The images were saved in two separate file folders for the annotation stage.  The 
annotation process used the LabelImg software, resulting in files with the extension.txt. Next, all 
the annotated images were divided into training and testing datasets with a ratio of 80:20 
%.  The training dataset was uploaded to Google Drive for YOLOv4 tiny Model training using the 
Cloud GPU platform. Later, the trained model information is inserted into the defect detection 
program to identify the defective oil pal FFBs. In the testing stage, the model applied the 20% 
dataset.  Finally, the model's performance was evaluated using the confusion matrix, and the 
YOLO configuration was updated. The filter parameter size used for YOLO configuration during 
the training process was (5 + n_class) * 3 = 21.

Dataset preparation is the crucial part in building a YOLO detection model. The dataset images 
were obtained using validated FFBs at a government-owned palm oil plantation, PTPN V, Riau 
Province, Indonesia. The detection model classified the oil palm FFB samples as normal and 
defective FFBs. In this study, ripeness level features are excluded. The defective FFBs consist of 
four categories according to standard criteria, as rotten, long stalk, empty, and thorny or 
undeveloped bunches [17], as shown in the Figure. 3. Figure 3 shows the FFB images taken 
using the IMS camera (744 x 480 pixels). We adjusted the raw image using the correction 
settings for each category detail (no pre-adjustment was made for the dataset images). Figure 3 
displays four categories of defective FFBs and the variation of each defect category.  Each class 
of normal and defective had 200 oil palm FFBs; each defect category had 50 FFBs. 
Image acquisitions were performed on the conveyor using the computer vision unit. Each 
FFB had 6-8 images, which consisted of a back and front bunch. The front bunch is the part 
facing sunlight when intact on a palm oil tree, having higher fruit density, while the back part 
contains more infertile fruits and is lighter in color. For each part, the images were taken at four 
positions within the camera's field of view (FOV). Therefore, each bunch had 6-8 images. These 
different positions and parts aim to anticipate different orientations and positions of FFBs on 
the moving conveyor. Therefore, the two-class model had a dataset of 2400 images. We divided 
the dataset into a ratio of 80:20% for training and testing. Figure 3 shows the physical 
characteristics of the defective oil palm FFBs. Long stalk FFBs are for unripe, ripe, and overripe 
levels that have stalks longer than 2 cm. Thorny FFBs are undeveloped FFBs having long 
spikelets and small fruit size.  An empty bunch means fewer fruits left in the bunch, while rotten 
or damaged bunches have darker brown spikelets and fruits [17, 27].

2.2.2. Detection model  
The detection model for identifying normal and defective oil palm FFBs used the YOLOv4tiny 
algorithm. YOLOv4 algorithms have applications in fruit and vegetable sorting machine vision 
due to their more accurate and shorter processing time compared to other object detection 
algorithms. YOLOv4 tiny was the improvement of YOLOv4 to increase speed and decrease 
computational cost [29].  The parameters of the YOLOv4 tiny configuration file for oil palm FFB 
detection model include batch number (64), subdivisions (16), width (416), height (416), 
classes (2), and filters (21).  The parameters we used are generic from the Darknet framework. 
Some parameters, such as subdivision (16), max_batches (6000), classes, and filters, were 
changed according to the needs of the defect detection models. Max_batches = 6000 was due to 
the number of classes being less than 3 for the two-class model. The steps consisted of 80 % and 
90% of Max_batches. 
Training and testing are the final two steps for constructing an object detection model. 
These processes used Darknet and Google Colab. Darknet has a data file, a configuration file, and 
pre-trained weights. The data file is the custom dataset of annotated images of normal and 
defective oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs). The dataset was split into two training and testing 
datasets with a ratio of 80:20 %. The two-class model used a dataset of 2400 images. The 
YOLOv4 tiny model was pre-trained and fine-tuned using the configuration file as shown in 
Table 1 via the Darknet platform. Later, the optimal model with pre-trained weights was trained 
and tested using Google Colab, and its performance was measured.

the combined bounding box area, as written by Eq. (1).  IoU has values of 0 – 1 or %, which

3. Result and Discussion 
This study aimed to design YOLOv4 tiny models for the computer vision unit to detect normal 
and defective oil palm FFBs on a moving conveyor. The unit is a part of a machine vision system 
for sorting and grading oil palm FFBs. FFB sorting process looks for FFBs that meet standard 
criteria besides ripeness levels, such as long stalk, empty, thorny, and rotten FFBs, namely as 
defective FFBs. The two-class models divided the FFBs into normal and defective. 
3.1. Training results 
Figure 5 shows the graph of Loss and mAP versus iteration number. It depicts that the model 
has converged, where the Loss value reaches a minimum at 5900 (0.04), with a score of 
99.71%.  The Loss converges almost to 0, meaning the model has learned during the training 
phase. However, it cannot be translated as the model accuracy. The model accuracy will be 
evaluated on the test set. The input of this process was from an 80% dataset. The output of this 
process was weight files, which will be used for the testing and validation stages. In this stage,

3.3. Confusion matrix results  
Confusion matrix was used to measure the model's performance during the validation stage.  If a 
normal oil palm FFB is detected as normal, the condition will be defined as a True Positive (TP) 
because the detection result is identical to the labeled FFB. If the normal FFB is detected as 
defective, the condition is defined as False Positive (FP). The same rules are also valid for the 
labeled defective FFBs. True Negative (TN) is for labeled defective FFBs that are detected 
defective, otherwise as False Negative (FN). 
Table 2 shows the results for the validation stages of the YOLOv4tiny model for 60 FFB 
samples, comprising 30 FFBs for each class. It shows that the normal class has 90 % precision, 
close to 100 % Recall, and 94% F1-score. The defective class has 80 % precision, 100 % Recall, 
and 88% of F1-score. Table 2 also shows that there are some FP and FN scores. The problem can 
be overcome by using more classes for the detection model. The results show that the YOLOv4 
tiny model can detect both classes, but the defective class has less performance. The causes 
could be due to the need for more images for the four types of the defective class [14, 27].

4. Conclusion  
This study has successfully constructed a machine vision using the YOLOv4tiny algorithm to 
detect normal and defective oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs). The defective FFBs consisted of 
long stalks, empty, thorny, and rotten FFBs. The contribution of this study is the real-time 
machine vision for FFB sorting and grading at the reception stations of palm oil mills.  The 
hardware consisted of conveyor and control circuit units for the conveyor and for the separator 
arm. The software parts are the detection models, the GUI, and the information system. The 
training stage obtained a model accuracy of 99.71%. From the testing stage, the detection model 
has had an mAP of 99.08% and an average IoU of 87.79% at the iteration of 5000.  The 
validation stage obtained a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 99.6% for normal FFBs and 99.7% 
for defective FFBs.  
This study has limitations in that only a class model was considered. The aim was to 
accommodate one separator arm, so that the normal FFBs will pass, while the defective FFBs 
will be ejected.  The system and the model can be extended into a four-class model, which 
classifies FFBs as long stalk, empty, thorny, and rotten FFBs. Other than the four classes, the 
FFBs are considered normal or have passed the standard criteria. Another exploration was to 
construct a model for stacked FFBs, where several types of FFBs were in front of the camera; the 
model can simultaneously identify ripe, unripe, long stalk, empty, thorny, and rotten FFBs. 
However, for these models, other types of separator arms should be considered.
