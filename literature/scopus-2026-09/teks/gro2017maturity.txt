Abstract 
The determination of maturity stages of fresh fruit bunches (FFB) of oil palm is 
quite challenging as it depends very much on the competence of the human worker. 
The fruitlets at the bottom of each bunch are hardly visible or can only partially be 
seen, which results in high uncertainty. Therefore, study has been conducted to 
characterize maturity stages of FFB non-destructively and rapidly using multispectral 
camera. The chemical analyses were carried out on individual fruitlets (n=3) located 
at the top, middle and bottom of each bunch to make the total samples being equal to 
18. Images were acquired using multispectral prism camera, producing five images in 
5 wavelength ranges, i.e., red, green, blue, near-infrared (NIR) 1 (700-830 nm), and 
NIR 2 (830-1000 nm). The collected data were compared with the changes of 
chlorophyll and carotenoids extracted by means of Soxhlet method. Results indicated 
significant decrease of chlorophyll A and increase of red pigments from unripe to ripe 
FFB. Variation between FFB appeared enhanced compared to within bunch variability. 
Results revealed that multispectral imaging can be used to determine the maturity 
stages of FFB non-destructively. 
Keywords: fruitlet, multi spectral, NDVI, oil palm 
INTRODUCTION 
Palm oil is the most important renewable and edible source of energy in South East 
Asia and the western hemisphere. One key problem in harvesting practice is how to detect 
ripe fruits in high trees without damaging the fresh fruit bunches (FFB) (Cornelius, 1977). 
The oil needs to be extracted from the fruitlets which are attached to a 10 to 15 kg 
FFB, which grows in the axle of each palm leaf, often hanging 15 to 18 m high up in the tree. 
It takes three to four months from blossom to ripe FFB ready for harvest. A worker decides 
when to harvest the FFB visually by experience. The worker has to know that the optimum 
stage of maturity is indicated by color and the fact that only one fruitlet has to be dropped in 
order to be ripe (Corley and Tinker, 2003). As soon as the first fruitlet drops the oil content 
and the quality of oil will decrease, if it is not harvested and processed in the mill. Therefore, 
the harvester has a limited window of time to find these ripe bunches. 
The growth of the fruit bunches can be divided into five phases. As a drupe, it grows 
biphasically. In the study of Tranbarger et al. (2011) it was observed that the fruit increased 
in size and mass for the first time between 30 to 60 days after pollination. The second phase 
starts after 60 days and shows a lack of accumulation of fresh mass and peaks of auxins. 
After 100 days the third phase is initiated, ending 20 days later. It is defined by a reduction of 
auxin, increase of gibberellic acid, and cytokinin metabolites. The following 20 days are 
marked by a specific increase in size and lipid accumulation due to the beginning of 
maturation. During phase five other hormones like ABA and ethylene become active and are 
increasingly produced. The cell wall separation in the mesocarp takes place, which is 
embodied by the growth of mesocarp. Dry and fresh mass are enhanced and the distinct 
accumulation of lipid and carotenoids takes place. In the mesocarp cells of ripe fruits, 
chloroplasts are turned into chromoplasts containing carotenoids. The chlorophyll content

MATERIAL AND METHODS 
Fruit material 
The samples represented whole fresh fruit bunches. Samples were taken from the 
research field of University Putra Malaysia 5- to 10-year-old oil palm trees. FFB were easy to 
harvest due to relatively small size of the trees of Elaeis guineensis, cultivar ‘Tenera’. 
Six FFBs, representing three ripe and three unripe samples were harvested. Three FFB 
were sampled in July 2014, and second three samples two weeks later. Using a flat 
screwdriver some 20 fruitlets were unplugged circularly from centre, middle and outer 
position of each FFB. 
Multi spectral imaging 
The multispectral camera (5-CCD Condor 5 VNN, Quest Innovations, USA) was placed 
in a dark room. The camera recorded the images in top view towards the fruitlets 50 cm 
above ground connected to the PC. The fruitlets lay on white sheet of paper measuring 9-12 
fruitlets of each position of each FFB. A halogen lamp (40 watts) served as light source. Five 
images were recorded subsequently and saved as bitmap format for 5 wavelength ranges: 
red, green, blue, near-infrared (NIR) 1 (700-830 nm), and NIR 2 (830-1000 nm). 
The further procession was carried out by using image analysis software (ENVI 5.1, 
Harris Geospatial Solutions, USA). The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and 
index of red pigment (NRI) were calculated using the entire images (Zude, 2003; Zude et al., 
2007). 
NDVI=(NIR1-Red)/(NIR1+Red) 
NRI=(NIR1-Green)/(NIR1+Green) 
Pigment extraction 
Pigments were extracted in hexane and measured using a spectrophotometer (DR 
4000, Hach, USA). The extinction coefficients (E) were recorded and the contents were 
calculated using the equation of Lichtenthaler (Lichtenthaler and Buschmann, 2001) for 
chlorophyl A (CHL A), chlorophyll B (CHL B), and carotenoids (CAR): 
CHL A = 11.24*E660-2.04*E642 
CHL B = 20.13*E642-4.19*E660 
CAR = E480 + 0.114 * E663 - 0.638 * E645) 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Pigment changes over fruit development 
The chlorophyll contents varied during the development of fruitlets in the fresh fruit 
bunches (FFB). The decrease of chlorophyll A was highly significant, while chlorophyll B 
appeared less indicative (Table 1). This finding is consistent with studies on pip and stone 
fruit, showing no significant changes of chlorophyll B over fruit development. In more 
general review on chlorophyll breakdown, the degradation chain starts enzymatically from 
chlorophyll B over chlorophyll A and further. However, the reverse reaction from chlorophyll 
A to chlorophyll B may occur as well in early fruit development stages, before the separation

During the last decade, multi-spectral readings in the visible part of the spectrum 
became a promising technology for analysing fruit pigments (Olsen et al., 1969; Merzlyak et 
al., 1999; Zude-Sasse et al., 2002; Seifert et al., 2015). Earlier findings, mainly in pip fruits, 
were confirmed in the present study. In palm the non-destructive analysis of carotenes has 
been approached by means of Fourier-transform near infrared spectroscopy (Moh et al., 
1999). However, the approach presented provided even the spatial distribution of pigments. 
The coefficient of determination of spatially-resolved, multi spectral readings and extracted 
chlorophyll appeared encouraging for the non-destructive analysis (Figure 1). A potential for 
improved performance are more homogeneous lighting conditions and radiometric 
calibration. However, the enabled analysis of spatial distribution of chlorophyll was further 
looked at in the present study.

Spatial distribution of NDVI 
The multi spectral images were analyzed by masking the chlorophyll by means of 
normalized difference vegetation index, NDVI, and by means of normalized index for red 
pigments, NRI. The spatial distribution of both indices revealed pattern within each fruitlet 
(Figure 2). 
Typically, the tips appeared with enhanced values of NRI and decreased values of NDVI 
corresponding to the expected increase of carotenoids and phenols as well as reduced 
chlorophyll content. 
ANOVA results of multi spectral data considering the NDVI and NRI point to 
differences between unripe and ripe FFB (Table 2). Consequently, the methodology is 
suitable to distinguish FFB that appears ready for harvest. The data confirm results 
published by Alfatni et al. (2008, 2014). In the present work, within the FFB, lower values 
were found in the bottom pointing to sequential ripening within the bunch.

CONCLUSIONS 
The following conclusions can be drawn from the study: 
- Multi spectral images were recorded on unripe and ripe FFB of oil palm. 
- Using indices calculated from the passband of absorbance corrected by a passband of 
mere scattering, the green chlorophylls and red pigment were identified. 
- This approach enabled to analyse the spatial distribution of pigments in fruitlets. 
- Results point to the potential of non-destructive analysis. 
- The approach appears feasible for application in the field. 
- The NDVI was particularly suitable for this purpose.
