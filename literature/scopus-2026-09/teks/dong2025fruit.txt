A B S T R A C T
Camellia oleifera, an essential woody oil tree species, produces camellia oil, which is highly nutritious and edible 
after seed pressing. The demand for camellia oil has increased in recent years, so accurate estimates of camellia 
oil production are key to fruit harvesting and price setting. The rapid development of computer vision and deep 
learning techniques has found wide applications in agriculture, demonstrating significant fruit detection capa­
bilities. This study proposes a surface fruit detection algorithm in C.oleifera based on the improved YOLOv8 
model, with lightweight RepViT as the backbone of the model, a small object detection layer with p2 layer added 
on the multi-scale basis of FPN and PAN, and a network structure optimized by Shape IoU loss function. 
Meanwhile, the improved ByteTrack algorithm based on ByteTrack is utilized for multi-scale small-target 
tracking. Using LightGBM and linear regression to establish the relationship between the number of surface fruits 
and the number of individual fruits, combined with the number of typical sample trees and the average single 
fruit quality in the dataset, a production estimation model for the automatic detection of C.oleifera is constructed. 
The mAP value of the improved YOLOv8 algorithm on the C.oleifera fruit dataset reached 86.21 %, an 
improvement of 4.51 % compared to the original model. Combined with average single-fruit mass and the 
number of representative sample trees, these counts yielded whole-tree production estimates.The approach 
produced a fruit-number prediction R²of 0.945 and a yield- estimation R²of 0.902 across our sample trees, 
demonstrating strong linear relationships and validating the pipeline for automated C. oleifera yield forecasting.
1. Introduction
Camellia oleifera is a woody oil tree species unique to southern China, 
known for its high oil content (up to 90 % unsaturated fatty acids), 
which can promote metabolism and is considered a high-quality edible 
oil [1–3]. The main production areas of C.oleifera are located in the 
Yangtze River Basin, including Hunan, Hubei, Guangdong, and Guangxi 
[4]. The joint efforts of the Chinese government and enterprises have 
significantly expanded the C.oleifera industry in recent years. However, 
the alternate bearing phenomenon of economic forest tree species has 
led to fluctuations in the annual production of C.oleifera, making it 
difficult to stabilize the oil price of C.oleifera. Therefore, scientific 
management and accurate production estimation are crucial [5]. The 
traditional method of investigating oil tea production involves 
hand-picking, counting, and weighing fresh fruits. Although accurate, it 
is time-consuming, labor-intensive, costly, and environmentally 
damaging. Integrating factors such as soil type, meteorological data, and 
farmland management for yield prediction poses challenges for data 
collection, integration, and quality control [6] and may not capture the 
complex interactions and nonlinear relationships between crop param­
eters [7]. In addition, the variation of the tree parameters over time and 
space can further affect the prediction accuracy. Therefore, a simple, fast 
fruit identification and counting method is urgently needed.

tion studies focus on a single image but may be hampered by object 
occlusion and dynamic scenes. In contrast, video counting provides 
comprehensive target dynamics and adversarial occlusion and offers 
temporal information and motion features [8]. Traditional methods 
such as haar features, histogram of oriented gradients and adaptive 
boosting, support vector machine, and deformable part-based model 
rely on object features such as color, shape, texture, and edges [9,10] but 
often exhibit difficulties in natural orchard environments. Deep learning 
algorithms, especially those based on convolutional neural networks, 
have shown promise in crop detection [11]. The You Only Look Once v8 
series and R-CNN series of object detection networks have demonstrated 
excellent performance [12–16]. For example, You Only Look Once v5 
and Faster R-CNN were used for apple fruit detection, while deep simple 
online and realtime tracking was used for counting, with average 
counting errors of 20.07 % and 31.52 %, respectively [17]; ‘Man­
goYOLO’ is used for mango fruit detection, with an estimated error of 4.6 
% to 15.2 % in orchard fruit load [18]. We trained Faster R-CNN models 
for orange detection and long short-term memory models for yield 
estimation, achieving an approximation error of 4.53 % [19].
However, tracking fruits often faces challenges such as occlusion and 
different lighting conditions [20,21]. To improve detection perfor­
mance, Ge et al [22] introduced the YOLO DeepSORT visual object 
tracking network, Wu et al [23] proposed the NDMFCS automatic 
counting method, Farjon et al. [24] proposed the OrangeSort algorithm, 
Gao et al [25] selected tree trunks as single target tracking targets, and 
used lightweight YOLOv5 and improved DeepSORT for lotus yield 
counting [26]. These multi-objective tracking algorithms significantly 
improve the accuracy of fruit yield estimation in natural scenarios [17, 
25,27,28]. Researchers have further proposed various refinements to 
improve the efficiency and accuracy of fruit yield estimation. Gao et al 
[25] proposed a YOLOv4 mini method that integrates the channel and 
spatial reliability discriminant correlation filter algorithm to improve 
the accuracy of apple yield counting. Rong et al [29] introduced an 
improved tomato cluster counting method that combines YOLOv5–4D 
and ByteTrack, effectively overcoming the challenge of automatic yield 
estimation in intelligent greenhouse automation. Fu [30] proposed a 
small YOLOv3 network with computational complexity optimization for 
automatically detecting kiwifruit in orchards. Xia et al [31] introduced a 
new method that combines the CenterNet model with a patch-matching 
model based on the Kuhn Munkres algorithm, successfully solving the 
problem of duplicate counting in continuous images of apples and or­
anges. Oliveira et al [32] proposed a method combining linear regres­
sion and support vector machine algorithms to solve strawberry yield 
estimation’s efficiency and accuracy issues. Bai et al [33] introduced a 
method that combines leaf area index from Landsat satellite data with 
the WOFOST model to improve the accuracy of yield estimation for 
perennial jujube crops at the field scale. Mirhaji et al [34] proposed a 
deep learning method based on YOLO-V4 for automatic detection and 
counting of oranges in orchards. Zhang et al [35] proposed a deep 
learning algorithm that combines OrangeYolo and OrangeSort, effec­
tively solving the accuracy and duplicate counting problems in citrus 
counting.
However, most existing methods for estimating fruit yield rely on 
planar computation, and for counting videos, the issues of accuracy, 
real-time performance, and computational complexity have not been 
simultaneously addressed. Therefore, this study proposes a modified 
YOLOv8 method based on Reparameterized Vision Transformer for oil 
tea yield estimation in complex orchard environments. RepViT [36] 
combines CNN and ViT [37] to balance local and global feature 
extraction, making it suitable for mobile devices with limited resources. 
We optimize the backbone and neck of YOLOv8. We introduce 
state-of-the-art object tracking techniques to reduce the computational 
complexity of the model while increasing the accuracy and counting 
speed of oil palm localization.
The primary aim of this study is to improve the accuracy of fruit 
detection and yield estimation for C. oleifera using advanced computer 
vision techniques. Specifically, we focus on utilizing an enhanced 
YOLOv8 model integrated with a multi-target tracking algorithm. The 
study aims to address several key objectives: 
(1) Improve the accuracy of fruit detection in C. oleifera using an 
optimized YOLOv8 model. This objective is motivated by the need to 
overcome challenges such as occlusion and small object detection in 
natural orchard environments.
(2) Enhance the multi-target tracking performance with the 
improved ByteTrack algorithm. This objective is justified by the 
significant impact of tracking performance on fruit counting accu­
racy, especially in complex scenes with occlusion and varying 
lighting conditions.
(3) Establish a reliable relationship between the number of detected 
fruits and the yield of the tree, by integrating the fruit count data 
with fruit quality measurements. This objective is essential for 
improving yield prediction accuracy, as traditional methods often 
fail to account for variations in fruit quality.
(4) Develop an empirical yield estimation model for C. oleifera that 
incorporates both fruit count and fruit quality, using linear regres­
sion methods. This is necessary to address the challenge of yield 
estimation in varying environmental conditions, as current models 
often focus solely on fruit quantity without considering quality.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Collection of phenotypic data on fruits
All image and video data used in this study were collected at the 
Changshan Oil Tea Experimental Base (120.825542◦E, 29.748715◦N) of 
the Research Institute of Subtropical Forestry, Chinese Academy of 
Forestry(Fig. 1). Three varieties of C. oleifera (CL4, CL40, and CL53) 
were selected for investigation. For each variety, twenty trees of similar 
age, exhibiting healthy growth and free from pests and diseases, were 
chosen as sampling subjects.Data collection was conducted from 
November 15 to 18, 2023, between 08:30 and 17:00 local time, utilizing 
natural lighting conditions. To ensure dataset diversity and model 
robustness, data were captured under various natural conditions 
including different lighting intensities, occlusion scenarios, and shadow 
variations.Two smartphone models were employed for data acquisition: 
(1) Xiaomi 13 (Xiaomi Inc., China): 50 MP main camera, f/1.8 aperture, 
1/1.49″ sensor size. (2)Apple iPhone 13 (Apple Inc., USA): 12 MP main 
camera, f/1.6 aperture, 1/1.88″ sensor size.Both devices were set to 
record 4 K video at 60 fps. The data collection protocol involved circling 
each target tree at approximately 0.2 m/s while maintaining the 
smartphone at a 45◦upward tilt angle. The shooting distance was 
maintained between 0.6–1.5 m from the tree canopy. Video data were 
processed by extracting frames at 30-frame intervals, resulting in adja­
cent images with approximately 70 % overlap. A total of 3630 images 
were obtained through this process. All images were annotated using 
LabelImg, with 64,975 C. oleifera fruits identified and labeled using

2.2. Framework construction of detecting models
In this study, we employed the YOLOv8 model [39] for the detection 
and counting of C. oleifera fruits. YOLOv8 was selected due to its 
balanced performance in both accuracy and computational efficiency. 
While earlier YOLO versions, such as YOLOv4 and YOLOv5, demon­
strated effectiveness in agricultural produce detection, YOLOv8 in­
troduces several improvements. These include enhanced real-time 
performance, better precision in small object detection, and advanced 
features like the RepViT backbone and multi-scale detection layers. 
YOLOv8 was preferred over subsequent versions, from YOLOv9 to 
YOLOv12, due to its superior feature extraction capabilities and the 
computational efficiency of the RepViT backbone, which is optimized

for mobile and embedded devices. These advancements allow YOLOv8

However, YOLOv8 faces challenges when detecting small C. oleifera 
fruits in complex backgrounds, particularly when targets are occluded or 
feature data is sparse, leading to false positives and missed detections. 
To address this, we enhanced YOLOv8 by improving its network struc­
ture to capture more location information. While this modification re­
sults in a slight reduction in detection speed, it significantly boosts 
accuracy. The improved framework consists of a backbone feature 
extraction network, an enhanced feature extraction network, and a 
prediction network (Fig. 2). The backbone employs RepViT, which 
combines CNN and Vision Transformer architectures, and integrates 
FPN and PAN networks in the Neck section. The prediction network 
utilizes YOLOv8’s universal detection layer.
In the original YOLOv8 model, the backbone employs the 
CSPDarkNet-53 network. However, our improved model replaces this 
with RepViT, which better accommodates the constraints of mobile 
devices without sacrificing performance. RepViT integrates convolu­
tional layers and ViT’s self-attention mechanism after each layer, 
enhancing feature extraction while adapting to resource limitations 
through its lightweight design (Fig. 2). This structure improves perfor­
mance through structural reparameterization and deep convolutions.
During the inference process, we designed the RepViTSEBlock and 
RepViTBlock to merge multi-branch convolutional structures into a 
single-branch design, reducing computational and memory overhead. 
Additionally, we adopted a smaller expansion ratio, uniformly set to 2, 
to reduce computation and channel redundancy while enhancing overall 
width. For input processing, RepViT uses a simplified stem module and 
early convolution operation, allowing it to process high-resolution input 
feature maps effectively and avoid information loss caused by down­
sampling. Similar fine-tuned YOLOv8 approaches have been success­
fully applied in greenhouse environments for detecting capsicum fruits 
and assessing their growth stages, further confirming the adaptability of 
YOLOv8 for horticultural applications [40].
In the Neck section, YOLOv8 utilizes FPN and PAN networks to 
detect objects of various sizes. FPN captures multi-scale target features, 
while PAN improves small target detection by enhancing semantic 
feature flow (Fig. 3). The introduction of the P2 layer enables better 
detection of small targets, which are more challenging to detect due to 
their small pixel size. Related research in uncontrolled environment 
settings has highlighted the necessity of specialized detection and seg­
mentation strategies when dealing with low-light or night-time sce­
narios, such as peduncle detection in capsicum, which underscores the 
importance of tailoring vision models to specific environmental 
challenges [41].

2.3. Fruit counting method based on multi-target tracking algorithm
This study uses a multi-object tracking algorithm for counting 
C. oleifera fruits. Multi-target tracking relies on computer vision to track 
target objects in a video sequence. The common paradigm is "Tracking 
by Detection" [42], which involves object detection in each frame, fol­
lowed by target association to track them across frames [43]. In each 
frame, an object detection algorithm locates the C. oleifera fruits and 
extracts their features. A unique identifier (ID) is assigned to each target 
to maintain consistency and track its trajectory.
The core of the multi-target tracking algorithm is data association, 
which matches the predicted trajectory with the detection box in the 
current frame to track the target. The accuracy of this matching de­
termines the overall tracking precision. To improve data association, we 
introduce the ORB algorithm’s feature extraction and matching methods 
into the ByteTrack algorithm (Fig. 5). ORB, a fast and stable feature 
point detection algorithm, uses FAST for corner detection, and solves 
scale and rotation invariance with pyramid and grayscale centroid 
calibration. Descriptors are constructed using BRIEF, and the similarity 
between features is calculated.
In tracking, if a sequence exists, the Kalman filter predicts the tra­
jectory, generating expected positions. Simultaneously, object detection 
is performed on the current frame. The detection confidence threshold is 
set to 0.001, with high (Thigh) and low (Tlow) score thresholds for the 
bounding box. High-score boxes are matched to tracked trajectories, and 
if matched, the Kalman filter updates the trajectory. Unmatched boxes 
are processed with ORB feature extraction and matching. After three 
consecutive frames of unmatched boxes, new trajectories are created 
and added to the tracking sequence.
2.4. Yield prediction method
The yield prediction of C. oleifera in this study was achieved through 
a three-stage pipeline integrating fruit detection, tracking-based count­
ing, and regression modeling. First, an improved YOLOv8 model was 
employed for fruit detection, in which the RepViT backbone replaced 
the original CSPDarkNet structure, a P2 layer was added to the 
FPN–PAN neck to enhance small-object detection, and the Shape IoU 
loss was introduced to optimize bounding box regression, thereby 
enabling robust recognition of small fruits in complex orchard envi­
ronments. Second, to obtain reliable fruit counts from video sequences, 
we adopted an improved ByteTrack algorithm following the tracking-by- 
detection paradigm, in which ORB feature extraction and matching were 
incorporated into the data association process to maintain consistent 
fruit identities across frames, reduce duplicate counts, and ensure ac­
curate tracking under occlusion and variable lighting. Finally, the 
regression modeling process was constructed in two steps: (i) estab­
lishing a mapping between the number of detected surface fruits and the 
actual total fruit load per tree, where surface counts serve as the inde­
pendent variable and manually measured total fruit numbers as the 
dependent variable; and (ii) incorporating the average weight of indi­
vidual fruits, obtained from phenotypic sampling, into the model so that 
total yield could be expressed as the product of the predicted fruit load 
and the average fruit weight. Linear regression was selected as the pri­
mary modeling method due to its simplicity and interpretability, while 
additional machine learning regressors (e.g., Random Forest, Extra­
Trees, and LightGBM) were also tested to verify robustness. Recent 
studies in greenhouse fruit monitoring have shown that combining 
multiple detection strategies, including hybrid and fusion models, can

2.5. Experiment evaluation metrics
In this experiment, we used the AMD EPYC 7T83 64-core processor 
and RTX4090 GPU for model training. The software environment in­
cludes CUDA version 11.8, Python version 3.8, and Pytorch version 
2.0.0. To ensure optimal model performance during training, we adop­
ted specific parameters and hyperparameter settings. The image size 
during training was set to 640 × 640, with the number of iterations set to 
300. To improve computational efficiency and model convergence 
speed, we fixed the batch size to 16 and disabled the Mosaic data 
augmentation technique in the last 10 epochs.
For hyperparameter selection, we employed a gradient-based SGD 
optimizer for model optimization. The initial learning rate was set to 
0.01 and decreased to 0.0001 by the end of training. The momentum 
was set to 0.937, and the weight decay was set to 0.0005 to ensure 
stability and convergence speed during the training process. These 
hyperparameters were selected based on multiple experimental results 
and relevant literature, ensuring excellent model performance under 
different conditions (Table S2). This metric provides a standardized 
evaluation of detection accuracy under relaxed localization constraints, 
making it particularly suitable for real-world agricultural scenarios 
where precise bounding box alignment can be challenging due to small 
object size or partial occlusion.
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method in the context 
of C. oleifera fruit detection and counting, we adopted a comprehensive 
set of metrics that reflect both detection accuracy and practical 
deployability: Precision measures the system’s ability to avoid false 
alarms. For instance, a high precision indicates that the model rarely 
misclassifies leaves, branches, or shadows as fruits. In orchard auto­
mation systems, this reliability is critical for downstream applications 
such as robotic harvesting or yield mapping, where false positives may 
lead to inefficient resource allocation or inaccurate inventory estimates. 
Recall indicates how well the model captures all visible fruits, especially 
under challenging conditions such as foliage occlusion, low contrast, 
small target size, or uneven illumination. Since accurate yield estimation 
relies on minimizing missed detections, a high recall is essential for 
robust monitoring and effective harvest planning. mAP (mean Average 
Precision), particularly mAP@0.5, serves as a holistic measure of 
detection performance. A high mAP reflects consistent object localiza­
tion and classification across diverse environmental conditions. In our 
application, this translates to stable performance despite variations in 
tree density, fruit maturity, and lighting, which are common challenges 
in real-world C. oleifera orchards. FPS (Frames Per Second) quantifies 
the model’s inference speed, which directly affects its feasibility for real- 
time deployment on mobile platforms such as drones or ground robots. A 
higher FPS enables efficient, large-scale field monitoring without 
sacrificing timeliness, thus supporting practical use in precision agri­
culture. Together, these metrics provide a context-aware evaluation 
framework that goes beyond generic benchmarks, aligning technical 
performance with real-world agricultural requirements.

3. Results
3.1. Comparison of training results of classical object detection models
In this study, we compared the improved YOLOv8 model with Faster 
R-CNN, SSD, YOLOv3, YOLOv5, YOLOv7, YOLOv8, and the newer 
YOLOv9 to YOLOv12 series. Faster R-CNN, a classic two-stage detector, 
relies on a Region Proposal Network (RPN) but suffers from high 
computational cost, while SSD, though faster, faces challenges in 
detecting small objects. The YOLO series, from YOLOv3 to YOLOv12, 
reformulates object detection as a regression problem, providing a good 
balance of speed and accuracy. Among them, YOLOv8 has been widely 
adopted due to its stable performance and efficiency, making it a reliable 
baseline model.
Our results show that YOLO models generally outperform Faster R- 
CNN and SSD in both accuracy and efficiency, while our improved 
YOLOv8 further advances performance across all key metrics (Table 1). 
Specifically, it achieved FLOPs, parameter count, and model size values 
of only 23.2 G, 6.6 M, and 14.0 MB, significantly lower than most YOLO 
models. At the same time, it outperformed the standard YOLOv8 in 
precision (85.13 % vs. 81.06 %), recall (77.84 % vs. 74.07 %), mAP50

tional footprint. For example, despite YOLOv9–YOLOv12 requiring 
larger parameter sizes (12–17.6 M) and weights (16.9–22.8 MB), their 
performance remains lower than our improved YOLOv8, which com­
bines lightweight design with the highest accuracy. These results high­
light that the improved YOLOv8 achieves a better trade-off between 
efficiency and detection performance, making it more suitable for real- 
world applications where both accuracy and computational cost are 
critical.
3.2. Evaluation of the proposed detection model
In this study, the RepViT network replaces the original CSPDarkNet- 
53 backbone in YOLOv8. The neck design adds a P2 layer feature 
network, and the IoU section implements our proposed Shape IoU 
calculation in the loss function. To assess the effectiveness of the various 
improvement modules in the enhanced YOLOv8 model, ablation studies 
were conducted comparing YOLOv8 (baseline), YOLOv8 (RepViT), 
YOLOv8 (p2), and YOLOv8 (Shape IoU). These models were evaluated 
in terms of precision (P), recall, mAP50, and mAP50–95 (Table 2). The 
results show that different configurations significantly improve the ob­
ject detection system, with YOLOv8 (RepViT) yielding the most notable 
improvements. Depending on specific requirements, selecting the 
appropriate configuration enhances the system’s accuracy and stability.
YOLOv8 (RepViT) showed significant improvements over the base­
line YOLOv8 in precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50–95, with increases 
of 2.68 %, 3.36 %, 3.43 %, and 3.92 %, respectively. YOLOv8 (Neck) and 
YOLOv8 (Shape IoU) also showed improvements over the baseline 
model, with increases of up to 1.91 % in precision and recall. Ultimately, 
the improved YOLOv8 model outperformed all others with P, recall, 
mAP50, and mAP50–95 values of 85.13 %, 77.84 %, 86.21 %, and 48.97 
%, respectively, surpassing the baseline YOLOv8 by 4.07 %, 3.77 %, 
4.51 %, and 4.69 %.
Although box-loss, obj-loss, and cls-loss for the improved model were 
slightly higher than the original, precision, recall, mAP50, and 
mAP50–95 showed notable improvements (Fig. 6, 7). The improved 
YOLOv8 algorithm demonstrated excellent convergence across various 
performance indicators, confirming the reliability of the C. oleifera fruit 
counting model built on this detection framework. Lower values of the 
three losses indicate better model training, while higher values of the 
four evaluation metrics indicate improved detection accuracy.
A heatmap analysis was conducted to visualize the performance 
improvements (Fig. 8). The first column shows the original image, the 
second column displays the heatmap from the baseline YOLOv8 model,

To further improve interpretability, future work should focus on 
enhancing heatmap resolution and bounding box clarity. Clearer heat­
maps and more contrasting bounding boxes will make it easier to vali­
date detection results, especially in challenging conditions like occlusion 
and varying lighting.

4.2. The introduction of the ORB algorithm in the Bytetrack model 
significantly improves the accuracy and speed of multi-target tracking
Multiobject tracking tracks and localizes objects in subsequent 
frames based on the tracked object’s position in the initial frame, asso­
ciating the same object in the video. Among them, the tracking method 
based on the Kalman filter is widely used in the field of fruit tracking. 
Gan et al [46] used an extended KF-integrated autonomous navigation 
system to locate and track citrus fruits, but the tracking cost was 
expensive. To reduce costs, Wang et al [47] combined KF and Hungarian 
algorithms for mango tracking, reducing tracking costs and complexity, 
but there was a severe problem of mango occlusion. To minimize 
duplicate counting of fruits, Gao et al [25] proposed an improved 
Hungarian algorithm that preserves the tracking boxes that disappear 
due to occlusion during apple tracking and re-match them, improving 
the accuracy of apple tracking. However, for fruit trees with large or 
dense leaves, leaf occlusion of the fruit may result in the loss of the 
tracking target. To address this issue, Zhang et al [35] proposed 
OrangeSort based on DeepSORT, which reduces the impact of leaf oc­
clusion on the accuracy of citrus counting results for field citrus tracking. 
However, this approach ignores the appearance features of the target. Ge 
et al [22] proposed an improved YOLODeepsort for tomato tracking, 
which utilizes the appearance feature extraction network in DeepSORT 
for association matching, meeting the practical needs of tomato yield 
prediction. Since the above methods are all proposed as target tracking 
solutions in an ensemble scenario, they are not applicable for tracking 
and counting C.oleifera in a natural scenario. The tracking algorithm 
proposed in this work introduces the ORB algorithm into the ByteTrack 
tracking model to enrich the feature information of the target. The 
improved tracking algorithm achieved a MOTA of 63.7 %, a MOTP of 
80.2 %, and an IDF1 of 77.1 % in the C.oleifera fruit dataset. Compared 
to before the improvement, the accuracy of multi-target tracking has 
increased by 1.1 %, the accuracy of multi-target tracking has increased 
by 0.8 %, and the ID harmonic mean has increased by 0.4 %. Compared 
to other state-of-the-art tracking algorithms, it has a higher FPS and a 
more significant number of correct tracked trajectories with relatively 
fewer numbered jumps. When the relative distance between two targets 
moves closer and closer over time, traditional tracking algorithms rely 
solely on motion information for association matching, which may result 
in jumps in the number of target bins or even lead to incorrect matching 
or missing targets. The modified tracking algorithm performs 
frame-to-frame correlation matching through the dual action of motion 
and appearance information to improve the matching accuracy between 
predicted tracking and detection boxes. It effectively improves the 
problem of target-number hopping and target-box loss when matching 
solely on a single piece of information, greatly enhancing the tracking 
accuracy of the tracking algorithm. So, we propose an algorithm with 
high accuracy and fast tracking speed.
4.3. The estimation of fruit yield based on videos provides a new idea for 
determining the region yield of C.oleifera
Yield estimation in agriculture is critical, and diverse methodologies 
offer their own unique merits and challenges. Statistical methods, a 
traditional approach, analyze historical data for yield estimation. Their 
simplicity suits data- or resource-constrained settings but may 
compromise accuracy due to oversimplifying complex growth dynamics 
[48]. Conversely, physical models incorporate crop growth physics and 
environmental factors, offering precision but necessitating intricate 
construction, accurate parameters, and extensive data [49]. Meteoro­
logical analysis uses weather data to predict yields [50], correlating 
weather with yields but possibly neglecting other influential factors. 
Remote sensing, especially via UAVs, facilitates large-scale yield esti­
mation based on fruit counts. Its high spatiotemporal resolution captures 
crop dynamics but may be costly and less suitable for small-scale 
farmlands [51,52].
In contrast, video-based multi-objective tracking methods have 
shown unique advantages in crop yield prediction. It offers swift data 
acquisition, ease of use, and high accuracy. This approach utilizes 
refined object detection models to locate fruits precisely, assigns unique 
IDs via ByteTrack to prevent double counting, and employs machine 
learning to correlate canopy fruit counts with actual yields. This method 
achieved a fitting effect of 0.902 and RMSE of 0.415 in this study, 
demonstrating remarkable accuracy and practicality in predicting oil tea 
production. By integrating advanced detection and tracking technolo­
gies, this approach offers a robust solution for large-scale yield estima­
tion, fostering the sustainable growth of the C.oleifera industry.
Meanwhile, our model also has inconsistent prediction yield accu­
racy rates among different varieties. This discrepancy can be attributed 
to several factors. First, dataset imbalance likely impacted the model’s 
ability to learn from CL40, which may have been underrepresented in 
the training data, leading to a stronger model bias toward CL4. To 
address this, future work should focus on balancing the dataset or using 
data augmentation techniques to enhance the model’s ability to gener­
alize across different varieties. Second, the morphological similarities 
between CL4 and CL40, such as fruit size and shape, could contribute to 
difficulties in distinguishing between the two, highlighting the need for 
more distinct feature extraction methods. Additionally, the error dis­
tribution between predicted and actual yield values, particularly for 
CL40, suggests the model may be prone to false negatives, likely due to 
occlusion or complex background conditions, which could lead to 
missed detections. Further research could focus on improving the 
model’s sensitivity to such factors, including occlusion handling and 
multi-view tracking.
4.4. Future applications and deployment in real-world environments
While this study focuses on improving the YOLOv8 model for fruit 
detection and tracking in controlled environments, future work could 
explore the deployment of this system in real farm or forest environ­
ments on edge devices. The successful integration of the improved 
YOLOv8 and ByteTrack algorithm provides a strong foundation for real- 
time yield estimation in precision agriculture. Deploying this method in 
outdoor environments would require suitable hardware, such as mobile 
robotic platforms or drones equipped with edge devices like Raspberry 
Pi or NVIDIA Jetson, which can support real-time processing while 
maintaining high performance. These devices, coupled with high- 
resolution cameras (e.g., Sony IMX series for RGB cameras or depth 
cameras for improved occlusion handling), could be used for fruit

This future integration with mobile platforms would not only

5. Conclusion
A video sequence-based fruit counting algorithm for C. oleifera is 
proposed to address the small target characteristics of wild oil tea tree 
fruits and the challenges posed by different occlusion states in video 
sequences. The algorithm incorporates two sub-algorithms: improved 
YOLOv8 for fruit detection and enhanced ByteTrack for multi-object 
tracking. The improved YOLOv8 model, featuring a lightweight 
RepViT backbone, a minimal object detection layer with the P2-layer 
added on top of the multi-scale FPN+PAN, and a network optimized 
by the Shape IoU loss function, achieved an improved accuracy of 85.13 
%, with mAP50 reaching 86.21 %. Additionally, the ByteTrack model, 
enhanced with the ORB algorithm, performed significantly better than 
other tracking models, showing improvements in MOTA, MOTP, and 
IDF1 by 1.1 %, 0.8 %, and 0.4 %, respectively.
The yield prediction model developed in this study demonstrated 
strong performance, with R2 values of 0.989, 0.931, 0.914, and 0.945 for 
CL4, CL40, CL53, and mixed samples, respectively. The R² and RMSE for 
model accuracy tests were 0.901 and 10.989, respectively, showing 
good consistency between predicted and measured yield values. By 
incorporating the number of detected fruits and the average fruit qual­
ity, the model significantly improved production estimates, with R², 
MSE, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE values of 0.902, 0.297, 0.545, 0.415, and 
17.55, respectively.
While the proposed method has shown effectiveness in yield esti­
mation for C. oleifera, some limitations must be acknowledged. The 
model’s performance varies across different varieties, and the detection 
accuracy may be influenced by factors such as fruit size differences and 
tree size variability. Additionally, generalizing the model to other fruit 
types may pose challenges due to morphological differences. Environ­
mental constraints, such as lighting conditions and occlusion, could also 
impact the model’s robustness in diverse natural settings. Finally, scal­
ability issues may arise when applying the model to larger or more 
complex orchard environments.
In future work, we plan to extend the framework to incorporate 
multiple-view tracking data to further improve automatic counting and 
yield prediction. Addressing these limitations will help enhance the 
model’s applicability to a wider range of fruit types and real-world 
agricultural conditions.
