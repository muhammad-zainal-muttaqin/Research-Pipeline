A B S T R A C T
Video Instance Segmentation (VIS), also known as multi-object tracking and segmentation, represents a 
fundamental challenge in computer vision that requires simultaneous detection, segmentation, and tracking 
of object instances across video frames. This complex task has gained significant attention due to its crucial 
role in various real-world applications. The advent of deep learning has promoted VIS approaches, leading to 
numerous architectural innovations and performance improvements. This survey presents a systematic review 
of deep learning-based VIS methods, introducing a novel categorization based on temporal modeling strate-
gies: frame-by-frame, clip-based, in-memory feature propagation, and in-memory object query propagation. 
Comprehensive quantitative comparisons of existing work across three major VIS benchmark datasets are 
also provided. Additionally, emerging challenges in the field are explored, with several promising research 
directions identified, aiming to provide valuable insights for researchers and practitioners interested in VIS, 
while further advancing deep learning techniques for VIS.
1. Introduction
Video Instance Segmentation (VIS), a task formally introduced by 
Yang et al. [1] in 2019, extends traditional image segmentation by 
incorporating the temporal dimension of instance tracking across video 
frames. This complex task has emerged as a fundamental compo-
nent in numerous real-world applications across diverse domains. In 
surveillance systems, VIS enhances human action recognition and facil-
itates person re-identification [2–4]. For autonomous driving systems, 
it enables precise tracking of vehicles and pedestrians, contributing to 
safe navigation and decision-making [5–7]. The applications extend to 
healthcare, where VIS supports biomedical image analysis, pathology 
detection, and surgical automation [8]. Furthermore, its utility spans 
industrial sectors, including agriculture [9] and construction [10], 
demonstrating its broad practical significance.
Recent deep learning advances, particularly with sophisticated
multi-layer neural networks [11], have promoted VIS, leading to in-
creasingly complex architectures. Despite this progress, comprehensive 
VIS literature reviews are scarce. Existing surveys on related fields 
like image segmentation [12], Transformers [13], and video object 
tracking [14–16] focus on specific aspects with limited VIS coverage.

2. Preliminaries
Before analyzing recent VIS studies, it is essential to understand 
both the historical context of video segmentation and key concepts 
related to modern video segmentation tasks and deep neural networks.
2.1. Video segmentation
Video segmentation, which aims to isolate and identify elements 
within videos, has been an active research area in computer vision 
for several decades. Early approaches focused on frame-by-frame anal-
ysis and motion-based segmentation [20], tackling various aspects of 
tracking and segmenting objects at the pixel level.
The advent of deep learning has significantly evolved the field, 
leading to several specialized tasks, as shown in Fig. 1. Before dis-
cussing these tasks, it is important to distinguish between ‘‘objects’’ 
and ‘‘instances’’ in modern video segmentation: an object refers to a 
general category (e.g., pedestrians, cars), while an instance is a specific 
occurrence of that object. For example, multiple cars in a frame are 
treated as separate instances with unique labels, even if they belong to 
the same object category.
Video Object Segmentation (VOS): VOS is a binary task that 
segments foreground objects from the background in videos [18,20]. It 
focuses on identifying pixels associated with the salient object, without 
distinguishing between different instances of the same class. VOS is the 
foundation for other video segmentation tasks.
Video Semantic Segmentation (VSS): Originally developed for im-
ages, semantic segmentation classifies every pixel into a category [24]. 
Applied to videos, VSS assigns each pixel in a frame to one of several 
semantic classes, without differentiating between instances [25].
Video Instance Segmentation (VIS): VIS, introduced by Yang 
et al. [1], detects, segments, and tracks individual object instances in 
videos. In the same year, Voigtlaender et al. [26] extended the Multi-
Object Tracking (MOT) [2] to Multi-Object Tracking and Segmentation 
(MOTS), which is similar to VIS but requires non-overlapping masks 
during evaluation. VIS segments salient objects across multiple classes, 
while tracking distinct instances.
2.2. Deep neural networks
Numerous deep neural networks are employed in VIS. Below are 
some of the most popular ones:
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN): CNNs automatically ex-
tract features from images using convolution kernels [11]. They are 
widely used in object detection, tracking, and action recognition.
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN): RNNs are designed for sequen-
tial data, retaining context through cycles in the network [27]. Notable 
variants, like Long Short-Term Memory Networks (LSTMs), are used 
in VIS to learn sequential visual features frame-by-frame with CNN 
assistance [28].
Graph Neural Network (GNN): GNNs model dependencies among 
nodes in graph data through message passing, facilitating predictions at 
node, edge, and graph levels [29]. In VIS, GNNs enhance instance track-
ing and segmentation by modeling relationships among instances [30].
Transformers: Transformers utilize a self-attention mechanism for 
global perception of input sequences [13]. Vision Transformers (ViT) 
tokenize images into patches, achieving strong performance in various 
tasks, including VIS [31–33]. They enable frame-level object detec-
tion and segmentation while modeling long-range dependencies for 
improved instance tracking [34].

3.1. Frame-by-frame methods
Frame-by-frame VIS segments frames independently and links in-
stances temporally (e.g., optical flow). Building on image segmentation 
architectures, this approach benefits from simplicity and leverages 
image-level advances. Yet, it faces challenges in temporal consistency 
and handling complex interactions. Occlusions and motion blur notably 
degrade per-frame accuracy, causing tracking errors.
Optical flow is a traditional technique that estimates pixel-wise 
motion between frames. Porzi et al. [35] incorporated a tracking head 
into Mask R-CNN to utilize both region segmentation outputs and 
RoI features from the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) [97], allowing 
instance association without requiring a memory cache. Lin et al. [36] 
further introduced a two-branch model to extract instance-agnostic and 
instance-specific features from reference and target frames, combining 
them through a decoder for instance segmentation. However, while op-
tical flow eliminates the need for explicit memory storage, it struggles 
with handling occlusions and re-identifying instances that disappear 
and later reappear, especially when severe motion blur distorts object 
appearance.
As the You Only Look Once (YOLO) [98] algorithm has gained 
popularity for object detection, the YOLO-based approach has been 
adapted for VIS. For example, YOLACT [37] employs a two-branch 
structure for prototype mask generation and mask coefficient predic-
tion. Bae et al. [38] validated its robustness under occlusions, while 
SipMask [39] refined it by segmenting masks into sub-regions, im-
proving spatial preservation but lacking explicit temporal modeling. 
YolTrack [40] introduced a tracking decoder branch to enhance in-
stance association, achieving real-time inference at the cost of accuracy. 
To address feature propagation across frames, Li et al. [41] fused 
adjacent frame features into YOLACT’s two-branch structure, but strug-
gled with long-term tracking. Meanwhile, YolactEdge [42] optimized 
computational efficiency by processing only a subset of features on 
non-keyframes, making it more suitable for edge devices but reducing 
temporal coherence.
Siamese networks, with their multi-branch design, facilitate in-
stance association across frames by comparing feature embeddings. Wu 
et al. [43] introduced an association module for enhanced detection, 
while CrossVIS [44] leveraged dynamic filters to extract background-
agnostic instance representations. Wu et al. [45] applied contrastive 
learning to separate instance embeddings, while Jiang et al. [46] 
proposed a bi-directional spatiotemporal learning scheme to improve 
instance association accuracy. Zhu et al. [47] further introduced iden-
tification and association modules to address occlusions and reap-
pearances. Despite their efficiency, Siamese network-based methods 
struggle with tracking instances over long videos and re-identifying 
those that disappear for extended durations.
Transformer-based extensions of Siamese networks enhance inter-
frame attention between reference and target frames. Li et al. [48] 
introduced intra-frame attention for pixel- and instance-level feature 
fusion, while inter-frame attention modules ensured temporal consis-
tency. Similar inter-frame instance association techniques were pro-
posed in [45,49]. However, reference keyframe selection remains a 
challenge, as improper selection affects both computational efficiency 
and tracking accuracy. Yang et al. [50] attempted to mitigate this issue 
with an inter-clip attention mechanism that learns instance sequences 
through feature similarity, yet its effectiveness remains under-explored.
Semi-supervised VIS methods aim to reduce annotation costs. Zhou 
et al. [51] proposed a two-branch network, where D-Net generates

To improve feature propagation without initial frame masks, Lin 
et al. [53] incorporated a variational autoencoder into Mask R-CNN. 
Their architecture included a shared encoder for latent representation 
learning and three decoders dedicated to semantic segmentation, atten-
tive feature refinement, and feature aggregation. However, this design 
nearly doubles the computational complexity of Mask R-CNN, making 
it inefficient for real-time applications.
Transformer-based models have also been explored for VIS. Hu 
et al. [54] proposed a recurrent Transformer-based refinement strategy, 
while MaskFreeVIS [55] replaced mask annotations with bounding-box 
annotations for training, leveraging Mask2Former [99]. MinVIS [56] 
simplified training through bipartite matching in post-processing, im-
proving efficiency. While Transformer-based models excel at feature 
extraction and instance segmentation, their high computational cost 
remains a limiting factor.
3.2. Clip-based methods
Clip-based methods analyze multiple frames simultaneously, utiliz-
ing 3D convolutions or temporal aggregation to model spatiotemporal 
features, motion, and temporal dependencies. This enhances robust-
ness through motion and temporal context but necessitates balancing 
temporal receptive field size and computational efficiency.
When introducing the MOTS task, Voigtlaender et al. [26] proposed 
TrackR-CNN, which utilizes a 3D CNN for feature extraction. The 
method employs a Region Proposal Network (RPN) [100] to gener-
ate object proposals, followed by multiple heads that predict class, 
bounding box, binary mask, and association vectors for each RoI. The 
association vectors are used to compute Euclidean distances for in-
stance tracking across frames. While this approach effectively integrates 
spatial and temporal information, it requires significant computational 
resources due to the complexity of 3D CNNs.
Luiten et al. [57] introduced UnOVOST, which treats video tracking 
as a tree of multiple tracklets instead of a linear sequence. Their method 
combines instance segments from consecutive frames into tracklets 
based on spatiotemporal consistency cues, which are then merged 
into longer tracks using decision trees driven by appearance simi-
larity. Although UnOVOST enhances long-distance tracking accuracy, 
its computational and memory demands are considerably higher than 
those of frame-level techniques, making it less suitable for real-time 
applications.
To further refine feature extraction, Qin et al. [58] proposed an 
encoder–decoder model where three decoders generate latent distri-
butions, offset vectors, and appearance embeddings based on the ex-
tracted image features. This model improves AP by 3.5% over Mask-
Track R-CNN but comes at the cost of being twice as slow, limiting its 
applicability in time-sensitive tasks.
The self-attention mechanism of Transformers has been adapted 
for 3D spatiotemporal feature extraction in VIS. Cheng et al. [59,99] 
extended Mask2Former to incorporate masked attention on 3D fea-
tures, enabling more precise instance mask predictions across frames. 
Similarly, VisTR [34] built upon DETR [32] by integrating instance 
sequence matching and segmentation for concurrent mask predictions 
across multiple frames. While these Transformer-based approaches sig-
nificantly improve instance segmentation accuracy, they impose high 
computational and memory costs, limiting their scalability for long 
video sequences. Cai et al. [60] addressed this issue by incorporat-
ing temporal and spatial attention mechanisms at the clip level to 
refine instance representations. However, despite these optimizations, 
Transformer-based clip-processing methods remain challenging to de-
ploy efficiently on resource-constrained hardware.
3.3. In-memory feature propagation methods
These frameworks employ explicit memory to propagate image 
features across temporal sequences via a memory bank. This facilitates 
efficient instance tracking, segmentation, and temporal consistency. 
They are particularly effective for long-term dependencies and object 
reappearance by adaptively accessing historical features. Furthermore, 
they improve annotation efficiency by propagating labels from labeled 
to unlabeled frames.
One of the earliest works in VIS, MaskTrack R-CNN [1], extended 
Mask R-CNN [101] by introducing a post-processing stage for instance 
tracking across frames. This method employed a memory queue to store 
features of previously identified instances and integrated a tracking 
head that matches newly detected objects with stored representations. 
To improve instance tracking, Dong et al. [61] proposed a CNN-based 
approach for extracting features from multiple frames, using a Siamese 
network with cosine similarity to track temporal features. However, 
these methods introduce computational complexity due to additional 
processing overhead and reliance on randomly sampled frames, which 
can reduce feature comparison reliability.
To enhance instance re-identification, Feng et al. [62] introduced 
the Instance-Pixel Dual-Tracker (IPDT), which refines tracking by lever-
aging global context to filter out false positives and incorporating 
both instance-level and pixel-level embeddings to differentiate over-
lapping objects. Tran et al. [63] proposed a bi-directional instance 
segmentation strategy, where masks from adjacent frames serve as 
references for segmenting the current frame. Extending this idea, Tran 
et al. [66] introduced a multi-referenced guided segmentation ap-
proach, in which a first-pass mask propagation identifies reliable frames 
that are then stored as references for a second round of refinement. 
While effective, this iterative approach introduces efficiency challenges, 
particularly for long videos, due to the increased memory requirements 
and computational burden.
Further improving temporal coherence, Bertasius et al. [67] devel-
oped a method that propagates instance features from a specific frame 
across an entire video sequence, leveraging feature tensor differences 
to achieve clip-level tracking. This approach outperforms MaskTrack R-
CNN in handling overlapping instances but struggles with dense object 
scenes and fine-grained feature distinction [68], as it remains heavily 
dependent on the mask predictions generated by Mask R-CNN.
RNN-based approaches align well with the in-memory feature prop-
agation paradigm by leveraging recurrent processing to maintain and 
update instance-specific information over time. These architectures 
inherently capture sequential dependencies, enabling robust tempo-
ral reasoning and adaptive feature propagation. For instance, Con-
vLSTM [65] extends the traditional LSTM by incorporating convolu-
tional structures to better model spatiotemporal correlations. Sun et al. 
proposed a contextual pyramid ConvLSTM that processes multi-scale 
features from an FPN and utilizes a Mask R-CNN header [101] for 
instance prediction in subsequent frames, achieving an efficient balance 
between accuracy and speed for real-time applications. APANet [71] 
further refines this approach by adaptively aggregating spatiotempo-
ral contextual information across multiple scales, improving instance 
tracking and future frame predictions. To explicitly handle occlusions 
and object reappearances, Liu et al. [78] integrate a memory bank with 
a ConvLSTM-based mask prediction branch to store and retrieve motion 
patterns, enhancing robustness against rapid motion and temporary 
disappearances. However, while these RNN-based methods improve 
long-term feature propagation, they often face challenges of paralleliza-
tion, which hinders performance when processing large amounts of 
video sequences.

Beyond RNN-based methods, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have 
also been explored for in-memory feature propagation to enhance 
instance tracking across frames [74,79]. These approaches construct 
graphs from detected instances, where node embeddings are iteratively 
refined using LSTMs for historical aggregation and future tracking. 
Alternatively, Wang et al. [30] replace RNNs with GNNs to facilitate 
spatiotemporal feature aggregation between consecutive frames, lever-
aging memory mechanisms to cache and propagate mask information. 
While GNN-based methods improve instance association and tracking 
consistency, their effectiveness is highly dependent on detection accu-
racy, making them vulnerable to errors that can propagate through the 
graph and disrupt segmentation performance.
In addition to GNN-based approaches, saliency-driven approaches 
enhance feature propagation by focusing on key regions within
frames [25]. Le et al. [64] proposed a two-branch framework integrat-
ing semantic instance segmentation with salient object segmentation, 
where instance masks are propagated across frames and fused using an 
identity-tracking module to improve segmentation accuracy.
Mathematical modeling techniques have also been explored for in-
memory feature propagation. Ge et al. [69] introduced a correlation 
matrix-based approach that generates instance score maps, distinguish-
ing foreground from background by encoding historical frames as 
support embeddings and processing the current frame as a query. Addi-
tionally, dynamic programming methods [70] optimize global instance 
assignments across frames, improving segmentation consistency, par-
ticularly for overlapping instances. The VISOLO model [75] enhances 
tracking by partitioning frames into grid-based regions that generate 
semantic scores and instance masks. Its memory-matching module 
caches historical feature maps, computing similarity between grids for 
robust tracking and improved occlusion handling.
In the context of semi-supervised VIS, Lin et al. [72] proposed a 
propose-reduce paradigm inspired by [102], extending Mask R-CNN 
with a sequence propagation head that generates instance sequence 
proposals from multiple keyframes. These proposals are refined using 
non-maximum suppression (NMS) to eliminate redundancy. While this 
approach simplifies long-term instance tracking, it introduces chal-
lenges in balancing computational efficiency and tracking accuracy, 
particularly in determining the optimal number of sequence proposals. 
Improved keyframe selection strategies remain crucial for enhancing 
overall performance.
As Transformer becomes popular in computer vision, it has also 
been leveraged for in-memory feature propagation by establishing rela-
tionships between instances across feature maps. Cross-attention mech-
anisms [32] effectively model inter-frame associations, enabling on-
line VIS, while offline Transformer-based refiners further enhance seg-
mentation consistency. Fu et al. [73] incorporated frame-level and 
object-level attention modules into Mask R-CNN to refine region-of-
interest (RoI) proposals. Similarly, Abrantes et al. [76] employed a 
Transformer-based instance segmentation framework with temporal 
attention refinement, grouping object embeddings from current and 
past frames in memory. Zhang et al. [77] simplified the VIS pipeline 
by decoupling segmentation, tracking, and refinement tasks, utilizing 
dedicated attention modules to optimize each stage.
3.4. In-memory object query propagation methods
Building on Vision Transformers, in-memory object query propa-
gation maintains and updates object queries across sequences [80]. 
Leveraging transformer query power and temporal memory, this design 
enables robust tracking and segmentation by adapting queries to chang-
ing appearances and scenes. However, challenges persist in handling 
occlusions, ensuring long-term identity consistency, and maintaining 
efficiency, particularly in extended videos.
Hwang et al. [81] introduced a clip-based pipeline that employs 
dual Transformers, one dedicated to frame encoding and another for 
inter-frame communication, balancing performance and resource effi-
ciency. Meinhardt et al. [82] proposed TrackFormer, which builds on 
Deformable DETR [103] to establish a tracking-by-attention paradigm 
using auto-regressive track queries for instance detection and track-
ing. To improve tracking consistency, Koner et al. [83] developed 
InstanceFormer, integrating a memory queue to store prior instance 
representations. Heo et al. [84] further advanced long-term instance 
tracking with GenVIS, a clip-level query propagation framework based 
on VITA [94], which achieves state-of-the-art performance while main-
taining low computational overhead. Additionally, Fang et al. [85] 
introduced a method leveraging attention mechanisms to fuse all frame 
queries, enhancing instance representation. While these approaches 
improve query propagation efficiency, challenges remain in handling 
occlusions and maintaining identity consistency over extended video 
sequences.
To further enhance temporal consistency in VIS, various strategies 
have been introduced. The method in [86] incorporated clip-level 
queries to fuse information across frames, effectively combining re-
current and integrated instance queries. While this strategy enhances 
robustness, it significantly increases computational complexity and 
memory usage, making real-time inference difficult. InsPro [33] pro-
posed an alternative approach by propagating instance query-proposal 
pairs across frames, leveraging cached features and intra-query at-
tention to address occlusion and motion blur. Similarly, CT-VIS [87] 
introduced identity-switch simulations during training by adding noise 
to embeddings, improving instance association. Hannan et al. [88] 
proposed Gated Residual Attention for VIS (GRAtt-VIS), which applies 
gate activation as a mask for self-attention, selectively filtering unrep-
resentative instance queries. While GRAtt-VIS reduces computational 
overhead and supports online processing, it struggles with identity 
tracking in complex motion scenarios. Wang et al. [89] introduced a 
two-branch network that separately processes instance segmentation 
and video object segmentation, improving instance detail extraction but 
increasing model complexity.
To improve efficiency, recent methods focus on optimizing
Transformer-based architectures. SeqFormer [90] employs a multi-level 
deformable attention mechanism to refine both frame- and instance-
level queries, while TAFormer [91] introduces multi-scale temporal 
attention, slightly improving performance at the cost of increased com-
plexity. MSG-Transformer [104], initially designed for local attention, 
was extended into TeViT [92], optimizing efficiency for VIS while main-
taining high segmentation accuracy. EfficientVIS [93] improves VisTR’s 
convergence using clip-based queries, while VITA [94] minimizes dense 
feature extraction through object-aware context. Zhang et al. [95] 
proposed MobileInst, a lightweight VIS framework that incorporates 
a dual-transformer instance decoder and temporal query passing for 
improved tracking. Despite these efficiency gains, trade-offs persist 
between model accuracy and computational overhead, particularly in 
handling long video sequences. Knowledge distillation has also been 
explored as a technique to enhance model efficiency while reducing 
training costs [96]. However, its reliance on pre-trained models and 
additional training steps increases overall complexity.

5. Challenges and future research directions
Despite notable progress, several issues exist in VIS, demanding 
further research.
Occluded Video Instance Segmentation: Addressing the challenge 
of segmenting highly occluded instances has become increasingly im-
portant, particularly given the introduction of the OVIS dataset [105] 
which features three times more occlusions than YouTube-VIS. Cur-
rent research approaches this challenge through several key strate-
gies. Temporal context exploitation has proven particularly effective, 
where methods leverage non-occluded views from adjacent frames and 
employ memory-based tracking to maintain instance identity during 
occlusions [78]. Additionally, occlusion-aware feature learning mecha-
nisms have been developed [111], incorporating attention mechanisms 
that explicitly model occluder–occludee relationships. Recent advance-
ments in sequence modeling, particularly the Mamba [112] architec-
ture with its selective state space modeling, offer promising directions 
for handling occlusions. Mamba’s ability to selectively process and 
retain long-range temporal dependencies could enable more effective 
modeling of instance appearances across frames, potentially helping 
to recover occluded instances by leveraging information from non-
occluded views. However, adapting Mamba’s selective scan mechanism 
for visual occlusion handling remains an open challenge.
Motion-Blurred Video Instance Segmentation: Motion blur
presents a significant challenge in VIS, often distorting object appear-
ances and compromising segmentation accuracy [41]. There are several 
possible research directions to address this challenge. Pre-processing 
solutions incorporate neural deblurring networks [113] and multi-scale 
feature extraction to enhance motion-blurred frames. Robust feature 
learning techniques are promising, including blur-invariant feature 
extractors and motion-guided feature aggregation methods that main-
tain temporal consistency. Training enhancements also play a crucial 
role, with strategies such as synthetic blur augmentation, multi-task

learning with motion estimation, and curriculum learning [114] ap-
proaches that progressively expose models to increasingly challenging 
blur conditions.
Annotation-Efficient Video Instance Segmentation: The substan-
tial cost associated with video annotations has driven research to-
ward more efficient approaches to video instance segmentation. Self-
supervised learning methods leverage temporal consistency as supervi-
sion and employ contrastive learning across frames for instance discov-
ery [115]. Weak supervision strategies have shown promise, including 
point supervision propagation [116] and box-to-mask refinement tech-
niques [55]. Data efficiency improvements have been achieved through 
cross-dataset knowledge transfer and synthetic data generation, while 
active learning approaches help optimize the annotation process by 
selecting the most informative frames for labeling. Recent works, such 
as DINO [117] and CutLER [118], have shown promising results, with 
UVIS [119] demonstrating the feasibility of unsupervised learning built 
upon CutLER. However, significant performance gaps persist compared 
to fully supervised methods.
Unified Model for Multiple Tasks: Built upon VOS, VSS, and 
VIS, new segmentation tasks keep emerging, such as Video Panoptic 
Segmentation [120], which incorporates pixel-level segmentation of 
backgrounds. Therefore, a good research direction is a unified architec-
ture design, which addresses various segmentation tasks simultaneously 
while maintaining temporal consistency through memory-based mech-
anisms. Some researchers [121,122] have validated the feasibility of 
a unified model for integrated video segmentation tasks, though chal-
lenges in prediction accuracy and efficiency persist. More effective 
feature integration strategies remain a key focus to enable joint feature 
learning for object, background, and interaction relationships. Besides, 
efficiency improvement is also important for various applications, urg-
ing researchers to explore lightweight backbone networks and efficient 
memory mechanisms.
Open-Vocabulary 
Video 
Instance 
Segmentation: 
Open-
vocabulary VIS represents an emerging challenge in the field, aiming to 
detect and segment instances from previously unseen categories [123]. 
While the Large-Vocabulary Video Instance Segmentation (LV-VIS) 
dataset [124] provides a foundation for research, significant chal-
lenges remain in achieving robust performance across diverse object 
categories. Future research could focus on knowledge transfer and 
data enhancement to advance the field [125]. Knowledge transfer 
approaches may utilize language-vision pre-training and cross-category 
feature adaptation [126]. Data enhancement strategies involve syn-
thetic category generation and cross-domain adaptation [122].
Multimodal Video Instance Segmentation: Multimodal VIS fo-
cuses on integrating information from various modalities for improved 
segmentation performance [127]. Recent research has explored video-
language fusion [128–130] and video-audio fusion [131], though chal-
lenges in effective data fusion and annotation remain. Generative mod-
els like Make-A-Video [132] show promise in addressing data scarcity 
issues. A potential research direction is developing advanced modal 
fusion strategies that incorporate adaptive feature fusion and cross-
modal attention mechanisms while maintaining temporal synchroniza-
tion across modalities. Additionally, enhancing robustness by address-
ing missing modalities [133] and implementing noise-resistant fusion 
techniques [134] represents another promising area for exploration.
6. Conclusion
Video Instance Segmentation (VIS) is a fundamental task in com-
puter vision with wide-ranging applications across various domains. 
Substantial progress has been driven by the rapid development of 
deep learning techniques and the growing availability of computational 
resources. This survey provides a systematic review, analysis, and com-
parison of existing deep-learning methodologies in this evolving field. 
Existing approaches are categorized based on their temporal modeling 
strategies, including frame-by-frame, clip-based, feature propagation, 
and object query propagation methods. Subsequently, a quantitative 
comparison of existing methods across three widely used VIS bench-
mark datasets is presented. At last, the survey highlights several key 
challenges and promising research directions, offering valuable insights 
to researchers seeking to advance video segmentation techniques.
