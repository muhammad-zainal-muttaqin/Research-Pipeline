ChatGPT and general-purpose AI count fruits in pictures surprisingly well 
without programming or training

A B S T R A C T
General-purpose artificial intelligence (AI) can facilitate agricultural digitalization as many tools do not require 
coding. Yet, it remains unclear how well the emerging general-purpose AI technologies can perform object 
counting, which is a fundamental task in agricultural digitalization, in comparison to the current standard 
practice. We show that ChatGPT (GPT4 V) demonstrated moderate performance in counting coffee cherries from 
images, while the T-Rex, foundation model for object counting, performed with high accuracy. Testing with a 
hundred images, we examined that ChatGPT can count cherries, and the performance improves with human 
feedback (R2 = 0.36 and 0.46, respectively). The T-Rex foundation model required only a few samples for 
training but outperformed YOLOv8, the conventional best practice model (R2 = 0.92 and 0.90, respectively). 
Obtaining the results with these models was 100x shorter than the conventional best practice. These results bring 
two surprises for deep learning users in applied domains: a foundation model can drastically save effort and 
achieve higher accuracy than a conventional approach, and ChatGPT can reveal a relatively good performance 
especially with guidance by providing some examples and feedback. No requirement for coding skills can impact 
education, outreach, and real-world implementation of generative AI for supporting farmers.
1. Introduction
Since ChatGPT caused a technological breakthrough in the artificial 
intelligence (AI) domain and beyond [1], large language model (LLM) 
applications have been explored in plant science [2] and agriculture [3]. 
Another trend in the AI domain is to develop general-purpose AI [4], 
also known as foundation models. Foundation models are “the models 
which are trained on broad data that can be adapted to a wide range of 
downstream tasks” [5]. However, these techniques have not been tested 
for computer vision tasks in agriculture, where their adaptability and 
minimal data requirements could make them advantageous, for appli­
cations such as crop health monitoring, yield estimation, and disease 
detection, in addition to object counting.
As an application note, we show that the recent development of LLMs 
and foundation models can perform a context-specific image analysis 
well without requiring any model training effort and programming 
skills. In this study, we focus specifically on object counting, evaluating 
models for their ability to accurately quantify objects (coffee cherries) 
within images rather than object detection. For this demonstration, we 
tested the potential of GPT-4 V (OpenAI) and T-Rex (foundation model 
for counting objects in an image [6]) for counting coffee cherry fruit 
bodies in images taken by farmers in their farms (Fig. 1). These specific 
models (GPT-4 V and T-Rex) were chosen because they were the state of 
the art (as of January 2024) representing the functionality of LLM and 
foundation models that do not require extensive training on specific 
agricultural datasets [7,8]. We compared these models to a conventional 
deep learning approach in which we trained the current best object 
detection algorithm (supervised learning), YOLO v8, in the previous 
study [9]. YOLO v8 requires a specialized training dataset, which is not 
needed for GPT-4 V and T-Rex.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data collection
As a use case, we tested how well the models can predict the number 
of cherries in 100 images of coffee tree branches. We used the dataset 
from our previous study [9]. The images were collected in local farms

Various smartphone models, including Samsung, Xiaomi, and

vertically. Other technical specifications of the images included wide 
apertures (f/1.7 to f/2.2) and ISO values ranging from 25 to 594. The 
number of cherries per image was manually counted, ranging from 1 to 
80 in our dataset.
2.2. Model traning and setup
As a baseline, the YOLO v8 [10] model was trained using an anno­
tated dataset comprising 436 images with 35,694 labeled cherry objects 
in the previous study (see [9] for the details). All models were developed 
on a Windows 10 platform using Python 3.7.0 routines running on an 
Nvidia GeForce RTX 3080 GPU clocked at 1440 MHz. Note that the 100 
images used in this study were not used for training the model but solely 
for testing model performance to ensure an unbiased evaluation of

2.3. Implementation of ChatGPT-4 for cherry counting
We used ChatGPT-4 [11] on December 7, 2023, for the analysis. With 
zero-shot learning, the model was used without prior examples to make 
predictions, and with few-shot learning, it was provided with a small set 
of examples to improve accuracy on similar tasks. Groups of three im­
ages were uploaded to the ChatGPT-4 interface, each followed by a 
prompt “Could you count the coffee cherry which is visually seen in these 3 
images carefully please? Please provide the result as a table.” for the model 
to count visible cherries. This process was repeated across the entire set 
without any iterative feedback to ensure the model’s initial unbiased 
performance was accurately recorded.
In the few-show learning approach, a custom GPT model was created 
and provided the following instructions:
“The GPT, is guided with a dataset of 10 images and is primed for 
real-case scenarios. It is committed to delivering the most accurate 
cherry counts possible from the images provided. The GPT should 
carefully consider about individual cherries, accounting for clustering, 
varying ripeness levels, lighting, and background contrasts. The GPT 
will consistently apply its best analysis on every image, using the 
knowledge gained from the examples to ensure the counts are as precise 
as they can be.”
We then uploaded a separate dataset of 10 images to the GPT Builder 
and provided the prompt as follows: “Could you count the coffee cherry 
which is visually seen in an uploaded image carefully please?” If counting 
was correct, we replied as the count was correct. If counting was 
incorrect, we prompted: “The real amount is x. Please investigate and learn 
what made you count the cherries inaccurately. Please learn and overcome 
those difficulties.” After guiding through these ten iterative feedback 
cycles, this Custom GPT saved as a model.
2.4. Utilizing the T-Rex model for object counting
The T-Rex model’s [6] object counting proficiency was also used. 
The T-Rex model was selected because it was the state of the art model as 
of January 2024 as a foundation model specialized for object counting 
tasks without extensive dataset-specific training [8]. Using the online 
demo available on the Deep Data Space Playground (https://dee 
pdataspace.com/playground/ivp, last accessed on 05.11.2024), each 
image was uploaded individually. The online interface allowed for 
straightforward upload and annotation, enabling users to manually 
draw bounding boxes around objects for guidance in counting. Three 
boundary boxes were manually drawn around visible coffee cherries in 
each image to guide the T-Rex model in identifying and counting the 
cherries.
2.5. Evaluation of model performance and efficiency
These four model approaches (YOLOv8, GPT-4 Zero-shot, GPT-4 
Few-shot, and T-Rex) were evaluated based on their accuracy and 
total time required in counting coffee cherries in the 100 images. Ac­
curacy was quantified using R2 value ranges from 0 to 1, with values 
closer to 1 indicating higher accuracy. It was calculated with the 
‘R2_score’ function from the ‘sklearn.metrics’ library version 1.2.2 [12] 
in Python programming language version 3.10.12 [13]. Note that we do 
not use mAP or other metrics for object detection because our aim is to 
quantitatively estimate a number (i.e. the number of cherries).
Time efficiency was evaluated, including time for uploading images, 
writing prompts, waiting to receive results, providing guidance with 
additional images (for the few-shot learning only), annotation (for the 
YOLOv8 model only), and training (YOLOv8 only). The time required 
for obtaining predictions for the 100 images showed significant con­
trasts in processing efficiency. The ChatGPT-4 Zero-shot learning 
approach required approximately 105 min, including uploading images, 
writing prompts, GPT-4 analyzing, and documenting results, while the 
Few-shot approach required about 195 min, including additional time 
for providing guidance with ten additional images in Custom GPT. The 
T-Rex model needed about 50 min to process all images, allocating 
approximately 30 s per image for uploading, drawing 3 bounding boxes, 
analyzing, and documenting results. For the YOLOv8 model, the anno­
tation process required 160 h for 436 images containing 35,694 labeled 
cherries, the run-time for the model training took 75 min, and the 
average time predictions per picture was 30 milliseconds.
3. Results
3.1. Model accuracy and performance
The trained task-specific model, YOLOv8, exhibited robust predic­
tion capabilities with an R² score of 0.900 (as the benchmark; Fig. 2a, 
3a). In comparison, the foundation model specific for object counting, T- 
Rex, surpassed this performance (R² = 0.923; Fig. 2b, 3b), demon­
strating its superior predictive efficacy. The GPT-4 V recorded R² scores 
of 0.360 by just requesting to count the number of cherries (Fig. 2c, 3c). 
Yet, the performance was increased substantially by giving feedback by 
the user indicating under- or overestimation (R2 = 0.460; Fig. 2d, 3d). 
Both GPT-4 V approaches underestimated cherries, and they did not 
suggest numbers higher than 45 for any images, highlighting a limita­
tion in handling higher numbers, which was not observed with YOLOv8 
and T-Rex.
3.2. Time efficiency in object counting
The T-Rex model performed as the most time-efficient, completing 
the 100 images dataset analysis in approximately 0.83 h (Fig. 3). The 
GPT-4 V required a slightly extended period of 1.75 h (zero-shot) and 
3.25 h (few-shot) because of the restricted use (40 messages per 3-hour 
limit as of March 2024). The YOLOv8 required 161 h, including the 
annotation of 500 images with 30,000 cherries for preparing the training 
dataset, model training, and the administrative process for hiring 
personnel to do annotation.
4. Discussion
GPT-4 V and T-Rex have great potential, even though there are still 
some caveats and limitations in their applicability and efficiency. The T- 
Rex model, the foundation model for object counting without training, 
surprisingly outperformed the state-of-the-art YOLOv8 model with 
training. The finding is striking because employing a foundation model 
for object counting has not been mentioned in the latest review article 
about deep learning-based object counting methods in agricultural sci­
ence [14]. We imagine that the application of foundation models beyond 
object counting for various tasks could be a major approach in deep 
learning applications, including crop yield prediction [15–20], fruit 
health detection [21–23], immature/mature detection [24,25], and 
chemical usage planning [26], especially where traditional data labeling 
and processing methods are resource-intensive or difficult to implement. 
Also, no programming skill is required.
ChatGPT also revealed interesting potential, although the perfor­
mance was clearly worse than the T-Rex and YOLOv8 models. To our 
knowledge, this is the first attempt to estimate the ability of GPT-4 V for 
an object-counting task in a well-designed experiment. It is interesting to 
confirm that just offering prompt feedback if it was over-/under-esti­
mated can improve the model performance. Nevertheless, unlike the 
foundation model, securing reproducibility is a challenge when 
employing LLMs like GPT-4 V for academic research [27,28]. The 
inability to set a random seed or control certain parameters during the 
learning process, which is the scientific standard, could pose significant 
challenges in scenarios where consistent results are essential. This lim­
itation underscores the need for ongoing research and development to

address these methodological constraints. Yet, the speed of development 
is astonishingly fast, and therefore, this issue could be solved in the 
future.
In terms of scalability, YOLOv8 stands out as a winner due to its 
speed and lower energy demands once trained, especially as the model 
size is small [9]. This makes it a suitable choice for scenarios where rapid 
processing is needed, a large number of photos need to be processed, and 
low-energy consumption is critical (e.g., implementing the model in an 
edge device in a low-income country). However, when considering 
user-friendliness, the T-Rex model stands out. Its simple user interface 
and the ability to select specific object types make it an attractive option, 
especially in user-centric applications where simplicity is a key. More­
over, it is noted that there has been substantial progress in reducing the 
size of a large neural network model (e.g., BitNet [29]).
The domain-specific challenges in agricultural applications, as 
identified by [14], include target object variability, variations in illu­
mination, object occlusions, double counting, and distinguishing be­
tween background and foreground. Traditional deep learning models, 
while robust in structured environments, often falter when confronted 
with these agricultural-specific challenges due to their reliance on 
consistent, high-quality datasets. In contrast, foundation models like 
T-Rex exhibit a degree of adaptability to object variability and occlu­
sions, benefiting from a broader learning base. GPT-4 V, with its 
advanced understanding and contextual capabilities, could offer inno­
vative solutions to these problems, particularly in interpreting complex 
scenarios involving illumination and occlusions.
While some foundation models may encounter token or usage limi­
tations in certain applications, this study did not experience any token 
limitations with the T-Rex model during processing. However, with 
ChatGPT (GPT-4 V), we encountered a usage cap four times on both 
approaches, which temporarily limited the number of prompts we could 
submit in a given period. This usage cap indicates a potential constraint 
when using ChatGPT for high-frequency tasks. Nevertheless, this issue 
can be a matter of time because older models tend to be free of usage 
restrictions. Also, other open source models can be used alternatively 
when usage limitation is a bottleneck.
In conclusion, foundation models hold considerable promise for 
object-counting tasks, and multimodal LLMs have been showing some 
interesting potential, but their selection should be guided by specific 
requirements such as precision, scalability, energy cost, and user- 
friendliness. Task-specific models like YOLOv8 and foundation 
models, including T-Rex, offer advantages in terms of speed and preci­
sion, while general LLM models like GPT-4 provide ease of use and 
flexibility. The future of these technologies lies in their continued 
development and adaptation, making them more versatile and efficient 
across various applications.
5. Conclusion
This study demonstrates the significant potential of general-purpose 
AI models, such as ChatGPT and foundation models like T-Rex, for 
agricultural applications, particularly in fruit counting. Our findings 
highlight that foundation models can outperform task specific models 
like YOLOv8, by achieving higher accuracy with minimal training data 
and significantly reducing time and computational requirements. 
Furthermore, ChatGPT, despite its lower accuracy, shows promise in 
counting tasks when enhanced by user feedback. As this study was 
exploratory, these findings serve as an initial assessment and indicate 
the need for further validation through comprehensive testing. These 
advancements suggest that general-purpose AI tools can be valuable for 
farmers and agricultural professionals, offering scalable, accessible so­
lutions that require no programming skills. However, challenges such as 
reproducibility and scalability in specific contexts still require further 
research to maximize the full potential of these technologies for real- 
world agricultural use.
