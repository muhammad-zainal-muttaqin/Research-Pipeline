A B S T R A C T  
Accurate and early olive-fruit yield estimation is a greatly desired objective in oliviculture as a tool to increase 
profitability and sustainability of the exploitations. This paper presents the design and testing of a novel 
methodology that, unlike previous proposals using models fed with indirect variables, exploits computer vision 
techniques to estimate yield from visual features of existing visible fruit, as traditionally done by field experts. 
The design comprised a neural network fed with 16 descriptors, 8 calculated per canopy face, including the 
number of visible fruits (1 descriptor), the area of exposed fruit (1 descriptor), and other analytical descriptors 
aimed at mathematically modelling fruit dispersion (2 descriptors) and aggregation (4 descriptors). The meth­
odology was trained on a set of 37 sample points (74 images), and externally validated on a set of 10 (20 images), 
manually taken in a super-intensive olive orchard of the Picual Olea europaea L. variety located in Elvas 
(Portugal), two months prior to fruit harvesting. The total actual yield manually measured corresponding to the 
external validation set was 173.23kg, providing the methodology an estimated yield of 177.80kg, what implied a 
root-mean-square-error of 0.9914 kg per sample point, and an overestimation of 2.64%.   
1. Introduction 
The olive tree (Olea europaea L.) is one of the most iconic vegetal 
species in the Mediterranean basin, where its presence has influenced 
their populations for centuries on aspects such diverse as nutrition, 
religion, or even culture (Schicchi et al., 2021). Nowadays, the industrial 
exploitation of the olive tree is truly relevant for the region, as indicates 
the fact that more than 55 % of the olive fruits consumed worldwide in 
2018 were exclusively produced by Spain, Greece, Portugal, and Italy 
(FAOSTAT, 2018); only these four countries accumulate more than 44 % 
of the area harvested (FAOSTAT, 2018). 
Among the information inferable from olive orchards, the early and 
accurate yield estimation outstands in interest, as it would constitute a 
valuable supporting tool for the sector to increase profitability (Orlandi 
et al., 2010), by making oil transformation more efficient, by improving 
stock and human resources management (Aguilera and Ruiz-Valenzuela, 
2014), or by positively contributing to price stability (European Com­
mission: DG Agriculture and Rural Development (2011)). Productivity 
depends on a wide set of factors such as orchard category (Rallo et al., 
2013), seasonal bearing (Lavee et al, 2012), soil fertility, management 
practices, or climate and meteorology (Villalobos et al., 2006). 
Traditionally, olive orchard yield forecasting has been driven by 
expert farmers who conduct what is known as a field plot census. This 
approach relies on visually interpreting a limited number of plots to 
obtain an agronomic inventory of a given surface area, being this esti­
mation subsequently extrapolated. Fruit counting can complement this 
perceptual approach, but it is typically restricted to selected sampling 
units such as trees or sections of rows due to its labour-intensive nature. 
Different strategies can be followed, as described by Thompson (2012), 
to design this experimental frame with the sake of making it represen­
tative. Notwithstanding, manual yield estimations suffer from subjec­
tivity, high costs, and a higher margin of error (Anderson et al., 2021; 
Marani et al., 2021). Additionally, they are not exhaustive enough to 
account for natural variations among individual fruit trees or the het­
erogeneity present in large orchards, including differences in soil 
composition and other orchard parameters (L´opez-Granados et al., 
2004). 
In this context, precision agriculture is emerging as an alternative, 
proposing the use of GNSS positioning, advanced remote and proximal 
sensing, robotics, and artificial intelligence, to acquire objective and 
high accurate spatio-temporal information from crops aimed at creating 
technological solutions able to produce accurate yield estimations 
(Maheswari et al., 2021).

that vision systems are dominating most of recent investigations on 
orchard yield estimation solutions, especially when compared to those 
focused on estimation models fed with indirect information such as 
meteorological or pollination variables, among many other (He et al.,

able to mimic the ancient procedure carried out by farmers of estimating

of every type of fruit, the particularities of their visual exposure within 
the plant, and the significant differences in cultivation patterns. Actu­
ally, after a literature overview of deep learning applications for fruit 
detection and yield estimation, Koirala et al. (2019) encouraged authors 
to publicly share fruit-in-orchard image sets to favour further research 
and development. 
This paper describes the development and testing of a computer 
vision-based system designed to perform olive-tree yield estimations, 
within super-intensive olive orchards, by exploiting visual features 
exclusively extracted from RGB images taken from a lateral view. This 
study derives from that by Aquino et al. (2020), who presented a com­
puter vision solution for the identification and counting of olive fruits 
visible in RGB images of olive trees directly taken in the field. Thus, the 
estimation system presented here is composed by a neural network fed 
with 16 descriptors, 8 calculated per canopy face, including the number 
of visible fruits provided by the previously mentioned tool (Aquino 
et al., 2020), the area of exposed fruit computed by using a convolu­
tional neural network for semantic segmentation, and other analytical 
descriptors aimed at mathematically modelling fruit dispersion and 
aggregation. A number of studies exploring olive yield estimation using 
indirect variables have been presented over the last years (Maselli et al., 
2012; Sola-Guirado et al., 2012; Aguilera and Ruiz-Valenzuela, 2014; 
Ben-Dhiab et al., 2017, Candau-Fern´andez-Mensaque et al. (1998); 
Gal´an et al., 2008, Gonz´alez-Minero et al., 1998, Orlandi et el., 2010, 
Ribeiro et al., 2007, Ribeiro et al., 2017, Aguilera and Ruiz-Valenzuela 
(2019), and Stateras and Kalivas (2020)). Nevertheless, to the best of 
our knowledge, this is the first study exploring the hypothesis of viability 
of the use of computer vision to this effect, formulated on the basis of 
mimicking human field experts, which estimates yield by visually ana­
lysing the orchard. 
The rest of the paper is structured as follows: section 2 describes the 
experimental framework design and image acquisition; section 3 pre­
sents the computer vision system, describing the calculation of the 
image descriptors used to feed a neural network trained to derive yield 
estimations; section 4 analyses and discusses the obtained results; the 
paper ends developing the main conclusions of the study and outlining 
future work. 
2. Field experimental design, and image and reference data 
acquisition 
Experiments were carried out in a super-intensive olive orchard of 
the Picual Olea europaea L. variety located in Elvas (38◦49′19.1″N 
7◦07′57.2″W), region of Alentejo (Portugal). Orchard’s intra- and inter- 
row olive-tree spacing is around 1.5 m and 3.5 m, respectively, which 
configures a pattern of linear, continuous, and parallel tree rows 
delimited by accessible navigation paths. Canopy volume is limited in 
height (to around 2 m) and depth by periodical pruning (see the illus­
trating image contained in Fig. 1-(a)). This plantation scheme was 
selected as framework for this investigation since its adoption is rapidly 
increasing for being dramatically more productive than others (Bran­
quinho et al., 2021; Junta de Andalucía, 2019). Additionally, its struc­
tured design favours mechanisation and automation, which allows to 
greatly reduce agricultural practices costs (Lo Bianco et al., 2021), while 
also represents an optimum framework for the deployment of future 
ground robotic platforms implementing solutions such as the one 
described in this paper. 
A number 47 sample points were selected from the described plot 
(check black and white dots in Fig. 2), with 37 points consisting of a 
single tree, and the remaining 10 points comprising two consecutive 
olive trees positioned within a row; this selection allowed for obtaining a 
wider dispersion of data in terms of yield. Then, the two faces of every 
point were manually photographed from an inter-row lateral perspec­
tive, two months prior to fruit harvest, thus yielding an image set of 94 
images. Sample points were selected so as to be delimited by gaps on 
both sides caused by tree death (refer to Fig. 1-(b)). This selection cri­
terion ensured, at commercial harvest time, the accurate recording of 
the actual yield for the photographed sample points by manually col­
lecting, weighting, and registering fruit yield for every point, thus 
facilitating subsequent modelling. It is important to note that including 
sample points within continuous canopies could have led to potentially 
inaccurate reference data, as it is challenging to precisely determine, 
collect, and record fruits that strictly belong to the photographed canopy 
sections. 
Images were taken with a Sony α7II mirror-less RGB camera (Sony 
Corp., Tokyo, Japan), equipped with a Zeiss 24/70 mm lens (Carl Zeiss 
AG, Oberkochen, Germany). As in the study precursor to this one, image 
capture was performed at night-time with artificial illumination (consult 
motivation at Aquino et al., 2020); the Sony HVL-F32M external flash 
was employed for scene illumination in this case. The camera was 
configured in manual mode, setting focal aperture in f/6.3, shutter speed 
in 1/500 s, and ISO sensitivity in 640. Images were captured at a

3. Methodology for yield estimation in super-intensive olive 
orchards 
The proposed methodology has a neural network (NN) as core, which 
produces yield estimations by processing descriptors designed to extract 
from olive-tree images information about the amount of visible fruit, its 
dispersion and aggregation. Fig. 3 shows a conceptual diagram illus­
trating the methodology, which is described below. 
All the developments were implemented using the Matlab R2021b 
platform, including the Image Processing and Deep Learning toolboxes, 
release 2021b (The Math-Works Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA). 
3.1. Image preprocessing 
As it was previously described in section 2, trees delimited by gaps 
were photographed and selected as sample points to enable accurate 
yield registration for subsequent modelling (this situation was illus­
trated in Fig. 1-(b) and Fig. 2). As a consequence of this experimental 
design decision, it was necessary the computation of a region of interest 
(ROI) to be used as filtering mask to extract canopy pixels from the tree 
under study, thus discarding information from canopy segments 
belonging to other trees of the same row appearing in the image. Note 
that the calculation of a ROI in the practical application of this meth­
odology will not require filtering trees within the same row, so the 
investigation of a complex fully automated algorithm to this effect was 
out of the scope of this study, being the following semi-automated 
morphological approach (consult details about morphological opera­
tors used in this section in Soille (2004)) an optimum and operative cost- 
effective solution to complete the task.

3.2. Image descriptors calculation 
This processing stage is aimed at formalising a set of eight mathe­
matical descriptors modelling visual features influencing the observer’s 
perception about the amount of fruit contained in the trees, including 
the number of visible fruits, the area of exposed fruit, fruit aggregation, 
and fruit dispersion.

DeepLabV3 + is a semantic segmentation architecture composed of 
encoder and decoder modules. The encoder module is able to encode 
multi-scale contextual information, while the decoder is in charge of 
capturing sharper object boundaries by gradually recovering the spatial 
information. At the encoder module, the input image feeds a backbone 
convolutional network which is firstly in charge of computing a feature 
map, which is then inputted into an Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling 
(ASPP) module. The ASPP consists of a 1 × 1 convolution, three 3 × 3 
atrous convolutions with atrous rates 6, 12, and 18 (Chen et al. (2017)), 
and an image pooling layer. Thus, it probes the input features at multiple 
scales by applying atrous convolution with different rates of image-level 
features. As a result, the ASPP yields a set of derived feature maps, which 
are combined by concatenation, and then fused at the encoder’s output 
by applying a 1 × 1 convolution. At the decoder input, the feature map 
from the backbone net is fused by applying a 1 × 1 convolution, and then 
concatenated with the encoder’s output after bilinearly upsampling it 
for four times to match the spatial resolution of both sources. Finally, a 3 
× 3 convolution is used to refine the features followed by another simple 
bilinear four-times upsampling. InceptionResNetV2 is used as backbone 
network in this study, attending to its analysed performance compared 
to other architectures in terms of olive fruit characterisation, as it was 
studied in Aquino et al. (2020) and overviewed above. Fig. 5 illustrates 
the design of the described network.

used as input. Consequently, only a subset of I is analysed by the se­
mantic net, which contains the area of the image in which olive fruits are 
present.

3.3. Neural network design and training 
For a given sample point, the described set of 8 descriptors are 
calculated on the two images from both sides of the canopy, thus 
resulting in a set of 16 features. With the aim of developing a model with 
the ability of estimating yield from this information, a training set of 37 
sample points (74 images) covering the whole yield range was config­
ured, leaving a set of 10 sample points (20 images) with comparable 
statistical profile for subsequent external validation (check Table 1). 
Thus, the training set was used to feed and train an artificial neural 
network (NN), concretely a fully connected feedforward multilayer 
perceptron, structured in the following layers: 1) an input layer with 16 
neurons, which corresponds to the set of 8 descriptors calculated for 
both sides of the canopy of a sample point; 2) a hidden layer with 25 
neurons; 3) an output layer with a single neuron. The actual yield values 
per sample point, registered by individually collecting and weighting 
their production, were used as output for the net’s fitting, being the 
model trained using a leave-one-out-cross-validation (LOOCV) scheme. 
A rectified linear activation function was selected among other consid­
ered, as it was revealed to be the one favouring proper net’s fitting.

duction of 17.23 kg per tree. 
Regarding the relevance of the formulated descriptors in statistically 
explaining actual yield, the results of a correlation (R2) study and a 
principal components analysis (PCA) carried out on the training test is 
included in Table 3. According to the measured correlation values of the 
individual descriptors with the target variable, it can be concluded that 
none of them reaches to explain 40 % of its variability, what supports the 
choice of their combination. Additionally, the results of the PCA indicate 
that the principal component explains only 61.17% of the variability of 
the descriptor in the training set, being necessary to consider up to four 
components to reach the 98%. Moreover, the magnitude of the PC 
loadings of the four principal components for the eight descriptors, 
confirms their statistical relevance. These figures argue for their non- 
linear combination as it has been addressed in this work by imple­
menting a NN. 
5. Conclusions 
Typically, the scientific community has faced the investigation of 
technical solutions for olive yield estimation by exploring models built 
with indirect variables such as those meteorological, the amount of 
available pollen, or canopy characterisation, among other. This paper 
formulates and validates the hypothesis of using computer vision to 
estimate yield by mimicking the procedure followed by field experts, 
which relies in the observation and interpretation of visible fruit directly 
in the field. Indeed, the registered results fall within the expectations of 
the industry, which fixes the estimation target to fall in an interval of 
2–3 % for the case of fruit production tied to processing industries, such 
as the case of olive fruit. 
After this first work that has validated the initial hypothesis, there is 
still intense investigation to be conducted before being at the disposal of 
transferring a product to the industry. Indeed, the trained model has not 
been tested across different orchards or seasons. Therefore, factors such 
as canopy structure, leaf density, and other sources of uncertainty, must 
be thoroughly examined in future research to ensure its general appli­
cability. Furthermore, the current configuration of sample points, con­
sisting of one or two trees with missing neighbouring trees, although 
effective for creating an accurate reference dataset, may have an impact 
on the model’s direct applicability to long and continuous canopy rows.

The results presented in this paper provide strong justification for allo­

where entire rows will be considered as sampling points. Forthcoming 
initiatives will also be focused on fully automated image acquisition by 
using ground robots, and in widely increasing models’ base knowledge.

including: will the system need specific training to accurately perform
