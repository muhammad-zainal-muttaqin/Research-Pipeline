A B S T R A C T  
One of the applications of robotic farmer-assistant platforms equipped with machine vision systems is the 
evaluation of production yield before harvest without damaging the product. In this situation, farmers receive 
proper information for harvesting and post-harvesting management to decide about the required human re­
sources, harvesting equipment, storage space, transportation, and product marketing. In this study, a machine 
vision system on a tracked vehicle was designed and developed for yield estimation of kiwifruit by traveling 
along the kiwifruit trellis. Several features, i.e., intensity histogram, the histogram of oriented gradients, shape 
context, and local binary pattern, were extracted from the images captured from the plants, and the number of 
kiwifruits in the images was predicted using the support vector machine (SVM). To improve the performance of 
the SVM, its parameters were optimized using evolutionary optimization methods, namely, particle swarm 
optimization (PSO), ant colony optimization (ACO), differential evolution (DE), and genetic algorithm (GA). The 
performance of the proposed method was compared with several deep learning techniques. The R2 of predicting 
the number of kiwifruits in the images was obtained equal to 0.96, 0.91, 0.73, 0.83, 0.90, and 0.63 for the 
proposed method, FCN-8S, ZFNet, AlexNet, GoogleNet, and ResNet, respectively. Furthermore, the results 
showed that the SVM enhanced with PSO exerted the highest area under the precision-recall curve compared to 
the deep learning methods. The findings of this study can be useful for the proper implementation of precision 
agriculture and the management of agricultural inputs.   
1. Introduction 
Iran is the sixth main exporter of kiwifruit in the world, with a value 
of ca. 86 million USD in 2018 (FAO, 2019). Iran is now considered as one 
of the fastest-growing kiwifruit exporters since 2014, which means that 
kiwifruit yield estimation in farmlands of Iran can be a solution to gather 
proper information for harvesting and post-harvesting management for 
further decision-making on the required human resources, harvesting 
equipment, storage, transportation, and product marketing. 
So far, much research has been carried out on the machine-vision 
applications in crop yield estimation without physical contact in 
mechanized harvesting. Annamalai (2004), in a leading study, used a 
machine-vision system to detect citrus fruits in images and estimate 
citrus orchard yield. Arivazhagan et al. (2010) presented a method for 
detecting the fruits in images using color and textural features. They 
used the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) to determine image 
textural properties. The results showed that using textural properties 
resulted in at least 10% better performance than the color properties in 
fruit detection. 
Payne et al. (2013) presented an approach for counting mango fruits 
from daytime images of individual trees for vision-based estimation of 
mango crop yield. In their research, pixels were segmented into fruit and 
background pixels using color segmentation in the RGB and YCbCr color 
models and a texture segmentation based on adjacent pixel variability. 
They showed that the performance of the algorithm decreased as the 
number of fruits on the tree increased. Maldonado and Barbosa (2016) 
developed a method for feature extraction from the images from green 
fruits with the combination of the color model conversion, thresholding, 
histogram equalization, spatial filtering with Laplace and Sobel opera­
tors, and Gaussian blurring. They designed an algorithm to detect and 
count the fruits in the images, with a false-positives of 3% in images 
acquired in appropriate light conditions.

recognition in images for agricultural applications is based on

During the last five years, to overcome the shortcomings and present 
an efficient system for fruit detection in images captured in farmlands, 
researchers have widely used deep learning algorithms to detect agri­
cultural products in images (Zhang et al., 2018; Wan et al., 2018; Wan 
and Goudos, 2020). Recent progress in deep learning algorithms has 
shown the acceptable classification accuracy and robustness of these 
algorithms in different areas of science and technology (Zhang et al., 
2020). Today, the use of deep learning techniques on different plat­
forms, i.e., laptops, GPUs, and Raspberry Pies, has become very popular 
for the detection of a wide range of fruits and agricultural products in 
both harvesting (Altaheri et al., 2019) and yield estimation applications 
(Sa et al., 2016). 
Several efficient deep learning approaches such as FCN-8S (Williams 
et al., 2019), ZFNet (Fu et al., 2018), AlexNet (Kuang et al., 2019), and 
GoogleNet (Kuang et al., 2019), with approximate accuracies of 89, 92, 
99, and 99%, respectively, have been recently used for the detection of 
kiwifruits in images captured in natural field conditions. Although 
AlexNet and GoogleNet have resulted in very promising accuracies, they 
require very large training datasets (e.g., more than 40,000 training 
images) (Kuang et al., 2019) to perform well, which limits their appli­
cations in agriculture. Moreover, deep learning methods are black-box, 
and their performance can be remarkably affected by trapping in local 
minima. Furthermore, since these algorithms usually work based on 
image matching, their application is limited for use in nighttime con­
ditions, and the light should be controlled using an artificial lighting 
system (Williams et al., 2019). Besides, deep learning approaches 
require a trade-off between processing time and object detection rate. 
The faster the inference time, the lower the detection rate, and therefore, 
considering a compromise between desired running time and required 
detection rate is necessary. 
It seems that using a kernel-based machine such as the support vector 
machine (SVM) can be useful in the detection of kiwifruits in images 
captured by a robot for yield estimation of kiwifruit in orchards where 
the trellis system is used for growing the trees. A challenging task in the 
efficient application of SVM in image processing is the proper determi­
nation of SVM parameters. Therefore, evolutionary algorithms that their 
acceptable efficiency is proved in many optimization methods can be 
used to optimize the parameters of the machine. Therefore, the objec­
tives of this study were: (a) design and development of a robotic carrier 
for traveling between the rows in the kiwi farmland to capture required 
images for yield estimation, and (b) to introduce a robust image

2. Material and methods 
2.1. Robotic tracked framework 
The robotic tracked framework was obtained after several modifi­
cations. In this design, a safety factor of more than 2.5 was considered. 
SolidWorks 2012 (Dassault Syst`emes, France) was used to model the 
robotic framework (Fig. 1a). The rear wheels were considered driving, 
while the other wheels were driven. The main parts of the robot’s 
tracked system are shown in Fig. 1(b): 
Part (1): A St37-2G 05m steel sheet (DIN 1623T2) with a thickness of 
2 mm, which was the main chassis and other parts were mounted on it; 
Part (2): Bearings (UCFL204, HHB, China), which were installed to 
facilitate the rotational movement of the driving chain shaft; 
Part (3): The driving shaft, made of St50-2G steel (DIN 17 100) with a 
diameter of 20 mm, which transmitted the torque from the electro­
motors to the chain wheel; 
Part (4): The tracked propeller chain wheel (LS8cm, KBG, Iran), 
made of polyamide; 
Part (5): The fixed shaft for the driven chain wheels, made of St50-2G 
steel (DIN 17 100); 
Part (6): The driving chain wheel (LS8cm, KBG, Iran), made of 
polyamide; 
Part (7): The polyethylene column between the two steel sheets to 
keep their distance fixed. 
Table 1 shows the mechanical specifications of the robotic tracked 
system. Two DC gearbox electromotors (404.310, Buehler, Germany) 
were used to rotate the robot’s driving shafts independently. The 
maximum torque and rotational velocity of the motors at 24 V were 15 
kg-cm and 38 rpm, respectively. The location of the robot’s actuators is 
depicted in Fig. 2. Chain wheels were used to transmit power from the 
electromotors to the shafts, and the travel speed of the robot was 8.33 
cm/s. The maximum torque on each side of the tracked system was 45 
kg-cm. Fig. 3 shows a view of the constructed robot.

2.3. Machine vision unit 
2.3.1. Image acquisition 
A CCD camera (NT.6301/6, NEXT, China) was used for image 
acquisition. The camera had a sensor (1/3′′ Sony Digital CCD), video 
lens with autofocus, and PAL system to receive color images with a 
resolution of 512 × 576 pixels. An analog to digital converter (i-Tek USB 
Movie Editor, Dexatek, Taiwan) was used to transfer analog data of the 
camera to a laptop (F134FX, Sony, China) located on the robot (Fig. 7). 
All the images were recorded on the computer’s memory for image 
processing. The camera was positioned vertically (upward) to capture 
images from the kiwifruits hanging on tree clusters during the travel of 
the robot under the kiwi trees. 
2.3.2. Image processing 
After collecting all images from the orchard, an algorithm shown in 
Fig. 8 was used for counting the kiwifruits in the images. The algorithm 
was implemented using the MathWorks MATLAB R2010b programming 
environment installed on the computer located on the robot. According

to the figure, the algorithm consisted of several steps that are described 
below. 
Step 1. Converting the images to sub-images. The images were 
divided into 64 × 64-pixel sub-images for further analysis. Since the size 
of the images were 512 × 576 pixels, 72 sub-images were obtained from 
each image. 
Step 2. Image segmentation. A simple segmentation method based 
on RGB color thresholding was used to reduce the effects of the back­
ground. Firstly, image contrast was enhanced to sharpen the colors to 
make more difference between the objects and the background. In the 
next step, pixels with R, G, and B values in the range of those for kiwi­
fruit were extracted from the image. The values depend on the 
geographical location, weather conditions, and the amount of natural 
light during image acquisition. By an accurate selection of R, G, and B 
ranges in the image segmentation step, the algorithm can be applicable 
at the sunset and sunrise without the need for artificial lighting modules. 
Since in this study, the images were captured in a sunny day from 10 a. 
m. to 3p.m. in late October in the north of Iran, the R, G, and B ranges of 
the kiwifruits in an 8-bit image were selected equal to 80–255, 50–170, 
and 0–100, respectively. To keep the image segmentation as simple as 
possible, no morphological operators were performed on the images 
during the image segmentation. 
Step 3. Feature extraction. Several features, i.e., intensity histo­
gram (IH), the histogram of oriented gradients (HOG), shape context 
(SC), and local binary patterns (LBP), were extracted from the 
segmented sub-images. One of the limitations in the application of deep 
learning algorithms in the detection of objects in the images is that the 
type of features that can be extracted from the images are not completely 
controlled by the user. However, in the proposed method, features with 
different natures and vector sizes can be extracted for training the 
machine. 
IH is a graph of pixel intensity (on the x-axis) versus the number of 
pixels (on the y-axis) (Hafizah et al., 2012). The x-axis has all available 
gray levels, while the y-axis indicates the number of pixels that have a 
particular gray-level value. The image segmentation described in step 2 
lowers the computational complexity of extracting the IH features. 
The main thought behind the HOG features is that the local object 
appearance and shape within an image can be described by the distri­
bution of intensity gradients or edge directions (Liu et al., 2014). The 
image is divided into small connected regions called cells, and a histo­
gram of gradient directions is compiled for the pixels within each cell. 
HOG features are calculated based on the concatenation of these histo­
grams. To improve the performance of the system, the local histograms 
were contrast-normalized by calculating a measure of the intensity 
across the original 512 × 576-pixel image region of the image. This 
normalization leads to better invariance to changes in illumination and

The SC features involve shape similarity and the recovering of point 
correspondences. The basic idea is to pick n points on the contours of a 
shape. For each point pi on the shape, n −
1 vectors are obtained by 
connecting pi to all other points. Considering all these vectors is a rich 
description of the shape localized at a point but is far too detailed, which 
increases computational complexity and processing time. Therefore, for 
the point pi, the coarse histogram of the relative coordinates of the 
remaining n −
1 points is defined to be the SC feature of pi. 
LBP is a type of visual descriptor widely used for texture classifica­
tion in machine vision (Pan et al., 2017). It has been shown that when 
LBP is combined with the HOG descriptor, it improves the detection 
performance considerably (Ahonen et al., 2006). In this study, the LBP 
feature vectors were created for each sub-image during three stages. 
Firstly, each pixel was compared with each of its 8 neighbors according 
to a clockwise pattern. Where the center pixel’s gray-level value was 
greater than the neighbor’s value, 0 and otherwise, 1 was substituted to 
the pixel value to obtain an 8-digit binary value. Then, the histogram 
over each sub-image was computed based on the frequency of the 
occurrence of the 0–1 values. 
Step 4. Support vector regression. After extracting the features, 
they were used to train the SVM as an efficient supervised machine 
learning algorithm. Polynomial, Gaussian, and sigmoid kernels with 
kernel parameter of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 were used to implement the SVM for 
the prediction of the number of kiwifruits in the images. By default, in 
the SVM, the cost (C) and the parameter of the loss function (ε) are 
considered to be 1 and 0.1, respectively (Massah and Asefpour Vakilian, 
2019). However, in this study, several evolutionary optimization algo­
rithms, namely, particle swarm optimization (PSO), ant colony optimi­
zation (ACO), differential evolution (DE), and genetic algorithm (GA), 
were used to optimize the values of C and ε using the training data to 
improve the performance of the machine. Table 2 shows the parameters 
and specifications of the optimization methods during the training of the 
SVM. 
2.4. Performance evaluation of the robot 
For field testing, a kiwi orchard with T-shaped wingless scaffolds was 
selected in Babol, Mazandaran, Iran (36◦33′05′′ N, 52◦40′44′′ E). Plastic 
tubes of water were considered above the scaffolding for irrigation. 
There were no clutter and ridges in the orchard, and the floor was almost 
flat. The robot traveled parallel to the kiwifruit rows under the scaffolds 
(Fig. 9). The robot traveled along the rows for 1.45 m, and then, stopped 
for 1 s to capture the required image. The robot continued its traveling 
until the end of the last row (Fig. 10). The distance of 1.45 m was 
selected based on trial and error for the best coverage of the plant rows 
with minimum possible overlap among the images. 
A total of 100 images were obtained by the robot during its travel in 
the kiwi orchard. The images were then converted to sub-images for 
creating a database. The IH, HOG, SC, and LBP features were extracted 
from each sub-image. To complete the database, two labels were 
assigned to each sub-image. The first label was the number of kiwifruits 
in each sub-image, which was determined using visual observation by 
the human eye. A non-integer value, e.g., 0.5, was considered for the 
kiwifruits that the edges of the sub-images divided them into two pieces. 
The 5-fold cross-validation was used to train the SVM with the features 
and the label of the sub-images. The C and ε parameters of the SVM were 
optimized using the evolutionary optimization methods, where the 
objective function was to maximize the coefficient of determination (R2) 
of the prediction of the number of the kiwifruits in the training sub-

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Optimized structure of the SVM for the kiwifruit detection 
Table 3 shows the performance of the SVM enhanced with evolu­
tionary optimization methods in the prediction of the number of kiwi­
fruits in the sub-images. Since 100 images were captured in the kiwi 
orchard and each image was converted to 72 sub-images to form a 
database, the R2 values shown in Table 3 were obtained by comparing 
the number of observed and predicted kiwifruits in 7,200 sub-images. As 
shown in the table, the results are presented for three types of kernel 
function and three levels of kernel parameter. The C and ε parameters 
were optimized during machine learning using evolutionary algorithms. 
Table 3 shows that the value of R2 varied in the range of 0.75 to 0.94, 
which highlights the importance of selecting an appropriate kernel 
function, kernel parameter, and optimization method. The best perfor­
mance for the machine was obtained for the Gaussian function, the 
kernel parameter of 0.5, and the PSO optimization method. One of the 
reasons that the value of γ was not optimized by the optimization al­
gorithms was that it was observed that in some cases, the error rate in 
the algorithms that had to optimize the value of γ in addition to C and ε 
did not converge to a minimum error value and the algorithm could not 
provide an optimal value even after reaching the maximum number of 
iteration. 
Similar to the results obtained in this study, many studies have 
shown that the Gaussian kernel is a suitable function for the SVM 
regression (Xue et al., 2018; Asefpour Vakilian and Massah, 2018; 
Asefpour Vakilian, 2020). PSO, on the other hand, is an efficient opti­
mization method for machine learning algorithms. PSO has been widely 
used in optimizing the parameters of decision trees (Pashaei et al., 
2015), FIS (Fajri et al., 2017), ANFIS (Bagheri et al., 2014; Rini et al., 
2016), and so on.

of dynamic environments and lighting conditions. The FCN used in this 
study is an adapted form of the VGG-net16, presented by Simonyan and 
Zisserman (2014), called FCN-8S. Although several studies have shown 
the efficient performance of this type of deep learning method (Williams 
et al., 2019), the results of this study reveal the better performance of the 
SVM method. Although ResNet has been introduced in previous studies 
as a reliable and efficient learning algorithm (Brahimi et al., 2018), its 
results were not acceptable in this study. It seems that deeper learning 
algorithms require larger training datasets to perform well in classifi­
cation and regression problems. 
Furthermore, Table 4 shows the processing time required for the 
methods used in this study for counting the number of kiwifruits in an 
image using a code written in the MATLAB programming environment 
in the Microsoft Windows 7 operating system on a laptop (F134FX, Sony, 
China) with an Intel 2.8 GHz Core i7 processor and 4 GB of random- 
access memory (RAM). According to Table 4, the least processing time 
was obtained for the proposed method, while the highest processing 
time belonged to the GoogleNet algorithm. It was predictable since CNN 
methods include several neuron-based learning layers that increase the 
processing time compared to the kernel-based methods such as SVM. In 
contrast with CNNs, which are black-box methods, SVM contains a hy­
perplane that its equation is determined by the kernel’s type and 
parameter. The processing time in deep learning methods depends on 
the type of the objects that should be detected in an image, the type of 
the processor, the size and format of the image, and the image seg­
mentation method implemented in the algorithm. For example, previous 
studies have shown that the required time for kiwifruit detection in 
2352 × 1568-pixel images is ca. 0.270 and 0.350 s using the ZFNet and 
VGG16 methods on a computer with Xeon 3.6 GHz full-cash processor 
(Song et al., 2019). 
3.3. Precision-recall curves as the global performance of the methods 
One of the labels of each sub-image in the database was a binary 0 or 
1 value corresponding to the presence or absence of at least one kiwifruit 
in the sub-image. The SVM, along with deep learning methods, were 
used to predict either the presence or absence of kiwifruits in the images. 
Precision and recall parameters, which are widely used for the indica­
tion of the performance of the classification methods, were used in this 
study. The precision-recall curves of the implemented methods are 
depicted in Fig. 12. The area under the curves is equal to 0.967, 0.865, 
0.899, 0.902, 0.912, and 0.743 for the SVM enhanced with the PSO 
optimization method, FCN-8S, ZFNet, AlexNet, GoogleNet, and ResNet, 
respectively. 
According to the figure, the highest area was obtained for the pro­
posed methods, which indicates its efficient performance compared to 
the CNNs. Fig. 12 and Table 4 show that the proposed method for 
kiwifruit detection in images from field environment obtained by a 
regular low-cost CCD color camera located on a robot that travels in kiwi 
orchards can be used efficiently to perform higher-level tasks such as 
yield mapping and estimation. Under the natural lighting conditions, the 
overall recognition rate of the SVM enhanced with optimization 
methods was higher than that of the deep learning algorithms. The 
possible reason is that CNNs, due to their neuron-based architecture, can 
trap in local minima (Asefpour Vakilian and Massah, 2013; Sarlaki et al., 
2021), which can significantly affect their learning efficiency. Further­
more, sometimes, multi-layer learning algorithms only increase the 
computational complexity without any improvement in the prediction 
results, while an optimized hyperplane in the SVM can accurately model 
the data. 
4. Conclusions 
In this study, a tracked robot equipped with a machine vision system 
was designed and developed for yield estimation in kiwi orchards. The 
results of this study show that a robot that captures the images from the 
plant rows and uses the SVM to connect the IH, HOG, SC, and LBP 
features to the image labels, can be used efficiently for the kiwifruit 
estimation in the images. A comparison with conventional deep learning 
algorithms showed that the proposed method resulted in a better 
detection rate and lower processing time. The findings of this study show 
that a kernel-based method with parameters optimized by sophisticated 
evolutionary methods can generally perform better than neuron-based 
methods, even though these neurons are used in a multi-layer learning 
structure.
