A B S T R A C T  
This study looked at the development of six deep learning artificial neural network models for detecting ripeness 
stage in wild blueberries, along with developing models for yield estimation. The six networks used were 
YOLOv3, YOLOv3-SPP, YOLOv3-Tiny, YOLOv4, YOLOv4-Small and YOLOv4-Tiny. Both 3-class (green berries, 
red berries, blue berries) and 2-class (unripe berries, ripe berries) models were developed with YOLOv4 per­
forming the best with mean average precisions of 79.79% and 88.12% respectively. This result was further 
supported by YOLOv4 achieving the highest F1 score of 0.82. YOLOv4-Tiny performed the best from a 
computational load perspective having a mean inference time of 7.8 ms and a mean memory usage of 1.63 GB for 
single 1280 × 736 pixel images. Only minor differences in the accuracy of the nonlinear regression yield pre­
diction models were detected, with YOLOv4-Small performing the best with a mean absolute error of 24.1%. 
Despite this error, the results are encouraging, and this novel approach to yield estimation in wild blueberries 
will aid growers in making better, more localized, management decisions, improving yields and ultimately 
increasing profits by better understanding their fields ripening characteristics.   
1. Introduction 
Wild blueberries (Vaccinium angustifolium Ait.) are a perennial crop 
native to northeastern North America which are typically managed on a 
biennial cycle. The first year of development (sprout year) sees vigorous 
vegetative growth from spring until mid summer with wild blueberry 
plants reaching up to 30 cm in height [20]. Fruit buds develop 
throughout summer and early fall which is followed by the plant 
entering a dormant phase for over-wintering. Spring of the second year 
(crop year) sees far less vegetative growth with the plants focusing 
primarily on the development of the fruit. Fruit buds break in mid to late 
May and flowers emerge. Wild blueberries are typically pollinated by 
honey bees (Apis mellifera L.) which are placed in fields, although a 
number of native pollinators aid in the pollination process [15]. After 
pollination, fruit emerges as a small green berry. As the fruit ripen 
throughout the summer, the berries will transition from green to pink to 
red and finally to a blue or black color. Ripening characteristics are 
largely dependant on clonal variations within the wild blueberry species 
[2]. Ripe berries are then harvested in mid to late August through the 
middle of September. Following harvest, wild blueberry plants are 
pruned in late fall and the cycle is repeated. 
Determination of ripeness is a critical stage for any grower. Har­
vesting underripe berries reduces profit, as blueberries will not ripen 
after picking unlike other berries such as bananas and avocados [30]. 
This means that any berries harvested before they are fully ripe are not 
marketable and are removed as debris at the processing facility. Har­
vesting overripe berries increases the risk of damaging the softened 
berries. Further, leaving ripe berries in the field longer, increases the risk 
of predation from animals and increases the potential for weather 
related berry drop from wind and heavy rain. All of this in combination 
leads to overall yield and profit reduction. Peak ripeness for wild blue­
berries is typically only a few days to a week, making ripeness deter­
mination for any given field of high importance. Visual determination of 
ripeness can be a challenge, particularly if a grower has multiple fields 
they are trying to compare between.

chanically [16,51], there is a heavy reliance on wild blueberry har­

Harvesters will range in size depending on the tractor they are

wide and double headed configurations will be at least 6 m wide. This 
makes them too wide to safely drive between fields meaning, the heads 
must be removed after each field has been harvested. This is a time- 
consuming process and if a grower does not have an effective field 
plan in place, they can waste significant amounts of time transporting 
equipment. By better understanding the ripening characteristics of their 
individual fields, growers can better plan how best to move their 
equipment during the harvest season. This approach would result in 
more time harvesting berries at peak ripeness, fewer losses, and less time 
transporting equipment. 
On-vine yield determination is a further parameter which growers 
have expressed interest in. By attaining an estimate of yield in a 
particular field, growers can utilise tools such as the Wild Blueberry 
Management Tool [18] to better optimize their growing operation. 
Previously, growers were required to guess at what their yield might be 
based on previous seasons, however the development of an accurate 
yield estimator would eliminate the need for guessing. This would have 
the successive effect of allowing growers to make informed decisions on 
inputs and management techniques which will all serve to aid in 
maximizing profit [63]. had previously looked at wild blueberry yield 
estimation using blue pixel percentages from still images however, that 
study encountered issues with clonal variation where many ripe blue­
berries appear black in color (Zaman [62]). This meant that those berries 
were omitted from the yield determination leading to skewed results. 
Deep learning convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a form of 
image processing which provide highly accurate classification of images 
or objects within them [34]. YOLO (You Only Look Once) [44] is a 
popular object-detection CNN which has been cited in more than 9000 
publications indexed by Google Scholar1. YOLOv3 achieved a mean 
average precision (mAP50) score [19] of 57.9% [44] on the COCO 
dataset [36]. A smaller version of YOLOv3, YOLOv3-Tiny, processes 
images 6.3 times faster but only achieved a mAP50 score of 33.1% [43]. 
Huang and Wang [27] added spatial pyramid pooling (SPP) layers to 
YOLOv3. Their network, YOLOv3-SPP, achieved a mAP50 score of 60.6% 
on the COCO dataset in an evaluation by Bochkovskiy et al. [8]. YOLOv4 
[8] improved upon YOLOv3 through using cross-stage partial networks 
[55], achieving a mAP50 score of 65.7%. The authors included recom­
mendations for improving YOLOv4’s detection accuracy on small ob­
jects (https://github.com/AlexeyAB/darknet#how-to-improve-object- 
detection). YOLOv4-Tiny provides an 8.4 times speed increase over 
YOLOv4, while achieving a mAP50 score of 40.2% [7]. The input reso­
lution of YOLO CNNs ranges from 608 × 608 to 416 × 416 [8,44]. 
Increasing the processing resolution allows image features to be repre­
sented with more pixels, which can increase the accuracy of the network 
at the expense of speed [8,24,25,52]. 
CNNs have had success in a broad range of agricultural applications 
[31] such as weed detection [28,48,61], disease detection ([1,21]; A. 
[47]), tracking animal behaviour [56,59], and detecting fruit ripeness 
stages [22,54]. YOLOv3 was trained to detect three types of unwanted 
vegetation in 1280 × 720 images of plastic-covered, raised vegetable 
beds in Florida [49]. The CNN detected grasses, sedges, and broadleaves 
in the images with an overall F1-score of 0.95. Hennessy et al. [25] 
trained YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-Tiny to detect two weeds, hair fescue and 
sheep sorrel, in 1280 × 720 images of wild blueberry fields. The input 
resolution of the CNNs varied from 864 × 480 to 1280 × 736. Increasing 
the resolution resulted in the AP50 score for sheep sorrel detection 
improving from 42.06% to 60.18% with YOLOv3 and 39.40% to 54.02% 
with YOLOv3-Tiny. For hair fescue targets, which were generally larger 
in each image than sheep sorrel, the increased resolution had less effect. 
Changing the input resolution from 864 × 480 to 1280 × 736 improved 
the AP50 scores from 71.68% to 75.83% and 71.40% to 74.83% for 
YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-Tiny, respectively. YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-SPP 
were trained to detect debris, ripe berries, and green unripe berries in 
1280 × 720 images captured from a conveyor belt on a wild blueberry 
harvester [12]. The input resolution of the CNNs was set to 1280 × 736. 
YOLOv3-SPP produced AP50 scores of 79.18% and 74.90% for ripe 
berries and green berries respectively. Dataset augmentation involving 
modifying the sharpness, brightness, contrast, gamma, and saturation of 
the images improved the AP50 score for ripe berries to 83.58% but 
decreased the AP50 score for green berries to 72.82%. Augmentation 
improved results for YOLOv3, with AP50 scores for ripe and unripe green 
berries changing from 80.56% and 72.02% to 82.91% and 72.26%, 
respectively. Tian et al. [54] trained YOLOv3 to detect three apple

mann et al. [46] by expanding the list of tested models, evaluating both 
two and three class models, and developing regression equations which 
can be used for real time yield estimation. The results of this expansion 
are significantly improved models across all performance metrics and a 
yield estimation tool which growers can employ with confidence to 
augment their operations. A tool, such as the one discussed in this paper, 
will serve as a valuable asset for growers in helping to remove the 
guesswork from wild blueberry production cycle. This is a novel 
approach to estimating yield and ripeness in wild blueberries for an 
industry which until now, relies completely on visual estimation. 
2. Methods 
A flow chart outlining the methodology can be seen in Fig. 2. Further 
details on each of the components can be seen in the subsections of the 
methods. 
2.1. Data collection 
Data used in this study was collected across two different harvest 
seasons in 2018 and 2019. Due to a late frost in 2017, there was sig­
nificant wild blueberry yield reduction in 2018 [18]. The two-year 
production cycle meant that many fruit buds were damaged and could 
not develop into fruit in 2018. Therefore, yields in the second season 
(2019) were a more typical example of yields in central Nova Scotia. In 
each season, two different commercially managed fields receiving 
typical management inputs were used. Field A (4.02 ha; 45.425577◦N, 
-63.482175◦W) and Field B (4.57 ha; 45.443147◦N, -63.572210◦W) 
were used in 2018 while Field C (3.70 ha; 45.427235◦N, -63.481689◦
W) and Field D (6.48 ha; 45.482583◦N, -63.575851◦W) were used in 
2019. Across each of the four fields, 54 randomly selected points were 
used as experimental units. Fifty-four sampling points were chosen in 
order to best represent the significant clonal variation which exists 
throughout a typical wild blueberry field [3,14]. The initial point was 
selected by measuring in 35.35 m on a 45◦angle from one side of the 
field. Subsequent points were located 25 m from one another at 90◦
angles from one another moving into the expanse of the field. This was 
repeated until all 54 points had been placed. In effect, this created a grid 
pattern with 25 × 25 m squares with sampling points at each corner. 
0.5m x 0.5 m quadrats were placed at each sampling point for image and 
yield analyses (Fig. 3). 
Imaging of the quadrats was performed using a Canon Rebel T6 
(Canon Inc., Ota, Tokyo, Japan) at a resolution of 6000 × 4000 pixels. 
Images were taken between 07h00 and 12h00 ADT and were oriented 
perpendicular to the ground at a height of approximately 1 m. A tripod 
or other mount was not used for imaging as slight variations in image 
height and angle better match the expected conditions the CNN will be 
deployed in. 
In fields A and B, images were taken at four different dates of the 
same 0.5 × 0.5 m quadrat (Fig. 3). In fields C and D the quadrat was 
placed immediately adjacent to the original location in a randomly 
selected direction, either north, south, east or west. Once the quadrat 
had been moved and an image was taken, all the berries were harvested 
from within the quadrat using a hand rake. This approach maintained 
the randomization of the design while allowing for the plots to be har­
vested at each of the four dates. The imaging and harvesting dates for 
each of the fields can be observed in Table 1. 
Samples collected from fields C and D from within each of the 
quadrats were separated visually by color into green, red and blue dis­
tinctions. The berries were then weighed by color class for each of the 
sampling points. It was important to collect data across varying dates to 
attain images of the wild blueberries throughout their development. In 
this way, the models could be trained to identify berries at any stage in 
their development. 
2.2. Image analysis 
A total of 864 images were collected across all fields. Each of these 
6000 × 4000 pixel images were then cropped into 20, 1280 × 720 pixel 
images [12,25,49]. Cropping, rather than scaling the images to 1280 ×
720, allows each berry to be represented by more pixels in the image. 
This resulted in 17280 images, of which, 6766 were randomly selected 
for labeling. Wild blueberries within each of the images were labeled 
according to their color, either green, red or blue using a custom soft­
ware developed using the Lazarus compiler (https://www.lazarus-ide. 
org/). Of the 6766 labeled images, 4736 (70%) were used for training 
the YOLO models while 1015 (15%) were reserved for validating the 
models and a further 1015 (15%) were reserved for independent testing 
of the models. A second dataset was also generated where a python 
script was used to combine the red and green classes into a single class 
for comparison between ripe (blue berries) and unripe (red and green 
berries) berries. All other parameters were held constant. A second set of 
images was also collated which included each of the images taken from 
field C and D. Each of these images were cropped to the extent of the 
quadrat in the images (Fig. 4). These cropped images were used for 
correlating the ground truthed yield to the detections from the models. 
2.3. Convolutional neural networks 
As part of this study, six different networks from the YOLO family of 
networks were used to train the models. The first three networks were 
standard YOLOv3, the downscaled YOLOv3-Tiny [44], and YOLOv3-SPP 
[27], which adds spatial pyramid pooling layers. The newer YOLOv4 
and two variants, YOLOv4-Tiny and YOLOv4-Small [8], were also 
tested. As with v3, YOLOv4-Tiny is a downscaled version of YOLOv4. 
YOLOv4-Small is the full-sized YOLOv4 with modifications to some 
convolutional layers as prescribed by their original authors2. Each of the 
networks were trained using Darknet framework [7] and can be accessed 
at: https://github.com/AlexeyAB/darknet. 
Each of the models were trained using an Alienware Aurora R11 
desktop computer (Dell Inc., Round Rock, TX, USA) running the Win­
dows 10 Pro operating system (Microsoft Corp., Redmon, WA, USA). The 
computer contained an Intel i9-10900K central processing unit (CPU, 
Intel Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA), 128 GB of DDR4-3200 MHz random 
access memory (RAM), an Nvidia GeForce RTX 3090 24 GB graphics 
processing unit (GPU, Nvidia Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA), and a 2 TB 
NVMe SSD (Non-Volatile Memory Express Solid-State Drive). The Dar­
knet framework was compiled with Nvidia CUDA 11.3, cuDNN 8.2.0, 
and OpenCV 4.5.2 (The OpenCV Team) to train and test images with the 
GPU.

2.4. Yield estimation modelling

fields C and D were analyzed using the trained and validated 2-class

and unripe detections from each image, and these were used to develop

within the quadrat of each image (Fig. 4). A non-linear power concave 
model yielded the best fit for the data and was used to develop the 
regression models for each network. This was done following a cubic 
root transformation of the data to address concerns over the normality of 
the error terms. Other models including, linear, quadratic, cubic,

3. Results and discussion 
Each of the six YOLO networks were trained to convergence for both 
the 2-class and 3-class iterations. Average precision (AP50) and mean 
average precision (mAP50) of the trained, 3-class models when collected 
from an independent dataset can be observed in Table 2. 
In comparing the networks, YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-Small performed 
the best with mAP50 scores of 79.79% and 79.53% respectively. The 
smaller YOLOv4-Tiny also performed well given that the much 
computationally larger YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-SPP only surpassed it by 
approximately 3%. In looking at the reasoning behind the mAP50 scores, 
the lower AP50 score associated with the red berries across all networks 
seems to be reducing the quality of the models. This can be attributed to 
the high degree of variability which was observed between berries when 
they were deemed to be in their red stage. This variation is the result of 
clonal and temporal differences in the ripening characteristics of each 
wild blueberry plant [13]. Examples of this variation can be seen from 
images taken on July 31st 2019, in Fig. 5. It should also be noted that 
image labeling was performed by three separate individuals and while a 
common color scale was used, each of their own biases when labeling 
likely reduced the AP50 for each class. 
Due to the low AP50 experienced with the red berries it was hy­
pothesized that combining the red and green classes into a single class 
(unripe berries) would improve the quality of the models, while still 
addressing the overall goal of providing a percent ripeness 
determination. As both red and green berries are deemed to be unripe, 
the validity of training the 2-class models is justified. Results of these 2- 
class models can be observed in Table 3. 
In comparing the results of the 2-class and 3-class models there is a 
considerable improvement in the mAP50 with the 2-class models. On 
average there was a 9.73% increase in mAP50 across all 2-class models. 
Further, AP50 for the unripe class was superior to either the red or green 
class of the 3-class models when analyzed alone. This is an encouraging 
result, as it confirms the hypothesis that combining the red and green 
classes would result in a far stronger model. 
AP50 is not the only metric by which these models can be analyzed. 
Table 4 shows the precision, recall and F1-scores for each of the models 
for both the 2-class and 3-class variants. 
Across each of the YOLO networks the F1-score was superior in the 2- 
class models by an average of 6%. This further justifies the combination 
of the green and red classes into a single unripe class. 
Determining which model performed the best will depend on its 
implementation. In terms of mAP50 and F1-score, YOLOv4 trained with 
2-classes performed the best with a mAP50 of 88.12% and an F1-score of 
0.82. The 2-class YOLOv4 produced higher recall (0.86) than precision, 
(0.79), indicating that errors were more likely to be false positive de­
tections than false negatives. While YOLOv3 and YOLOv3-SPP also saw 
F1-scores of 0.82, their precision and recall were much closer to one 
another when compared with YOLOv4. Despite this, if computational 
load is a concern in one’s application, YOLOv4-Tiny could be justified as 
the best model. This is due to the comparatively lower inference time 
and memory usage. Inference time and memory usage for a single 1280 
× 736 pixel image can be seen in Table 5. 
When factoring in the memory usage and inference times, YOLOv4- 
Tiny can be justified as the best model. Despite YOLOv3-Tiny have the 
lowest memory usage, YOLOv4-Tiny only requires 0.16 GB more per 
image while drastically reducing the inference time by 12.3 ms per 
frame. When you factor in that the F1-score was only 4% lower than the 
full version of YOLOv4, YOLOv4-Tiny should be considered the optimal 
model if processing time and memory use are significant concerns. This 
result is similar to Hussain et al. [29] and Hennessy et al. [25], which 
both found that YOLOv3-Tiny was preferable to YOLOv3 due to 
increased processing speed and comparable accuracy on their respective 
datasets. 
The second part of this study was to develop a regression model 
which could be used for ripe yield estimation from a series of images. 
The strongest model of the data as determined by lack-of-fit and S value 
was a power concave model, using the number of ripe detections (x) to 
predict total ripe yield (y).Results of these power concave models for 
each network can be observed in Table 6. It should be noted that for each 
of the developed regression models the lack-of-fit was insignificant (p >
0.05). 
In comparing each of the models, there is very little difference be­
tween any of the S values. That said, YOLOv4-Small performed the best

within the wild blueberry industry. Principally, the development of a 
ripeness detection algorithm serves as a critical foundation for eventual 
deployment of on vine ripeness detection software. This resource will 
allow growers to properly estimate their yields prior to harvest and 
better manage agrochemical inputs to ensure maximum profitability. 
Proper yield estimation further increases the value of the already widely 
employed Wild Blueberry Management Tool, (Esau et al. [18]) as yield

estimation is the most important and traditionally most difficult 
parameter to approximate in profitability calculations. Beyond yield 
estimation, the development of the ripeness detection model will serve 
as a critical resource for the eventual automation of the wild blueberry 
harvester head. As DBE are currently aiming to fully automate the wild 
blueberry harvester (Esau et al. [17]), the developed ripeness model in 
combination with a 3D camera represents a promising solution for 
determining fruit zone and thereby, properly locating the head’s picking 
teeth, based off these two metrics. Further applications for the harvester 
include real time yield mapping as the operator is harvesting. In the 
short term, this will allow operators to better adjust harvester parame­
ters such as travel speed and head rotation speed to suit picking con­
ditions and in the long term, could be tied in with the system to 
automate the adjustment of these same parameters based on the yield 
prediction. Yield mapping during harvest would also provide the grower 
with site specific yield data which could be used to make better man­
agement decisions in subsequent years about areas within a field as 
opposed to the entire field as a single entity. Currently, the only yield 
data growers receive is a total yield measured by the receiving shed 
when berries are dropped off. This process typically takes place across 
several days and is averaged across an entire field or region. Being that 
clonal variations amongst wild blueberries can in some cases have more 
than a five-fold effect on yield [6,26,60], it does not make sense to 
manage one’s fields on a gross basis. A similar system could likewise be 
mounted onto a sprayer boom to provide yield estimation and mapping 
throughout the growing season, while the grower applies their tradi­
tional management strategies. Once again, this augments the amount of 
data in the grower’s hands and allows them to make more informed 
decisions pertaining to their operation. Moreover, the same sprayer 
setup could be used for in-season applications of herbicides which are 
known to damage fruit bearing wild blueberries such as SPARTAN® 
(tribenuron methyl) but have shown good control of problem weeds like 
bunch berry (Cornus canadensis) and bracken fern (Pteridium aquilinum) 
[40]. By controlling the spray based on the absence of berries, a grower 
could apply troublesome agrochemicals, without the fear of crop dam­
age. Beyond yield estimation and spraying, the system could be adapted 
to identify berries on the rear conveyor of the harvester and be used for 
color, size and shape exclusion reducing the amount of damaged or 
underripe berries making their way to the processor. Further, it could be 
combined with the work of Das  [12] to produce a holistic debris and 
damage exclusion system on the harvester’s rear conveyor. As 
improving fruit quality and reducing shrink are two of the biggest 
concerns for processors, this model could help to address these concerns 
in field. Finally, the developed models could have significant benefits for 
crop insurance personnel. Currently, wild blueberry crops are insured on 
a per acre basis and not on the yield of the field [42]. By incorporating an 
improved means of yield prediction, insurers could get a better idea of 
what is actually in a field and more accurately insure the crop in 
question. 
In the near future, the goals will be to develop a smart phone 
application which combines both the ripeness detection and yield esti­
mation models for in-field usage. Due to its comparatively high degree of 
accuracy coupled with its low memory usage and inference time, 
YOLOv4-Tiny represents the best option of network for this application. 
The application will also combine other networks developed by the 
authors of this paper such as [25] which looked at hair fescue (Festuca 
filiformis) and sheep sorrel (Rumex acetosella) detection in wild blueberry 
fields. The application should prove to be a comprehensive tool which 
growers can use to make informed management decisions on any 
number of wild blueberry production related queries. 
4. Conclusion 
This study was successful in training six different models for iden­
tifying ripeness stage in wild blueberry using the YOLO family of con­
volutional neural networks. From an accuracy and precision standpoint, 
YOLOv4 performed the best returning a mAP50 of 79.79% for the 3-class 
model (green berries, red berries, blue berries) and 88.12% for the 2- 
class model (ripe berries, unripe berries). When considering inference 
time and memory usage, YOLOv4-Tiny performed the best with a mean 
inference time of 7.8 ms and a mean memory usage of 1.63 GB for single 
1280 × 736 pixel images. As the goal of this study was to develop a 
model for an eventual smart phone app, YOLOv4-Tiny was deemed the 
best network as it only slightly compromised accuracy and precision for 
drastically improved memory usage and inference time. Regression 
equations were also developed for each of the 2-class models. These 
models saw little in the way of difference between one another with 
YOLOv4-Small performing the best with an absolute mean error of 
24.1%. While there is room for improvement with the regression 
equations, they do provide a reasonable estimate of yield for an industry 
which currently relies solely on visual observation. Potential improve­
ments could be achieved through examining the size of the bounding 
boxes or by detecting berries with an image segmentation CNN. In all, 
this study lays the groundwork for the development of a smartphone app 
which, will be able to provide growers real time ripeness detection and 
yield estimation and should prove to be a valuable tool for those who 
employ it for a variety of wild blueberry management practices.
