A B S T R A C T
In greenhouse tomato cultivation, fruit detection and counting are critical tasks for yield prediction and auto­
mated harvesting. However, actual greenhouse environments often present challenges such as illumination 
variations, dense leaf occlusion, and complex background interference. To address these issues, this study pro­
poses a dual-branch fusion detection model guided by high-frequency information (FGfuse-Net). The model 
introduces high frequency image information through Fourier transform and designs a frequency guided feature 
fusion module (FGFF) to effectively integrate spatial domain enhanced features, thereby improving the model's 
adaptability to illumination variations. Meanwhile, a multi-scale wavelet extractor (MSWE) module is introduced 
to enhance fine-grained texture representation in complex backgrounds via wavelet transform, thus improving 
detection performance for small and occluded fruits. Furthermore, a Unified-IoU loss function is adopted to 
optimize bounding box regression, alleviating the gradient imbalance problem in dense scenes and further 
enhancing detection stability. The model was trained and evaluated on a self-constructed greenhouse tomato 
dataset. Experimental results demonstrate that it achieves 96.4% mAP@0.5 and stable counting performance. 
Moreover, it maintains robust performance even under low light conditions, effectively addressing multiple 
detection challenges in complex greenhouse environments.
1. Introduction
Tomato, one of the world’s most important vegetable crops [1], 
continues to experience growing market demand, which has exacer­
bated the reliance on manual labor in greenhouse production, especially 
during harvesting. Under the long-cycle, high-density cultivation model 
[2], current manual-dependent fruit detection techniques are not only 
inefficient but also prone to misjudgment and missed detection due to 
factors such as worker fatigue and distraction. Faced with labor short­
ages and rising harvesting costs, automated detection has become an 
inevitable choice for the transformation of the tomato industry. With the 
continuous advancement of machine vision and artificial intelligence 
technologies, object detection has been deeply integrated into agricul­
tural production practices [3]. This technology can assist in monitoring 
the growth status of tomatoes and identifying the maturity stage, 
thereby providing crucial support for yield estimation, precise fertil­
ization, and automated harvesting. This, in turn, promotes the 
optimization of production methods and resource allocation, and en­
hances the sustainability of the agricultural system [4,5].
Traditional object detection algorithms typically rely on handcrafted 
features such as texture, shape and color, and combine these features 
with classifiers to perform detection and classification. Such approaches 
have been widely applied in fruit and vegetable detection, including 
apple [6], pear [7], tomato [8], peach [9], and grape [10]. Rakun et al. 
further integrated color, texture, and three dimensional morphological 
features to achieve fruit detection and counting [11]. However, models 
that rely solely on RGB information exhibit extremely low robustness, 
especially in low-light conditions. In such scenarios, the amount of color 
information captured by the camera significantly decreases, resulting in 
a decline in detection accuracy. Some researchers have addressed the 
detection challenges in low light conditions by combining different color 
spaces [12,13]. However, such traditional machine vision techniques 
rely heavily on experienced feature engineering and struggle to effec­
tively capture the subtle distinctions necessary for maturity

limited.
In contrast, deep learning methods leverage their powerful capability 
for automatic feature learning to extract discriminative features directly 
from data, thereby achieving higher accuracy and stronger robustness in 
object detection tasks [14]. These methods have now been widely 
adopted in the field of intelligent agricultural detection [15–17]. Zhao 
et al. developed an enhanced Faster-R-CNN framework for apple 
recognition. This model optimizes the Region Proposal Network struc­
ture and introduces two new windows, achieving an accuracy rate of 
89.10% [18]. Zhong et al. presented an improved Faster R-CNN algo­
rithm for detecting and localizing clustered pod peppers. Their method 
uses more high-resolution anchors, RoI Align instead of RoI Pooling, and 
ResNet+FPN as the feature extractor, achieving an AP@0.5 accuracy of 
87.30% [19].
The complexity of these two-stage architecture results in high 
computational latency, which fails to meet real-time detection re­
quirements. In response, single-stage models such as SSD [20,21] and 
the subsequent YOLO series have been proposed. Zhang et al. enhanced 
YOLOv5s with RepVGG and Focal EIoU loss function for simultaneous 
detection and segmentation of mango fruits and stems, with a recogni­
tion accuracy of 92.19% and a stem picking point success rate of 89.84% 
[22]. Wang et al. presents Jujube-YOLO, an enhanced YOLOv11 model 
for detecting and recognizing jujube fruits in complex environments. It 
integrates DCSE, RCM, and MBCA modules, achieving an mAP of 0.905 
[23]. Liu et al. introduced a multi-scale attention (EMA) module into the 
YOLOv8 backbone network to perform pixel-level attention allocation, 
enabling the detection of green plums in real orchard environments 
[24]. Qi et al. proposed a lightweight network combining YOLOv8 
detection and Deep SORT tracking, which can simultaneously achieve 
maturity assessment of cherry tomato clusters [25]. Based on YOLOv8, 
Fan et al. developed a novel, lightweight, and efficient algorithm for 
unripe pomegranate detection and counting. By employing GhostConv 
convolution for downsampling, their method mitigates the potential loss 
of detailed information during feature extraction, significantly 
improving detection speed while maintaining high accuracy [26].
When facing illumination variations, conventional object detection 
networks typically enhance model adaptability by increasing the di­
versity of training samples [27,28]. However, such approaches fail to 
fundamentally analyze the impact of illumination changes on image 
features. In fact, although images captured under different exposure 
conditions exhibit significant differences in brightness, they often retain 
similar structural information. Based on this observation, numerous 
studies have adopted multi-scale decomposition strategies [29,30] to 
progressively separate and reconstruct the coarse-scale luminance 
components and fine-scale structural information of images.
Nevertheless, the accurate detection of multi-stage tomato maturity 
still faces numerous challenges. From the perspective of color charac­
teristics, tomatoes at different maturity stages often exhibit a continuous 
spectral gradient, while the high color similarity between immature 
fruits and the background foliage can easily lead to model confusion. 
Under uneven illumination conditions, the edge regions of fruits are 
often cast in shadow, making their fine structures difficult to capture 
effectively. It is worth noting that the edge and texture information 
crucial for detection is typically embedded in the high-frequency com­
ponents of an image, which are relatively less affected by illumination 
variations. Consequently, frequency-domain correction has become a 
key approach for enhancing image quality [31–33]. For instance, Huang 
et al. analyzed the frequency characteristics of images captured under 
different exposure conditions and constructed a deep Fourier exposure 
correction network. By leveraging information interaction between the 
spatial and frequency domains, their method achieves collaborative 
reconstruction of brightness and structure, effectively enhancing 
underexposed images [34].
Such studies provide theoretical support for improving the quality of 
underexposed images; however, in the agricultural domain, there is a 
lack of research exploring their application in downstream detection 
tasks. Based on the observations and analyses above, this paper proposes 
a frequency-guided dual-branch fusion network for multi-stage maturity 
tomato detection. First, frequency analysis is employed to extract high- 
frequency information that is less affected by underexposure, which is 
then used to enhance spatial-domain image features. Second, a dual- 
branch network is constructed to simultaneously extract RGB features 
and frequency-enhanced features. Meanwhile, a dual-channel informa­
tion interaction module is designed to compensate for the degradation of 
subtle RGB features caused by insufficient illumination, thereby 
enhancing the model's capability to detect edge details. The main con­
tributions of this study are as follows: 
(1) A comprehensive tomato image dataset for complex greenhouse 
environments is constructed, covering diverse lighting condi­
tions, maturity stages and occlusion scenarios, which provides a 
rich and challenging sample set for tomato detection tasks.
(2) A multi-stage maturity tomato detection method based on fre­
quency guided feature fusion was proposed. Specifically, a dual- 
branch feature extraction architecture and an information fusion 
module are designed to enhance the model’s ability to charac­
terize subtle features, thus improving its discrimination of green 
tomatoes against similar backgrounds. A multi-scale wavelet 
extraction module is introduced to capture global contours and 
local details simultaneously via multi-band decomposition, 
which effectively alleviates blurred fruit edges and texture loss. 
The Unified-IoU loss function is adopted to tackle the gradient 
imbalance in dense fruit scenes, further boosting detection 
accuracy.
(3) The effectiveness of the proposed model was validated on the self- 
built tomato dataset. Experimental results show that the pro­
posed model significantly outperforms current mainstream object 
detection networks and can robustly handle challenges such as 
varying lighting conditions, complex backgrounds, and branch/ 
leaf occlusion. This study not only provides a technical reference 
for the design of multi-branch architectures in detection tasks but 
also offers empirical evidence for the application expansion of the

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials
2.1.1. Data acquisition and splitting
In this study, images of tomato fruits were collected in June 2024 
from the tomato greenhouse (166.98 E, 31.05 N, 16 m above sea level) at 
the Agricultural Science Research Center in Shucheng County, Anhui 
Province, China, as illustrated in Fig. 1. A manually operated remote 
control four-wheel differential speed intelligent vehicle was utilized for 
data collection; it was equipped with an Intel RealSense D455 camera 
and traveled at a constant speed of 1 m/s along the rows. The video data 
were recorded in real time on an onboard industrial computer. The 
camera was mounted on the upper cover of the chassis, oriented forward 
at a height of 0.75 m above ground level, with a shooting distance 
ranging from 0.1 to 1.5 m and an image resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels 
at a frame rate of 30 frames per second.
To take into account the variations in environmental lighting, we 
collected images of tomato fruits at different stages of maturity during 
different time periods. The collected images underwent normalization 
and cropping to achieve dimensions of 640 × 640 pixels each. Ulti­
mately, a total of 800 images were randomly divided into training set, 
validation set, and test set according to a ratio of 7:2:1 for model training 
and evaluation purposes.

To enhance the model’s generalization ability, we apply standard

2.2. Methods
2.2.1. Motivation and ideas
In complex greenhouse environments, traditional models struggle to 
distinguish unripe green tomatoes from leaves due to color overlap and 
similar low-frequency textures. This problem is aggravated under low- 
light conditions, where color and fine texture features degrade 
severely, causing frequent false positives and missed detections. To 
address this, we propose a frequency guided fusion mechanism based on 
Fourier transform and Gaussian high-pass filtering (see Fig. 3), and 
applied it in the preprocessing stage. Fourier transform decomposes 
images across all channels into illumination-sensitive low-frequency 
components (global structure, illumination trends) and illumination- 
stable high-frequency components (textures, contours), capturing the 
intrinsic spatial-domain-invisible differences between green tomatoes 
(dense texture, regular contours) and leaves (sparse veins, irregular 
edges). Gaussian high-pass filtering adaptively retains these high- 
frequency features and suppresses low-frequency illumination noise, 
with its function defined as:

related zero-frequency components; (2) Multiply the spectrum by 
G(u, v) to enhance high-frequency structural features and suppress low- 
frequency redundancy; (3) Apply inverse FFT to reconstruct a grayscale 
high-frequency feature map encoding intrinsic target information; (4) 
Normalize and fuse the high-frequency map with the original RGB image 
at a 1:1 ratio, forming a frequency guided spatial-domain enhanced 
representation. (5) Construct a dual-branch encoder network for feature 
extraction, and design a frequency guided feature fusion (FGFF) module 
to facilitate further information interaction, thereby generating fused 
features for downstream detection tasks.

2.2.5. The unified-IoU loss function
To improve bounding box regression accuracy for tomato detection 
and mitigate gradient imbalance in traditional Intersection over Union 
(IoU) for dense fruit distributions, the Unified-IoU loss function [38] is 
adopted as the regression loss. This approach shifts the model’s focus 
from low-quality to high-quality prediction boxes, optimizing detection 
accuracy.
Unified-IoU incorporates the Focal Box method to dynamically 
assign weights by scaling predicted and ground truth boxes, with the 
scaling ratio controlled by a hyperparameter ratio. Three adjustment 
strategies are defined: Linear descent, Cosine descent, and Fractional 
descent. In early training (ratio > 1), boxes are enlarged to prioritize 
low-quality predictions and accelerate convergence; as training pro­
ceeds, ratio is gradually reduced (ratio < 1) to focus on high-quality 
boxes. The hyperparameter ratio is initialized to 2 for early accuracy 
and set to 0.5 in later stages to emphasize high-quality predictions, ul­
timately enhancing overall detection quality. The relationships between

2.3. Experimental environments
To ensure the fairness of tomato detection experiments, all tests were 
conducted under a consistent hardware and software environment. The 
hardware configuration includes an AMD Ryzen 9 7940H processor 
(4.00 GHz, integrated with Radeon 780 M Graphics) and an NVIDIA 
GeForce RTX 4060 GPU with 16 GB of RAM. For the software, the ex­
periments were built on Python 3.8 and the PyTorch 2.2.0 deep learning 
framework, accelerated by CUDA 12.1, with PyCharm 2023 as the in­
tegrated development environment. Notably, the FGfuse-Net model does 
not utilize any pre-trained weights. The input image size is set to 640 ×
640 pixels, and the model is trained on a single GPU for 200 epochs with 
a batch size of 16 and 4 worker threads.

3. Experimental results
3.1. Data augmentation validity analysis
We evaluated the impact of data augmentation on model perfor­
mance by comparing mainstream models’ detection results on original 
and augmented datasets. As shown in Table 1, all compared models 
exhibit better performance on the augmented dataset. Specifically, 
FGfuse-Net achieves the most notable improvement: its mAP@0.5 and 
F1 score rise from 92.3% and 90.6% (original dataset) to 96.4% and 
94.5% (augmented dataset), with increments of 4.1% and 3.9%, 
respectively. And other models follow a similar trend. Data augmenta­
tion expands sample coverage, allowing models to better learn tomato 
features under diverse lighting and poses. Meanwhile, FGfuse-Net fully 
leverages edge details and diverse scene information in the augmented 
data, further enhancing the effect of data augmentation.

3.3. Hyperparametric experiment
3.3.1. Hyperparametric experiment with Kernel_Size
To evaluate the impact of MSWE’s Kernel_Size hyperparameter on 
detection performance, we set it to values ranging from 3 to 7. Table 3
results show that Kernel_Size = 5 achieves the best performance: Pre­
cision=94.4%, Recall=94.6%, mAP@0.5 = 96.4%, F1=94.5%. Smaller 
kernels have fewer parameters but limited receptive fields, hindering 
fruit contour/texture capture and reducing accuracy. At Kernel_Size=5, 
the wavelet basis covers more frequency bands, significantly enhancing 
fruit edge and occluded target discrimination and boosting detection 
accuracy. However, further increasing Kernel_Size to 6 or 7 causes 
mAP@0.5 to decline, due to two factors: (1) Overly large kernels 
introduce redundant high-frequency noise, weakening key features. (2) 
Increased parameters and computation raise overfitting risk on limited 
data, reducing generalization. Thus, we set Kernel_Size=5 in the MSWE 
module to optimize tomato detection.
3.3.2. Hyperparametric experiment with Transformer layers
To verify the impact of Transformer layer count in the FGFF module 
on model detection performance and computational complexity, ex­
periments were performed to identify the optimal layer configuration. 
With the MSWE module and Unified-IoU loss fixed, we only adjusted the 
stacked Transformer layers of the FGFF module, and tested the model’s 
core detection metrics and computational complexity under identical 
experimental settings. The results are summarized in Table 4. The 
number of Transformer layers significantly affects model performance: 
from 4 to 8 layers, detection performance improves gradually with a 
slight increase in parameters, verifying that the multi-layer Transformer 
can fully capture the long-range dependence of heterogeneous features 
in spatial and frequency domains. When layers increase from 8 to 10, 
accuracy plateaus or even slightly declines, accompanied by rising 
computation and parameter costs, resulting in feature extraction and 
computational redundancy. Considering the trade-off between detection 
accuracy and computational complexity, the 8-layer Transformer is 
determined as the optimal setting for the FGFF module.

FocalBox dynamic scaling and annealing ratio adjustment. This design 
shifts the model’s optimization focus from low-quality to high-quality

imbalance from dense tomato clusters and reducing overlapping sample 
interference. Compared to SIoU (optimizing gradient direction through

3.6. Improvement effect of the FGFF module on unripe tomato detection
Ablation experiments (Section 3.2) verify that the FGFF module 
remarkably enhances the model’s ability to distinguish green unripe and 
halfripe tomatoes from green leaves. Specifically, integrating the FGFF 
module leads to increases of 0.5% in Precision (P), 3.3% in Recall (R), 
1.7% in mAP@0.5, and 1.9% in F1 score. For tomatoes at different 
maturity stages, their AP@0.5 values rise by 1.0% for ripe ones, 1.7% for 
halfripe ones, and 2.3% for unripe ones, with the most prominent 
improvement observed in the detection of unripe and halfripe tomatoes. 
To visually demonstrate the module’s effect in reducing missed de­
tections caused by color similarity between green tomatoes and leaves, 
Fig. 12 presents detection results under complex occlusion scenarios 
(blue circles mark missed detection areas in the baseline). The results 
confirm that the FGFF module effectively mitigates missed detections, 
particularly those induced by the color confusion between green to­
matoes and leaves.

3.9. Generalizability experiments
To verify the robustness and adaptability of FGfuse-Net, experiments 
were conducted on five public datasets covering agricultural crops 
(PlantVillage-Grape, Fruits-360-Apple, ACFR Orchard Fruit-Mango) and 
general objects (Stanford Cars, CUB-200–2011-Birds), without addi­
tional data augmentation or preprocessing to simulate direct application 
in unfamiliar scenes, with mAP@0.5 as the core evaluation metric (re­
sults shown in Table 9). FGfuse-Net achieved stable and significant 
improvements over the baseline YOLOv8n across all datasets: for agri­
cultural crops, mAP@0.5 reached 93.4% (+3.2%) for Grape, 89.2% 
(+4.5%) for Apple, and 94.3% (+2.5%) for Mango, as the FGFF module 
enhanced target-background discrimination and the MSWE module’s 
multi-scale extraction adapted to crop size variations to offset cross- 
domain performance loss; for general objects, it achieved 87.1% 
(+5.6%) on Stanford Cars and 84.6% (+7.7%) on CUB-200–2011, with 
the larger gap for birds attributed to their small size, complex textures, 
and the model’s tomato-detection-focused training (inherent domain 
gap), while its consistent superiority over YOLOv8n confirms FGfuse- 
Net’s balanced design between specialization and generalization.

complex greenhouse environment. The model incorporates three syn­
ergistic innovations, the first of which is the FGFF module that effec­
tively integrates spatial domain enhanced features, thereby improving 
the model's adaptability to illumination variations and its ability to 
distinguish backgrounds. The MSWE module replaces traditional
