A B S T R A C T
In vertical farming, accurately and automatically estimating cherry tomato maturity remains an unsolved 
challenge with significant potential to enhance agricultural efficiency and crop quality. This study addresses the 
complexities of maturity estimation and occlusion detection in greenhouse environments, where variable lighting 
and frequent occlusions complicate visual assessments. This work presents a Lightweight Cherry Tomato Multi- 
Task Detection (CT-MTD) model, a deep learning-based multi-task framework designed to perform maturity 
estimation and occlusion detection. The model utilises a shared MobileVIT backbone for efficient feature 
extraction, with task-specific branches for regression-based maturity estimation and classification-based occlu­
sion detection. Coordinate Attention (CA) is integrated before each branch to capture spatial and channel-wise 
dependencies. To support precise data labelling, a custom annotation tool allowing horticultural experts to 
annotate maturity stages and occlusion statuses was developed. The experiments demonstrate that the CT-MTD 
model achieves a RMSE of 0.048 for maturity estimation, and Accuracy and F1 scores of 0.817 and 0.778 for 
occlusion detection, validating the effectiveness of the MobileVIT and CA combination for multi-task learning in 
precision agriculture. Finally, to validate the maturity estimation algorithm in inspection robots, a fruit counting 
method for tomatoes at different maturity levels was developed. The mean maturity prediction accuracy for 
tomato counting in two greenhouses was 86.3 % and 80.2 %, demonstrating the model’s robustness across 
different greenhouse conditions. This work can help greenhouse operators anticipate daily output, facilitating 
market forecasting and efficient logistics for transportation.

1. Introduction
Vertical farming is gaining momentum worldwide, particularly in 
China, where the construction of glass greenhouses has accelerated. In 
these controlled environments, crops like tomatoes are cultivated 
vertically, maximising space and enhancing yield. This farming model 
brings a pressing need to increase greenhouse automation, with robots 
playing a crucial role in daily inspection and harvesting tasks (Rong 
et al., 2024; Vasconez et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). The integration of 
robotic technology aims to improve operational efficiency, reduce la­
bour demands, and maintain consistent crop quality, positioning auto­
mated vertical farming as a promising solution for modern agriculture 
(Van Delden et al., 2021).
Accurate detection of fruit maturity is vital for automated harvesting 
and inspection (Tang et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022), 
enabling decisions about immediate picking or scheduling a future 
harvest (Jin & Han, 2024). However, assessing fruit maturity in cherry 
tomato farms is challenging due to varying lighting conditions and 
partial occlusions from stems and leaves (Dai et al., 2022; Mirbod et al., 
2023). 
Traditional 
machine 
vision 
techniques, 
ranging

colour-space analysis to spectral imaging, have shown promise but are 
often sensitive to environmental factors such as glare, occlusions, and 
complex greenhouse layouts (Feng et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2022; Surya 
Prabha & Satheesh Kumar, 2015). Moreover, these methods typically 
rely on manual feature extraction, which can be time-consuming and 
less adaptable to diverse fruit appearances (Deulkar & Barve, 2018; Liu 
et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2024). Recent years have seen a clear trend to­
ward using deep learning techniques to address the challenges of fruit 
maturity classification (Yang & Xu, 2021). Deep learning models can 
automatically learn complex feature patterns from large datasets, 
enabling them to adapt to variations in lighting, occlusions, and envi­
ronmental changes, which are particularly important for the dynamic 
and variable conditions in agricultural production. Miao et al. (2023)
proposed a maturity estimation algorithm for truss tomatoes that first 
uses YOLOv5 to detect tomato regions, then converts the detected areas 
into the HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) colour space to calculate the ratio 
of mature to immature regions. This ratio determines the initial maturity 
level, which is then adjusted by a compensation value to account for 
interference from green leaves and stems, enhancing the accuracy of the 
maturity assessment. Chen et al. (2022) presented a citrus fruit maturity 
detection method that combines visual saliency with a 4-channel 
ResNet34 model, leveraging RGB images and saliency maps to classify 
fruits into three maturity levels. The proposed approach, which in­
tegrates YOLOv5 for fruit detection and improved saliency detection for 
maturity assessment, achieved an accuracy of 95.07 %, outperforming 
traditional RGB-based CNN and machine learning models. Qi et al. 
(2024) employed a cascaded object detection model combined with a 
classifier, framing tomato maturity prediction as a classification task 
with five distinct maturity levels. Deep learning methods can effectively 
identify tomatoes within images, but reliance on manually defined 
thresholds leaves performance vulnerable to factors like occlusion and 
glare. This approach does not fully harness the ability of deep learning to 
extract complex features from large datasets. Moreover, deep 
learning-based classifiers face challenges in defining clear boundaries 
for tomatoes at critical maturity stages. It is difficult to set precise 
thresholds for distinguishing between ripe and unripe tomatoes, making 
intermediate samples prone to adding noise to the classification process.
In visual tasks of agricultural harvesting and inspection robots, fruit 
maturity is crucial for determining harvesting strategies. Accurate 
maturity estimation helps robots harvest crops at the optimal time. 
However, occlusion is also a concern. When fruit is partially or fully 
covered by leaves, stems, or other fruits, maturity estimates become less 
reliable, potentially leading to errors in decision-making (Sun et al., 
2024). Therefore, combining maturity estimation with occlusion anal­
ysis is essential. By incorporating occlusion assessment, the vision sys­
tem can determine fruit visibility, increasing the credibility of maturity 
estimations and enabling more precise harvesting strategies for the 
robot.
This study addresses the practical challenges associated with man­
aging cherry tomato cultivation in complex greenhouse environments, 
where dense foliage, varying planting densities, and unpredictable oc­
clusions from stems or leaves frequently hinder precise monitoring and 
harvesting decisions. In commercial tomato production, accurate 
maturity estimation is crucial for optimal harvest scheduling, yield 
management, and ensuring consistent fruit quality and economic 
returns. However, conventional maturity estimation approaches, which 
typically rely on discrete classification of maturity stages, inherently 
encounter several critical issues. First, by categorising maturity into 
independent and discrete classes, these methods disregard the natural 
continuous progression of fruit ripening, thereby failing to represent 
nuanced daily maturity variations. Second, tomatoes located near 
maturity boundaries frequently suffer misclassification due to subtle 
visual differences, which can significantly affect harvesting precision 
and downstream quality control. Third, these classification-based 
methods become especially unreliable under partial occlusion condi­
tions, common in dense greenhouse environments, as occlusion drasti­
cally reduces available visual cues, leading to ambiguous maturity 
judgments and increased error rates. Consequently, such inaccuracies 
often result in premature or delayed harvest decisions, negatively 
affecting fruit quality and profitability.
To better manage these practical biological challenges, this work 
proposed a multi-task deep learning model specifically designed to 
provide accurate, real-time maturity assessments and occlusion analyses 
under real greenhouse conditions. By framing maturity estimation as a 
continuous regression task, this model effectively detects incremental 
ripeness variations crucial for precise harvest timing. Concurrently, by 
explicitly detecting occlusions as a separate classification task, the 
model proposed in this work ensures robust performance even when 
fruits are partially obscured, which frequently occurs in densely culti­
vated greenhouses. The combined approach significantly enhances 
operational reliability in daily greenhouse management. Furthermore, 
the proposed method integrates seamlessly with a fruit-counting system, 
enabling growers to continuously track and estimate tomato yields 
across different maturity stages. This capability directly supports critical 
greenhouse management decisions, including dynamic harvest sched­
uling, labour resource allocation, and production planning. Overall, the 
approach proposed in this work helps to optimise biological crop man­
agement in commercial greenhouse environments, bridging the gap 
between advanced sensing technologies and real-world agricultural 
operations.

is combined with a fruit tracking and counting method to achieve

2.1. Data collection and annotation
2.1.1. Tomato data analysis and annotation tool design
In this study, cherry tomato maturity levels were annotated by hor­
ticultural experts (Tongqiang Chen), who classified tomatoes into six 
distinct maturity levels, as shown in Fig. 1. It should be noted that this 
classification is a company-specific standard based on anticipated har­
vest date, rather than an internationally recognised standard. Besides, 
field images of cherry tomatoes were collected, and each maturity level 
was marked accordingly. To facilitate maturity analysis, a specific re­
gion of each tomato was manually selected, and the average Hue value 
in the HSV colour space was calculated for that region. The HSV colour 
space was chosen because its Hue component better isolates colour in­
formation from brightness and saturation, providing a more robust 
representation of the continuous transition from green to red that typ­
ifies tomato ripening. In this study, average Hue values were derived 
using a custom Python-based annotation tool developed with the 
OpenCV library, which automates region selection, performs colour 
space conversion, and calculates statistical measures necessary for reli­
able maturity assessment.
The statistical results of the Hue values for tomatoes at different 
maturity stages, as annotated by horticultural experts, are presented in 
Table 1. The full growth cycle of cherry tomatoes can be divided into the 
germination stage, seedling stage, flowering and fruit setting stage, and 
fruiting stage. During the fruiting stage, the fruit begins to change 
colour, transitioning from green to red. In this study, we focused 
exclusively on tracking the maturity levels of tomatoes during the 
fruiting stage. For the ripening process from green to red, our horticul­
tural experts divided it into six stages (labelled #0 to #5). The experts 
collected tomatoes at various stages and categorised them accordingly. 
In addition, they annotated images captured by the camera, labelling 
each tomato with its respective maturity level. Once the classification 
was complete, we manually selected a region on the tomato surface and 
used a Python-based tool (incorporating OpenCV) to convert the region 
from RGB to HSV. We then extracted the Hue values of all pixels in that 
region and calculated the average, minimum, and maximum Hue for 
each fruit. About 25 tomatoes per category were analysed to gather 
robust statistics. Finally, the results were aggregated to form the 
category-specific Hue distributions. For fully mature tomatoes (stage 
#5), two distinct regions were selected to capture any colour variability 
across the fruit surface, providing a more comprehensive representation.
Table 2 presents the standard Hue ranges associated with different 
colour categories (red, orange, yellow, and green) in the 0–180 scale as 
used by OpenCV. These ranges serve as reference benchmarks for 
aligning our empirical maturity annotations with widely accepted 
colour intervals. To objectively correlate the empirical results from 
Table 1, which reflect expert-defined maturity classification, a 
comparative analysis was performed. Table 2 was primarily utilised to 
define and validate the Hue boundaries, specifically emphasising the 
clear distinction between fully mature (red) tomatoes and completely 
immature (green) tomatoes. Establishing these critical boundaries 
ensured precision in interval definitions, significantly enhancing the 
accuracy and robustness of the maturity classification framework pre­
sented in Table 3. By aligning the statistical Hue data obtained from 
annotated tomatoes with the standard Hue intervals shown in Table 2, a 
comprehensive mapping framework was developed, as summarised in 
Table 3. In this framework, each maturity level is associated with spe­
cific Hue intervals (defined by hmin and hmax) and assigned a corre­
sponding weight (ω) that indicates its relative position within the 
maturity continuum. The weights (ω) are designed to quantify the 
relative maturity level of tomatoes along a continuous scale ranging 
from 0.0 to 1.0. Each weight corresponds to a specific maturity stage, 
progressing from unripe to fully mature tomatoes. The rationale behind 
assigning these weights is to create a numerical representation of 
maturity that reflects the gradual ripening process. This structured 
approach provides a systematic basis for classifying tomato maturity 
levels, enabling consistent and reliable annotations.

2.1.2. Image acquisition and dataset creation
Tomato images were collected at the Ni-hui Agricultural Base in 
Suzhou, China from March to May 2023, and the Kaisheng Haofeng 
(Xiaogang) Smart Agriculture Industrial Park in Fengyang, China from 
May to July 2024. These farms utilise advanced greenhouse cultivation 
techniques, employing a high-wire system in which tomato vines are 
suspended vertically and guided along support wires. This method 
involves periodic adjustment of support ropes to accommodate plant 
growth and maintain the tomatoes at an ideal picking height during the 
ripening phase. Additionally, selective leaf pruning is conducted during 
the colour-changing stage. This practice reduces nutrient competition, 
allowing the tomatoes to ripen more uniformly, while also minimising 
pest and disease risk.
In this study, the Intel RealSense D435i camera and the Microsoft 
Azure Kinect DK camera served as the primary imaging equipment, 
capturing high-definition images at a resolution of 1920*1080 pixels.

2.2. Multi-task model for maturity estimation and occlusion judgement
2.2.1. Model design
To meet the requirements for estimating cherry tomato maturity and 
analysing occlusion, this work developed the Cherry Tomato Multi-Task 
Detection (CT-MTD) model, a deep learning-based multi-task model. 
This model simultaneously outputs both maturity estimation and oc­
clusion status by decoupling the regression and classification tasks. The 
detailed structure of the CT-MTD model is illustrated in Fig. 6.
The CT-MTD model proposed in this work utilises a lightweight 
MobileViT backbone for efficient feature extraction, while the multi-task 
framework is custom-designed for maturity estimation and occlusion 
status classification. The model structure is illustrated in Fig. 6. To 
ensure consistent and efficient feature extraction, all input images are 
standardised to a resolution of 224*224 pixels before being fed into the 
CT-MTD model. Although the model’s fully convolutional structure 
enables it to theoretically handle variable input resolutions, using a 
fixed size offers more stable performance and simplifies batching oper­
ations. Specifically, the preprocessing pipeline involves resizing images 
according to their longer side, zero-padding them to a 224*224 resolu­
tion, converting them into tensors, and applying normalisation. The 
input images are firstly processed through a series of convolutional 
layers, where an initial 3*3 convolution with down-sampling is applied, 
followed by a sequence of MobileViT blocks and mobile inverted 
bottleneck (MV2) layers to progressively reduce spatial dimensions 
while enhancing feature extraction capabilities (Mehta & Rastegari, 
2021). These layers form the shared backbone for both tasks, enabling 
the model to utilise common low-level features, which enhances the 
efficiency of the multi-task detection.
In the LCT-MTD model used in this work, the Coordinate Attention 
(CA) mechanism (Hou et al., 2021) is introduced before each 
task-specific branch, as shown in Fig. 6. The CA module selectively 
emphasises spatially important features by encoding position informa­
tion, making it particularly effective for complex greenhouse scenes 
with occlusions and overlapping foliage. After the CA mechanism, a 
Fusion Block is applied to integrate features and further optimise the 
information flow for each task.
Subsequently, a Fusion Block is applied after the CA mechanism to 
integrate and refine the extracted features, optimising the information 
flow for each individual task. Specifically, as shown clearly in Fig. 6
(expanded green box at the top), the Fusion Block consists of a 
sequential arrangement of a 3*3 convolution, Batch Normalisation, 
ReLU activation, Average Pooling, Flattening, Linear projection, fol­
lowed by another ReLU activation and Dropout layer. This structure 
effectively merges and enhances multi-scale feature representations, 
ensuring optimal task-specific feature refinement for both maturity 
estimation and occlusion detection branches.
For the maturity estimation task, formulated as a nonlinear regres­
sion problem, the Fusion Block is followed by a linear layer and a sig­
moid activation function. This structure outputs a continuous maturity 
score between 0 and 1, accurately representing the subtle progression of

2.2.2. Training strategy
This study employed a comprehensive training strategy to optimise 
the performance of our LCT-MTD model for estimating maturity and 
analysing occlusion of cherry tomatoes. The training process was 
carefully designed with data augmentation techniques, customised loss 
functions, and a learning rate schedule to ensure robust and efficient 
model convergence. The key components of our training strategy are 
described as follows.
Data pre-processing. In the data preprocessing pipeline executed, 
the augmentation techniques were carefully selected to avoid trans­
formations that might negatively impact maturity estimation and oc­
clusion analysis. The main data augmentation steps include: (1) Resize 
and Padding, (2) Horizontal Flip (50 % Probability), and (3) Random 
Rotation (±10 Degrees). These steps ensure sufficient variability for 
generalisation while preserving critical visual cues for accurate analysis.
Loss functions. Since the LCT-MTD model is a multi-task model 
designed to predict maturity and occlusion status, we used a combina­
tion of loss functions for these tasks: (1) For the maturity estimation task 
(regression loss), we used Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the loss func­
tion. This penalises the difference between the predicted maturity score 
and the ground truth. (2) For the occlusion detection task (classification 
loss), we employed Cross Entropy Loss, which is commonly used for 
binary classification. This calculates the negative log likelihood between 
the predicted occlusion probabilities and the true labels, improving the 
model’s ability to differentiate between occluded and non-occluded

2.3. Fruit counting method at different maturity levels
This section presents a tomato maturity-level-based counting method 
that integrates fruit tracking and our proposed maturity estimation 
framework. Accurate estimation of tomato maturity is particularly 
challenging under occluded conditions, where parts of the fruit are 
obscured by stems or other tomatoes. To address this, our method jointly 
predicts both maturity levels and occlusion states, allowing for more 
reliable updates of maturity estimations over multiple frames.
Accurate maturity estimation requires sufficient visual features of 
the tomato surface. As illustrated in Fig. 7, three different perspectives of 
the same cluster during robot movement were compared. In Fig. 7(a) 
and (b), where occlusion is minimal, the predicted maturity values (e.g., 
around 73.3 % and 72.7 %) closely match the manually annotated 
ground truths (78.9 % and 77.2 %). The small difference arises primarily 
from minor lighting variations. However, in Fig. 7(c), partial occlusion is 
present (some tomatoes are obscured by the plant stem), and the pre­
dicted maturity level (64.1 %) is lower than the ground truth (76.8 %). 
This discrepancy occurs because critical colour and textural features are 
hidden, making the model less confident in detecting full ripeness.

The EMA mechanism is applied to refine maturity estimates for each 
fruit instance over multiple frames. For a tracked fruit t, if it is not

smoothing factor (α = 0.5). Conversely, under occlusion (O=Yes), a 
smaller smoothing factor (α = 0.1) is used to minimise the impact of 
potentially unreliable estimates. Specifically, the maturity estimate Mt 
at the current frame is computed using the EMA: 
Mt = α⋅Mnew + (1 −α)⋅Mt−1
(3) 
where Mnew is the newly predicted maturity value obtained directly from 
the model at the current frame, Mt−1 denotes the previously calculated 
maturity estimate from the last frame, α is the smoothing factor, which 
determines the relative weighting between the current estimate and 
historical estimates.
The rationale for this EMA update rule is that a higher smoothing 
factor allows the model to respond promptly to reliable visual cues in the 
absence of occlusion, ensuring timely and accurate tracking of maturity 
changes. Conversely, when occlusion occurs, visual information may be 
incomplete or misleading; hence, we adopt a lower smoothing factor to 
prioritise historical stable estimates and mitigate sudden fluctuations in 
maturity predictions. This approach ensures more stable and accurate 
maturity tracking across frames, effectively leveraging temporal infor­
mation for improved robustness.
To provide a comprehensive maturity-level-based count, the matu­
rity estimation and occlusion-aware updates are integrated with a fruit 
tracking and counting framework (Rong et al., 2023). The tracking 
model (BYTETracker) assigns consistent IDs to individual fruits across 
frames, enabling the aggregation of maturity scores for each tracked 
fruit. In our system, the fruit detections are provided by a YOLOv5-4D 
model, a variant of YOLOv5 we previously designed and trained. After 
YOLOv5-4D detects the tomatoes and outputs their bounding boxes, 
BYTETracker uses these detections to create or maintain object tracks, 
ensuring each tomato is assigned a persistent ID across consecutive 
frames. By maintaining consistent IDs, we can aggregate each fruit’s 
maturity estimations which calculated by our multi-task model over 
multiple frames. This is especially valuable when fruits are partially 
occluded or move in and out of the camera’s field of view. For fruits 
located within the region of interest (ROI) defined for counting, their 
updated maturity levels are then categorised into predefined ranges, and 
each category’s count (or bin) is updated in real-time. This arrangement 
provides a continuous, frame-by-frame view of tomato distribution at 
various maturity stages, facilitating more accurate yield estimation and 
harvest scheduling.

2.4. Experiments and metrics
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Lightweight Cherry 
Tomato Multi-task Detection model (LCT-MTD) for maturity estimation 
and occlusion analysis, comprehensive experiments using different 
backbone networks were we conducted. All experiments, including 
model training and inference, were performed on a laptop equipped 
with an AMD Ryzen 5800H CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU. 
The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets with ratios of 
7:1.5:1.5. Each model was trained for 400 epochs. This evaluation 
included Accuracy, F1 score, RMSE, R2 score, and runtime efficiency. We 
experimented with various backbone networks, including ResNet34, 
ResNet50, MobileVIT-S, MobileNetV3, GhostNetV1, and ConvNextV1, 
while keeping other network components unchanged. During the 
training process, we saved model weights corresponding to the best 
accuracy (classification-optimised) and the best RMSE (regression- 
optimised) for each model. These two sets of weights were then used 
separately to evaluate model performance on the test dataset.

3.2. Ablation study
In this ablation study, experiments with five-fold cross-validation 
over 400 epochs were conducted to evaluate the impact of different 
attention mechanisms on our multi-task model’s performance. Each 
model performance was assessed using several key metrics, including 
Accuracy, F1 score, RMSE, R2 score, and inference speed. The best model 
weights were saved based on a combination of the highest F1 score and 
R2 score achieved on the validation set, ensuring an optimal balance 
between classification and regression performance.
Several attention modules were tested, including the Convolutional 
Block Attention Module (CBAM) (Woo et al., 2018), Efficient Channel 
Attention (ECA) (Wang et al., 2020, pp. 11534–11542), Coordinate 
Attention (CA) (Hou et al., 2021), and Attention Cross Module Mix 
(ACMix) (Cui et al., 2022). ECA focuses on channel-wise dependencies of 
feature maps, enhancing the importance of certain channels. CBAM, CA, 
and ACMix are hybrid mechanisms that combine both channel and 
spatial attention. CBAM sequentially applies channel and spatial atten­
tion, while CA combines channel attention with positional encoding, 
making it particularly suited for spatially complex tasks. ACMix also 
integrates both channel and spatial attention, allowing it to capture 
cross-modal dependencies within the feature space.
As shown in Table 7, the results of the ablation study demonstrate 
that incorporating attention mechanisms significantly enhances the 
performance of our multi-task model for both maturity estimation and 
occlusion detection. Compared to the baseline model without attention 
(Original), all attention modules resulted in improvements across key 
metrics, including accuracy, F1 score, RMSE, and R2 score. Among the 
attention mechanisms, CA achieved the best overall performance. It 
recorded the highest R2 score of 0.980 and the lowest RMSE of 0.048, 
indicating superior accuracy in maturity estimation. CA’s integration of 
channel attention with positional encoding enables it to effectively 
capture spatial relationships within the feature maps, making it partic­
ularly advantageous in the greenhouse environment, where spatial 
variations and occlusions are prevalent. Additionally, CA maintained a 
competitive inference speed of 2.1 ms, balancing accuracy with effi­
ciency, which is crucial for real-time applications. CBAM also demon­
strated strong performance, with an RMSE of 0.052 and an R2 score of 
0.975. By sequentially capturing both channel and spatial information, 
CBAM improved both accuracy and F1 scores over the baseline. How­
ever, it was slightly less effective than CA in addressing spatial 
complexity. ACMix and ECA yielded moderate improvements. ACMix, 
while effective in capturing cross-modal dependencies with its hybrid 
structure, had a relatively high inference time (3.2 ms) and slightly 
higher RMSE (0.060). ECA, a lightweight channel-only attention 
mechanism, offered a balanced trade-off between performance and

3.4. Discussion
This study proposed a multi-task deep learning model designed 
explicitly for maturity estimation and occlusion analysis of cherry to­
matoes in complex greenhouse environments. This approach represents 
a clear advancement over conventional discrete maturity classification 
methods commonly reported in prior works, as it systematically ad­
dresses inherent limitations associated with classification-based ap­
proaches. Specifically, by modelling maturity estimation as a continuous 
regression task, our method effectively captures subtle, incremental 
ripeness changes that traditional methods frequently miss due to rigid 
class boundaries. Furthermore, explicitly incorporating occlusion clas­
sification into the model directly addresses challenges frequently 
encountered in real greenhouse conditions, such as partial visibility and 
dense fruit distributions, thereby significantly enhancing the robustness 
of maturity predictions under realistic operating scenarios.
The results from the experiments highlight several important find­
ings and implications. Under non-occluded conditions, the proposed 
model achieved excellent performance, demonstrating high maturity 
prediction accuracy, as shown in Fig. 7. Specifically, the predicted

The integration of occlusion classification and maturity regression 
represents a significant practical advancement, enhancing model reli­
ability under realistic greenhouse scenarios. Nevertheless, the model’s 
reduced performance under occlusion conditions points to critical areas 
requiring further development. For instance, integrating occlusion 
scores directly into the maturity prediction confidence levels could 
dynamically adjust maturity estimation reliability, particularly down­
grading the confidence of heavily occluded fruit samples. Furthermore, 
multi-frame fusion methods present a promising strategy to mitigate the 
impacts of transient occlusions and lighting variations frequently 
encountered in greenhouse operations. Aggregating maturity and oc­
clusion predictions across multiple consecutive frames could substan­
tially improve prediction stability, reliability, and overall robustness.
Another important consideration raised during review was the 
choice between classification and regression for maturity estimation. 
While traditional classification methods are simpler and intuitive, they 
treat maturity classes as discrete and independent, overlooking the 
natural continuity and gradual transition between stages. This discrete 
treatment often leads to misclassification, especially for fruits at the 
maturity boundaries. In contrast, our continuous regression approach 
inherently reflects the biological reality of incremental ripeness pro­
gression, offering more precise and actionable information for green­
house management. However, future studies should quantitatively 
assess and compare the effectiveness of regression versus classification 
approaches through detailed ablation studies and possibly explore 
hybrid methods that leverage strengths of both paradigms.
Despite these advancements, the study has certain limitations that 
warrant further investigation. First, the experiments did not include 
tests under nighttime conditions, which are often essential for contin­
uous greenhouse monitoring. Future research should therefore focus on 
enabling robots to conduct nighttime inspections and update maturity 
data seamlessly, ensuring consistent prediction accuracy under varying 
illumination. Second, while our approach reliably estimates maturity 
levels and provides counts of tomatoes at different stages, it does not 
directly produce overall yield estimates. Instead, it represents an initial 
step toward automated yield management by collecting and aggregating 
maturity-level distributions. Achieving accurate yield forecasting will 
require integrating these maturity data with fruit weight models and 
historical growth patterns. Finally, the robustness and practical appli­
cability of our algorithm depend on large, diverse training sets that 
capture the full variability of real-world greenhouse conditions. 
Consequently, future work should emphasise collecting broader and 
more diverse datasets to further enhance model performance and 
generalisability.
In subsequent research, we envision closer integration of our multi- 
task maturity estimation approach with practical greenhouse manage­
ment applications. Specifically, inspection robots could autonomously 
and systematically gather tomato maturity distributions across different 
greenhouse zones, providing timely and precise fruit counts at various 
ripeness stages. To further optimise yields, these data should be com­
bined in a comprehensive decision-support model, incorporating 
maturity distributions, environmental parameters (e.g., temperature, 
humidity, CO2 concentration), and historical crop information. Such an 
integrated framework would enable accurate yield forecasts for an up­
coming week or even longer timeframes. For greenhouse managers, 
timely and precise yield predictions are invaluable, underpinning pro­
active decisions on harvest scheduling, labour allocation, and market 
logistics. Ultimately, this holistic approach could significantly improve 
supply chain efficiency, allowing supermarkets and distributors to better 
manage inventory, refine pre-sale estimates, and reduce waste­
—effectively translating accurate crop maturity estimation into tangible 
economic and operational benefits throughout the agricultural value 
chain.
4. Conclusion
This study presents the Lightweight Cherry Tomato Multi-Task 
Detection (CT-MTD) model, a deep learning-based approach for simul­
taneous maturity estimation and occlusion detection of cherry tomatoes 
in greenhouse environments. The model uses a shared MobileVIT 
backbone for efficient feature extraction, which is then divided into two 
branches for task-specific outputs. The first branch performs maturity 
estimation through a regression approach, while the second branch 
addresses occlusion detection as a classification problem. Coordinate 
Attention is added before each task-specific branch to capture spatial 
and channel-wise dependencies. To support accurate data annotation, 
we also developed a custom annotation tool that enables horticultural 
experts to label tomatoes across six maturity stages and assign occlusion 
statuses. A custom annotation tool was also developed to support ac­
curate data annotation, enabling horticultural experts to label tomatoes 
across six maturity stages and assign occlusion statuses. The tool cal­
culates Hue values for selected regions and automatically generates 
maturity levels. On the test set, the model achieved an RMSE of 0.048 for 
maturity estimation, along with Accuracy and F1 scores up to 0.817 and 
0.778, respectively, in occlusion detection. Our experiments validate the 
effectiveness of the combination of MobileVIT and Coordinate Attention 
(CA) for our multi-task network design. Future work could explore the 
fusion of near-infrared hyperspectral imaging with RGB image to 
improve the CT-MTD model’s robustness and versatility. Finally, we 
developed a fruit counting method for tomatoes at different maturity 
levels. The mean maturity prediction accuracy for tomato counting in 
two greenhouses was 86.3 % in a low-density setting and 80.2 % in a 
high-density setting, demonstrating the model’s robustness across 
different greenhouse conditions.
In conclusion, the CT-MTD model, along with the integrated fruit 
counting method, offers an effective solution for real-time monitoring of 
tomato maturity and yield estimation in greenhouse environments. 
Future work could explore the fusion of near-infrared hyperspectral 
imaging with RGB images to further improve the model’s robustness and 
versatility in complex greenhouse conditions.
