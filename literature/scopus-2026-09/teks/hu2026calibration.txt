A B S T R A C T
Accurate 3D localization remains a bottleneck for strawberry-harvesting robots due to occlusions, calibration 
errors, and ineffective multi-view fusion. This paper presents a comprehensive solution integrating a two-sided 
checkerboard calibration with residual modeling (TCC-RM) method for high-precision extrinsic calibration of 
dual RGB-D cameras and hand–eye systems, alongside a multi-RGB-D fusion pipeline for robust strawberry 
perception under occlusions. The TCC-RM method combined rigid initialization with sphere-based residual 
compensation to address nonlinear distortions. For multi-view perception, we introduced a modified 3D 
Intersection over Union (IoU) metric to enhance occlusion-resistant strawberry matching and compare the 
sequence of coordinate transform among multiple RGB-D frames and the base frame. Experimental results 
demonstrated that the TCC-RM reduced the calibration errors (RMSE) to 7.23 mm (dual-RGB-D) and 6.74 mm 
(hand–eye), achieving 49.23% and 69.3% improvements over the traditional TCC method, respectively. 
Compared with single-RGB-D configuration, the multi-RGB-D fusion reduced the Euclidean localization errors 
by 2.84%–39.21% and expanded the perception range by 42.86% (mild occlusion). The strawberry perception 
pipeline achieved RMSEs of 6.31 mm (mild occlusion) and 12.62 mm (moderate occlusion), representing 
minimum improvements of 11.7% over any single-view configuration. The framework, validated online on the 
HarvestFlex platform, achieved high-precision 3D localization in greenhouse environments, thereby enhancing 
the deployment readiness of strawberry-harvesting robots.
1. Introduction
As global agriculture advances toward intelligent and unmanned 
operations, fruit and vegetable harvesting robots have emerged as a 
key technology to improve efficiency and ensure food security. Straw-
berries, as delicate and high-value horticultural crops, are a major focus 
due to their short ripening period, limited shelf life, and susceptibility 
to damage (Parsa et al., 2023). Perceiving and localizing strawberries 
constitute the initial and most critical phases of automated harvesting. 
However, the complexity of the strawberry harvesting environment in-
troduces considerable challenges to the reliability and accuracy of these 
processes. Dense clustering, occlusions from leaves and neighboring 
fruits, and irregular structures hinder the stable 3D localization (Fu 
et al., 2020; Tituaña et al., 2024).
To address the challenge of occlusion in strawberry localization, 
researchers have explored a variety of strategies. Ge et al. combined

To achieve high-precision 3D localization of strawberry targets, 
multi-camera systems are widely adopted due to their complemen-
tary viewpoints. However, their performance largely depends on the 
accuracy of inter-camera extrinsic parameters, making calibration a 
central research focus. The classic checkerboard method (Zhang, 2000) 
is effective in static or close-range setups but relies on overlapping 
fields of view, limiting its use in wide-baseline systems. To address 
this, researchers have proposed dual-sphere targets (Wei et al., 2016) 
and auxiliary high-precision cameras (Sun et al., 2016), which im-
prove flexibility but increase complexity and cost. Optimization-based 
methods such as baseline correction (Darwish et al., 2019), multi-
temporal adjustment (Kumar et al., 2018), moving-sphere trajecto-
ries (Su et al., 2018), and robust circle detection (Kwon et al., 2019) 
enhance calibration under challenging conditions but often lack real-
time efficiency. To improve robustness and adaptability, Liu et al. 
(2022) introduced a circular calibration board with sub-pixel edge 
extraction. Yuan et al. (2025) proposed an evaluation strategy for non-
overlapping views based on residual analysis, while Xu et al. (2021) 
used person re-identification to establish correspondences across wide-
baseline views without dedicated targets. Hou et al. (2025) developed 
a hierarchical framework combining multiple modalities, though with 
high system complexity. In recent years, deep learning techniques 
have been widely applied in multi-camera system calibration. For 
instance, Duan et al. proposed a deep neural network-based method 
that achieves high-precision calibration using small calibration boards 
in large field-of-view setups, improving efficiency and accuracy (Duan 
et al., 2024). Additionally, Talakoob introduced a real-time neural 
network recalibration method for multi-camera systems, dynamically 
adjusting camera parameters in real-time based on changing poses, en-
suring system robustness (Talakoob, 2024). However, their reliance on 
substantial data and computational resources, coupled with challenges 
in interpretability and generalization, raises concerns about practical 
deployment in resource-limited settings.
Hand–eye calibration is essential for establishing spatial transfor-
mations between vision systems and robotic manipulators, enabling 
accurate execution based on visual perception. The classical AX=XB for-
mulation (Tsai et al., 1989) offers efficient computation but is sensitive 
to pose noise and large viewpoint changes. To improve robustness, Wu 
et al. (2023) used a single-sphere point cloud model combined with 
quaternion and Kronecker product methods to jointly solve rotation and 
translation, though performance depends heavily on point cloud qual-
ity. Shin et al. (2024) enhanced observability through pure rotational 
motions around orthogonal axes, requiring precise motion control. Cui 
et al. (2024) proposed a two-stage closed-form method with RANSAC 
and error refinement, achieving higher accuracy under noisy conditions 
but increasing computational burden. Wang et al. (2025) introduced 
pose graph optimization to jointly estimate transformations and system 
errors, improving global consistency but reducing real-time feasibility. 
Geometric constraint methods, such as those by Yang et al. (2022) 
and Zhong et al. (2025), simplify deployment but are limited by joint 
precision or depth perception. Recent intelligent approaches, including 
neural network modeling with evolutionary optimization (Zhu et al., 
2022), and target-free self-calibration via point cloud matching (Li 
et al., 2024b), improve autonomy but rely on high data quality. Multi-
sensor fusion strategies (Gautam et al., 2025) enhance adaptability 
but impose strict synchronization requirements. Recent advancements, 
such as the methods proposed by Valassakis et al. (2021) and Tang et al. 
(2025), have significantly improved calibration processes. Valassakis 
et al. explored deep learning for eye-in-hand camera calibration from 
a single image, showcasing the potential of direct regression models to 
outperform classical methods in noisy environments (Valassakis et al., 
2021). Tang et al. proposed Kalib, a markerless hand–eye calibration 
method that uses reference point tracking, forward kinematics, and 
a PnP solver to estimate the camera–robot transformation, offering a 
more user-friendly, low-effort solution for dynamic environments (Tang 
et al., 2025).
Despite recent progress, achieving reliable 3D localization in un-
structured field environments remains hindered by key challenges. 
Existing perception methods are sensitive to occlusion, multi-camera 
calibration under wide baselines or limited overlap faces a trade-
off between accuracy and efficiency, and hand–eye calibration often 
lacks robustness and deployability. These issues limit the effective 
transformation of visual data into precise robotic actions.
To address these limitations, this study developed a multi-view 3D 
localization system tailored for agricultural scenarios and makes two 
key contributions:
(1) A two-stage calibration method (TCC-RM) that combined rigid 
initialization with residual modeling was proposed to correct 
nonlinear distortions and significantly improve the calibration 
accuracy.
(2) The proposed multi-RGB-D fusion pipeline enhanced strawberry 
localization accuracy and expanded perception coverage through 
occlusion-aware matching and optimized coordinate transforma-
tions.
2. Methodology
2.1. Overall framework and hardware configuration of the strawberry har-
vesting system
This study focused on table-top grown strawberries and proposed 
a multi-view RGB-D vision-based robotic system for this task. The 
entire system architecture, illustrated in Fig. 1, consisted of three 
core functional modules—Perception, Processing, and Localization—
implemented on the newly developed robotic harvesting platform, 
HarvestFlex (https://xiong-lab.cn/), as shown in Fig. 2. HarvestFlex 
integrated a mobile base, vision system, robotic arm, and end-effector.
Perception Module: The vision system adopted a dual RGB-D 
camera setup (Intel RealSense D455F), symmetrically mounted on both 
sides of the mobile base to create a complementary field-of-view (FOV) 
configuration, with the robotic arm’s motion rail centrally positioned 
between the cameras. The cameras were optimally oriented at an 
outward-facing horizontal angle of approximately 110◦ and an upward 
tilt of about 55◦, effectively covering the robot’s operational workspace, 
typically ranging from 1.2 to 3 m. RGB and depth images were acquired 
at 640 × 480 pixels to balance detail and real-time performance.
Processing Module: This module performed dual-camera and hand–
eye calibrations to unify heterogeneous data from RGB images, depth 
maps, and robot poses. We propose a two-stage TCC-RM calibration 
(Section 2.2), combining checkerboard initialization with residual mod-
eling for high accuracy. This is followed by a dedicated coordinate 
transformation study (Section 2.3) that implements and compares CF 
and BF strategies.
Localization Module: The 3D bounding box of each strawberry 
was generated from 2D detections and depth information. A modified 
IoU metric refined cross-view matching, while a weighted multi-view 
fusion integrated complementary observations (Section 2.4). The final 
3D coordinates were transmitted to the robotic system for precise 
manipulation and harvesting.

2.2. TCC-RM: A two-stage calibration method
Camera calibration is essential for geometric consistency across 
sensors and the robotic arm. While traditional methods like Zhang’s 
and Tsai-Lenz provide global alignment, they fail to correct nonlinear 
errors, especially prominent in RGB-D cameras due to depth-dependent 
biases and edge distortions. Pure error compensation methods, though 
capable of modeling such biases, often lack generalizability without 
structural constraints.
To address these issues, we proposed a unified two-stage calibration 
framework, two-sided checkerboard calibration with residual modeling 
(TCC-RM) (Fig. 3). The first stage established rigid transformations for 
global consistency, while the second stage employed residual modeling 
to explicitly compensate for depth-related errors. This hybrid approach 
combined structural stability with precise bias correction, providing a 
millimeter-accurate foundation for multi-view fusion, as validated in 
Section 3.
2.2.1. Stage 1: Rigid initialization
The first stage of the TCC-RM framework aims to establish the initial 
extrinsic parameters. Two types of transformations are involved:
Dual-camera calibration employed a novel two-sided checkerboard 
design, enabling simultaneous feature capture from both cameras. This 
approach minimized occlusion and improved data collection efficiency. 
Using Zhang’s method (Zhang, 2000), the relative pose between the 
cameras was computed by leveraging the known rigid transforma-
tion between the checkerboard sides. To minimize calibration drift, 
multiple checkerboard poses covering diverse spatial configurations 
were sampled, and the pose with the minimum average re-projection 
error was selected to determine the final transformation matrix 𝐓. This 
matrix establishes the coordinate mapping foundation for subsequent 
sphere-based error modeling.
Hand–eye calibration adopted an eye-to-hand configuration. Using 
the Tsai-Lenz method (Tsai et al., 1989), the AX=XB problem was 
solved by combining robot end-effector poses with camera observa-
tions of the calibration target, yielding the initial camera-to-robot 
transformation.
While this stage provided a consistent geometric foundation, it 
remained susceptible to sensor noise, depth-dependent biases, and non-
linear distortions—limitations that were addressed in the subsequent 
residual modeling stage.

2.4. Strawberry matching and multi-view fusion
To integrate detections from dual RGB-D cameras, we developed a 
multi-view fusion strategy comprising detection alignment, matching, 
and weighted fusion. For enhanced robustness in occluded agricultural 
environments, we introduced: (i) a modified 3D IoU metric tolerant 
to partial occlusion, and (ii) a weighted fusion scheme incorporating 
projection area and depth reliability. These adaptations were essential 
for achieving stable and accurate multi-view localization in practice.
2.4.1. Strawberry matching
In the dual-RGB-D configuration, the same strawberry may be ob-
served from multiple viewpoints, resulting in multiple 3D boxes that 
provide complementary information of the same target. To integrate 
these observations, a cross-view matching process is applied, with 
candidate pairs filtered based on a center-distance threshold. For the 
remaining pairs, spatial overlap is evaluated using the modified 3D 
Intersection-over-Union (IoU), which is widely used as an evaluation 
metric in object detection (Yu et al., 2019), but here adapted as a 
geometric criterion for cross-view matching.
The traditional 3D IoU often produces low similarity scores under 
viewpoint changes or partial occlusion. To address this issue, a mod-
ified 3D IoU metric is used, which is more tolerant to occlusion and

2.4.3. Hybrid localization strategy for computational efficiency
The overall process, from recognition to fusion, is illustrated in 
Fig. 7. To maximize computational efficiency, a hybrid localization 
strategy was implemented based on the fixed geometric configuration 
of the dual cameras. Prior to operation, the non-overlapping regions 
of the two cameras’ fields of view were calculated. During runtime, 
strawberries detected within these pre-defined non-overlapping regions 
were directly localized using single-camera data, effectively bypassing 
computationally expensive cross-view matching. For detections in the

was used for matching. Successfully matched pairs were then processed

or accurate multi-view fusion—were transformed into the robot-base 
frame for picking. This strategy ensures optimal trade-off between 
computational speed and localization precision.

3.1. Calibration performance evaluation
To validate the effectiveness of the proposed TCC-RM calibration 
method, we conducted experiments for both dual-RGB-D camera extrin-
sic calibration and camera-to-robot-base (hand–eye) calibration. Fig. 8 
showed images of the checkerboard and calibration sphere from the 
two camera views. The effectiveness of the method was systematically 
evaluated at three levels: error statistics, sample trends, and error distri-
bution. Quantitative analysis was based on four standard error metrics: 
Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Standard 
Deviation (STD), and Maximum Error (MAX), where MAE and RMSE 
reflect overall error magnitude, while STD and MAX capture error 
dispersion and outlier behavior (Hodson, 2022). These metrics were 
applied to the Euclidean error in the estimated 3D position of a target 
point (e.g., calibration sphere center), as well as to its decomposition 
along the X, Y, and Z axes.
3.1.1. Dual-RGB-D calibration accuracy
For the dual-RGB-D calibration task reported in Table 2, the calibra-
tion error refers to the Euclidean distance between the sphere center 
position directly observed in the reference camera’s coordinate frame 
and the position transformed from the other camera, as well as its 
components along the X, Y, and Z axes. As quantified in Table 2, 
TCC-RM achieves substantial absolute reductions in all error metrics. 
Most critically, the Euclidean RMSE was reduced by 7.01 mm (from 
14.24 mm to 7.23 mm, a 49.2% reduction). A major improvement is 
seen in the Y-axis MAE, which saw an absolute decrease of 5.48 mm 
(from 7.97 mm to 2.49 mm). Significant absolute error reductions were 
also achieved on the X-axis (4.27 mm) and Z-axis (2.38 mm). These 
improvements, particularly on the Y-axis, are potentially linked to the 
effective compensation of systematic biases related to sensor placement 
and the camera’s field-of-view limitations.
Fig. 9 illustrates per-sample error trends. Post-compensation curves 
(red) exhibit enhanced stability across all axes with fewer outliers. Y-
axis and Euclidean errors cluster tightly near zero, indicating effective 
suppression of systematic bias. Fig. 10 provides statistical validation: 
Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) curves shift toward zero and narrow 
significantly after compensation. The Euclidean error mean decreased 
from 13.68 mm to 6.70 mm (51.0% reduction), with standard devia-
tion dropping from 4.06 mm to 2.79 mm (31.28% reduction). Y-axis 
improvements dominate, aligning with its MAE reduction.
Collectively, Table 2, Figs. 9, and 10 demonstrate TCC-RM’s efficacy 
in modeling directionally structured residuals—particularly for Y-axis 
correction. This validates its physical and geometric rationality while 
highlighting robustness in multi-camera calibration scenarios.
3.1.2. Hand–eye calibration accuracy
TCC-RM delivered even more pronounced improvements in hand–
eye calibration between Camera 1 and the robotic arm (Table 3, Fig. 
11), reducing errors to the millimeter level. For the hand–eye cali-
bration task reported in Table 3, the calibration error refers to the 
Euclidean distance between the sphere center’s position transformed 
from the camera to the robot base frame and its ground-truth position 
in the robot base frame, as well as the errors along the X, Y, and Z axes. 
The most dramatic absolute improvement was on the X-axis, where the 
MAE was cut by 16.51 mm (from 18.58 mm to 2.07 mm) and the stan-
dard deviation was reduced by 3.29 mm. This optimization stems from 
the effective modeling and compensation of major systematic biases, 
such as initial sensor misalignment. The Z-axis MAE was also substan-
tially reduced by 5.54 mm(65.1% reduction), effectively suppressing 
depth residuals caused by quantization noise and geometric distortion 
(Fig. 11). On the Y-axis, where the baseline MAE was already relatively 
low (5.43 mm), the absolute improvement was smaller (0.48 mm), yet 
the method still improved stability, as evidenced by a reduction in 
standard deviation (1.20 mm). It is noteworthy that the error for the 
TCC-RM method on the Y-axis is larger than that of the TCC method in 
some samples (Fig. 11). This is likely because the data used to build 
the compensation model had a relatively limited range of variation 
along the Y-axis, while the validation data exhibited greater variation in 
this axis. Nevertheless, the TCC-RM method demonstrates significantly 
higher overall robustness.
KDE-based error distributions (Fig. 12) further confirm: post-
compensation errors shifted closer to zero with tighter concentration. 
Euclidean error mean decreased from 21.42 mm to 6.50 mm, and STD 
dropped from 4.78 mm to 1.84 mm. The most pronounced KDE curve 
narrowing occurred for X-axis and Euclidean errors, demonstrating 
TCC-RM’s capability to compensate structured residuals and improve 
calibration robustness.
TCC-RM achieved Euclidean RMSEs of 7.23 mm (dual-RGB-D) and 
6.74 mm (hand–eye), meeting millimeter-level accuracy requirements 
for fruit localization. These results validate residual modeling as a prac-
tical and scalable error compensation strategy for agricultural vision 
systems.

3.2. Performance of modified IoU matching under occlusion
Robust matching across viewpoints under occlusion is critical for 
multi-view fusion. We evaluated the proposed modified IoU strategy 
against the traditional IoU in 21 multi-view strawberry samples exhibit-
ing mild occlusion. The IoU threshold was set to 0.4 for both strategies. 
The performance difference between the two strategies is systematically 
evaluated from three dimensions: Precision, Recall, and F1 score.
Table 4 summarizes the average performance comparison results 
of the two strategies. Traditional IoU has high precision (100%) but 
very low recall (19.75%), indicating that although this method has 
almost no false positives, it misses a large number of real strawberries, 
and the F1 score is low (32.99%), reflecting poor overall performance. 
Modified IoU has high precision (93.65%), recall significantly improved 
(72.84%), and a higher F1 score (81.94%), indicating that this method 
maintains a low false positive rate while reducing missed detections, 
with overall performance superior to traditional IoU.
Fig. 13 illustrates a sample-by-sample comparison of matched straw-
berries across the 21 test sets. The traditional IoU strategy could only 
match 1–2 targets in most samples, with significant missed detections. 
In contrast, the improved strategy matched over 4 strawberries in sev-
eral samples, closer to the true count, demonstrating stronger matching 
ability. In typical scenes like samples 4, 5, and 7, the improved strat-
egy perfectly matched all true targets, showing higher accuracy and 
robustness.
The modified IoU strategy, leveraging minimum volume normal-
ization, substantially enhanced the robustness of instance association 
under occlusion, reducing missed matches by 53.1% (Recall increase) 
and improving overall matching quality (F1-score increased 48.95%). 
This is vital for reliable multi-view data fusion.

BF achieves the highest overall 3D accuracy, attaining the lowest Eu-

the maximum Euclidean error by 36.3% (from 14.73 mm to 9.38 mm) 
in unoccluded scenes and retained a 10.5% RMSE advantage over C1-

under challenging conditions is further illustrated in the occlusion 
error distributions (Fig. 16). Whereas single-camera baselines exhibit 
broad, skewed curves with pronounced tails, CF and BF both yield 
markedly sharper and more concentrated distributions—confirming 
their enhanced reliability.
Furthermore, we evaluated the localization performance for straw-
berries under moderate occlusion in a denser field environment. In this 
scenario, instead of fixing strawberries to the fixture, the tip of the 
metal pin was positioned directly beneath the center of each occluded 
strawberry to establish ground truth, and the localization error was 
calculated against the visual prediction. Fig. 17 shows the front view of 
the strawberry scene along with the corresponding images from Camera 
1 and Camera 2. Quantitative results (as shown in Table 6) reveal 
that the BF method achieved the lowest overall RMSE (12.62 mm), 
demonstrating the best precision in Euclidean localization under oc-
clusion. The CF method followed closely with an RMSE of 13.65 mm, 
and notably achieved the lowest Euclidean MAE (12.01 mm), indicating 
high accuracy. In contrast, the C2-C1-B method exhibited the largest 
errors across almost all metrics, including the highest Euclidean MAE 
(18.38 mm), MAX error (34.85 mm), and RMSE (25.37 mm), highlight-
ing its limitations in handling occlusions in dense environments. The 
single-camera methods (C1-B, C2-B) showed intermediate performance, 
with C1-B yielding a lower Euclidean MAE than C2-B (13.34 mm vs. 
13.79 mm), but a comparable RMSE (14.32 mm vs. 14.72 mm).
As occlusion grew from slight to moderate, all methods showed 
a corresponding increase in localization error. The root cause was 
two-fold: structured-light cameras provided fewer valid depth points 
for occluded targets and suffered from point cloud adhesion in dense 
scenes. Under mild occlusion, enough strawberry pixels remained for a 
basic 3D approximation. However, moderate occlusion reduced usable 
data and introduced depth errors that skewed calculations. The incom-
plete data also impaired filtering, often discarding good points with 
the noise (Ge et al., 2023). These issues—combined with point cloud 
adhesion and the flattening of occluded shapes (Zhang, 2018)—confirm 
the problem lay in the camera’s perceptual limits and algorithmic 
shortcomings, not in calibration. Thus, the resulting 3D bounding boxes 
were inaccurate, increasing localization error.
Despite this overall performance degradation, the proposed fusion 
strategies maintained their relative advantage. The BF method achieved 
the lowest overall RMSE (12.62 mm), while the CF method also 
demonstrated competitive performance with a lower Euclidean MAE 
(12.01 mm) than BF (13.00 mm). These results confirm that both 
fusion frameworks offer superior robustness compared to single-view 
baselines under challenging occlusion conditions. It should be clarified 
that our study also attempted to evaluate performance under heavy 
occlusion; however, the current perception model failed to detect 
strawberries in such scenarios, preventing a quantitative localization 
analysis. Therefore, the discussion and results presented here are appli-
cable to conditions ranging from unoccluded to moderately occluded. 
The reported localization errors represent the cumulative effect of 
errors from perception, calibration, robotic arm motion, and ground-
truth measurement. Meanwhile, the full pipeline—from perception and 
multi-target matching to base-frame transformation—was executed at 
an average of 250 ms per frame.

•  B: Number of strawberries detected by the right camera (Cam2).

Fused = A + B - C. Using the maximum detection count from a single

This metric measured the net increase in total detected targets by 
the multi-view system, excluding redundancy caused by overlap, and 
can more objectively reflect the completion capability of the perception 
range. No false positives were encountered in this experiment. How-
ever, missed detections did occur for smaller strawberries, strawberries 
under very strong lighting, and those with severe occlusion. Missed 
detections were not considered during the experiment.
As summarized in Table 7, the multi-view RGB-D fusion system 
outperformed the single-view baseline, with an average coverage gain 
of 42.86%. Notably, 85.7% of samples achieved gains above 30%. Each 
sample yielded an average of 2.33 matched pairs, indicating moderate 
overlap, while 80.4% of detections came from non-overlapping views, 
highlighting bilateral complementarity. Some samples (e.g., Sample

due to asymmetric camera mounting or scene-specific occlusion. Such 
variations are common in field settings and underscore the benefit of 
multi-view fusion in mitigating single-view bias.
Overall, the dual-RGB-D setup expands perception range and im-
proves robustness by reducing occlusion-related blind spots.
3.5. Discussions
This study systematically evaluated the performance of the TCC-
RM calibration method and the multi-view perception system. Our 
TCC-RM method demonstrated superior hand–eye calibration accu-
racy compared to the deep learning-based Kalib method, even when 
operating under an inherent disadvantage. Using an external configu-
ration, we achieved errors of 2.07/4.95 mm (X/Y), which outperforms 
Kalib’s (Tang et al., 2025) reported errors of 4.8 mm (eye-to-hand) 
and 3.0 mm (eye-in-hand). This is significant because the external 
configuration is theoretically less precise than the setups Kalib employs, 
Computers and Electronics in Agriculture 241 (2026) 111221 
13 

S. Hu et al.
making our method’s higher observed accuracy a strong testament to 
its robustness. Furthermore, the illumination sensitivity of the sphere-
based residual modeling was addressed by performing this step as 
a one-time calibration under controlled indoor conditions, ensuring 
long-term parameter stability during field operation.
In terms of occlusion robustness, the upward-looking side-view 
camera configuration was employed to circumvent frontal occlusion 
from leaves and better observe fruits within the canopy. A characteristic 
of this perspective is the convergence of spatial occlusions (where lower 
fruits block higher ones) and projection overlaps in the 2D image plane. 
The multi-view fusion strategy effectively addressed this by integrating 
geometric information from disparate viewpoints to reconstruct an 
accurate 3D scene. Consequently, the system significantly enhanced lo-
calization stability from unoccluded to moderately occluded conditions. 
Nonetheless, perception under heavy occlusion remains a fundamental 
challenge.
The high-precision localization, quantified under static conditions, 
provides a reliable input for the current open-loop system, where the 
250 ms processing time is viable within the 8-second cycle. However, 
transitioning to dynamic closed-loop harvesting introduces system-level 
challenges, as final success depends not only on perception accuracy 
but also on robot control and motion planning. Thus, this work es-
tablishes a precise perceptual foundation, while identifying dynamic 
tracking and extreme occlusion handling as key targets for future 
development.
4. Conclusion
This study established a high-precision, multi-view RGB-D percep-
tion system for strawberry harvesting, delivering a comprehensive 
solution from calibration to occlusion-aware fusion. The cornerstone 
of this system was the TCC-RM calibration method, which reduced 
dual RGB-D and hand–eye calibration errors by 49.23% and 69.25%, 
respectively. The resulting accuracy enabled a robust fusion pipeline, 
wherein the Camera-Frame and Base-Frame strategies offered distinct 
performance trade-offs, both significantly outperforming single-view 
methods under occluded conditions. The integrated system operates at 
4 Hz, meeting the requirements for open-loop harvesting of stationary 
fruit, as validated in this work. This research conclusively demonstrates 
that accurate static perception forms the cornerstone of reliable robotic 
picking. Building on this foundation, future work will address current 
limitations through a structured plan: first, by prioritizing the develop-
ment of advanced algorithms for localizing fruits under heavy foliage; 
second, by increasing the perception speed to enable real-time, closed-
loop tracking of dynamically moving fruit; and finally, by integrating 
and validating these improvements within a complete robotic system 
for end-to-end dynamic harvesting.
