Abstract
Person/vehicle re‐identification aims to use technologies such as cross‐camera retrieval to
associate the same person (same vehicle) in the surveillance videos at different locations,
different times, and images captured by different cameras so as to achieve cross‐
surveillance image matching, person retrieval and trajectory tracking. It plays an
extremely important role in the fields of intelligent security, criminal investigation etc. In
recent years, the rapid development of deep learning technology has significantly pro-
pelled the advancement of re‐identification (Re‐ID) technology. An increasing number of
technical methods have emerged, aiming to enhance Re‐ID performance. This paper
summarises four popular research areas in the current field of re‐identification, focusing
on the current research hotspots. These areas include the multi‐task learning domain, the
generalisation learning domain, the cross‐modality domain, and the optimisation learning
domain. Specifically, the paper analyses various challenges faced within these domains
and elaborates on different deep learning frameworks and networks that address these
challenges. A comparative analysis of re‐identification tasks from various classification
perspectives is provided, introducing mainstream research directions and current
achievements. Finally, insights into future development trends are presented.

INTRODUCTION
Person/vehicle re‐identification [1, 2] aims to locate the
desired persons/vehicles from a collection of non‐overlapping
camera‐captured images or videos in a distributed multi‐
camera system. The re‐identification task has a wide range of
practical applications, such as criminal searches, multi‐camera
tracking, missing person searches, traffic monitoring etc. The
basic task of re‐identification is to learn distinctive features of
persons/vehicles and associating the query images with the
most matching person/vehicles in the gallery images or videos.
The pipeline of re‐identification is illustrated in Figure 1. These
person/vehicle images are processed by feature extraction al-
gorithms, extracting them into feature vectors that represent
the ID of person/vehicles. By measuring the similarity between
the target feature vector and the feature vector of the candidate
person/vehicle, a similarity ranking list is obtained. Person/
vehicle images represented by two feature vectors with high
similarity are considered to have the same identity.
The development process of the re‐identification tasks can
be broadly categorised into two phases: the phase based on
traditional handcrafted features and the phase based on deep
learning‐based features. The task of re‐identification based on
handcrafted features includes two main components: feature
extraction and similarity measurement. In terms of feature
extraction, Farenzena et al. [3] proposed a symmetry‐driven
local feature accumulation method (SDALF), which parti-
tions foreground information from target images and extracts
three complementary colour visual features to describe the
appearance characteristics of the target. Zhao et al. [4] utilised
filters to obtain salient regions in the target image and assigned
different weights to the obtained salient regions based on

quality. Due to the very limited number of parameters allowed
in the feature design process, the generation of effective fea-
tures requires a lengthy process, and these features tend to have
relatively weak generalisation capabilities.
Overall, handcrafted features are more easily influenced by
objective factors such as fixed viewpoints, low resolution, and
unfavourable lighting conditions. For example, the colour of the

identify the same target as different identities. In contrast, CNN‐

tributes to construct target features. Therefore, in real‐world
environments, the target features extracted by deep learning
methods are generally more stable compared to handcrafted

field is increasingly focusing on practical hot issues and
attempting to integrate with more research areas, presenting a
research trend of combining multiple modalities and domains.
Each year sees a concentration of research emerging to address
existing challenges, such as cross‐modal tasks, occlusion chal-

multi‐camera tracking, marking person re‐identification as an
independent computer vision task. Li et al. [9] introduced deep
learning into the re‐identification task, utilising Convolutional
Neural Networks (CNNs) to build models for feature extraction
and matching. They incorporated cross‐entropy loss based on

lenges that are difficult to overcome in re‐identification tasks
gradually emerge. As shown in Figure 3, the left side illustrates
the challenges and problems in pedestrian re‐identification
tasks. Influenced by objective environmental conditions, the
apparent differences in features of the same target captured by

variations in the visual features of the same target and causing
inaccurate re‐identification. Additionally, the same identified

various backgrounds and experience varying levels of occlu-

the significant features of the target in images or videos. As the
video‐based pedestrian re‐identification task develops, the
vehicle re‐identification task also faces similar or even more
challenging challenges. Unlike the significantly distinctive in-
dividual features of pedestrian targets, most vehicles are mass‐
produced on automated assembly lines, and vehicles of the
same brand and model exhibit high similarity. The high inter‐
class similarity makes distinguishing between different vehicles
difficult.

on handcrafted features, using various sensors to obtain
vehicle information, which was then processed into vehicle
features for re‐identification tasks. After the introduction of
the VeRi dataset by Liu et al. [11], various large‐scale vehicle
re‐identification datasets emerged. These datasets aim to
exploit discriminative features of vehicles, supervising the

licence plates, vehicle structure, and interior decorations. As a
result, deep learning‐based vehicle re‐identification frame-

detection model to find bounding boxes for certain parts of
vehicles, helping address approximate repetitions in vehicle re‐
identification. Zhou et al. [13] proposed a viewpoint‐aware
attention generation model to generate vehicle images from
specific angles, aiding in understanding cross‐view features.
With the explosive popularity of Transformer models in
natural language processing, the computer vision field has
gradually begun to pay attention to this novel network archi-
tecture. The emergence of Vision Transformer (ViT) [14] has
changed the dominance of convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) in the Re‐ID field. The ViT does not rely on con-
volutional operations; instead, it uses an attention mechanism to
model global dependencies between inputs and outputs. He
et al. [15] encoded images as a series of patches and built a
powerful baseline model called TransReID based on the
Transformer architecture. This method includes two innovative
modules: (1) the Jigsaw Patch Module (JPM), which rearranges
patch embeddings through shifting and patch shuffling opera-
tions, thereby generating more discriminative and diverse robust
features; (2) the Side Information Embedding Module (SIE),
which integrates non‐visual cues by inserting learnable em-
beddings to reduce the bias of features under camera/viewpoint
changes. Wang et al. [16] proposed an Attribute‐guided Trans-
former (AiT), which explicitly utilises pedestrian attributes as
semantic priors for discriminative representation learning.
Specifically, the authors first introduced an attribute learning
process that generates a set of attention maps, highlighting key
informative regions in pedestrian images. The Transformer ar-
chitecture has already demonstrated its powerful performance
in large pre‐trained models and various downstream tasks.
However, the enormous computational demands of the
Transformer architecture have hindered many researchers. To
further reduce the complexity of attention‐based models, State
Space Models (SSM) have gained increasing attention in recent
years as a potential alternative to self‐attention‐based Trans-
former models. The Mamba model [17], one of the most
notable representatives, has spawned models suitable for visual
tasks, named VMamba [18] and Vim [19]. Wang et al. [20] used
VMamba and Vim models as backbone models for re‐
identification, exploring the potential of Mamba in re‐
identification tasks across several common re‐identification
datasets. Zhang et al. [21] proposed a novel fusion frame-
work called MambaReID, which includes three components:
Three‐Stage VMamba (TSV), Dense Mamba (DM), and
Consistent VMamba Fusion (CVF). TSV efficiently captures
global contextual information and local details with low
computational complexity. DM enhances feature discrimina-
bility by fully integrating cross‐modal information from
shallow and deep features through dense connections. Addi-
tionally, CVF provides finer‐grained modal aggregation by
aligning well‐aligned multimodal images, thereby improving
feature robustness. The Mamba model has achieved compa-
rable results on re‐identification datasets, demonstrating its
applicability in the re‐identification domain.
The methods above all focus on tailoring approaches for
specific tasks. However, there is a growing trend to explore
universal encoders, leading to the proposal of human‐centric
omnipotent models [22], named Omni models. These models
unify various human‐centric tasks and can be fine‐tuned or
quickly adjusted for different human‐centric visual tasks. These
models leverage large‐scale pre‐training on diverse datasets and
highly correlated human semantics across different human‐
centric tasks, achieving remarkable results. Hulk [23] unifies
data formats and task modelling by compressing different task
inputs and outputs into four modalities and treating hetero-
geneous human‐centric tasks as modality conversion tasks.
In practical monitoring scenarios, due to insufficient lighting
in dark environments at night, the appearance of pedestrians
captured by visible light cameras may become blurry. However,
infrared devices can overcome this limitation, capturing rela-
tively clear pedestrian images even in dark environments.
Therefore, visible‐infrared pedestrian re‐identification tech-
nology is of high practical value, as infrared cameras compen-
sate for the shortcomings of visible light cameras under low‐
light conditions, becoming an important data source. Wu et al.
[24] proposed a single‐stream network using a depth zero‐
padding method to effectively recognise visible‐infrared cross‐
modality person images. They created a benchmark dataset
SYSU‐MM01 for infrared person image re‐identification.
Meanwhile, the types of monitoring devices have been greatly
expanded, and data collection devices are no longer limited to
ordinary surveillance cameras. This results in the same target
appearing in different modal data scenes. The heterogeneity
between different modalities greatly reduces the recognition
accuracy of re‐identification models. Therefore, cross‐modal re‐
identification has been proposed, typically mapping data from
different modalities to a shared feature space for similarity
measurement. The network proposed by Pan et al. [25] achieved
good transferability and effectiveness on multiple datasets. Shen
et al. [26] introduced a network with both CNN and LSTM for
vehicle re‐ID, using an auxiliary path to explore complex
spatiotemporal regularisation.
With the development of re‐identification technology,
some a priori assumptions proposed due to low technical ca-
pabilities have gradually become new research directions in the
re‐identification field. Traditional person re‐identification as-
sumes that a person does not change clothes in a short period.

ReID with multi‐task learning
Multi‐task learning (MTL) [29] is a machine learning approach
aimed at improving the generalisation ability and performance
of a model by simultaneously learning multiple related tasks. In
traditional single‐task learning, a model is optimised for a
specific task, while multi‐task learning allows a model to handle
multiple tasks simultaneously, enhancing overall effectiveness
by sharing information and knowledge. In multi‐task learning,
it is assumed that different tasks are inherently correlated.
Therefore, the approach involves integrating training data from
different tasks, followed by jointly training the model param-
eters for multiple tasks to improve the model's learning
capability and enhance its generalisation.
In the context of ReID tasks, multi‐task learning has been
widely applied. Examples include re‐identification methods
based on detection, human parsing, segmentation, multi‐scale

ReID with detection algorithm
As a kind of target retrieval task, ReID aims to locate a specific
object from a set of scene images, presenting an extremely
challenging
task
involving
fine‐grained
recognition
and
retrieval. An object search system must generalise to separate
people from the background and specialise in distinguishing
identities. It is a multitasking endeavour that combines object
detection and ReID.
In feature learning, there is a conflict between object
detection and recognition. The goal of object detection is to
learn features that generalise across different people, dis-
tinguishing objects from the background. On the other hand,
the goal of ReID is to learn features that do not generalise
across different people but effectively distinguish between
objects. When relying on either strategy, it is challenging to
balance the importance of the two subtasks in different sce-
narios. Therefore, existing work follows either the ‘ReID‐first’
or ’detection‐first’ principle, prioritising one subtask.
ReID can be broadly categorised into two‐step and end‐to‐
end methods. Two‐step methods involve performing ReID on
cropped object patches. And they combine an object detector
(such as Faster R‐CNN [30], FCOS [31]) and a ReID model
sequentially. These steps handle detection and ReID separately.
Initially, an off‐the‐shelf detection model is used to locate
multiple people. Subsequently, the detected person is cropped
and sent to the ReID network to extract distinctive features. For
instance, Wang et al. [32] designed a person search system
comprising an identity‐guided query detector and a ReID model.
While the two‐step model can achieve satisfactory results, the
separate handling of these two tasks is time‐consuming and
resource‐intensive. On the other hand, an end‐to‐end approach
integrates these two models into a unified framework to improve
efficiency. The end‐to‐end method first applies the ROI align
layer to aggregate features within the detected bounding boxes.
Subsequently, by sharing these features between detection and
ReID, simultaneous optimisation of both tasks becomes
feasible, supervised by the corresponding loss functions based
on task preferences. For instance, Chen et al. [33] shared
detection and ReID features and decomposed them in a polar
coordinate system. However, since these models also adopt two‐
stage detectors like Faster R‐CNN, they inevitably inherit limi-
tations associated with two‐stage detectors, such as high
computational complexity caused by dense anchors and high
sensitivity to hyperparameters (including anchor box size, aspect
ratio, and quantity). Although they achieve high accuracy, the
computational cost is substantial.
Therefore, transitioning to an anchor‐free framework for
person search is crucial, but several challenges need to be
addressed:
Many anchor‐free models use Feature Pyramid Networks
(FPN) [34] to learn multiscale features for achieving scale
invariance in object detection. However, this leads to the
misalignment problem (scale misalignment) in the ReID task
because the query person needs to be compared with a person
of various scales in the gallery set. Without operations like ROI
Align, anchor‐free models cannot align features for re‐
identification
and
detection
based
on
specific
regions,
increasing the task difficulty. As a multi‐task framework, person
search needs to find a better balance between detection and
ReID tasks. In the AlignPS method [35], a feature alignment
person search network is proposed, introducing an anchor‐free
framework tailored for this task. It follows the ‘ReID first’
principle to generate more discriminative and robust feature
embeddings.
Similarly, in the field of vehicle re‐identification, detection
methods are also used for vehicle re‐identification tasks. He et al.
[12]introducedaninnovativeframework that underwentend‐to‐
end training incorporating both local and global constraints
through the incorporation of a detection branch. The local
module targeted part features, discerning subtle differences in
visual features such as front and back lights, front and back
windows, and the vehicle brand. The global module underwent
regularisation based on part attention derived from the local
module. This method, utilising part‐regularised discriminative
feature preservation, heightened the perceptual capability to
discern subtle distinctions. Ma et al. [36] introduced an enhanced
part model designed for the acquisition of an effective feature
embedding. This refined part model employed a Grid Spatial
Transformer Network (GSTN) to autonomously identify the
vehicle's location and partition local features accordingly.
Furthermore, residual attention was employed to provide addi-
tional fine‐grained refinement for precise identification. The
resulting refined part features were amalgamated to generate an
efficient feature embedding, consequently enhancing the accu-
racy of vehicle re‐identification.
The field of multi‐task learning is rich and significantly
expands the application scope of the ReID task. The fusion of
various tasks in the visual domain provides new perspectives
for both domains, enhancing the recognition accuracy of tasks.
Local features, as an indispensable part of the ReID task,
directly influence the final discriminative results. Therefore,
exploring more representative and applicable local features has
become the mainstream direction of the ReID task. Currently,
person recognition focuses on representative biological fea-
tures such as pose estimation and gait, while vehicle recogni-
tion utilises specific vehicle information such as licence plates,
vehicle types, and interiors for discrimination. The main di-
rections are to find or analyse more efficient, more discrimi-
native local features to assist tasks and to explore more
powerful network models or methods to fully utilise existing
local information, such as the incorporation of graph convo-
lution methods. Various ReID tasks are based on these two
approaches. Meanwhile, in reviewing the upstream and
downstream fields of ReID tasks, the continuous development
of person search tasks has further enhanced ReID accuracy.

ReID with parsing
Images used for re‐identification algorithms are typically ob-
tained by cropping the bounding boxes of the detected person,
which can be significantly influenced by the background region
of the image. Treating all pixels in the image equally may
introduce bias in the learning algorithm. This is because the
focal points to be considered are either vehicles or people, and
creating high similarity between images with similar back-
grounds is not conducive to recognition tasks.
To address this issue, one option is to employ fine‐grained
semantic segmentation tasks to process the collected images.
This involves dividing the people and vehicles in the image into
multiple semantically consistent regions, thereby eliminating
the influence of the background and improving recognition
accuracy. More specifically, in person re‐identification tasks,
human body parsing algorithms are often used as assistance
and integrated into the re‐identification task network to learn
appearance features describing the person.
In Figure 5, human body parsing algorithms aim to label
each pixel in the input human image as either background or
one of the predefined human parts labels (such as head, legs,
upper body, shoes, etc.). In recent years, this has been primarily
addressed through deep learning methods. Liu et al. [38]
employed both parametric and non‐parametric methods. They
first retrieved the most similar images from a human parsing
database and then used a deep learning‐based matching
approach to transfer labels from the database images to the
input human image. Liang et al. [39] utilised Long Short‐Term
Memory (LSTM) networks to combine short‐range and long‐
range spatial dependencies for human parsing. Liang et al.
[40] proposed a contextual convolutional neural network ar-
chitecture that integrates cross‐layer context, global image‐level
context, superpixel‐wise contextual information, and cross‐
superpixel neighbourhood context into a unified network to
address human body parsing problems. Sun et al. [41] pro-
posed a part‐based convolutional baseline (PCB) that divides
feature maps into several horizontal stripes and learns part‐
level features through multiple classifiers. Building upon
PCB, Fu et al. [42] introduced a multi‐scale, finer‐grained
partitioning method to enhance the discriminative ability of
different parts of a person. To address the issue of body parts
being occluded, Meng et al. [43] introduce a new framework
called Body Topology Information Generation and Matching.
This framework comprises two main modules: the Body To-
pology Information Generation Module and the Body To-
pology Information Matching Module. The former employs an

ReID with additional information
Exploring the use of implicit person dynamic motion cues in
gait features to address re‐identification tasks. While it can be
challenging to identify a person when they wear different
clothes or distinguish between different people when they wear
similar/same clothes, leveraging their unique gait allows for
accurate identity matching. Although the same person is
wearing different clothes, their gait images exhibit a high de-
gree of similarity. On the other hand, among different people
wearing similar clothing, despite the high visual similarity in
appearance, there is significant variability in their gaits. This is
because gait, as a unique biological feature, possesses superior
invariance compared to other easily changeable visual features
such as face, body shape, and silhouette. Additionally, even
with low‐quality camera imaging, gait can be verified at a
considerable distance. Therefore, gait recognition, as auxiliary
information, demonstrates robustness.
Most gait recognition work emphasises spatial feature
extraction and temporal modelling. The former is widely
applied, but these methods overlook significant differences in
different body parts during gait tasks. Many existing works,
such as those by Chao et al. [46] and Zhang et al. [47], tend to
model temporal changes or directly compress the entire gait
sequence into a single frame, attempting to learn gait features
from video samples of person. On the other hand, gait
recognition assisting re‐identification tasks primarily focus on
modelling body shape from multi‐modality information (such
as silhouette and sketch) to avoid interference from clothing.
They often discard the original RGB frames and model gait on
the skeleton and silhouette, extracting clothing‐independent
features such as facial features, hairstyle, body shape, and
gait. However, this approach has the drawback of not fully
utilising information in the original RGB images that are un-
related to clothing. The latest CAL framework [48] uses gait
information as assistance while simultaneously exploring
clothing‐related features from the original RGB images. It
outperforms all SOTA methods in clothing‐changing re‐
identification problems.
For the ReID task of vehicle, unlike persons, vehicles lack
gait information. However, they possess additional informa-
tion such as colour and vehicle type. By embedding this in-
formation into the feature vectors extracted from the network,
the accuracy of re‐identification tasks can be improved to some
extent. He et al. [49] proposed the Side Information Embed-
ding (SIE) method that incorporates additional information,
such as camera or viewpoint details, into the embedding
representations. Inspired by position embeddings, learnable 1‐
D embeddings are introduced to retain side information. To
address the challenge of distinguishing vehicles with similar
models and manufacturers, Liu et al. [50] proposed a Region‐
Aware deep Model (RAM) that emphasises the importance
of local regions, such as decorations and inspection stickers,
for better Re‐ID performance. RAM extracts features from
both global and local regions, leveraging distinctive visual cues
from the latter. The learning algorithm incorporates vehicle
IDs, types/models, and colours, enhancing the discriminative
capabilities of both global and regional features.

The advantages and disadvantages of re‐
identification using multi‐task learning
Multi‐task learning (MTL) aids the re‐identification task by
leveraging other tasks. MTL enables the model to learn richer
and more generalised features by sharing underlying feature
representations across multiple tasks. Since different tasks may
involve different environments or data distributions, the model
can better capture the general features in the data by learning
these shared representations, thereby performing well across
various environments. Moreover, MTL allows for simultaneous
optimisation of multiple objectives within an end‐to‐end
framework, simplifying the model design and deployment
process.
Some multi‐task learning approaches are based on other
deep learning algorithms, such as object detection and parsing.
These methods can help identify the target and segment its
fine‐grained features. The different tasks complement each
other. However, such methods often involve one or more
large‐scale, computationally expensive networks, making them
difficult to deploy in resource‐constrained industrial produc-
tion environments.
Methods based on additional information avoid the
aforementioned issues. These methods use extra information
to construct auxiliary tasks, making target features more
distinctive. However, the use of additional information also
requires more annotated data. To fully utilise the advantages of
MTL, a large amount of annotated data is needed to cover all
tasks. Insufficient or imbalanced data may affect the model's
performance.
Even without additional labelled information, multi‐task
learning remains effective. During training, some pre‐train
deep learning models can be used to label unlabelled data,
which can then be used to build multi‐task learning systems,
thereby effectively avoiding the high costs of manual labelling.
Additionally, methods such as knowledge distillation can be
employed to construct auxiliary tasks, providing extra training
signals for multi‐task learning. For example, by creating a data
transformation recognition task, the model can learn invariant
features by identifying transformations applied to the input
data (such as rotation, flipping, cropping, etc.). During testing
and inference, the trained model already possesses the neces-
sary feature extraction capabilities, thus eliminating the need
for auxiliary tasks in the feature extraction process and

Generalisation learning
2.2.1

Fully supervised ReID
Completely supervised ReID methods, mostly based on
labelled datasets, can be broadly categorised into two main
branches. One focus is on designing effective optimisation
metrics for ReID, known as metric learning. Examples include
triplet loss and circle loss. On the other hand, there is an
emphasis on learning fine‐grained features. PCB and MGN
achieve accurate person ReID by manually segmenting each
overall image into multiple subparts to leverage the local fea-
tures of person images. Additionally, data augmentation is
another approach that emphasises enriching the diversity of
training data. For instance, generating adversarial occluded
samples to increase the variability of training data and utilising
GAN‐generated images as supplementary information to aid
training.

Semi‐supervised ReID
In many practical applications, obtaining a large amount of
labelled data is very costly, while unlabelled data is usually more
accessible. Semi‐supervised learning leverages large amounts of
unlabelled data, enabling the training of well‐performing
models even when labelled data is limited. In this section, we
primarily
introduce
commonly
used
methods
in

supervised learning, such as pseudo‐label methods and
knowledge distillation methods.
Pseudo‐label methods are also known as the clustering‐
based approach, employing an iterative process alternating
between clustering and fine‐tuning. In Figure 7, images are
encoded into high‐dimensional features through a feature
extraction network. These features are then clustered into three
categories using a clustering algorithm, assigning different la-
bels to them. This clustering information is utilised as labels
for fine‐tuning the ReID network. Despite its simplicity, this
straightforward method yields satisfactory results. For example,
Yang et al. [52] proposed a Self‐Similarity Grouping (SSG)
method, assigning different pseudo‐labels to global and local
features. To mitigate the impact of noisy hard pseudo‐labels,
Ge et al. [51] introduced Mutual Mean Teaching (MMT),
employing a teacher–student framework where two networks
are jointly trained using hard pseudo‐labels generated by each
network and soft pseudo‐labels generated by the average
network, refining the pseudo‐labels in the target domain.
Moreover, they proposed SpCL [53], which incorporates a
novel clustering reliability criterion to mitigate the amplifica-
tion of training errors due to noisy pseudo‐class labels. This
approach incrementally produces more reliable target‐domain
clusters, enabling the learning of enhanced features within
the hybrid memory, which subsequently improves clustering
performance. CycAs [54] and TSSL [55] utilise auxiliary tracklet
information to predict pseudo‐labels more accurately. The ISE
[56] emphasises the issues of sub‐clustering and mixed clus-
tering, proposing a label‐preserving loss to constrain support
samples to be close to their cluster members, aiming to refine
clustering results and improve performance. To avoid the hard
quantisation loss in clustering, SoftSim [57] directly employs
similarity‐based soft labels to train the ReID model, enabling it
to learn from similar images under a smooth constraint.
However, the inherent label noise in pseudo‐labels de-
grades performance. Recent research has focused on address-
ing this issue. RLCC [58] uses a label propagation scheme
based on a clustering consensus matrix to refine pseudo‐labels
during the training process. PPLR [59] introduces a pseudo‐
label refinement framework based on local features, oper-
ating in a self‐supervised manner without auxiliary networks. It
designs a cross‐protocol score to capture reliable comple-
mentary information, calculated through the similarity between
k‐nearest neighbours of global and local features.
Knowledge distillation is a model optimisation technique
that involves transferring the knowledge of a complex deep
neural network (teacher model) to a simpler neural network
(student model). The goal of this process is to enhance the
performance of the student network, enabling it to efficiently
operate on resource‐constrained or embedded devices. It aims
to maintain performance close to that of the teacher network
while reducing parameters and computational requirements.
Consequently, the student model can achieve similar perfor-
mance to the teacher model while exhibiting higher efficiency
and lower computational costs. The process of knowledge
distillation is illustrated in Figure 8. The feature extraction
capability of the teacher model, which is designed for data, is
transferred to the student model through the knowledge
distillation algorithm. This enables the student model to
perform data inference with lower memory or faster execution
speed. In the context of re‐identification, knowledge distilla-
tion is often used to transfer the required knowledge, assisting
the main network in learning relevant feature knowledge
through a mutual learning framework.
Currently, applications include refinement of weakly su-
pervised noisy label tasks [41, 54, 58], transfer of temporal
knowledge for video re‐identification tasks [27, 42], and

Unsupervised learning ReID
Supervised methods have achieved remarkable results. How-
ever, they require a large amount of labelled data, involving
expensive annotations, limiting their practicality in large‐scale
real‐world problems. Due to this challenge, unsupervised
methods that learn discriminative features from unlabelled data
have received widespread attention in recent years, particularly
in the more challenging domain of unsupervised learning. Un-
supervised methods are generally divided into two categories:
unsupervised domain adaptation re‐identification (UDA‐ReID)
and domain‐generalisable re‐identification (DG‐ReID).
The first UDA‐ReID methods transfer knowledge from
labelled source data to unlabelled target data. These methods
still require annotation for a portion of the data. In practice,
due to domain gaps between training and new environment
data, models trained on source domains often experience sig-
nificant performance degradation when deployed to new en-
vironments. Hence, unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA)
has been extensively studied to help deployed models adapt
better to new environments, by transferring learnt knowledge
from labelled source domains to unlabelled target domains.
This is achieved using domain translation methods that focus
on style transformations from the target domain to the source
domain, modifying source images to obtain images with source
content but target appearance. In this way, they obtain images
from source images similar to the target, with corresponding
label annotations. These generated images are then used to
refine network parameters. The latest work in this category
explores the integration of generative models, such as Cycle-
GAN [62], to bridge the domain gap by translating target
images from the source domain to the target domain. Some
work also aims to maintain self‐similarity between translated
and untranslated images and dissimilarity between translated
source images and target images.
The DG‐ReID methods are domain‐generalisable person
re‐identification (DG‐ReID), aiming to learn a robust model
that achieves good performance on unseen target domains
without requiring additional updates. DG‐ReID has garnered
increasing interest in recent years. In the context of DG‐ReID
based on multiple source datasets, Song et al. [63] built a large‐
scale domain‐generalisation ReID benchmark to verify the
generalisation capability of DG models. They designed a
Domain‐Invariant Mapping Network (DIMN) to learn the
mapping between person images and their ID classifiers.
Model‐Agnostic Meta‐Learning (MAML) [64] has been pro-
posed to learn good initial parameters with any model trained
through gradient descent, suitable for diverse learning prob-
lems, including classification, regression, and reinforcement
learning. Meta‐learning aims to train a model on various
learning tasks so that it can effectively address new tasks with
minimal training samples. Using MAML, the model parameters
are explicitly trained to facilitate quick adaptation with a small
amount of training data from a new task through a few
gradient steps, emphasising generalisation performance.
While supervised learning has been a traditional method
for re‐identification tasks, achieving high recognition accuracy,
more and more unsupervised methods are being researched to
align with practical applications. In the unsupervised domain,
the generalisation of re‐identification tasks has gained wide-
spread attention. To deploy more effectively in the real world
lacking target domains, various methods have been cited,
including style transfer for source‐to‐target domain trans-
formation, further utilising strategies like meta‐learning to help
models adapt to domain changes or using artificially syn-
thesised datasets to assist in generalisation tasks. These have
become focal points in the re‐identification field.

learning, the model's performance heavily depends on the
balance between labelled and unlabelled data. Typically, the
quantity of unlabelled data far exceeds that of labelled data.
This class imbalance issues will cause the model to focus more
on the unlabelled classes, while neglecting the labelled ones,

Cross‐modality learning
2.3.1
|
Visible‐infrared methods
In dark environments or during the night, Infrared (IR) Images
become indispensable in many visual systems. To achieve this,
it is necessary to match RGB images with infrared images,
where the infrared images are heterogeneous and possess
significantly different visual characteristics. For the task of
person re‐identification, this is a highly challenging cross‐
modality problem.
Wu et al. [24] were the first to propose the person re‐
identification task addressing the cross‐modality problem in
low‐light conditions and at night. They collected an RGB‐IR
dataset and contributed a large benchmark dataset (SYSU‐
MM01), demonstrating the feasibility of matching these two
modalities. Inspired by this work, various cross‐modality ReID
approaches have emerged in abundance. In the past few years,
numerous methods have been proposed to alleviate cross‐
modality differences. Based on the choice of mitigating mo-
dality differences, existing methods can be classified into the
following three groups:
Network‐based approaches aim to learn discriminative
representations shared across modalities
Models based on modality‐shared feature learning seek to
embed features from different modalities into the same feature
space and reduce cross‐modality differences through the use of
certain feature‐level constraints.
A series of shared feature learning methods leverage CNN
to perform cross‐modality feature alignment. Today, methods
based on CNN networks have become the primary approach
for cross‐modality ReID tasks, with representative models
being dual‐stream network models, whose structural features
are illustrated in Figure 9. The dual‐stream network comprises
two pathways, each consisting of a shallow network structure
for a specific modality and a deep network structure with
shared parameters. This architecture can map images of per-
sons/vehicles from different modalities to the same feature
space, obtaining cross‐modality shared features. Some recent
studies have extended this foundation by incorporating
improved dual‐stream networks with deep metric learning or
attention mechanisms to learn modality‐agnostic representa-
tions tailored for modality differences and high sample noise.
Existing research has primarily focused on learning local fea-
tures while neglecting the correlations between these local
features. He et al. [68] proposes an Integrated Graph Attention
Network that leverages a graph structure to capture the
complete correlations among local features. To achieve this, the
authors
incorporate
coarse‐to‐fine
attention
weights
by
considering both local details and global information, facili-
tating the aggregation of local features.
Metric‐based design methods aim to design different metrics
or losses to alleviate modality differences
In metric learning‐based work, Ye et al. [69] extract multi‐
modality shareable features during the feature learning phase,
then project different modalities features into a common space
and narrow the distance through metric learning. Hao et al.
[70] map extracted features onto a hyperspherical manifold and
calculate the angle‐based difference between two samples. Feng
et al. [71] leverage constraints to reduce cross‐modality gaps.
Ye et al. [72] first consider intra‐ and inter‐modality variations
and then learn discriminative features through loss. Subse-
quently, Ye et al. [73] extended this work by introducing bidi-
rectional centre‐constrained loss, further improving image‐
based person ReID performance. In various metric designs,
whether using ranking loss or further employing triplet loss,

Methodologies based on modality transformation aim to
find strategies for transforming or enhancing to bridge the
gap between modalities
In order to effectively reduce the differences between different
modalities, cross‐modal image synthesis methods typically
employ Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to transfer
style characteristics, thereby synthesising fictitious RGB/IR
images. Through this approach, the network can better learn the
commonalities between modalities, achieving more natural and
consistent image synthesis. Specifically, Wang et al. [76] intro-
duced CycleGAN in their study. This method can transform the
style attributes of infrared (IR) images into visible light (RGB)
images while preserving their identity information for joint
pixel and feature alignment. This approach not only contributes
to the generation of realistic images but also enhances the
consistency of images through feature alignment while main-
taining individual identity information. This advancement
provides a more effective and promising solution for the field of
cross‐modal image synthesis, opening up new possibilities for
the study of information transformation between modalities.
Some studies achieve feature learning through adversarial
and disentangled learning. For instance, Choi et al. [77] sepa-
rated recognition features from recognition exclusion features
in cross‐modality images and then combined them to generate
images that are modally distinct but recognition‐consistent.
However, this method has high computational complexity, and
poorly generated images negatively impact performance.
Additionally, some research applied paired pixel alignment and
intermediate modality generation to further eliminate appear-
ance differences between modalities.
Another approach is to compensate for modality‐specific
information, attempting to fill in missing modality‐specific
information in existing modalities. FMCNet [78] follows this
idea of modality‐specific information compensation. This
method compensates for missing modality‐specific informa-
tion at the feature level rather than the image level, directly
generating missing modality‐specific features from existing
features of one modality. This allows the model to primarily
generate distinctive modality‐specific features relevant to per-
sons and discard those without discriminative power, as shown
in Figure 10. The proposed model differs from existing models
by compensating for missing modality‐specific information at
the feature level rather than the image level. It directly gener-
ates missing modality‐specific features from the shared features
of another modality. The single‐modality feature decomposi-
tion module is employed to decompose single‐modality fea-
tures into modality‐specific features and shared features. Then,
through the feature‐level modality compensation module,
missing modality‐specific features are generated from the
existing shared features. Finally, the existing features and the
generated features are combined using the feature‐based fusion
module.
However, applying GANs for modality transformation is
not a straightforward task. This might be due to the lack of
colour information in IR images, making it challenging to
preserve identity information in generated RGB images and
resulting in identity inconsistencies during the complex
adversarial training process. In the field of modality trans-
formation, besides the mentioned methods, Ye et al. [79] also
address the image characteristics of RGB/IR images, propos-
ing a novel joint learning strategy through channel enhance-
ment and simulated random occlusion. SMCL [80] introduces a
novel fusion modality co‐learning model to bridge the gap
between modalities. It automatically constructs a new modality
that blends heterogeneous image features to guide the gener-
ation of modality‐invariant representations. By combining
enhanced homogeneity learning and auxiliary distribution
similarity learning, different modality features are projected
into a unified space, expanding inter‐class differences and
enhancing recognition. Additionally, image alignment and
pattern alignment are utilised to alleviate differences.

methods follow a similar learning scheme: (1) using identity loss
to suppress intra‐class variations in each method and (2) cross‐
modality matching supervised by automatically generated pos-
itive or negative labels, depending on whether they originate
from the same identity.
Li et al. [82] and Chen et al. [83] computed image‐word
affinity to explore local hierarchical relationships between vi-
sual space and text space. Zheng et al. [84] and Zhang et al. [85]
focused on learning cross‐modality objective functions for joint
embedding. Chen et al. [83] learnt local correlations using noun
phrase reconstruction of image regions. Sarafianos et al. [86]
introduced an adversarial cross‐modality learning framework to
blur modality information. Recent research decomposes the
human feature space into multiple spaces corresponding to
different body parts using auxiliary algorithms (such as pose
estimation and human parsing).
The aforementioned methods are fully supervised, heavily
relying on identity annotations, but labelling identities across
camera views is costly and time‐consuming. Therefore, recent
research has shifted towards the weakly supervised domain,
where only text‐image pairs are available, eliminating the need
for identity annotation during training. CMMT [87] proposed a
cross‐modality mutual training framework. Specifically, to alle-
viate intra‐class variations, a clustering method is used to
generate pseudo‐labels for visual and text instances. To further
refine the clustering results, a mutual pseudo‐label refinement
module is provided, utilising clustering results in one modality to
refine the clustering results in another modality constrained by
text‐image pairwise relations. The academic sphere has recently
been engrossed by the prowess of Contrastive Language‐Image
Pretraining (CLIP) [88] due to its sturdy semantic concept
learning and broad multi‐modal knowledge, which show po-
tential in tackling the challenges mentioned. In light of this, Yan
et al. [89] introduce a CLIP‐driven framework called CFine for
fine‐grained information excavation, designed to harness CLIP's
knowledge for the task of Visible‐Text ReID. A multi‐grained
global feature learning module (MGF) that thoroughly extracts
discriminative local information from each modality is designed
to efficiently transfer multi‐modal knowledge, highlighting
identity‐related discriminative cues by enhancing the interaction
between global images (text) and informative local patches
(words). Following this, they propose the Cross‐grained Feature
Refinement (CFR) and Fine‐grained Correspondence Discovery
(FCD) modules, which foster cross‐grained and fine‐grained
interactions (image‐word, sentence‐patch, and word‐patch)
across modalities. To ensure that the model has the underlying
alignment capabilities necessary for matching multi‐modal data,
Jiang et al. [90] present a cross‐modal Implicit Relation
Reasoning and Aligning framework (IRRA) that learns relations
between local visual‐textual tokens and enhances global image‐
text matching without requiring additional prior supervision.
Despite the plethora of TIReID techniques that have
shown promising results, they often assume without question
that the image‐text pairs used for training are accurately
matched. These pairs, in fact, can be poorly correlated or even
incorrectly matched; this phenomenon is referred to as noisy
correspondence (NC), caused by subpar image quality and
annotation mistakes. To tackle this issue, Qin et al. [91] have
developed a novel approach called Robust Dual Embedding
(RDE), designed to establish visual‐semantic solid connections
even in the presence of NC. Given the inherent uncertainty
associated with pedestrian images and their corresponding
textual descriptions, Zhao et al. [92] introduce a novel method
that integrates multi‐modal uncertainty modelling with se-
mantic alignment for TI‐ReID. This method represents pe-
destrians'
image
and
text
feature
vectors
as
Gaussian
distributions, estimating the multi‐granularity uncertainty of
these distributions by including batch‐level and identity‐level
feature variances for each modality. This multi‐modal uncer-
tainty modelling serves as a feature enhancement and provides
a richer representation of the semantic relationship between
images and text. A bi‐directional cross‐modal circle loss is
proposed to more effectively align the probabilistic features
between images and text in a self‐paced manner.
To alleviate the problem of a large manual annotation
workload, Tan et al. [93] investigate the transferable text‐to‐
image ReID challenge, where they train a model on a large‐
scale dataset and directly apply it to different datasets for
testing without further training. This method employs MLLMs
to caption images using a variety of templates. These templates
are generated through a multi‐turn dialogue with a Large
Language Model (LLM), enabling the construction of a large‐
scale dataset featuring diverse textual content. By using the
similarity between the text and all patch token embeddings
within the image, MLLMs can automatically detect words in a
description that do not align with the image.

Visible‐synthetic methods
In recent years, the generalisation problem of person re‐
identification has gained increasing attention. One approach
to improving generalisation is to develop large‐scale and
diverse training datasets. However, collecting images of a
person from surveillance videos raises privacy concerns, and
further data annotation is expensive. Therefore, synthetic
datasets have been actively developed in recent years due to
their advantages such as no privacy issues and no annotation
costs. SyRI [94] and PersonX [95] use manually crafted 3D
human models to generate data. In contrast, RandPerson [96]
proposes another method by randomly generating 3D clothing
models with colours and texture patterns and utilising Make-
Human [97] to generate characters. To simulate a real‐world
environment, they also simulate a camera network in Uni-
ty3D to synthetic and record videos of moving characters.
Ultimately, it is demonstrated that models trained on synthetic
data can generalise well to real‐world datasets. Following
RandPerson, UnrealPerson [98] enhances accuracy by creating
virtual
characters
using
real‐world
human
images
and
rendering four large scenes using the powerful Unreal Engine
4 (UE4).
However, in existing datasets, synthetic persons are often
cartoon characters with randomly matched clothing, limiting
the performance of synthetic people. To address this issue, the
latest ClonedPerson [99] proposes an automatic method that
directly clones the entire outfit from real‐world human images
to virtual 3D characters, as shown in Figure 11. Two UV
mapping cloning methods are designed, namely Registered
Costume Mapping and Uniform Costume Expansion. Regis-
tered mapping is targeted at regular UV maps where the
costume exhibits a conventional shape and structure. By
detecting key points of the costume on real‐world human
images and marking them on the UV map, registered mapping
applies perspective homography to transform the real‐world
costume into corresponding portions on the UV map. Uni-
form expansion is used to handle invisible parts of the costume
and irregular UV maps. As a result, any virtual character will
appear very similar to a real‐world person. This enhances the
difficulty of model learning in terms of visual similarity and
enriches
the
sample
distribution.
Experimental
results
demonstrate that models trained on ClonedPerson exhibit
better generalisation performance.
Similarly, in the vehicle re‐identification task, synthetic data
are also used to increase the amount of data to achieve better
recognition results. Tang et al. [100] utilised the Neural Dataset
Synthesiser (NDDS) in Unreal Engine 4 (UE4) to generate a
synthetic dataset. To create this dataset, 3D vehicle meshes
were imported, and 36 3D keypoints were manually annotated
for each vehicle model. The dataset comprises 41,000 unique
images with 402 identities and includes annotations such as
keypoints, orientation, and vehicle attributes (colour and type).
Randomisation techniques, including background sampling
from CityFlow, were applied during dataset generation, and
training involved various post‐processing steps like scaling,
cropping, flipping, and occlusion. Lee et al. [101] introduced a
synthetic vehicle dataset generated by the VehicleX synthetic
dataset generator, comprising 192,150 images from 1362
distinct vehicles. Like Figure 12, this synthetic dataset serves as
an augmented training set and provides additional information
such as the colour, type, and orientation of the objects. In
contrast, the real dataset only includes vehicle IDs. Vehicles in
the synthetic dataset are categorised into 12 colours and 11
types, with the orientation represented by a rotation angle on
the horizontal plane within the range of [0, 360). Abundant
additional information enables the network model to have the
capability to extract finer‐grained visual features.

Fine‐tuning methods
The creation of large‐scale, high‐quality labelled datasets for
ReID tasks is expensive. Compared to other visual tasks,
existing fully labelled datasets have limited scale and diversity.
Therefore, pre‐training models have become crucial. However,
due to the lack of large‐scale ReID datasets, most previous
methods only use models pre‐trained on the ImageNet dataset,
which is labelled for general object recognition, leading to
limited improvements in performance.
It can be inferred that pre‐training weights on ImageNet
for model initialisation are not optimal for ReID tasks. This
results in poor fine‐tuning performance and slow convergence.
There are two main reasons: inappropriate preprocessing
methods and significant domain gaps between ImageNet and
ReID datasets. Specifically, ImageNet tends to favour coarse‐
grained classification, and there is a large domain gap be-
tween general images in ImageNet and the focused person/
vehicle images required for ReID tasks. Therefore, pre‐training
tasks specifically for ReID have been proposed.
Fu et al. [102] made a groundbreaking attempt at ReID pre‐
training and introduced a new large‐scale unlabelled ReID
dataset called LUPerson. However, it directly transferred a
contrastive learning‐based general pre‐training process designed
for ImageNet classification to ReID tasks, overlooking the fact
that ReID is a fine‐grained classification problem. This solution
faced two key issues: unlike coarse‐grained classification prob-
lems on ImageNet, the discriminative attributes of person im-
ages are easily disrupted by enhancement operations. For
example, in ImageNet classification, although augmentations to
images (such as cats and cars) may lead to a lack of region in-
formation, the remaining parts still have sufficient discrimina-
tive capability to support models for distinguishing them.
However, when the same enhancements are applied to person
images in ReID, it causes a disaster, disrupting the most dis-
tinguishing attributes (such as pants colour) and making them
indistinguishable. Second, in previous pre‐training methods,
fine‐grained information about human body images was not
sufficiently explored. They usually focus only on learning global
feature representations at the image level. However, as a fine‐
grained classification task, ReID requires not only global fea-
tures but also detailed local features for accurate identity
matching. Fine‐grained clues (such as backpacks and licence
plates) contribute more to distinguishing different people and
vehicles than global features.
ReID network training typically includes two phases: pre‐
training and fine‐tuning. (a) The model (such as ResNet50) is
first pre‐trained in an unsupervised manner through a proxy
task on a large‐scale dataset (such as LUPerson). (b) Then, the
pre‐trained model is used to initialise the backbone, and fine‐
tuning is performed using a small‐scale labelled or unlabelled
person ReID dataset (such as Market‐1501). Therefore, by
summarising the previous pre‐training tasks and stages, it is
concluded that for better pre‐training tasks, research needs to
focus on two aspects: dataset augmentation and optimisation
of the corresponding model structure design.
In terms of the model structure optimisation, the latest
UP‐ReID [103] explicitly points out the differences between
general pre‐training and ReID pre‐training. It attempts to
establish a ReID‐specific pre‐training framework for the first
time. Considering the specificity of ReID tasks, UP‐ReID in-
troduces intra‐identity regularisation, instantiated at both the
global image level and local patch level in L2 regularisation. It
first performs global consistency to enhance the robustness of
pre‐training to data augmentation. It further introduces a prior‐
based hard mining strategy for intrinsic contrast constraints
between local person image blocks to fully exploit local
discriminative clues.
Regarding dataset augmentation, PNL [104] builds a new
large‐scale noisy labelled person ReID dataset, LU‐Person NL,

Attention mechanisms
Attention is one of the most fundamental mechanisms of
human visual perception. Faced with complex scenes, humans
can choose regions of interest and use attention to narrow
down the search space, speeding up the recognition process.
Many efforts have been made to simulate the attention
mechanism in human vision within computer vision systems.
The goal is to promote high‐performance recognition by
discovering discriminative regions and alleviating negative im-
pacts such as different visual appearances, cluttered back-
grounds, occlusion, and pose variations. Therefore, attention
mechanisms play an irreplaceable role in fine‐grained visual
recognition tasks. In person/vehicle ReID tasks, attention
mechanisms can address image‐matching issues and enhance
the discriminative ability of convolutional features. For
example, Liu et al. [107] and Lan et al. [108] use attention
models to locate discriminative salient regions in images,
improving person ReID. Xu et al. [109] and Zhao et al. [110]
design a body part detector to utilise the human body structure
in attention models. Yao et al. [111] designed a part loss
network to minimise the empirical classification risk on the
training set and achieve discriminative capabilities for unseen
persons. Zheng et al. [112] proposed a coarse‐to‐fine pyramid
model to combine local information, global information, and
progressive cues between them, mitigating the impact of
inaccurate bounding boxes.
In video‐based ReID tasks, attention mechanisms are often
employed to discover key parts in videos. Khorramshahi et al.
[113] propose an adaptive attention model that significantly
improves the level of vehicle ReID tasks.
Existing attention mechanisms generally fall into two cat-
egories: supervised and weakly supervised. Supervised atten-
tion research addresses the problem of learning effective
attention by guiding the training process with strong supervi-
sion signals. Attention models should focus only on distinctive
patterns, namely primary clue regions while encouraging the
model to attend to all attributes of objects to enhance gener-
alisation and obtain attention regions with clear meanings and
robustness. A simple solution is to use additional annotations,
such as bounding boxes or segmentation masks, to explicitly

Transformer
As a comprehensive embodiment of attention mechanisms, the
transformer has deeply penetrated the field of computer vision.
However, existing vision transformers are stacked layer by layer,
lacking direct information exchange between each layer. He et al.
[15] proposed a transformer‐based framework for robust
feature representation, named TransReID. They think CNN‐
based methods process one local neighbourhood at a time,
and this kind of frameworks may suffer from information loss.
TransReID encodes images as sequences of patches and es-
tablishes a transformer‐based baseline with critical improve-
ments, achieving competitive results on ReID benchmarks.
Inspired by DenseNet, Ma et al. [116] propose a Dense Trans-
former framework (referred to as Denseformer) that connects
each layer to another through class tokens. Wang et al. [16]
proposed an Attribute‐guided Transformer (AiT), which
explicitly leverages pedestrian attributes as semantic priors for
discriminative representation learning. Specifically, the authors
initially introduce an attribute learning process that generates a
set of attention maps highlighting the informative parts of
pedestrian images. Subsequently, they design a Feature Diffu-
sion Module (FDM) to iteratively inject attribute information
into global feature maps, with the aim of suppressing unnec-
essary noise and inferring attribute‐aware representations. Li
et al. [117] introduce a novel approach that approximates the
effect of increased data diversity by dividing the embedding
space into numerous distinct and cohesive subspaces, called the
Diverse and Compact Transformer (DC‐Former). These
compact subspaces facilitate the learning of more robust and
discriminative embeddings for the identification of akin classes.
Furthermore, the amalgamation of these varied embeddings,
which encompass more nuanced information, serves to
augment the performance of ReID. The DC‐Former specifically
employs multiple class tokens within the visual transformer to
characterise the varied embedding spaces. A self‐diverse
constraint (SDC) is then applied to these spaces to diverge
them from one another, ensuring that each embedding space is
both diverse and compact. Additionally, a dynamic weight
controller (DWC) is crafted to moderate the relative significance
among these spaces during training. In order to model various
relationships between body components simultaneously, Rao
et al. [118] introduce a novel Transformer‐based Skeleton Graph
prototype contrastive learning (TranSG) framework with a
structure‐trajectory prompted reconstruction technique, which
aims to comprehensively capture skeletal relationships and the
rich spatial‐temporal semantics encoded in skeleton graphs for
person ReID. This method involves the following key steps:
first, we develop the Skeleton Graph Transformer (SGT) to
concurrently learn the correlations between body parts and
motion dynamics within skeleton graphs, effectively integrating
significant node features into graph‐level representations. Sub-
sequently, a Graph Prototype Contrastive learning (GPC)
strategy is proposed to identify the most characteristic graph
features (graph prototypes) unique to each individual and to
contrast the intrinsic similarities between graph representations
and diverse prototypes at both the skeleton and sequence levels,
thereby learning discriminative graph representations.

Design of loss function
The key to the ReID problem lies in extracting discriminative
features. Most existing efforts focus on the overall design of
network models to improve the discriminative ability of fea-
tures. However, the choice of loss functions also plays a crucial
role in extracting discriminative features. In ReID, cross‐
entropy loss and triplet loss are commonly used, with fewer
instances of other forms of loss functions.
Inspired by NormFace in face recognition (FR) [119], Fan
et al. propose SphereReID [120], which experimentally vali-
dates that features embedded on a hypersphere are more
discriminative than those extracted using cross‐entropy loss.
Sun et al. introduce a more flexible loss function called
CircleLoss [121], which outperforms other loss functions in
both FR and ReID tasks. However, manually designing loss
functions requires strong domain knowledge and is time‐
consuming. Therefore, with the development of automated
machine learning, attempts have been made in the field of loss
function search (LFS).

loss functions:

Finally, these new features from the same image are merged
into a vector for re‐ranking. Sarfraz et al. [128] proposed a
novel unsupervised automatic re‐ranking framework to achieve
state‐of‐the‐art re‐ranking performance. They demonstrate
that, compared to current state‐of‐the‐art re‐ranking methods,
this approach does not require the computation of a new
ranking list for each image pair and achieves improved per-
formance by utilising simple direct ranking list comparisons or
even solely utilising precomputed Euclidean distances between
images.

Multi‐task omni models
Previous methods primarily focused on creating unique designs
tailored for specific tasks, which involved extensive work in
task‐specific design, fine‐tuning, and deployment; there is now
an emerging trend to explore general‐purpose representative
encoders, also known as human‐centric foundation models.
These models can be fine‐tuned or prompt‐tuned for various
human‐centric visual tasks. They leverage large‐scale pre‐
training on diverse datasets and capture highly relevant hu-
man semantics across different human‐centric tasks, achieving
notable performance across a broad spectrum of functions,
such as SOLIDER [129] utilises prior knowledge from human
images to create pseudo‐semantic labels and integrates more
semantic information into the learnt feature representation. By
introducing a conditional network with a semantic controller,
after model training, users can input different parameter values
into the controller to generate feature representations with
varying degrees of semantic information. This approach ad-
dresses the challenge that different downstream tasks often
require varying proportions of semantic and appearance
information.

However, human‐centric models still require fine‐tuning
for each specific downstream task, which leads to a unique
model for different tasks. Ci et al. [22] propose an omni Model
for Human‐Centric Perceptions (UniHCP), which unifies
various human‐centric tasks with the head‐up converter ar-

across all human‐centred tasks and datasets to extract gener-
alised human‐centric knowledge. UniHCP defines task‐specific
queries shared across all datasets with the exact task definition
to produce outputs for various tasks using the omni model.
These queries are transformed into different output units
through a task‐guided interpreter shared across different
datasets and tasks. Using task‐specific queries and a versatile
interpreter, UniHCP avoids widely used task‐specific output
heads, minimising task‐specific parameters for knowledge
sharing and enabling reusable backbone encoding features
across different tasks.
Although UniHCP performs five human‐centric tasks
simultaneously through its model, it still faces difficulties in
handling multi‐modality input and output, as well as modelling
more complex tasks. In response to this issue, Wang et al. [23]
proposed a universal human‐centric knowledge transformer
named Hulk. Hulk compresses diverse task inputs and outputs
into four modalities, treating different human‐centric tasks as
modality conversion tasks to unify data formats and task
modelling. To avoid using different task heads, the approach
employs tokenisers/de‐tokenisers for the four modalities to
handle various human‐centred tasks. Furthermore, Hulk re-
structures diverse human‐centred multimodal tasks into mo-
dality conversion tasks and consistently integrates differently
designed
task‐specific
models
into
an
encoder–decoder
architecture.
Omni models play an important role in computer science
and artificial intelligence. By integrating multiple tasks or
datasets, the omni model can realise cross‐task knowledge
sharing and task execution. The scope of tasks covered by the
omni model and its model capability are limited by resource
constraints and training costs. Overcoming these obstacles still
requires a lot of future work. Despite these limitations, the
omni model will have more inclusive and efficient model
performance in the future.

The advantages and disadvantages of re‐
identification using optimisation learning
In re‐identification (Re‐ID) tasks, optimising learning methods
through careful design and adjustment can enhance model
performance.
Fine‐tuning leverages models pre‐trained on large datasets,
which have already learnt some general features. This can
accelerate training and improve performance compared to
training from scratch. Fine‐tuning significantly reduces training
time and computational resources. However, if the fine‐tuning
dataset is small, the model may overfit the new dataset, leading
to a decrease in generalisation performance.
Different encoder architectures can be used for various re‐
identification tasks. By designing encoders tailored to re‐
identification tasks, more distinctive and robust features can
be extracted, thus improving model performance. However,
designing and debugging encoders are a complex process that
requires extensive experimentation and validation. Additionally,
complex encoder designs may incur high computational costs.
Loss design involves creating appropriate loss functions
that clearly define optimisation goals, enabling the model to
learn better to distinguish between different identities and
significantly enhancing the performance of re‐identification
tasks. Designing practical loss functions requires a deep un-
derstanding of the task and data characteristics, and various
tasks may require different loss functions. Moreover, balancing
the weights of different loss functions requires meticulous
adjustment.
Post‐processing steps, such as re‐ranking, can further
improve the accuracy of initial retrieval results, especially in
cases where the data is noisy or the initial results are subop-
timal. Re‐ranking can effectively compensate for the short-
comings
of
the
initial
ranking
by
utilising
additional
information (such as local features and contextual informa-
tion). However, re‐ranking typically involves additional com-
putations on the initial results, increasing computational costs
and time consumption. The effectiveness of re‐ranking also
depends on the quality of the initial retrieval results. If the
initial results are poor, the improvement brought by re‐ranking
is limited.

Vehicle datasets
The VeRi‐776 [139] consists of 49,357 vehicle images from 776
unique vehicle IDs. It is divided into two subsets: a training set
and a test set. The training set comprises 576 vehicles with a
total of 37,781 images, while the test set includes 200 vehicles
with a total of 11,579 images. During the testing process, a
cross‐camera search was conducted, meaning that images of a
vehicle from one camera were used to search for the trajectory
of the same vehicle in other cameras. Additionally, there were
1678 query images and 2021 test trajectories for image‐to‐
trajectory searches in the dataset.
The VehicleID dataset [140] comprises 221,763 images
from 26,267 vehicles with vehicles positioned in either a frontal
or rear view. During evaluation, the dataset is divided into three
test subsets based on the vehicle size (small, medium, and
large). For each subset, one image of each person is randomly
chosen for the gallery set, while the remaining images serve as
the query set.
The VeRi‐Wild dataset [141] was acquired from an exten-
sive CCTV camera network comprising 174 cameras, operating
continuously for one month (30  24h) in unconstrained
scenarios. These cameras are strategically placed across a vast
urban area covering more than 200 km2. Utilising YOLO‐v2,
this dataset performed vehicle detection to obtain bounding
boxes for the vehicles. The initial dataset consisted of 12
million vehicle images, which underwent a thorough cleaning
and annotation process conducted by 11 volunteers over the
course of one month. After this process, it contains 416,314
vehicle images corresponding to 40,671 distinct identities.
Both VD1 [142] and VD2 [142] are compiled from frontal
perspectives of vehicles, sourced from traffic cameras and
surveillance footage, respectively. Each vehicle image in these
datasets is accompanied by attribute vectors detailing the ve-
hicle's colour, model, and identification number. VD1 consists
of 846,358 images showing 141,756 vehicles across 11 colours
and 1232 models, split into training and testing subsets. VD2,
on the other hand, has 807,260 images depicting 79,763 ve-
hicles across 11 colours and 1112 models. These datasets
present a simplified version of the vehicle ReID challenge, as
the majority of images are from a single viewpoint. Conse-
quently, the performance metrics on these datasets have
reached a plateau with a recent approach achieving accuracy
rates of 97.8% on VD1 and 95.5% on VD2, respectively.
The VRIC dataset [143] is comprised of 60,430 images
representing 5622 unique vehicle identities, gathered by an
elaborate surveillance system equipped with 60 cameras. These
cameras operate around the clock, capturing complex traffic
scenarios in various light conditions. Spanning 24 different
surveillance spots, the dataset captures a broad spectrum of
vehicle appearances, accounting for differences in image res-
olution, motion blur, weather variations, and occlusions. The
dataset is partitioned into training and test subsets. The training
set comprises 54,808 images corresponding to 2811 vehicle
identities, while the test set includes 5622 images of the same
2811 identities. Each image in the dataset is meticulously an-
notated with information about the car model, colour, and
type.
The CityFlow dataset [147] was gathered by forty non‐
overlapping surveillance cameras. It features 229,680 vehicle
images capturing 666 unique vehicle identities, with each
vehicle recorded by at least two cameras. Additionally, a subset
of CityFlow, named CityFlowReID, has been created for
image‐based vehicle ReID. CityFlowReID comprises 56,277
vehicle images representing 666 different vehicle identities,
with an average of 4.55 camera views per identity. It includes a
training set with 333 vehicle identities and 36,935 images, as
well as a testing set with 333 vehicles and 18,290 images.
The RGBN300 dataset [144] comprises 50,125 pairs of
images, each pair featuring a vehicle in both RGB and near‐
infrared modalities. The images for each vehicle range in
quantity from 50 to 200 pairs. The dataset offers a minimum
of 2 and a maximum of 8 camera views per vehicle, averaging
out to approximately 6.7 views per vehicle. A total of 150
vehicles, represented by 25,200 image pairs, have been
randomly chosen for the training set, while the remaining 150
vehicles, with 24,925 image pairs, form the testing set, also
known as the gallery. Additionally, we have randomly selected
4985 image pairs from the gallery to serve as the query or
probe set. To comprehensively assess the robustness of the
proposed multi‐spectral vehicle Re‐ID approach in the sub-
sequent section, this paper has captured an additional 17,250
TIR (Thermal Infrared Radiation) images of 100 vehicles from
the RGBN300 dataset. These supplementary TIR images,
along with their corresponding RGB‐NIR image pairs, form a
dataset comprising 17,250 image triples named RGBNT100.
In RGBNT100, the training set consists of 50 vehicles with
8675 image triples, while the testing/gallery set comprises the
remaining 50 vehicles with 8575 image pairs from which 1715
image triples have been randomly selected as the query/
probe set.

4.1

Multi‐modality re‐identification
Multi‐modality
re‐identification
refers
to
re‐identification
tasks that retrieve targets based on data from multiple
different modalities. With the advancement of technology,
various monitoring devices and methods have been invented
and applied in surveillance systems. This also produces
different types of multi‐modality surveillance data. As the
types of devices and data increase, the demand for re‐
identification tasks using multiple modalities of data is also
growing.
Currently, multi‐modality re‐identification tasks are typi-
cally limited to two or very few modalities, such as visible‐to‐
infrared cross‐modality re‐identification and text‐to‐image re‐
identification. The former is adapted for infrared surveillance
cameras used in low‐light scenarios, enabling re‐identification
tasks between RGB images and infrared images. The latter is
associated with the popular AIGC technology, enhancing the
generality of re‐identification tasks in different scenarios
through short descriptive texts. However, most of the current
work only uses English text, and the support for other lan-
guages is weak.
However, communication between only two modalities
is insufficient to meet the current situation with multiple
modalities of information. Enhancing the generality of re‐
identification models across various modalities is an urgent
issue.
Drawing
from
the
approach
of
cross‐modality
re‐identification tasks, the text‐to‐image re‐identification task
can be expanded in terms of text language variety. For example,
in criminal investigations, law enforcement agencies rely on
suspect photos and witness testimonies to narrow the search
scope and supplement suspect information. Descriptions of the
same target image might come from speakers of different native
languages. Therefore, supporting different language texts will
undoubtedly become one of the required capabilities of future
re‐identification tasks. Beyond this, re‐identification methods
that can simultaneously process multiple language texts and
various modality image data will meet higher‐level demands in
the re‐identification field.
As it develops, multi‐modality re‐identification tasks will be
able to support multiple languages, multiple image types, or
more types of data simultaneously. And it will have a significant
impact on global criminal investigations and disaster rescue
operations.

recognition. The design philosophy of a multi‐task omni model
is to enhance efficiency and performance by utilising shared
representations and parameters. It allows multiple tasks to
share the same model parameters and representations, reducing
memory and computational resource usage. Additionally,
different tasks can learn from each other, using knowledge
from one task to improve performance in another.
Currently, omni models can use a single model to simul-
taneously perform different deep learning tasks. But after
training, they can only handle a fixed number of tasks. How-
ever, designing and training an omni model that can effectively

Real‐time re‐identification
Real‐time re‐identification refers to the ability to infer results
with lower requirements and faster speed. In practical applica-
tions, whether a method can be widely adopted in real life or not
depends on its accuracy. More importantly, its ability should
meet the efficiency demands of the application domain.
Multiple‐detection can significantly improve detection accuracy;
even a method with 60% accuracy can ensure at least 90% ac-
curacy after triple verification. Therefore, the inference speed of
the model dramatically influences the evaluation of a method's
merits.
In recent years, re‐identification (ReID) research has mainly
focused on improving accuracy and robustness rather than real‐
time performance. This has led to a lack of comprehensive
studies comparing the inference times of various ReID models
under the same conditions. Researchers typically benchmark
efficiency based on different scales, sizes, or methods with their
models but rarely systematically compare other methods' infer-
ence times. This highlights the need for more research and
transparent reporting on the real‐time capabilities of re‐
identification models.
In network structure optimisation, neural architecture
search (NAS) and knowledge distillation are two commonly
used methods. Building upon current re‐identification models,
neural architecture search utilises evolutionary algorithms like
genetic algorithms to progressively optimise model structures.
Through operations such as selection and crossover, NAS can
provide a new candidate structure. Alternatively, knowledge
distillation transfers ‘knowledge’ from a large, complex re‐
identification model to a smaller, simpler model, aiming to
imbue the smaller model with as much of the generalisation
ability of the larger model as possible.
Real‐time performance is crucial in many application sce-
narios. Whether it is tracking targets in surveillance systems or
identifying pedestrians in autonomous driving, speed is an
essential factor. Therefore, future research should not only
continue to improve model accuracy but also make break-
throughs in inference speed and computational efficiency to
meet the multifaceted demands of real‐world applications.
4.4
|
Privacy protection of re‐identification
Although pedestrian re‐identification technology holds great
potential for enhancing security surveillance, it raises concerns
about personal privacy protection. Similar to the widespread
application of facial recognition, the use of pedestrian re‐
identification involves the collection and storage of sensitive
personal information.
In current re‐identification datasets, pedestrian or vehicle
data that have not been anonymised are still present. This
could lead to risks such as privacy breaches, unauthorised ac-
cess, sale, or misuse of personal data for other inappropriate
purposes.
Firstly, regarding datasets used for re‐identification tasks,
creators should anonymise the datasets before release. For
example, in vehicle re‐identification datasets, licence plates
should be obscured, and facial blurring should be applied to
pedestrians. Secondly, adversarial attack algorithms can be used
to assess the performance of re‐identification models when
facing manipulated or forged input data, aiding in the detection
and mitigation of potential security vulnerabilities in practical
applications. In real‐world scenarios, re‐identification methods
using adversarial attack algorithms can also be used to defend
against adversarial attacks, such as identifying and rejecting
adversarial samples, thereby enhancing the security and reli-
ability of re‐identification systems.
In conclusion, the importance of privacy protection in re‐
identification technology cannot be underestimated. It con-
cerns individual rights and directly affects the sustainable
development and societal acceptance of technology. Only by
ensuring adequate privacy protection can pedestrian re‐
identification technology genuinely play its role in security
surveillance, achieving a win‐win situation between techno-
logical advancement and social ethics.
