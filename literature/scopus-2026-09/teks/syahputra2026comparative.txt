Abstract. The titration method is the conventional approach for measuring free 
fatty acid (FFA) content in oil palm fruit but is destructive and time-consuming. 
Since FFA is a critical quality parameter in Indonesia’s palm oil industry, there is 
an urgent need for rapid, non-destructive alternatives. This study presents a 
comparative analysis of two non-invasive sensing technologies for estimating oil 
palm FFA: a portable visible–near infrared (VIS-NIR) spectrometer and a 
multispectral imaging system. Fruit samples at various maturity stages were 
analyzed using both methods. The VIS-NIR spectrometer captured spectral 
reIlectance in the red band (660–670 nm), while the multispectral camera 
provided corresponding image intensity features. Principal Component Analysis 
(PCA) of the red band reIlectance showed that PC1 accounted for 98.47% of the 
variance, clearly separating all ripeness classes. Regression analysis conIirmed 
strong correlations with FFA content, with the VIS-NIR spectrometer achieving R² 
= 0.85 and multispectral imaging R² = 0.79. Expanding the dataset across multiple 
cultivars and environmental conditions, combined with advanced multivariate 
modeling, could further improve prediction accuracy. These Iindings demonstrate 
that both technologies can provide accurate, rapid, and non-destructive estimation 
of FFA, supporting real-time quality monitoring and improved harvest 
management in Indonesia’s palm oil sector. 
1. Introduction 
The oil palm industry is a signiIicant contributor to the agricultural economies of Southeast Asia, 
particularly in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand (1). Indonesia, with an oil palm plantation area 
covering 16,833,985 hectares, stands as one of the world’s largest producers of palm oil. The 
country’s annual production reaches 47,084 thousand tons in 2023 (2). As of 2024, global palm 
oil production is estimated at 78.93 million metric tons, with Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand 
being the top three producers. Indonesia is the leading producer, contributing 46 million metric

production of 19.4 million metric tons, with key producing states such as Sabah, Sarawak, Pahang, 
Johor, and Perak. Thailand is the third-largest producer, generating 3.33 million metric tons, 
mainly from its southern region (3). This sector contributes substantially to national GDPs. 
The oil palm (Elaeis guineensis) is the leading oil-producing crop globally. It produces two 
distinct and commercially important types of oil: palm oil, which is extracted from the mesocarp 
of the fruit, and palm kernel oil, which is derived from the inner seed kernel (4). The consistent 
global demand for palm oil is driven by its wide range of applications, including food production, 
cosmetics, soaps, detergents, and personal care products (5). Ensuring the quality of palm oil is 
therefore of critical importance. Various factors, such as fruit maturity and harvesting methods, 
can signiIicantly inIluence the physicochemical properties of the oil, particularly its Free Fatty Acid 
(FFA) content. FFA is a key parameter used to assess palm oil quality. Delayed harvesting or over-
ripeness of fruit can result in elevated FFA levels (6). This condition promotes the hydrolysis of 
triglycerides, leading to the release of free fatty acids and a consequent reduction in oil quality. 
High FFA content lowers the commercial value of the oil due to its increased susceptibility to 
rancidity and a shorter shelf life. Therefore, monitoring and controlling FFA levels is essential for 
maintaining high-quality palm oil production (7). 
The traditional method for analysing FFA content in palm oil involves the Soxhlet extraction 
and titration technique, which is a multi-stage, labour-intensive, and time-consuming process (8). 
In this procedure, the mesocarp of the oil palm fruit is Iirst separated, and the oil is subsequently 
extracted. The FFA content is then quantiIied through titration, in which a known concentration 
of alkali is used to neutralize the free fatty acids. The volume of alkali required is measured to 
calculate the FFA concentration (9). Although this method is widely regarded as reliable and 
accurate, it is inherently destructive, requires extended processing time, and is unsuitable for real-
time monitoring. Furthermore, it demands substantial labour input and involves the use of 
hazardous chemicals. These limitations reduce the practicality of the technique, thereby 
underscoring the need for non-destructive and rapid methods for palm oil quality assessment. 
Recent advancements in non-destructive technologies, including portable VIS-NIR 
spectrometers (10), multispectral imaging systems (11), and Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) 
spectroscopy (12), present promising alternatives for assessing quality parameters in agricultural 
commodities. These methods have been applied to analyse chlorophyll content (13), volatile 
compounds (14), and even internal fruit characteristics, such as ripeness (15,16), without the 
need for sample extraction or destruction. By capturing spectral data from the surface and 
applying advanced analytical algorithms, these technologies offer a rapid, reliable, and non-
invasive way to assess quality parameters across a range of agricultural products, improving 
efIiciency in quality control and reducing the need for destructive testing methods. Although VIS-
NIR spectroscopy has proven effective for laboratory-based FFA measurement (17) and 
multispectral imaging is widely used for ripeness grading (18), no published work has directly 
compared these two portable sensors for in-situ FFA estimation on intact oil-palm fruitlets. 
Existing studies focus either on extracted oil or use benchtop instruments unsuited to plantation 
environments. This study aims to address this gap by comparing a portable VIS-NIR spectrometer 
and a multispectral imaging system for non-destructive estimation of oil palm fruit quality, with a 
focus on FFA content. The goal is to evaluate their feasibility as rapid, in-situ, and Iield-practical 
alternatives to conventional destructive methods.

2. Materials and Method 
2.1 Sample collection 
Fresh oil palm fruit samples (Elaeis guineensis, Tenera variety) were collected from the Teaching 
Factory of Palm Oil, University of Jember, located in Bondowoso, Indonesia. Eight Fresh Fruit 
Bunches (FFBs) were selected to represent four distinct ripeness stages: unripe, under-ripe, ripe, 
and over-ripe, with two bunches per category. Ripeness classiIication was based on standard 
physical indicators such as fruit colour, Iirmness, and detachment level, as shown in Figure 1. From 
each FFB, ten fruitlets were randomly selected from the middle region of the bunch, which is 
considered the most representative of overall ripeness (Figure 2). This resulted in a total of 80 
fruitlets (10 per bunch), with 20 fruitlets representing each ripeness class. 
2.2 Spectral Acquisition

reIlectance spectra of oil palm fruitlets in the range of 400–1020 nm. This device was selected 
because it enables fast, accurate, and non-destructive measurement. In this study, the spectral data 
were used to estimate the FFA levels of the fruitlets. The estimation was carried out indirectly by 
analysing the reIlected light from the fruit surface, which carries information related to the 
internal chemical composition. The spectrometer operated using the reIlectance method (19), 
where light from a halogen source was directed onto the fruitlet surface and the reIlected light was

2.3 Image Acquisition 
For image-based analysis, a Parrot Sequoia multispectral camera was used. The camera includes 
a monochrome sensor with 1.2-megapixel resolution and 10-bit depth, which allows the capture 
of narrowband images at speciIic wavelengths. These spectral bands are particularly useful for 
identifying differences in fruit characteristics that are linked to FFA levels. Multispectral images 
provide more detailed information that can be related to the chemical quality of the fruit. The 
imaging process was conducted inside a custom-built lighting box measuring 15 × 15 × 15 cm. The 
box was 3D printed using black Iilament, which helped to minimise reIlection and block ambient 
light interference. The fruitlets were placed on a non-reIlective surface to reduce background 
effects. Illumination was provided by LED light strips Iixed inside the box, ensuring stable and 
uniform brightness. The light intensity was carefully maintained at 240 ± 2 lux to provide 
consistent imaging conditions for all samples (20). The image acquisition setup is shown in Figure 
3(b). 
2.4 FFA Analysis

(SNI 01-2901-2006) (21). The mesocarp was Iirst chopped into small pieces, then dried in an oven 
at 60°C for 24 hours. After drying, the oil was extracted using the Soxhlet method. The FFA content 
was determined through titration using a standardized sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. The

3. Results and Discussion 
3.1 VIS-NIR Measurement 
The VIS-NIR reIlectance spectra showed clear differences among fruitlets at various ripeness 
stages, with unripe samples exhibiting lower reIlectance than more mature fruitlets. This 
difference was most pronounced in the 550–750 nm range, where distinct separation occurred 
among unripe, under-ripe, ripe, and over-ripe fruitlets as presented in Figure 4. The increase in 
reIlectance with ripening is associated with biochemical and structural changes, including 
chlorophyll degradation, carotenoid accumulation, and reductions in moisture content, which 
alter both surface and internal optical properties (22). The red wavelength region between 660–
670 nm is especially sensitive to changes in fruit maturity (17), as conIirmed by the observed 
differences in average reIlectance across ripeness categories (Table 1). The clear separation in this 
region suggests that reIlectance in the red band can serve as a reliable proxy for monitoring 
ripeness-related chemical transformations that inIluence FFA content. In addition, the near-
infrared region (900–1000 nm), although showing some Iluctuations, may reIlect variations in 
moisture (23) and oil content, both of which are indirectly linked to FFA development. These 
spectral features demonstrate that VIS-NIR spectroscopy captures both surface pigment changes 
and internal compositional shifts relevant to oil quality. 
To further evaluate variability and ripeness discrimination, Principal Component Analysis 
(PCA) was performed on the 660–670 nm spectral range (Figure 5). The Iirst principal component 
(PC1) accounted for 98.47% of the total variance, capturing the primary differences among 
fruitlets due to ripeness. PC2 explained only 0.19% of the variance, indicating minimal additional 
variability beyond PC1. The PCA score plot shows clear clustering of unripe, under-ripe, ripe, and 
over-ripe fruitlets, conIirming that reIlectance in the 660–670 nm region is sufIicient to 
distinguish maturity levels.

3.3 FFA Analysis Result 
The laboratory-measured FFA values for the oil palm fruitlets are provided in Table 3. These values 
were used as the reference for evaluating the relationship between fruit ripeness and non-
destructive measurements obtained from the VIS-NIR spectrometer and multispectral imaging. 
The relationship between laboratory result of FFA values was evaluated using both the VIS-NIR 
spectrometer and multispectral imaging data. The performance of each method was assessed by 
calculating the R². As shown in Figure 7, the regression analysis demonstrates that the 
spectrometer-based approach achieved an R² value of 0.85, indicating a strong correlation 
between reIlectance and measured FFA content. Meanwhile, the multispectral image-based 
method yielded an R² of 0.79, showing a moderately strong predictive capability based on pixel 
intensity values. These results conIirm that both non-destructive techniques are capable of 
estimating FFA levels, with the VIS-NIR spectrometer providing slightly higher predictive accuracy.

correlations. The strong correlation observed between FFA values, and the red band reIlectance 
or image intensity can be explained by the biochemical processes during ripening. As fruit 
matures, chlorophyll degrades and carotenoids accumulate, while lipids in the mesocarp undergo

4. Conclusion 
This study demonstrated that a portable VIS-NIR spectrometer and multispectral imaging can 
effectively estimate oil palm fruitlet FFA non-destructively. ReIlectance in the red band (660–670 
nm) and corresponding image intensity clearly distinguished all ripeness classes, supported by 
PCA where PC1 explained 98.47% of the total variance and PC2 only 0.19%. Both methods showed 
strong correlations with laboratory-measured FFA content, with the VIS-NIR spectrometer 
achieving R² = 0.85 and the multispectral imaging method R² = 0.79. Despite the limited number 
of samples with two replicates, the consistency of spectral, image-based, and PCA trends conIirms 
the reliability of these non-destructive approaches. Future work will involve Iield trials under 
variable sunlight, expanding the dataset to over 500 fruitlets across multiple cultivars, and 
applying multivariate models such as Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) and ArtiIicial 
Neural Networks (ANN) to reduce prediction error below 5%. These efforts aim to transition from 
laboratory proof-of-concept to plantation-scale quality assurance, supporting sustainable and 
proIitable management in Southeast Asia’s palm-oil sector.
