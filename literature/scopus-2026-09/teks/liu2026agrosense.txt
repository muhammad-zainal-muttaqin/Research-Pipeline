A B S T R A C T
Precision orchard management requires robust sensing systems that operate reliably under unstructured field 
conditions while providing actionable, tree-level information. Despite advances in computer vision and edge 
computing, most approaches focus on isolated tasks and lack integration into deployable, orchard-scale systems. 
This study presents Next-Generation Agrosense (Agrosense v2), an AI-enabled, system-oriented framework for 
real-time orchard sensing and tree-level monitoring. Agrosense v2 integrates tree detection and counting, canopy 
density classification, tree height estimation (inherited from Agrosense v1, not validated in detail in this study), 
and detection-based fruit counting on both the tree and the ground into a unified pipeline. Compared with the 
previous version (Agrosense v1), Agrosense v2 improves tree identification through temporal tracking, 
strengthens canopy density classification by leveraging image sequences, and adds fruit counting via image-level 
detection, with all outputs aggregated into tree-level spatiotemporal summaries, presented by a self-developed 
tree-level visualization tool. Field experiments in three citrus orchards with contrasting management condi­
tions totaled 3,696 trees across two commercial orchards (Field A&B) and a research station (SWFREC). Tree 
counting accuracies were 95.96% (SWFREC), 92.73% (Field A), and 97.79% (Field B), and canopy density 
classification accuracies were 94.1% to 96.8%. For fruit detection, the transformer-based RT-DETR model ach­
ieved the highest mAP@0.5 up to 0.837 and 0.722 for fruits on the tree and ground, respectively, demonstrating 
robustness under dense canopy and cluttered ground conditions. To our knowledge, Agrosense v2 is the first real- 
time, field-deployable orchard sensing system that delivers multiple tree-level attributes within a single frame­
work. By unifying perception, inference, and geo-referenced aggregation, Agrosense v2 provides a scalable 
foundation for data-driven precision orchard management.
1. Introduction
Precision orchard management increasingly relies on accurate, 
timely, and scalable sensing systems to support tree-level monitoring, 
yield estimation, and data-driven field operations (El-Ansary, 2025; 
Sishodia et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Among tree crops, citrus or­
chards represent a particularly challenging production environment due 
to dense canopies, complex branch structures, and highly variable fruit 
visibility. These characteristics make citrus orchards an ideal testbed for 
evaluating next-generation orchard sensing systems under real-world 
conditions (Costa et al., 2021; Dian et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2023). 
Recent advances in sensing hardware and artificial intelligence (AI) 
have enabled the collection and analysis of large volumes of orchard 
data using vision-based (Bulanon et al., 2025; Longchamps et al., 2022) 
and edge-computing platforms (Jiao et al., 2025; Rui et al., 2024), 
creating new opportunities for precision agriculture applications.
Despite this progress, robust orchard-scale deployment remains 
challenging. Visual sensing in orchards is affected by occlusions (Lu and 
Sang, 2015), illumination variability (Choi et al., 2016), motion-induced 
blur (Huang et al., 2025), and spatial variation across trees and rows 
(Perry et al., 2010). In traditional citrus orchards, these challenges are 
further compounded by complex three-dimensional canopy structures,

which must not only achieve acceptable accuracy but also operate 
reliably, efficiently, and continuously in unstructured outdoor envi­
ronments (Lacerda et al., 2025; Trentin and Ampatzidis, 2024). As a 
result, performance demonstrated in controlled experimental settings 
often degrades when systems are deployed in real orchards, highlighting 
the gap between algorithm development and practical application (Liu

Existing research has largely focused on individual components of 
orchard sensing and analysis pipelines. Representative studies have 
investigated fruit detection using deep learning-based object detection 
models (Zhang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021), image- or video-based 
fruit counting (Wang et al., 2018; Gan et al., 2018; Malik et al., 2016), 
and yield estimation at the tree or plot level (Dorj et al., 2017, Trentin 
et al., 2024; Vijayakumar et al., 2023), as well as sensor-driven or vision- 
guided systems for orchard operations such as precision spraying (Partel 
et al., 2021; Khot et al., 2012). While these approaches have demon­
strated promising results within their respective scopes, above 70% of 
these studies were typically developed as independent solutions tar­
geting only one or two components of the orchard sensing pipeline 
instead of an overall monitoring. The lack of integration across 
perception, inference, and downstream deployment limits their scal­
ability and hinders adoption in operational orchard environments (Liu 
and Ampatzidis, 2026; Rial-Lovera, 2018).
From a system perspective, effective orchard management requires 
more than isolated algorithmic advances. Practical deployment de­
mands a unified framework capable of supporting continuous sensing, 
aggregating information at the tree-level, and interfacing with down­
stream decision-making and actuation processes. In such systems, 
perception modules must generate reliable and interpretable outputs 
that can be consumed by higher-level inference components, while 
satisfying real-time constraints and hardware limitations imposed by 
field-deployable sensing platforms (Duckett et al., 2018; Oliveira et al., 
2021). These requirements motivate a modular and extensible system 
design that explicitly bridges algorithm development and real-world 
orchard deployment.
To address these needs, the primary objective of this study is to 
develop and evaluate Next-Generation Agrosense (Agrosense v2), an AI- 
enabled, field-deployable sensing system that integrates perception, 
inference, and spatial aggregation into a unified real-time framework for 
tree-level orchard monitoring. Specifically, this study aims to: (1) sup­
port reliable perception of orchard features, including tree counting, 
canopy density classification, and visible fruit detection under real or­
chard conditions; (2) enable tree-level inference through stable tree 
identification and aggregation of frame-level observations during field 
traversal; and (3) generate geo-referenced tree-level representations for 
orchard monitoring and management. While a complete orchard auto­
mation pipeline would also include actuation, this study focuses on the 
perception and inference as the critical foundation for scalable, data- 
driven orchard decision-making. To the best of our knowledge at the 
time of this publication, this study introduces the first citrus orchard 
sensing system using a side-view mobile platform capable of simulta­
neously and in real time collecting multiple forms of tree-level, practi­
cally actionable information within a single framework. Here, a unified 
framework refers to a sensing pipeline that integrates data acquisition, 
preprocessing, and inference from onboard vision without separate 
sensing systems or offline post-processing. These objectives are directly 
motivated by practical orchard-management needs. Stable tree-level 
identification supports orchard inventory and longitudinal monitoring, 
canopy density classification supports assessment of tree condition and 
structure, fruit detection contributes to yield-related assessment, and 
geo-referenced aggregation enables spatial inspection of orchard 
variability.
Building upon earlier orchard sensing efforts (Zhou et al., 2025), 
Agrosense v2 extends prior work toward a more integrated orchard 
sensing framework. Compared with Agrosense v1, which relied pri­
marily on frame-level outputs and did not integrate fruit counting or a 
real-time tree-level visualization, Agrosense v2 introduces four key ad­
vances: (1) temporal trunk tracking for stable tree identification across 
frames; (2) sequence-based canopy density inference rather than single- 
image classification; (3) integrated fruit detection with tree-level ag­
gregation of on-tree and on-ground fruit observations; and (4) a user- 
friendly geo-referenced visualization workflow for real-time tree-level 
orchard monitoring. The system explicitly accounts for partial canopy 
visibility, viewpoint variation, and operational constraints inherent to 
mobile orchard sensing platforms (Azizi et al., 2024; Mena et al., 2024; 
Porwollik et al., 2010). By unifying multiple tree-level measurements 
within a single, real-time system, Agrosense v2 moves beyond 
algorithm-centric evaluations and demonstrates a practical sensing 
framework that directly supports orchard-scale situational awareness 
and precision management. This paper presents the Agrosense v2 system 
design and core sensing modules, then evaluates system performance in 
real citrus orchards, and finally discusses the limitations of the current 
study and future directions for scalable orchard deployment. Fig. 1
shows the overall workflow of Agrosense v2.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Agrosense v2 mechanical design and workflow
The Agrosense v2 system is a fully integrated, portable sensing 
platform designed for tree crop monitoring. Its core hardware compo­
nents include an embedded edge computing module Jetson AGX Orin 
(NVIDIA Corporation, Santa Clara, California, USA); a depth-sensing 
unit consisting of two RGB-D cameras, one mounted on each side of 
the platform, using either Intel RealSense D435i (Intel Corporation, 
Santa Clara, California, USA) or ZED X (StereoLabs Inc., San Francisco, 
California, USA) with the 2.2mm lens, for acquiring synchronized 
RGB–depth image pairs based on different field conditions and tasks; 
and a global positioning and inertial sensing unit composed of an RTK- 
enabled GNSS receiver SparkFun GPS-RTK Board (u-blox ZED-F9P, 
SparkFun Electronics, Niwot, Colorado, USA), and 9-axis IMU BNO055 
(Bosch Sensortec GmbH, Reutlingen, Germany) for real-time localiza­
tion. Additional components include a capacitive touch display (Wave­
share Electronics, Shenzhen, China) for user interaction, an extra 
Samsung 990 Pro NVMe M.2 SSD (4 TB; Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd., 
Suwon, South Korea) for high-throughput data storage, and a 
rechargeable lithium battery that enables field operation without 
external power. The device enclosure is custom-designed and fabricated 
using 3D-printed materials to reduce weight while maintaining struc­
tural durability (Fig. 2). The software stack is built on Linux Ubuntu 
22.04 and integrates drivers, data acquisition modules, and our custom 
Agrosense v2 data-collection interface. During field operations, the 
Agrosense v2 unit is mounted at the distal end of an adjustable height 
trunk-extension rack at a height of 1.7 m and oriented laterally toward 
the tree rows, which provides a rigid forward extension from the vehicle 
to position the sensors at a stable, optimal viewpoint relative to the tree 
canopy. At a typical stand-off distance of 2–4 m, the ZED X camera 
covers approximately 5.7–11.4 m in width and 3.4–6.8 m in height, 
allowing a single camera to capture the entire tree. RGB-D image 
acquisition and localization data collection were executed continuously 
using timestamp-based synchronization. Each incoming frame was 
processed online through depth-based preprocessing, tree trunk detec­
tion, bounding-box refinement, and tracking update. Sequence-based 
canopy density inference and tree-level fruit-count aggregation were 
then updated incrementally as tracked observations became available. 
The system was deployed on the Jetson AGX Orin using batch size 1 for 
frame-by-frame inference and was able to keep pace with the 15 FPS 
image acquisition rate used in this study. Fig. 2 presents the Agrosense 
v2 use case in the field and the hardware components.

2.2.1. Tree trunk detection

its end-to-end single-stage detection capabilities. It can simultaneously 
locate and classify targets in a single forward pass, balancing detection 
speed and accuracy. This makes it ideal for tree trunk detection tasks 
that require real-time processing. Furthermore, YOLO has continuously 
optimized its network structure and feature extraction capabilities over 
multiple iterations. It is highly adaptable to small targets, complex 
backgrounds, and occluded scenes (Ali & Zhang, 2024). Its mature open- 
source implementation and extensive deployment solutions facilitate

The trunk detector operated on depth-filtered RGB images in which 
non-target-row regions were masked before training and inference. 
Model training was conducted on a MacBook Pro (Apple Inc., Cupertino,

using Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) as the hardware acceleration 
backend. Training was performed using default hyperparameters with 
an early stop mechanism, and an input image resolution of 640 × 640 
pixels. During deployment, inference was executed on the NVIDIA Jet­

Precision at IoU 0.5 (mAP@0.5), and mean Average Precision across IoU

2.2.2. Detection bounding box selection
The detection outputs from the YOLOv11n model are subsequently 
processed by a counting algorithm, which calculates the total number of 
trees and generates an identity image for each tree to support down­
stream processing. However, because of the structural complexity and 
visual similarity within citrus orchards, the detector may occasionally 
misidentify non-trunk objects, such as branches, ground shadows, or 
other background artifacts, as tree trunks (Fig. 3). To improve the ac­
curacy and efficiency of the counting algorithm, detected bounding 
boxes are refined using a bounding box selection criterion, designed to 
retain the most probable trunk detection while removing false positives. 
This process consists of three main filtering steps. First, only bounding 
boxes whose normalized vertical center satisfied ycenter < thresholdnorm 
and whose normalized area satisfied A = w × h ≥thresholdarea were 
retained, where w and h denote the normalized width and height of the 
bounding box, respectively (Fig. 3a-b). In this study, thresholdnorm = 0.3 
and thresholdarea = 0.005 were selected empirically based on pre­
liminary inspection of detection results from a validation run. These 
thresholds were then fixed for all orchards evaluated in this study. 
Because they are heuristic parameters related to orchard geometry and 
visual appearance, some recalibration may be required when applying 
the system to orchards with substantially different tree sizes, planting 
densities, or ground conditions. This assumes that tree trunks are 
physically connected to the ground and that very small bounding boxes 
are unlikely to correspond to trunk structures. Second, among the 
remaining candidates, only one bounding box that is closest to being 
vertically aligned with the ground is retained (Fig. 3c). This constraint 
reflects the orchard geometry, where only one tree trunk is expected 
along the vertical axis perpendicular to the ground plane within the field 
of view. Third, for the remaining bounding box candidates, a non-black 
pixel ratio is calculated in a predefined region directly above each 
bounding box (Fig. 3d). As shown below, the non-black pixel ratio rt is 
defined as the proportion of pixels with non-zero intensity values within 
this region.

2.2.3. Tree tracking algorithm
Following YOLOv11n detection and bounding box selection, a new 
tracking algorithm was developed to estimate the total number of trees 
in the orchard and to extract a front-view identity image for each tree. 
Tracking is necessary because trees appear across multiple frames dur­
ing vehicle motion, and frame-level detections alone can cause double- 
counting or missed trees under occlusion and viewpoint changes. The 
algorithm incorporates the non-black pixel ratio as an auxiliary feature 
to support robust tracking. In Agrosense v1 (Zhou et al., 2025), the

introducing temporal associations across consecutive frames. By con­
straining detection motion between frames and validating detection

identification of individual trees. The workflow of the proposed tracking 
algorithm is shown in Fig. 4.
To achieve stable cross-frame tree identification and representative 
frame selection, the algorithm first filters detection candidates within 
each frame by extracting the normalized horizontal center coordinate 
(xcenter ∈[0,1], where 0 = left edge and 1 = right edge of the image) of 
every detected object. If only one candidate is detected, its xcenter value is 
directly recorded. If multiple candidates are present, the detection 
whose xcenter is closest to the xcenter of the retained detection in the 
previous frame is selected. This step assumes that trees move gradually 
across the image in small, consistent steps as the platform advances. An 
active tracking list (activetrack) is then maintained to store the contin­
uous trajectory associated with a single tree. Two detections are 
considered to belong to the same tree if the difference in normalized 
horizontal bounding box center between consecutive frames satisfied 
Δxt = |xt −xt−1| < xgap where Δxt is the normal horizontal displacement 
between consecutive frames (dimensionless, Δxt ∈[0, 1]), and xgap =
0.25 (dimensionless, xgap ∈[0, 1]). This threshold was determined 
empirically from validation videos by examining Δxt values for correctly 
matched detections. In addition, the non-black pixel ratio at the image 
center, rt, was required to follow an increasing-then-decreasing trend, 
consistent with a new tree entering and exiting the field of view. This 
pattern is expected because distance-based masking suppresses 
adjacent-row trees, causing the non-black ratio in the image center to 
increase as a tree enters the field of view and decreases as it exits. If these 
conditions are not met, a new tree tracking sequence is initiated. When 
the observation sequence for a tree ends, the representative frame is 
selected from the activetrack as the frame whose xcenter is closest to the 
image center (0.5). This frame is saved as the tree’s identity image. A 
valid tree was defined only when detections persisted for at least five 
consecutive frames, corresponding to approximately 0.33s at the 15 FPS 
acquisition rate used in this study. This criterion was selected empiri­
cally to suppress transient false detections while retaining stable tree 
observations. This threshold was calibrated for the 15 FPS acquisition 
rate and approximately 8 km/h vehicle speed used in this study, 
corresponding to approximately 0.33 s of continuous detection. If the 
frame rate or vehicle speed changes substantially, this parameter should 
be re-tuned to maintain a consistent temporal window of approximately 
0.3–0.5 s; for example, at 30 FPS and the same speed, a threshold of 
approximately 9–10 consecutive frames would be appropriate. Other­
wise, it is discarded as noise or a transient detection. Finally, if any 
tracking data remain unprocessed at the end of the image sequence, the 
same validation and representative frame selection procedure is applied 
to finalize the last tracked tree. After tracking, the frame in which the 
tree is closest to the image center is saved as the tree’s identity image.
2.3. Tree canopy classification algorithm

classified into three classes: Low, medium, and high. Image sequences 
are first obtained through detection and tracking. Canopy density is then 
classified using the YOLOv11 classification model, which reuses the 
YOLOv11 backbone and replaces the detection neck and head with 
global average pooling and a classifier to output image-level canopy

2.4. Fruit counting framework
Fruit detection and counting in orchards are challenging due to 
several real-world factors. Fruits are often small and exhibit colors 
similar to surrounding leaves, causing weak visual contrast (Azizi et al., 
2024; Júnior et al., 2024). Dense and irregular canopies lead to frequent 
partial occlusion, and fruits may appear both on the tree and on the 
ground, introducing large variations in scale and spatial distribution. In 
addition, orchard illumination varies substantially across frames, from 
strong backlighting to diffuse cloudy conditions, making detection 
sensitive to exposure changes and shadows. These characteristics 
necessitate evaluating detectors with different capacities and architec­
tural designs to assess robustness under diverse orchard conditions.
To establish comprehensive baseline performance, five representa­
tive object detection models were selected: YOLOv11n, YOLOv11s, 
YOLOv11m, YOLOv11l, and Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT- 
DETR) (Zhao et al., 2024). The YOLOv11 family provides a structured 
progression of convolutional architectures with increasing model ca­
pacity, ranging from the lightweight n variant to the large-scale l model. 
This allows a systematic analysis of how network size influences 
detection accuracy and generalization under varying canopy densities 
and occlusion levels. Smaller models (YOLOv11n/s) offer insight into 
the feasibility of running fruit detection on edge devices, while larger 
models (YOLOv11m/l) approximate upper-bound performance under 
high-capacity settings. In contrast, RT-DETR represents a transformer- 
based real-time detection architecture, incorporating global attention 
mechanisms that are designed to improve robustness in cluttered envi­
ronments. Including RT-DETR enables direct comparison between con­
volutional and transformer-based paradigms, particularly in scenarios 
where fruits are embedded in dense foliage or distributed irregularly 
across the canopy. Together, these five models provide a balanced and 
complementary set of baselines for evaluating fruit detection perfor­
mance across complex orchard conditions. All the models were trained 
using default hyperparameters, with an input resolution of 1,024 ×
1,024 pixels. Early stopping was applied based on validation loss. 
Inference was performed using default confidence and Non-Maximum 
Suppression (NMS) thresholds for each model.

Detection performance was assessed using standard object detection 
metrics, including precision, recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95. For 
tree-level fruit counting analysis, ground-truth tree-level fruit counts 
were not available because exhaustive manual field counting was not 
conducted. Instead, reference counts were obtained through manual 
visual counting of fruits in the images, which is a commonly used proxy

3. Experimental design
3.1. Data collection
Data collection was conducted at three locations with distinct man­
agement conditions: (i) an experimental orchard with controlled 
experimental blocks at the Southwest Florida Research and Education 
Center (SWFREC); (ii) Field A, a poorly managed commercial orchard; 
and (iii) Field B, a well-maintained commercial orchard. Fig. 8 presents 
the satellite overview and target collection areas of each field.
All data from all orchards were acquired under natural illumination, 
covering both sunny and cloudy conditions, with a mixture of front-lit 
and back-lit scenarios to capture diverse lighting variations. The Agro­
sense v2 system was mounted on a truck traveling at approximately 8 
km/h (5 mph) between the citrus tree rows. A camera was utilized on 
each side of the Agrosense v2 to capture side-view RGB and depth im­
ages at 15 FPS. At SWFREC, the Intel RealSense D435i was used for RGB- 
D acquisition, with a resolution of 640 × 480. The later collections at 
field A and field B employed the StereoLabs ZED X, whose higher- 
resolution 1,920 × 1,200 images, higher mounting viewpoint, and 
wider field-of-view enable the entire tree canopy to be covered in a 
single frame, which is beneficial for consistent fruit detection and can­
opy analysis. Both cameras were used with the factory- or SDK-provided 
calibration parameters available at runtime. Both data collection plat­
forms were equipped with an RTK-enabled GNSS receiver and an IMU 
for real-time localization. The IMU and GPS signals were synchronized 
using timestamps. Because the current implementation did not rely on 
explicit cross-camera geometric fusion between the two side-mounted 
cameras, additional project-specific inter-camera extrinsic calibration 
parameters were not used as core inputs in the present study. The total 
number of collected RGB-D frames across the three orchards is in the 
order of tens of thousands per field and exceeds several hundred thou­
sand frames in total.
Data at the SWFREC were collected on May 27 and July 22, 2025, 
covering 32 rows and 1,884 trees. The first collection occurred before 
fruiting, and no further collection could be performed later due to 
disease-induced tree removal. Data from Field A was collected on 
October 30, 2025 (ten rows, 948 trees), and data from Field B was 
collected on November 19, 2025 (ten rows, 864 trees).

mAP@0.5 of 0.973 (Table 2). The optimal confidence threshold was 
determined to be 0.513, at which the average F1-score reached 0.98, 
reflecting a well-balanced trade-off between precision and recall. Most 
missed detections (representative examples in Fig. 10) occurred when

False positives were the second major source of error. Typical cases

As shown in Fig. 10a-c, the detector sometimes produces false positives 
on shadows, branches, and other vertical farm objects. In some cases, the 
trunk is not detected at all (Fig. 10d). To mitigate these errors, a 
bounding box selection filter was applied after detection. The filter 
removes boxes that are too small, retains only the vertical bounding box 
closest to the ground when multiple candidates appear (Fig. 10e), and 
rejects bounding boxes with a low non-black pixel ratio (Fig. 10f). In 
practice, this filtering step reduced false positives by approximately 
85%, significantly simplifying the subsequent tracking stage. However, 
certain challenging false positives (e.g., Fig. 10g) remain, particularly 
when a vertical branch satisfies all filter criteria. These cases are 
resolved later by the tracking algorithm to avoid double-counting. When 
no bounding box is produced by the detector (Fig. 10h), the selection 
filter is not applied.

In contrast, Agrosense v2, when relying on trunk detection alone, 
substantially overcounted trees across all test fields, with predicted tree 
counts averaging 130% of the ground-truth values. Although the de­

that strong detection performance alone is insufficient for reliable tree 
enumeration in continuous field traversal scenarios. By incorporating 
the bounding box selection criteria, the overall tree count accuracy 
improved to an average of 87.57%. However, this approach remained 
sensitive to detection-level errors, including frame skipping and dupli­

grating the tracking algorithm, Agrosense v2 achieved an average 
accuracy of 95.49%, the highest among all tested configurations. The 
tracking mechanism associates detections across consecutive frames and 
evaluates the full temporal trajectory of each tree, effectively reducing 
the impact of local detection errors and improving system robustness. 
This approach addresses duplicate counting, mitigates the effects of 
frame skipping, and enables reliable tree counting based on multiple 
observations rather than a single frame. In addition, the use of the non- 
black ratio metric helps alleviate cases where trunks are partially 
obscured by large canopies or shadows, supporting more stable counting 
under real-world outdoor conditions. Overall, the addition of temporal 
tracking significantly reduces both overcounting and undercounting 
caused by detection uncertainties.
Although performance varied across the three fields, similar trends 
were observed. These differences were primarily driven by variations in 
canopy structure, ground conditions, and orchard management prac­
tices. Agrosense v2 achieved the highest accuracy in Field B, which 
featured more regular tree spacing and a more uniform canopy struc­
ture, improving trunk visibility and supporting both spatial filtering and 
temporal tracking. In contrast, Field A presented more challenging 
conditions, including irregular canopy shapes, excessive foliage growth, 
and dense tree spacing, which reduced trunk visibility and resulted in 
more fragmented detection trajectories. Agrosense v2 exhibited inter­
mediate performance in the SWFREC field, where trunk visibility was 
intermittently affected by grass coverage and depth filtering, leading to 
occasional detection discontinuities. It should be noted that this study 
does not quantitatively measure the occlusion rate or the coefficient of 
variation in tree spacing across the three fields. Controlled experiments 
could be conducted in future work to quantify these influencing factors 
and establish more rigorous performance predictors.
Agrosense v2 still encounters challenges when trees are not consis­
tently detected throughout their full appearance in the image sequence. 
This includes cases involving very small trees that are difficult to detect, 
trees with extremely dense canopies where the trunk is never visible, 
and trees located very close to adjacent trees with overlapping canopies 
that appear as a single merged region. In these cases, both the tracking 
algorithm and the non-black pixel ratio metric may fail to fully resolve 
counting errors. Future work could incorporate orchard planting priors, 
such as expected within-row tree spacing, to infer potentially missed 
trees when a tracking gap exceeds the nominal spacing.
4.3. Tree canopy density classification
The tree canopy density classification was evaluated on the test 
dataset from the three orchards and shows strong performance across all 
cases, as presented in Table 4. Overall, Agrosense v2 consistently out­
performs Agrosense v1 in all fields, indicating a clear improvement in 
classification robustness. The performance gain is especially noticeable 
in more challenging fields, such as field A, where dense foliage and 
complex canopy structures increase class ambiguity. Compared with 
Agrosense v1, the improved performance of Agrosense v2 highlights the 
benefit of utilizing global observations along the moving path rather 
than relying on a single frontal view. By incorporating information from 
the full sequence, Agrosense v2 achieves more stable canopy density 
estimation, reducing sensitivity to local visual variability, including 
residual visual interference from background vegetation. However, this 
interference is mitigated rather than fully eliminated, particularly in 
low-density canopies where ground vegetation remains partially visible 
(Figs. 11a and b).
Despite the overall improvement, some limitations remain. Because 
canopy density estimation relies on sequence-level observations, 
extremely dense foliage or very small tree spacing can cause adjacent 
canopies to visually merge across consecutive frames, reducing the 
clarity of individual canopy boundaries. In addition, excessive branch 
growth within dense canopies can introduce ambiguity between me­
dium and high-density classes (Fig. 11c and d). Under such conditions, 
sequence-based aggregation may lead to reduced classification accuracy 
when canopy structures extend beyond the image field of view.

fruits typically exhibit smaller object sizes, stronger illumination vari­

by the confusion matrix analysis and recall–confidence trends. In 
contrast, fruits on the tree are more visually salient and exhibit more 
consistent appearance patterns, resulting in higher detection accuracy 
across all models. Detection performance in Field B was consistently 
higher than in Field A for both fruit categories (on the tree and ground). 
This improvement is likely attributed to the more organized planting 
structure in Field B, which reduces fruit occlusion and background 
clutter caused by grass or weeds. As a result, Field B provides a more 
favorable visual environment for fruit detection, benefiting both YOLO-

detection results for representative orchard scenes, illustrating the dif­

and more complete detections, particularly in cluttered regions, while 
YOLO-based models tend to miss small fruits under strong shadows, 
fruits clustered in proximity, and ground fruits partially covered by 
leaves. To further analyze failure modes, Fig. 13 highlights typical error 
cases, including missed ground fruits and hard background negatives, 
providing visual evidence that supports the quantitative findings. YOLO- 
based detectors primarily exhibit false negatives in these challenging 
conditions, whereas RT-DETR reduces misses but can introduce occa­
sional false positives on hard background negatives, such as specular 
highlights, bright leaf patches, or fruit-like foliage textures.
It is important to note that the fruit counting performance reported in 
this study is based on images capturing the full tree canopy from a field 
traversal perspective. Therefore, these results should not be directly 
compared with studies that perform fruit counting using close-range 
images of localized canopy regions, which typically enable higher 
counting accuracy under controlled viewing conditions.

5. Limitations and future work
While Agrosense v2 demonstrates strong performance across multi­
ple orchard sensing tasks and field conditions, several limitations remain 
that motivate future research and system refinement. These limitations 
fall into two categories: inherent challenges of visual sensing in un­
structured orchards and algorithm-specific failure modes of our current 
detection and tracking pipeline. First, visual sensing becomes difficult 
under severe occlusion and complex canopy interactions. Dense foliage, 
tall grass, strong illumination variability, and overlapping canopies can 
reduce visible cues and destabilize image-based perception, especially 
when fruit or structural features are partially or fully obscured. In the 
current framework, when tree trunks are consistently not visible, trunk 
detection and subsequent temporal tracking may fail to establish 
persistent tree identities, and very small or irregularly shaped trees or 
merged canopies in tightly spaced orchards can lead to fragmented or 
ambiguous trajectories. In addition, adjacent-tree separation relies 
mainly on trunk-based temporal tracking rather than explicit canopy- 
boundary segmentation and therefore becomes less robust when 
neighboring canopies are visually merged, and trunk cues are weak or 
intermittently missing. Prior knowledge of orchard geometry, such as 
row orientation and approximate tree spacing, could be incorporated as

a tracking step before constraining feasible inter-frame associations and 
reducing identity switches or trajectory fragmentation. Future work will 
investigate multi-view sensing strategies, including front–rear or multi- 
angle camera configurations, to increase canopy coverage and reduce 
blind regions caused by side-view occlusion. Observations of the same 
tree from multiple viewpoints could improve recovery of partially hid­
den fruits and help maintain tree identity when trunk cues are inter­
mittently missing. In addition, complementary sensing modalities such 
as LiDAR or radar could provide more stable structural information on 
canopy geometry and tree boundaries under challenging visual condi­
tions, thereby improving robustness under severe occlusion.
Second, tree-level visible fruit counts produced by Agrosense v2 are 
derived from detection-based estimates and not evaluated against 
exhaustive ground-truth measurements. In this study, manual counting 
of all fruits on each tree and on the ground was not conducted due to 
labor and time constraints, limiting the ability to perform a full quan­
titative validation of yield estimation accuracy (which is out of the scope 
of this study). Moreover, because Agrosense v2 relies on side-view 
sensing in traditional citrus orchards with complex three-dimensional 
canopies, the system captures only the visible portion of the fruit pop­
ulation along the platform trajectory. As a result, whole-canopy fruit 
enumeration remains difficult under practical field conditions, which is 
more suitable for relative tree-to-tree comparison and spatial mapping 
than for direct whole-tree yield estimation. Future efforts will focus on 
targeted ground-truth campaigns at selected trees or plots to enable 
more rigorous validation and calibration of tree-level fruit count 
estimates.
Third, fruit detection performance remains sensitive to challenging 
illumination conditions and background clutter, particularly for fruits 
on the ground. Shadows, soil texture, fallen leaves, and partial occlusion 
can lead to missed detections or false negatives, even with transformer- 
based models. Although RT-DETR demonstrated superior robustness 
compared with convolutional detectors, further improvements may be 
achieved through task-specific image pre-processing, data augmenta­
tion, domain adaptation across orchards and seasons, and the incorpo­
ration of temporal consistency constraints at the detection level.
From a system perspective, Agrosense v2 currently focuses on 
perception and inference, serving as a sensing foundation rather than a 
complete decision-support or actuation system. In addition, the present 
study was conducted only in citrus orchards, and the adaptability of the 
framework to other fruit tree species remains to be validated. Different 
orchard architectures and tree species may require different sensing 
configurations, threshold settings, or interpretation of tree-level out­
puts. For example, in orchards with stronger inter-tree overlap, less 
regular canopy structure, or different fruit size and distribution patterns, 
visible fruit counts may be more appropriately interpreted as partial- 
observation indicators rather than direct whole-tree totals unless addi­
tional viewpoints or structural priors are introduced. Although RT-DETR 
provided strong detection performance in this study, its computational 
complexity imposes relatively high demands on edge devices, and the 
current hardware configuration may increase deployment cost and limit 
large-scale adoption. Future work will therefore extend the framework 
to additional orchard systems and fruit tree species, investigate model- 
lightweighting strategies, and lower-cost hardware configurations. In 
addition, although temporal information is already used for sequence- 
based canopy inference and fruit counting, the current framework 
does not yet fully exploit multi-temporal data collected across repeated 
field campaigns or different growth stages. Extending the system toward 
multi-temporal analysis could support advanced applications such as 
growth trend prediction, canopy-change monitoring, and anomaly early 
warning. It will also support downstream applications, including yield 
forecasting, stress detection, and variable-rate management actions such 
as precision spraying or harvesting support. Integration with cloud- and 
AI-based platforms, such as Agroview (Ampatzidis et al., 2020; https 
://agintel.ai/products/agroview), will enable scalable data storage, 
multi-temporal analysis, and user-friendly interaction with tree-level 
orchard intelligence.
Overall, these limitations point to clear opportunities for advancing 
Agrosense v2 from a robust orchard sensing system toward a fully in­
tegrated, adaptive, and autonomous orchard management framework 
capable of long-term deployment across diverse tree crop systems. For 
full deployment, the most critical limitations to address are robust tree 
identity maintenance under severe occlusion and merged canopies, 
along with improved reliability of tracking when trunk cues are absent.

In contrast, limitations such as occasional missed fruits under heavy

6. Conclusion
This study introduced Agrosense v2, a unified, AI-enabled orchard 
sensing system designed to deliver multiple practical tree-level infor­
mation, including tree count, canopy density and visible fruit counts in 
real time during field traversal. Moving beyond task-specific perception 
pipelines and improving upon Agrosense v1, which relied largely on 
frame-level detections and a center-based counting strategy without 
persistent tree identities or unified tree-level aggregation, Agrosense v2 
integrates trunk detection, a new temporal tracking algorithm for stable 
cross-frame tree identification, canopy density classification, fruit 
detection, and tree-level spatial aggregation within a single operational 
framework, enabling consistent tree-level monitoring under realistic 
orchard conditions. Field experiments conducted across three commer­
cial citrus orchards demonstrated that incorporating spatial filtering and 
temporal tracking substantially improves tree counting reliability. While 
trunk detection alone resulted in significant overcounting, the addition 
of bounding box selection increased average counting accuracy to 
87.57%, and further integration of temporal tracking improved accuracy 
to 95.49%, highlighting the importance of sequence-aware reasoning for 
orchard-scale enumeration. These results confirm that robust tree 
counting in continuous field operation cannot rely on single-frame 
detection alone.
For fruit detection, transformer-based models (RT-DETR) showed 
improved robustness under dense canopies and cluttered ground con­
ditions, whereas lightweight YOLO-based models provided competitive 
performance with lower computational cost, supporting flexible 
deployment across hardware platforms. Canopy density classification 
benefited from sequence-based inference, reducing sensitivity to local­
ized occlusions and illumination variability. Importantly, aggregating 
image-level detections into persistent, geo-referenced tree identities 
enabled stable monitoring despite partial visibility and viewpoint 
changes.
To the best of our knowledge, Agrosense v2 represents the first in­
tegrated sensing framework for citrus orchards using a side-view mobile 
platform capable of simultaneously estimating tree count, canopy den­
sity, tree height, and visible fruit counts (on-tree and on-ground) in real 
time within a single system. By bridging image-level perception and 
orchard-scale spatial representation, Agrosense v2 provides a practical 
foundation for scalable orchard monitoring and serves as a critical 
sensing layer for future decision-support and autonomous orchard 
management systems. Although the current study demonstrates the 
feasibility of Agrosense v2 in real citrus orchards, further work is needed 
to improve robustness under severe occlusion and to validate the system 
across more diverse orchard conditions. As a practical tree-level sensing 
framework, Agrosense v2 has strong potential to support precision 
agriculture applications such as yield forecasting, stress detection, and 
precision spraying.
