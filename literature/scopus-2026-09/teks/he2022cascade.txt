A B S T R A C T  
Estimation of fruit yield is of great importance to agricultural management and production decision-making. 
Fruit counting based on computer vision is faced with many challenges, particularly dense occlusion and diffi­
cult detection. To address the problems that exist in agricultural scenarios, we propose a fruit counting pipeline 
based on multiple features matching. Fruit counting is regarded as a multiple object tracking problem based on 
tracking-by-detection framework. The proposed method combines object detection with deep learning, Kalman 
filter, and cascade matching, which integrated motion and appearance features for frame-by-frame data asso­
ciation. Using the detection results of YOLO-v3, cascade matching is leveraged to associate detection bounding 
boxes with tracks. In cascade matching, the appearance features of fruit, Mahalanobis distance, and intersection 
over union metric were fused to match objects frame-by-frame. Mahalanobis distance is used to screen detection 
bounding boxes initially. Furthermore, the vector of locally aggregated descriptors image retrieval method is 
used to calculate the similarity of appearance between the two objects. In the final step of cascade matching, 
residual unmatched tracks and detection candidates are matched using intersection over union metric. Moreover, 
the Kalman filter is optimized for predicting the trajectories of undetectable objects to enhance the accuracy and 
robustness of fruit counting. In the experiments, the results of predicted fruit counting for camellia is 44 while the 
ground truth is 38 for a video. For apple counting, the total predicted number of fruits for three videos is 310 
while the actual number is 292. And compared to the method of SORT, our method is better in counting ac­
curacy, reduced the number of ID switches, and had more robustness when the detector performance degen­
erated. All the above mentioned metrics indicate that the proposed method is well performance in fruit counting 
regardless of whether the fruit is sparsely or densely grown.   
1. Introduction 
With the rapid development of agricultural intelligence, fruit 
counting has played an important role in agricultural production man­
agement. In addition to estimating the fruit yield, fruit counting can also 
be used to calculate the fruit maturity rate, so as to determine the 
picking period. Fruit counting from videos stream can be regarded as a 
multiple object tracking problem based on tracking by detection (TBD). 
However, fruit counting approaches using TBD are susceptible by oc­
clusion and detection performance, especially for densely growing fruits 
with near-background color. 
TBD approaches include two phrases: object detection, followed by 
tracking. Using the object detection results, TBD approaches associate 
object bounding boxes frame by frame. Deep learning has demonstrated 
a powerful performance in object detection, and can achieve state-of- 
the-art performance using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) 
instead of manually designed features. A large number of deep learning 
methods based on object detection methods have been proposed in 
recent years. Object detection models can be roughly divided into two 
types: one-stage and two-stage. Two-stage architectures have high effi­
ciency in term of precision and recall because of decoupling localization 
and classification. Examples include R-CNN (Girshick et al., 2014), SPP- 
Net (He et al., 2015), Fast R-CNN (Girshick et al.,2015), Faster R-CNN 
(Ren et al., 2017), and Sparse R-CNN (Sun et al., 2020). One-stage object 
models consist of a unified end-to-end network with a proficient running 
speed, but they have lower precision than two-stage frameworks. Ex­
amples include YOLO (Redmon et al., 2016) and single shot multibox 
detector (Liu et al., 2016).

Multiple object tracking is the downstream task of object detection. 
Boosted by the development of object detection based on deep learning, 
a large number of multi-object tracking methods based on detection 
have been proposed. Bewley proposed a simple online real-time tracking 
method called SORT (Bewley et al., 2016), which uses the object 
detection results of Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2017) to perform data 
association using the intersection over union (IoU) metric and Hungar­
ian matching algorithm frame-by-frame. The tracking speed can achieve 
60 FPS on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1050 GPU. Wojke et al. (2017) 
further proposed Deep-SORT, which is based on the SORT method, and 
leverages a deep neural network to extract the feature embedding from 
the object appearance and then re-identifies the object according to a 
fusion of the motion and appearance features. In Deep-SORT, the 
detection network and the appearance feature extracting network for re- 
identification are computationally time-consuming. The entire inference 
time is approximately the sum total of the two components. This method 
of separating detector and appearance feature extractor (SDE), there­
fore, leads to a slow tracking speed. Additionally, the deep network used 
for re-identification requires an additional classification data-set for 
training. Wang et al. (2020) and Zhang et al. (2020) proposed a tracking 
paradigm of joint detection and appearance embedding (JDE) to unify 
detection and re-identification networks. Unlike SDE methods, JDE 
methods need to be trained on video stream data-set, and the cost of data 
collection and annotation is high. 
In recent years, multiple object tracking approaches based on TBD 
have been widely applied in fruit counting tasks. Wang et al. (2013) 
developed a fruit counting system that uses stereo cameras to count 
apples. Nellithimaru et al. (2019) presented a grape counting pipeline 
that combines SLAM, deep learning, and three-dimensional (3D) 
reconstruction. Stein et al. (2016) designed a fruit counting system that 
assembles expensive sensors, including a monocular camera, LiDAR, and 
GPS/INS. Liu et al. (2018) combined the Hungarian matching algorithm, 
Kanade-Lucas-Tomasi tracker, and Structure from Motion algorithm for 
fruit counting. To reduce the cost of sensors, Liu et al. (2019) further 
presented a monocular camera based fruit counting pipeline via 
improving their previous work (Liu et al., 2018) and demonstrated that 
their approach achieved state-of-the-art performance on a mango 
dataset. H¨ani et al. (2018) presented a TBD-based fruit counting method 
for clustered apples, which improved counting accuracy. Using an object 
tracking algorithm that uses LSTM (Milan et al., 2017), Vasconiz 
compared the effects of different detectors on fruit detection and 
counting (Vasconiz et al., 2020). Wang et al. (2019) proposed a counting 
method involved YOLO-v3 detector, 4 dimensional Kalman filter and 
Hungarian assignment for mango. Weyler et al. (2021) proposed a novel 
single-stage object detection approach that localizes crops and weeds in 
the field, while detecting plant-specific leaf keypoints to estimate leaf 
count at a plant level. 
Although a variety of mature multiple object tracking methods have 
been proposed and widely used in fruit counting, challenges remain in 
open agricultural scenarios. Many fruit counting methods based on TBD 
associate the bounding boxes with tracks frame by frame according to 
the IoU distance (Milan et al., 2017), depth information, or optical flow 
(Liu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019). Although this achieves good perfor­
mance overall in terms of tracking precision and accuracy, a relatively 
high number of ID switches are returned. The cause for this phenomenon 
is that the fruit is very easily occluded in the agricultural environment, 
and detection performance is unstable because of light changes. Some 
tracking methods introduce appearance feature matching to reduce ID 
switches (Wojke et al., 2017). Few fruit counting methods involving 
tracking directly use the fruit appearance feature for match of fruit 
across frame. The methods of tracking only using a single feature is not 
robust. However, many TBD methods with appearance feature matching 
may not suitable for fruit counting. Deep-SORT (Wojke et al., 2017) 
achieves state-of-the-art performance in pedestrian tracking. In addition 
to a dataset for training the object detector, Deep-SORT requires a CNN 
that has been trained on a large-scale re-identification dataset, which 
leads to the high cost of data annotation and collection. Different from 
tasks in the security field, such as pedestrian tracking, there are few 
public dataset of fruit Re-ID. Some fruit counting work uses optical flow; 
however, that method is sensitive to light changes in an outdoor envi­
ronment. Moreover, association based on the optical flow method is not 
robust to occlusion. 
To address the above challenges, in this paper, we introduce a fruit 
counting pipeline via motion and appearance features matching. The 
main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follow: 
• We introduce an image retrieval algorithm without a CNN to mea­
sure image similarity as an appearance feature, to avoid using a 
large-scale dataset to train the Re-ID component.  
• We introduce a cascade matching method based on the fusion of 
motion feature and appearance feature to associate data frame by 
frame to improve counting accuracy and reduce ID switches.  
• The code will be open sourced at https://github.com/eastoc/Casca 
deSORT to help reproduce our proposals and compare with other 
methods as a baseline for research. 
The remainder of the paper is arranged as follows: In Section 2, we 
introduce the experimental materials. In Section 3, we introduce our 
counting method, which incorporates a detector and tracking. In Section 
4, we introduce the relevant experiments, including the experimental 
setup, evaluation metrics, and results of a comparison between Cascade- 
SORT and SORT. In Section 5, we discuss the limitations of Cascade- 
SORT and provide solutions. Finally, we provide conclusions and pro­
pose future work.

3. Methodology 
As shown in Fig. 2, the TBD framework based fruit counting method 
is divided into three parts: detection, data association, and trajectory 
prediction. The detection part outputs bounding boxes of fruit, including 
position, size, and category information. The above information can be 
obtained via a deep neural network, such as YOLO. Data association 
matches the detection information with the trajectory data. In this step, 
some metrics are introduced to measure the distance between the 
detection data and tracks, and a data assignment algorithm is required to 
match the data. Considering the agricultural scenarios, the performance 
of object detection is easily affected by illumination and occlusion, 
which easily lead to detection interruption and disappearance, and then 
overestimate of the fruit yield. Therefore, predicting the motion of the 
trajectories is required to make up for the missing detection information 
caused by interruption. Our main focus is on the data association part. 
3.1. Detection 
TBD approaches depend on the results of object detection. We used 
YOLO-v3 (Redmon and Farhadi, 2018) as the detector. YOLO-v3 is an 
improvement of YOLO-v1 and YOLO-v2 (Redmon and Farhadi, 2017). 
YOLO regards object detection as a regression problem. A single end-to- 
end CNN predicts bounding boxes and class confidence directly from 
entire images. 
YOLO divides an image into an S × S grid. If the center point of an 
object falls into a grid, the grid is responsible for detecting the object. 
Each grid cell predicts n bounding boxes and the confidence of these 
bounding boxes. Confidence is defined as. 
confidence = Pr(Object) × IoU
(1) 
where IoU is the intersection over the union between prediction 
boxes and ground truth. If there is an object in the grid, Pr(Object) = 1， 
otherwise, Pr(Object) = 0. Additionally, YOLO predicts C conditional 
class probabilities Pr(Class i|Object). 
YOLO-v3 uses Darknet-53 as the backbone network. In comparison 
with YOLO-v2, YOLO-v3 uses multiple independent logistic classifiers 
instead of Softmax for classification, and a feature pyramid network 
structure is introduced for multi-scale prediction.

3.2. Multiple object tracking 
Most TBD approaches, such as SORT, used the combination of IoU 
distance feature and Hungarian algorithm for data association and used 
Kalman filter to predict the trajectory of the occluded objects. 
We present a multiple object tracking pipeline in Fig. 4. This paper 
fuse the Mahalanobis distance and appearance features of the objects to 
cascade match detection results and trajectory. The matched trajectories 
use the Kalman filter to update the covariance matrix and motion state. 
The unmatched trajectories use the Kalman filter to predict the current 
motion state. 
For tracks in the tentative state, the duration of occurrence is less 
than three frames; hence, the covariance and velocity of objects cannot 
be obtained. Therefore, we use the IoU metric to match tracks in the 
tentative state. The IoU threshold is 0.3. 
Cascade matching is used to track objects: the Mahalanobis distance 
threshold matching is used at first, followed by appearance feature 
matching and IoU matching. After cascade matching, there are three 
kinds of data: unmatched tracks, unmatched detection boxes, and 
matched tracks. Using unmatched detection, new tentative tracks can be 
generated. If a tentative track can be matched for three consecutive 
frames, the track is switched from the tentative state to the confirmed 
state. If an unmatched track is in a tentative state, it will be deleted 
directly; If it is in the confirmed state, the age of the track after its first 
appearance is assessed. If the age is greater than the maximum lifetime, 
it is considered to have left the current region and is deleted. Otherwise, 
the position in the next frame is predicted by the Kalman filter. 
3.2.1. State estimation 
The Kalman filter has been extensively employed in many object 
tracking approaches. This section, this paper introduces the Kalman 
filter to estimate the state of the trajectories. 
We assume that the ego-motion information of the camera is un­
known in the tracking scenario. Generally, agricultural scenarios are 
open environments. Object detection is easily affected by light; hence 
some objects cannot be stably detected. Moreover, fruit is easily 
occluded by branches and leaves. Thus, this study leverages the Kalman 
filter to predict the trajectories of undetectable and occluded objects. 
The state of each object can be defined in eight-dimensional space as.

the ratio of the height to width of the bounding box, which can be 
written as r = w/h. 
The Kalman filter can only predict the motion state of the next frame 
according to the motion state of the previous frame, however, as the 
disappearance time increases, the uncertainty of Kalman filter predic­
tion increases. In addition, predicting the target trajectory with the 
Kalman filter may fail when the camera motion is discontinuous, such as 
camera shaking or switching between horizontal and vertical motion. In 
an agricultural scene, fruit is a static target, and the camera is in motion. 
The motion state of the target is essentially the motion state of the 
camera. That means the fruit movement information in the same field of 
vision is highly correlated simultaneously. Therefore, we can use the 
matched trajectory state to optimize the prediction of the temporarily 
disappeared trajectory state. 
In the camera field of view, the motion state of the object differs at 
different depths. A distance object moves more slowly than a nearby 
object. If we use global object motion optimization to estimate the 
vanishing trajectory, it is not accurate. Therefore, we use the nearest 
neighbor to determine the nearest matched trajectory of the vanishing 
object, followed by optimizing the prediction of the vanishing object.

3.2.2. Appearance features extracting 
In pedestrian tracking, a CNN has been used to extract the appear­
ance features of the objects and re-identify objects, which requires a 
large-scale classification dataset for training. Different from pedestrian 
tracking, for the same fruit, the appearance feature changes negligibly 
frame by frame. A traditional image retrieval method can also achieve 
high re-identification accuracy instead of CNNs. We consider object re- 
identification as an image retrieval problem and take advantage of the 
vector of local aggregating descriptors (VLAD) (Schmid et al., 2012) 
method to measure the similarity between different objects. 
VLAD is a simple and efficient image retrieval algorithm that was 
used before the rise of deep learning. The pipeline is shown in Fig. 5. 
First, VLAD computes the descriptors of the SIFT feature points X(1), X(2) 
in the two objects. A SIFT feature descriptor is a 128-dimensional vector. 
X(n) is a dn × 128 matrix that represents all the SIFT descriptors of the n- 
th object image, where n = 1 or 2 and dn is the number of SIFT feature 
points in the n-th object image. Then, VLAD finds k centroids C = [c1,…, 
ck] as visual words in the descriptors [X(1); X(2)] via the k-means method. 
Each descriptor x is related to its nearest visual word ci = NN(x). cij is the 
nearest visual word associated with the j-th SIFT descriptor x(n)

to distinguish the different objects. In this study, k = 10.

4. Experiments 
4.1. Experimental details 
In this section, we describe the implementation details. We used a 
single NVIDIA RTX 2080Ti GPU with 12 GB of memory on the Ubuntu 
16.04 system to train YOLO-v3 and test its performance. We used k- 
means clustering on the training dataset bounding boxes to obtain the 
size of the anchor boxes. We adjusted the input images to 608 × 608 
resolution. To accelerate convergence in training, we used the initial 
weights of the backbone network pre-trained on Image-Net (Deng et al., 
2009). Additionally, the weight decay, initial learning rate, batch size, 
and momentum are 0.0005, 0.001, 2, and 0.9 respectively. To speed up 
the training process, we set the initial learning rate to 0.001. We set the 
total training iterations of the model to 4,000 and decreased the learning 
rate to 0.0001 after 3,200 iterations and 0.00001 after 3,600 iterations. 
The hyper-parameters of the YOLO-v3 are directly copied from the 
default settings of the Darknet. The default settings are summarized via a 
lot of training experiments, which can be used for reference directly. 
We trained YOLO-v3 using the Darknet framework and deployed it 
using the OpenCV4.4 package. We used the sci-kit learn library to 
implement k-means clustering in the VLAD approach. We implemented 
the full counting program in Python 3.8. 
4.2. Performance evaluation metrics 
To measure the performance of counting, we require evaluation in­
dicators. In previous work, many researchers evaluated counting per­
formance using recall and the average counting error (Liu et al., 2018; 
Liu et al., 2019; Hanni et al., 2018). The average counting error is the 
ratio of the count to the ground truth. These indicators do not consider 
the impact of missed detection and ID switching on counting perfor­
mance. For example, missed detection and ID switching offset each other 
in counting. Missed detection reduces the count, but ID switching is 
likely to increase the count. As we observed, ID switching is mainly 
caused by the generation of new tracks for the same target resulting from 
the missed detection. This indicates that, in the fruit counting task, ID 
switching mainly leads to over counting. If a method has a large number 
of missed detection and ID switches simultaneously, the count number 
may be close to the ground truth, but the actual counting performance 
deviates from the ground truth. Therefore, we introduce the evaluation 
system of the multi-object tracking method to measure our counting 
performance. 
This paper selects MOTA and ID switch to evaluate the target 
counting performance, which is proposed by Bernardin et al. (2008). 
MOTA is multiple object tracking accuracy, and we use this index to 
measure the accuracy of object counting.

the ground truth that is counted by the human to evaluate the overall 
performance of the counter.

while improving MOTA. The number of ID switches reduced by 46.77% 
and MOTA improved by 0.18. Moreover, we observed that Cascade- 
SORT tracked through a longer term of occlusion than SORT. This in­
dicates that the extension of the VLAD component enhanced the ability 
of object re-identification through occlusion. 
A visualization of counting is shown in Fig. 8 and each image is 
separated by 5 frames. In the first frame, we focus on fruit No.7 at the 
bottom, circled in red. In Cascade-SORT, the ID fruit of No.7 only 
switched twice. In SORT, the ID of fruit No.7 switched three times. 
Moreover, in Cascade-SORT, fruit No. 8, circled in yellow, was tracked 
stably without switching. In SORT, the ID of the fruit switched once from 
No. 11 to No. 14. 
The longest occlusion period that an object experiences until being 
re-tracked by Cascade-SORT can be seen from Fig. 8. From the first 
image to the second image in Fig. 8, Cascade-SORT kept the ID of No.7 
fruit but SORT generated one ID switching. In the fifth and sixth images, 
the same phenomenon occurred in No.8 fruit. However, from the fourth 
image to the sixth image, both methods generated one ID switching for 
fruit circled in red. These visualization comparison results are indicated 
that, for occlusion or miss detection within 5 frames, Cascade-SORT can 
keep up with the original trajectory through VLAD. 
4.4. Influence of detection performance and camera motion 
The performance of the detector affects the tracker (Bewley et al., 
2016). Different trackers have different robustness to the change of 
detector performance. Like the impact of detection performance 
degradation, occlusion also leads to the interruption of detection results. 
Therefore, we estimated the impact of occlusion on the counting results 
by comparing the impact of different performance detectors for our 
counting approach. We trained two YOLO-v3 detectors by adjusting the 
sample batch size in training. Their detection performance on the same 
validation dataset is shown in Table 3. AP, recall, and IoU are the 
detection indicators. In terms of AP, recall, and IoU, the weak detector 
was significantly weaker than the strong detector. 
Counting performance is easily influenced by camera motion in 
video streams. Therefore, we also tested the impact of camera motion on 
the counting results. To compare the performance fairly, we tested the 
performance of Cascade-SORT and SORT on two video streams with 
different camera motion. The video tested in Table 4 are consistent with 
that in Table 2, and the camera motion is at a smoothing speed, while 
that in Table 5 the camera motion in the test video is discontinuous and 
the speed is faster. 
As shown in Table 4 and 5, the results indicate that the detector with 
good performance improved counting performance, particularly in 
reducing ID switches, miss counting, and false positives. However, there 
is an exception in Table 4. Seen from Table 4, the SORT with a weak 
detector had one less ID switch compared with that with a strong de­
tector. This is mainly because the more the miss fruit, the lower the 
chance to switch identities. Additionally, the above results indicate that 
Cascade-SORT reduced ID switches caused by occlusion. Moreover, we 
observed that compared with that using Cascade-SORT, the ID switches 
of SORT increased less after detector performance degeneration. This is 
because the VLAD component only mitigates ID switches caused by 
occlusion of short periods of about 5 frames, but not for long-term oc­
clusion or misses. Overall, the counting performance of Cascade-SORT 
was more robust than that of SORT. 
The results of Table 5 shows that when the camera motion state is 
unstable and the fast, both trackers produce more ID switching. This is 
because we use IoU as the motion metric in data associate and linear 
Kalman filter to predict trajectories for those missed detections. When 
two bounding boxes do not overlap, the IoU is zero. That means that the 
trajectory tracking is not on the detection box. Therefore, rapid hori­
zontal movements lead to over-counting and ID switching. In addition, 
the instability of the camera motion state can increase the uncertainty of 
the linear Kalman filter prediction, resulting in the deviation of the 
prediction for the trajectory missed detection from the actual value. That 
also indicates that the performance of the fruit counting methods based 
on tracking relies on the stability of the camera motion.

5. Discussion 
Cascade-SORT outperformed SORT, with a higher MOTA score and 
lower ID switches on our custom dataset. The robustness analysis 
showed that Cascade-SORT was more robust to the degradation of de­
tector performance. Despite the overall improvement in performance, 
Cascade-SORT still has limitations, which we can discuss from three 
aspects: the detector, data matching, and counting system. 
A powerful detector can improve counting accuracy performance. 
However, the performance of the object detection model in agricultural 
scenarios is easily affected by many factors, such as illumination, oc­
clusion, and feature changes at the fine-grained level. This is because of 
the poor generalization performance of deep neural networks. Transfer 
learning can bridge the gap between fruit with different feature distri­
butions and enhance generalization. Moreover, the detector is the most 
time-consuming component. Knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 
2014), network architecture search (Zoph and Le, 2016), and network 
pruning approaches can be used to compress the detection model and 
improve the network inference speed. More simply, a small detection 
model such as YOLO-X (Ge et al., 2021) can be deployed as our detection 
component. 
From the perspective of data matching, a feature extractor can 
reduce ID switches and help the counting accuracy. However, in the 
image similarity measurement task, the performance of VLAD was not 
robust, according to our test results. In some cases, the similarity mea­
sure of VLAD was not accurate. A typical example in which the change of 
similarity between the same fruit was consistent with that between 
different fruit as the k value in k-means changed (Fig. 10). Our experi­
ments suggest that image re-identification methods without deep 
learning still need to be improved. Embedding optical flow into cascade 
matching is a flexible solution. Moreover, the running speed of VLAD 
can be improved. We tested the time cost of VLAD using different 
numbers of SIFT feature points, which was approximately 50 ms 
(Fig. 11). Thus, k-means occupied a large amount of time overhead. To 
reduce the VLAD running time, two approaches can be used. We can 
leverage principal component analysis to reduce the dimension of de­
scriptors or SURF using a 32-dimensional feature descriptor that can be 
used as key points instead of SIFT. By contrast, we can use some accel­
erated optimization methods for k-means (Bottesch et al., 2016). 
Recent advances in image re-identification have been based on deep 
learning, but those methods may not be directly extended to fruit 
counting. The reason is that there is few ReID dataset for fruit. This study 
performed fruit counting using Deep-SORT with the ReID trained on the 
pedestrian dataset. However, the result is worse than SORT and more ID 
switches are produced. This study think this is caused that there is a 
domain gap between pedestrians with fruit so ReID can not extract fruit 
features well.

In summary, the conditions of fruit growth can significantly affect 
the accuracy of fruit counting. The occlusion of the fruit will lead to a 
decrease in the number of counts due to missed detection; the clustered 
growth of fruits is prone to ReID, which may increase the number of 
fruits. Therefore, it is difficult to accurately predict the number of fruit 
on a single tree due to the uncertainty of fruit growth conditions. 
As discussed above, there is still a challenge for improvement in 
detection and data association of the fruits. From the perspective of the 
counting system, the tracking-based approaches are easily affected by 
camera motion. Introducing some methods from the SLAM system, such 
as visual odometry, loop detection, and 3D reconstruction, should 
enhance the performance of fruit counting. 
6. Conclusion 
In this paper, we proposed a robust fruit counting framework that 
incorporated motion and appearance features to associate data. 
Furthermore, we introduced a set of reasonable evaluation indicators 
different from other studies. We can draw the following conclusions:  
1. Because of the introduction of multiple features cascade matching, 
Cascade-SORT outperformed SORT in terms of counting accuracy, 
with the number of ID switches and over-counts reduction on both 
camellia fruit and apple counting tasks.  
2. We verified that counting performance relied on the performance of 
the detector. The counting performance will improve with the 
enhancement of detector performance.  
3. Our method is still more robust than those of SORT when detection 
performance deteriorated. This indicates that our approach tracked 
fruit through a period of occlusion or vanishing, unlike the SORT 
approach.  
4. The fruit counting method can accurately predict the yield of fruits in 
the orchard rather than the number of fruits for a single fruit tree. 
As our experiments and discussion highlighted the importance of 
detection and re-identification in tracking, in future work, we should 
introduce an efficient approach to measure appearance feature simi­
larity instead of using the VLAD approach. By contrast, counting 
performance depends on detection. Thus, in future work, we should pay 
more attention to improving detection performance for fruit and in 
agricultural scenarios. Additionally, we should design a SLAM system 
for fruit counting.
