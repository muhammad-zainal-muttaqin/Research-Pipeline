Perception Scheme for Fruits Detection in Trees for
Autonomous Agricultural Robot Applications
Bourhane Kadmiry1;
Chee Kit Wong;
Christoph Burger
Callaghan Innovation – Sensors & Automation Group – Auckland, N.Z
bourhane.kadmiry / kit.wong / christoph.burger@callaghaninnovation.govt.nz
Abstract—This article describes an approach to autonomous
robotic perception for farm-based applications. The technological
setup aims at extracting information about fruits in a tree;
based on estimation of their position and visual and features,
through a color/depth vision system; and further robustly assert
this information for localization and manipulation, through the
build of a graph-based model. The scientiﬁc contribution is
the implementation of a detection/extraction algorithm, using 2
cameras (monocular-color and time-of-ﬂight) for data acquisition,
GPS and IMU for global localization; and a set of Machine
Vision (MV) (detection, extraction,. . . ). The algorithm fuses the
sensors’ data, and Machine Learning (ML) for point-matching
and tracking features. The extracted features are stored in
a graph representation for fast search/traversal/retrieval and
update purposes. This in turn will feed learning and recognition
schemes promoting dynamic monitoring and ofﬂine manipulation
processes (separately). Global tree-mapping is performed using
NAV-sensor data, and locally made using color/depth images
superposition and feature localization. Whilst the preliminary
experimental results in- and outdoors –based on a ﬁxed platform–
demonstrated the usefulness and simplicity of this approach,
further outdoors testing with the mobile platform is required
to determine the system ability to cope with different scenarios
in the naturally uncertain and varying environment.
I. INTRODUCTION
Economic, environmental and food safety pressures neces-
sitate the adoption of spatially variable crop management, aka
precision agriculture. Among precision farming technologies,
yield estimation (Y.E.) and mapping (Y.M.) are of prime
importance [1]. The higher end of our project is to develop
an intelligent perception system, for an outdoor mobile robot;
so that it interprets a scene and deﬁne objects of interest with
their attributes, then determine how each object is linked with
other objects. To achieve this goal, a biologically-inspired per-
ception system augmented with Cognition an A.I. paradigms
is necessary to identify, locate and map objects in a scene. The
objects are discriminated from the background and assigned
individual attributes and linkages with other objects.
The characteristics for such a system are it capabilities to:
• detect and extract features (image/ ﬂow processing, com-
puter vision (CV) →dicriminate objects and background)
• separate, characterize, and model objects (MV, ML →
model and optimize models for each potential object/
agent in the environment)

• characterize a scene through objects/agents and their re-
lations (learning, heuristics, algebra →abstract concepts
and layout of the vocabulary for the semantics)
The ﬁrst phase of this work is to address the MV and ML
aspects of objects detection, extraction, characterization and
tracking. We use tangerines Y.E/M. as a base scenario for
this research: The problem of identifying the total number of
fruits on trees has long been of interest in crop estimation
work. Yield prediction of fruits and vegetables in practical
environment is one of the hard and signiﬁcant tasks to obtain
better results in crop management systems to achieve more
productivity w.r.t moderate cost [2]. Early Y.E. is necessary
for efﬁcient resource allocation during the growth season and
harvest [3]. Proper dilution during growth results in bigger
fruits , which usually have a higher price [4].
High-value horticultural crops have also been investigated;
among which ﬁgure:
1. Citrus [4], [5]: where color MV system (MVS) to identify
citrus fruits and estimate yield of the citrus grove in real-
time was presented in [6], whereas [7] presented an automatic
MVS, and estimated citrus yield.
2. Apples [8]: recognition algorithms based on color features
to estimate the number of fruits and models for early yield
prediction were introduced in [9]. [10] described a CV-based
model to estimate diameter and number of apples on a tree.
3. Grapes [12]–[14]; or cranberries, olives and tomatoes.
For most of those -excluding [3] where an automated Y.E.
system was developed for apples based on stereo-vision; [1]
where mobile platform is used to yield-map manual fruit
picking, and [15] where spatio-temporal variation in yield was
directed dynamically, based on features (mass, color,ﬁrmness)
manual determination– Y.E. has ben carried out in a static
manner; through manual tagging of fruits; based on a set of
images, versus automatic estimation and a statistical approach
to determine the yield. Current efforts on tangerine Y.E.
using CV solely explored two avenues: (1) detecting tangerine
ﬂowers (thresholded image-based statistical approach), and (2)
counting the number of tangerines in orchards (manual fruit
labelling vs. automatic matching, on image). Both methods are
described in [2], [11].
Important to this work is the requirement for combined
point-cloud data with RGB images. Whilst RGB-D devices
such as the MS Kinect exists; and have been used for various
applications (people detection, gesture recognition,. . . ) [16],
mobile robot navigation [17] and industrial processing [18];

these devices’ main limitation is their unability to operate
in outdoor environment; more speciﬁcally when not sheltered
from sunlight [17]. Whilst stereo cameras remain a feasible
alternative; and have been used in numerous outdoor appli-
cations [19], its applications are limited by variable lighting
conditions, high computing complexity, and the need for tex-
ture for computing depth. ToF cameras measure the distance
to objects by measuring the phase shift of the reﬂected light
signal to the phase of the near-IR light source, for every
pixel of the camera. It provides three signiﬁcant advantages:
works without textured surfaces, does not required complex
calculations and is not affected by variable lighting conditions.
A detailed review on ToF cameras, as well as comparisons
to camera and laser based systems are described in [20].
However, a ToF camera drawback is low resolution and lack of
colour information of the environment. As such, it makes sense
to merge depth and color cameras to provide richer information
in outdoor environment. Though some works have used an
integrated ToF-RGB system [21], limited studies apply such
conﬁgurations outdoors [22].
Fig. 1. Robot Navigation using GoogleEarth, a WP-trajectory plan and NAV
Our research’s goal is to revisit automatic fruit detec-
tion/extraction for Y.E. and localization, through the develop-
ment of a dynamic algorithm for detecting tangerines on a tree,
while a robot is in motion around/along trees. Additional abil-
ity to our perception system is the extraction of relevant data
and its storage in a proper data-structure model (graph); in the
view of further localization for manipulation task (automatic
fruit picking) or dynamic yield monitoring. That is different
from the purely automated CV approach presented in [3],
where the main weakness was the recount of alredy detected
apples. Our system uses a fruit labelling –based on pattern
recognition in the adopted graph representation of the fruits
information– to retrieve and reduce fruit recount occurring
with the CV-based system. For this objective to be met,
perception (MV/ML system) is substituting to vision, as part of
a larger framework including learning and planing; thus, going
from an (a ﬂow of) image(s) to a set of abstractions ﬁt with
the visual contents; in the scope of using these abstractions
in planning robot motion and manipulation tasks. Though we
focus on tangerine trees, the approach with this methodology
should be tunable to varieties of fruits (oranges,apples,.. . ).

II. ROBOTIC PLATFORM
A. Scenario
In an orchard monitoring scenario, the robot traverses the
orchard, using its NAV system and a manually designed plan
(Fig.1) which trajectory is a set of waypoints around individual
trees (Fig.2) or along the tree-rows (Fig.3). While performing
this task, the robot uses its MV platform to direct its gaze at
the trees. Doing this, the MV-ML system extracts information
relating to the fruits in the tree, in terms of location, number,
size, color, texture,.. . and estimates, the tree yield and deliver
positions of each labeled fruit. Additional applications poten-
tially beneﬁting of this result are statistical engines capable of
fusing the information and deliver qualitative data about the
full orchard yield, such as percentage of ripe fruits, size/class-
wise sorting information, and labeling of the fruits for quality
and tracability purposes. It can furthermore serve determining
quantities (trees load along the seasons) and hint about time
to harvest based on fruit ripeness, load variations,.. .
Fig. 3. Robot Monitoring Organized-Orchard Trees
This exercise is very challenging due to the uncertain en-
vironment conditions (light, operating distance, terrain topol-
ogy,..), but also to the difﬁculty for sensors to operate properly
in a noisy environment (perturbed navigation, NAV drifts,
Cameras efﬁciency,. . . ). CV has proven to be very useful
for such robotic tasks, because non-invasive. Yet, monocular
visual information alone is unsufﬁcient, while stereo-vision is
easily impaired due to its dependancy to operating distance.
ToF cameras are now a mature technology that is widely
being in robotic applications, giving location information vital
to manipulation and spacial representation [23]. Using the
superposition of monocular RGB and ToF cameras allows to
extract both position and features. The problem of incorpo-
rating elaborate vision systems –for artiﬁcial perception to be

viable in agricultural applications– is yet widely a problem to
solve.
B. System Overview
In our architecture, the vision system is an eye-in-hand
conﬁguration, based on a camera-rig mounted on a manip-
ulator solidary to the mobile platform (Fig.4-b). This will in
term serve to gaze at the tree from different angles to assert
fruits partially detected due to folliage occlusion. The latter
capability is out of the scope of this article, to the beneﬁt
of the detection/extraction algorithm developement. Additional
efforts need to be made in the stabilizatin of the manipulators’-
end-effector, for the robot in motion.
For simpliﬁcation purposes, this article describes a ﬁxed
conﬁguration, so to focus on the perception algorithm. The
MV is preforming the data acquisition, segmentation, feature
extraction of the image ﬂow. The perception next operation
relies on the algorithm to extend from signal acquisition to
determination of the fruits position and features, through data
fusion and characterization. Further MV component of the
algorithm uses a mathematical graph as a data structure to
store fruit information and a simple agebra aims at asserting a
basic understanding of the fused data into useful information.
The estimation of the yield of fruits in a tree, is determined
by implementing and updating the graph through updating
its nodes, to assert existing fruits information, and adding
new nodes for newly detected fruits, if these appear along
the scrutiny around the tree. Partially appearing fruits are
checked through change of perspective along motion, and
the graph is upadated accordingly to correct/complete the
partial information due to occlusions by folliage/branches.

1) Hardware: The system HW consists of 2 major parts:
1.1. A color/depth camera-rig composed of a Basler ACE
acA1300-30gc RGB camera, and a PDM CamCube3.0 ToF
camera (Fig.4-a). The ToF camera is an all-solid-state 200x200
pixels Time-of-Flight 3D camera. The device has an operating
range of 0.3m to 7.5m and provides real-time distance and
grayscale information at 40fps, through USB 2.0 connection.
Detailed discussion on the camera can be found in [27]. The
CamCube 3.0 system provides three pieces of information
for every image pixel: intensity, depth and amplitude. The
intensity image is similar to grayscale image from a tradi-
tional 2D-camera. The amplitude data indicates the amount of
modulated light reaching the sensor and hence, it can be used
to measure the reliability of its corresponding distance reading.
In the depth image, the values in each pixel correspond to the
distance between the ToF camera to an observed objects. Using
the cameras intrinsic properties, the 3D point cloud data can be
computed. The RGB camera is a high-resolution 1.3MP (1294
pixels x 964 pixels) monocular color camera with a frame
rate of 30fps. It is powered by 12VDC and connects to a PC
via GigE Ethernet connection. The camera is equipped with a
6mm Fujinon DF6HA-1B lens. Both video stream and rafale-
like image acquisition are possible. While both need global
trigger to synchronise RGB and ToF, the latter is synchronized
through software. The RGB camera is rigidly mounted on top
of the ToF camera, and both are triggered synchronously at
1 Hz. The system is designed for day use. Yet, experiments
performed in- and outdoors helped to appreciate the impacts
of unpredictable natural illumination on the fruit detection and
characterization. For an optimal exposure, the rig is about 2
meter distance from the target.
1.2. A support vehicle Husky A200 from Clearpath
Robotics. An autonomous vehicle developed at Callaghan
Innovation will be used as the carrying platform. The platform
on its own is able to travel around a tree, or through orchard
aisles, at preset constant speed by following fruit tree-rows;
given a waypoints set plan for the desired trajectory. The speed
is set up to 1 m/s but can be reduced for data acquisition
improved stability. The camera rig will be attached to the
end-effector of a manipulator, ﬁxed at the front of the vehicle
(Fig.4-b).
2) Software: The system SW is also deviced as 2 compo-
nents, both develloped at Callaghan Innovation:
2.1. The crop yield estimation system, which has two major
parts: (1) online centering of the image and image/ﬂow ac-
quisition, and (2) ofﬂine, signal processing, feature extraction,
data fusion, feature characterization and data storage in a graph
representation. The online processing controls the start and the
stop of data acquisition, it is implemented in C++ / Python.
The ofﬂine software processes the acquired data and images
for detection, registration, and ﬁnal fruit count. This part is
implemented in Python / OpenCv.
2.2. The navigation system for the robot platform, responsible
for generating a trajectory plan and guiding the platform
accordingly along the designed plan (Fig.1). The system is
basd on ROS architecture and is implemented in C++ / Python.
C. Calibration
As with any camera system, the cameras have to be cali-
brated before utilization. To obtain the intrinsic properties of
both cameras as well as the extrinsic characteristics between
them, classical stereo calibration algorithms based on the
intensity image of the ToF and the color image of the RGB
camera can be performed [24] [25]. Unfortunately, due to the
low resolution of the ToF camera, the calibration target can
usually only be localized poorly which leads to erroneous,

non optimal parameters [20]. Previous work has been done to
reﬁne the calibration by also using the depth information with
the intensity image to obtain better results [26].
In this work, we use the calibration procedure introduced
in [29]. A SW to calibrate a ToF camera together with one or
more color cameras following this method is available [28].
The algorithms also include a polynomial depth correction
model to compensate for systematical depth errors. For our
speciﬁc conﬁguration, the generated polynomial function

III. EXPERIMENTS AND ANALYSIS
A. Experimental setup
To simulate the platform’s travel, we used –indoors– a
robot-arm to move a moc-up tree, simulating appearant robot
velocity in circular- (scenario sc-1-1) and rectangular-shape
(sc-1-2) patterns around the tree (Fig-6-a). Outdoors, we
simulated the platform motion by carrying the camera at
constant height and known positions around the moc-up tree,
in circular(sc-2-1) and rectangular (sc-2-2) shape patterns (Fig-
6-b). The circular patterns are performed in order to appreciate
pure rotation transformation and corelate computed estimation
of the objects with data sensors-derived information. The
rectangular shape scenarios introduce the additional translation
to the rotation transformation. This helps estimating positions
of features extracted, asserting the adequation of the matched
features and further their proper and robust labeling.
In the experiments, the vision system is mounted on a
tripod and positioned manually. The geographic coordinates
of the camera-rig are known through direct measurement. This
location is used by the software to calculate the geographic
position of every fruit detected on the tree: We use the global
(optical center) coordinates to register the location of fruits,
and by that, avoid node (fruit) redundancy in the graph; and
we use local (image) coordinates (distance between fruits in
the tree) to service faster graph traversal (pick closest fruit,
next, in a potential manipulation task) and retrival for labelling
(elimination of recount in monitoring task). The acquired
sequential images provide multiple views of the tree from
different perspectives, to reduce fruit occlusion and assert
fruits already detected (continuity).

B. Analysis and Discussion
The routine CaptureF rames(Cc,Cd) is responsible to synchro-
nize acquisition of the RGB and ToF images. These images
are unequal in size and their optical centers are different.
An alignment of the two frames acquired is insured by the
routine SuperposeColor&Depth(Ick,Idk). This routine provides
the RGB/Depth data and the region of interest (RoI) illustrated
by the rectangle in Fig.9 for instance. The RoI serves, later
on, to reject objects detected out of its region. The routine
DenoiseColor(Ick) is responsible , through segmentation opera-
tions, to blur and operate an opening operation on the original
color image. Tuning of the parameters for threshholding,
erosion, dilation; and the size of the ﬁlters utilized is manulally
determined. It is very difﬁcult to automate this process, as
the level of noise depends on the background and light levels
the camera is subjected to; but the high-size (7x7) Sobel-like
kernels for ﬁltering help to both remove noise and detect sure
back- and foregrounds, to separate bjects in the image. Fig.8-b
and Fig.13-b shows how such a separation between fore- and
backgorund is successful n both in- and outfoors conditions;
while Fig.12 show the difﬁculty in ﬁltering outdoor images.

IV. CONCLUSION
This work presents an algorithm aiming at determining yield
estimation and mapping in a tangerine tree. The process is
performed by the use of RGB/Tof camera-rig mounted on
a mobile platform. The algorithm uses image acquisition,
superposition and data fusion to determine objects and their
characteritics. Furthermore, results on indoor and outdoor ex-
periments showed feasibility. The determination of the objects
of interest was estimated after a number of revolutions around
the tree and established proper count with a variance of
about 15%. Further robustiﬁcation of the algorithm have to
be implemented in order to insure proper and more accurate
estimation. This can be done by automatically determining
optimal parameters for thresholding, color-band selection, op-
timal equalization and further enhancement of the heuristics
used to exclude outliers and avoid recounts in the graph.
Paradigms of inductive learning and reinforcement learning
will be explored to check feasibility. Pattern matching of
feature in graphs will be revisited in order to optimize the
search to more precise estimation of the yield through a better
mapping of the tree contents.
