Abstract 
The use of image analysis to estimate fruit tree yields is increasing. Tools are often 
based on RGB images acquired from the two opposite sides of the tree and fruit 
detection using machine learning algorithms. Due to fruit occlusion and detection 
errors, a correction model is needed to estimate the actual number of fruits from the 
number of detections. This study aimed to establish the best correction model for 
estimating mango yield in a diversity of orchards without using any other data 
extracted from the image than the number of detections. A set of images was acquired 
at the mature fruit stage on 325 ‘Kent’ mango trees from 27 orchards representing the 
main cropping systems (from monoculture to diversified orchards) found in two 
distinct production regions in West Africa. Mangoes have been detected with a YOLOv5 
neural network to obtain the detected count. The actual count of fruits was measured 
on all trees and used to calibrate different correction models for tree production 
estimation. Results showed that a linear model with region and cropping system as 
categorical covariates obtained the best performance (R2=0.66, normalised RMSE or 
NRMSE=11%) compared to a simple linear model (R2=0.34, NRMSE=20%). Square root 
transformations of actual and detected counts were necessary to fit the regression 
assumptions of residuals normality and homoscedasticity. Although the use of linear 
mixed-effect models with the orchard as a random effect might be interesting, their 
performances were comparable to linear models and they did not respect the 
regression assumption. This study showed that one simple linear regression might be 
insufficient when applying fruit yield estimation in different orchards, even for the one 
cultivar and the same fruit development stage. We also highlight the need to test the 
regression assumptions before applying the correction model. 
Keywords: West Africa, Mangifera indica L., CNN, modelling, computer vision system, 
homoscedasticity 
INTRODUCTION 
Preharvest crop yield forecasting provides critical information for farmers, particularly 
for decision making, preparation of harvest logistics, and marketing strategies. Yield forecasts 
are also important for other stakeholders, including agronomists, to better understand crop 
yield variation. In fruit tree crops, yield forecast is still primarily based on historical 
knowledge of the field’s yields and visual observation or direct counting of fruits from a 
sample of trees (Anderson et al., 2021). However, these methods show several limitations, 
including imprecision and time and labour costs. 
In West Africa, fruit trees are mostly grown in smallholder orchards, which are very 
diverse in species, cultivars, and tree age and dimension (Sarron et al., 2018). An important 
limitation of fruit crops in West Africa is the lack of data measurement and sharing on tree 
yields among stakeholders (farmers, private sector, and national institutions) (Sarron et al., 
2022). Most farmers do not assess their yield before and after harvest, limiting their efficiency 
and, subsequently, their income and food security. 

348 
Digital agriculture is seen as one solution to counter the data situation in West Africa by 
improving data quality and access, especially for farmers (Sarron et al., 2022). Digital 
technologies present exciting results when applied for tree yield measurement. The most 
precise tools for yield estimation are based on ground remote sensing (Anderson et al., 2021). 
Machine vision systems can use different sensors to take images from video, multiview or 
dual-view of the lateral tree crown. Several studies reported the use of machine learning (ML) 
algorithms to analyse tree images, and fastly and accurately locate and count fruits within the 
tree crown (Koirala et al., 2019). The tool used in this study is based on a simple mango yield 
estimation from RGB images of the two opposite sides of the tree. This approach aimed to be 
implemented on a smartphone to be accessible to the diversity of smallholders in West Africa. 
The main limitation of fruit yield estimation based on ground image acquisition is fruit 
occlusions within the tree crown (Koirala et al., 2021). Unseen fruits are not captured in the 
tree image due to branches, leaves, or other fruits overlapping. In addition, detection ML 
algorithms are prone to error and do not detect all the visible fruits in the image, especially in 
heterogeneous contexts (e.g., Sarron et al., 2020). Most studies introduced a correction 
method to relate the number of detection with the actual number of fruits within the tree to 
offset detection errors. The simplest and most common correction method uses the slope 
(‘correction factor’) of a linear regression fitted between detected and actual counts (Cheng 
et al., 2017; Dorj et al., 2017; Sarron et al., 2020). This empirical method relies on the count of 
fruits for a set of calibration trees representative of the orchard. 
However, tree canopy architecture might vary between production basins, orchards, 
and even between trees of the same orchard due to cropping practices (pruning, fertilisation, 
and irrigation), environmental conditions, and cultivar. Variabilities in canopy architecture 
imply a variation in the number of occluded fruits related to the visible fruits conducting to a 
variation of occlusion factors between trees. Some authors recommend estimating an 
occlusion factor for each orchard or even for each tree by using additional information 
extracted from images (e.g., canopy area) or metadata (Koirala et al., 2021). However, 
solutions extracting additional information from the image are costly in terms of computing 
time and requirement. 
Contextual effects on the relationship between the detected and the actual counts of 
fruits pose constraints for deploying yield estimation tools in heterogeneous contexts like the 
one observed in West Africa. Indeed, potential effects of the orchard, cropping system, and 
production region have to be taken into account by using calibration trees that represent the 
entire context. In addition, performances of the correction model need to be stable across 
acquisition contexts and growing seasons. This study tests the addition of contextual effect in 
the correction model. An interesting type of model to test is the linear mixed-effect model 
(LMM), which includes grouping variables (e.g., orchard) as random effects (Harrison et al., 
2018). The introduction of a random effect allows to explicitly model the non-independence 
of data within each group (i.e., level of the random effect) and take into account the variance 
of the grouping variable, which is not necessarily to be analysed. 
Although correction models are validated by measuring error of yield estimation on a 
validation set, in most studies, models are developed without consideration of the 
assumptions associated with linear regression (Schmidt and Finan, 2018). The most critical 
assumptions are the normality of the residuals and homoscedasticity, which is an equal 
variance of the residuals across the values of the dependent variable (i.e., no trend trends 
between the residuals and the predicted values). Non-compliance with these assumptions 
may lead to an invalidation of the linear regression (Yang et al., 2019). 
This study explores and tests different correction models for mango tree yield 
estimation in heterogeneous contexts of production regions, cropping systems, and orchards 
in West Africa. In a set of 325 mango trees, mangoes were detected using a YOLOv5 deep 
learning algorithm and the actual number of fruits was evaluated. Different models were 
developed using linear or linear mixed-effect models with different associations of categorical 
covariates and with or without data transformation. Models were evaluated in terms of 
predictive performance and compliance with regression assumptions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Data acquisition 
This study used data of 325 mango trees from three data sets acquired between 2017 
and 2021 in 27 mango-based orchards in the Niayes and Poro regions, two main production 
basins in Senegal and Côte d’Ivoire, respectively. Between 9 and 20 trees were sampled in each 
orchard. Mango trees were all of the ‘Kent’ cultivar, a cultivar mostly grown for export markets 
in West Africa. In the Niayes region, each selected orchard belonged to one of the three types 
of mango-based cropping systems observed in the basin (Sarron et al., 2018): i) extensive 
smallholder orchards composed of mango trees planted with none or a low level of 
management practices; ii) diversified smallholder orchards composed of mango trees along 
with other species (mainly citrus), occasionally with market gardening underneath the trees; 
and iii) monospecific plantations composed of the ‘Kent’ cultivar with a high level of inputs. In 
the Poro region, all orchards were intensive orchards since it is the only cropping system 
observed in this region. In each orchard, trees were selected to depict the tree dimension and 
age observed within the orchard. Thus, we assumed that the data set depicted the variability 
in cropping systems and tree features found in both regions. Data are summarised in Table 1.

For each tree, two RGB images of the two opposite sides of the tree were acquired at the 
maturity stage of mangoes, from two days to two weeks before harvest. In addition, all fruits 
were visually counted in the tree by two experts. The number of fruits counted in the field was 
referred to as the “actual count”. 
Image analyses 
Images of mango trees were analysed using the YOLOv5 deep learning algorithm 
(Jocher et al., 2022). In brief, YOLO (you only look once) is a state-of-the-art convolutional 
neural network (CNN) that locates objects of interest by fitting a bounding box around each 
object. YOLO models are one-stage (single shot) detection algorithms that simultaneously 
predict multiple bounding boxes and class probabilities. 
Mango fruits were annotated on 736 images (311 from Senegal and 425 from Côte 
d’Ivoire), including trees of the data set described above and additional ‘Kent’ trees. Experts 
annotated mangoes on images by manually fitting a bounding box around each fruit as closely 
as possible to the fruit borders. In total, 48,500 annotated fruits were obtained, which can be 
assumed to depict the variability in mango appearance (shape, colour, sunlight condition, and 
occlusion) in the images. Training and validation of YOLOv5 were performed using 90% of the 
annotated images; the remaining 10% were used for testing the algorithm. The model was 
trained for 150 epochs. Fruits were considered as detected when the detection threshold 
passed above 0.01. The CNN performances were evaluated using Precision, Recall, and F1-
score value computed on training/validation and test sets (formulas described in Sarron et 
al., 2020). 
All the images were analysed through the trained YOLOv5 to obtain the “detected count” 
of each tree, i.e., the total number of objects detected as mangoes in the two images of the tree.

Calibration of correction models and data analysis 
A first step consisted in testing the effect of the region and cropping system on the slope 
and intercept of the linear regression between the actual and detected counts using an 
analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). The effect of the orchard on the relationship was tested 
separately due to collinearity between the orchard and cropping system. 
We constructed different correction models between actual count and detected count. 
The first model type was linear models (LM) fitted using the detected count alone or the 
detected count and contextual categorical covariates (the region, cropping system, or both) 
with interaction (Table 2). Since trees are sampled in different orchards, the latter might be 
considered a grouping variable. Thus, the second model type was linear mixed-effect models 
(LMM) with the orchard as a random effect and other covariates (detected count, region, 
cropping system) as fixed effects. Model formulas are specified in Tables 2 and 3 for LMs and 
LMMs, respectively. The distributions of actual and detected counts were slightly right-
skewed with a high presence of trees with null to low number of fruits (10% trees with less 
than 10 fruits counted) leading to high standard deviations (Table 1). Consequently, the same 
model formulas were developed with square root transformation of the two continuous 
variables. Model calibration was carried out on a calibration set constructed by randomly 
selecting 80% of the observations in each orchard. The remaining 20% of the observations 
were used as a validation set.

Model evaluation and comparison were based on the adjusted coefficient of 
determination (R2), root-mean squared error (RMSE), and normalised RMSE (NRMSE, %), 

 
 
 
 
 
351 
computed as the RMSE divided by the difference between the maximum and minimum actual 
count. RMSE and NRMSE were computed on the validation set only. For LMMs, two types of R2 
were obtained: the marginal R2 (R2abs), which is the proportion of variance explained by the 
fixed factors alone, and the conditional R2 (R2cond), which is the proportion of variance 
explained by both the fixed and random factors. For LMMs, predictive performances on new 
data were also evaluated by computing RMSE (noted RMSEnd) and NRMSE (noted NRMSEnd) 
on the validation set without the information on orchard. Finally, to validate the regression 
assumptions, Shapiro-Wilk (SW) tests at the significance level of 0.05 were performed to 
ensure the normal distribution of residuals. Analysis of scatter plot of fitted values vs. 
residuals validated the degree of homoscedasticity of the residuals. 
Model development and analysis were performed using the R software (R Core Team, 
2020). LMMs were developed using the function lmer of R package lme4 (Bates et al., 2020). 
RESULTS 
YOLOv5 performances 
On the training and validation images set, the Precision, Recall, and F1-score values of 
the YOLOv5 model were 0.40, 0.93, and 0.56, respectively. On the test set, YOLOv5 obtained a 
Precision of 0.85, a Recall of 0.94, and an F1-score of 0.89. Although it is impossible to strictly 
compare the performances of the YOLO algorithms obtained in different studies, the 
performances of the YOLOv5 obtained in our study were considered satisfactory and 
equivalent to the performances obtained on mango monocultures (Koirala et al., 2019). 
Correction models for yield estimation 
The first ANCOVA revealed the significance of region and cropping system effects on the 
slope and intercept of the relationship between actual and detected counts (P<0.001). This 
result reinforced the hypothesis of a corrective model integrating contextual variables as 
covariates was more appropriate. The second ANCOVA evidenced that the effect of the orchard 
was significant (P<0.001) on the slope of the relationship between actual and detected counts, 
suggesting a calibration of a corrective model even at the orchard scale. 
Tables 2 and 3 present the results obtained for LM and LMM models with and without 
square root transformation, respectively. The simple linear model with no transformation 
(LM1) fitted between actual and detected count had the lowest performance with an R2 of 0.34 
and an NRMSE of 20%. Comparison of LMs suggested that the addition of the region (LM2), 
cropping system (LM3), or both (LM4) as categorical covariates increased the predictive 
performance of correction models. However, these models with no data transformation did 
not respect the assumption of normality of residuals (SW test significant, indicating a 
distribution significantly different from a normal distribution). The models with square root 
data transformation had the same performance as the model without transformation (Table 
2). In addition, data transformation allowed to comply with the assumption of normality (SW 
test P>0.05 for LM2_sqrt, LM3_sqrt, and LM4_sqrt; Table 2) and to reduce heteroscedasticity 
(e.g., Figure 1) of the residuals for the linear models with categorical covariates. The best 
performances of LMs were obtained with model LM4_sqrt, including both region and cropping 
system as covariates. LM4_sqrt halves the prediction errors (RMSE and NRMSE) compared to 
the simple model LM1_sqrt. 
Results were similar for the LMM forms with the model LMM4, including the region and 
cropping system as covariates, reaching the best performance (Table 3). The predictive 
performances of LMM2 and LMM4 stayed stable when evaluated on new data (RMSEnd and 
NRMSEnd in Table 3), whereas it decreased for LMM1 and LMM3 but stayed equivalent to the 
corresponding LM form. Despite those performances, LMMs did not respect the linear 
regression assumptions even after the square root transformation of the continuous data (SW 
tests were all significant).

DISCUSSION 
This study highlighted the importance of model choice on tree yield estimation from 
image analysis. We showed that simple linear regression between detected and actual count 
was insufficient. Despite its attractive properties, the linear mixed-effect model only slightly 
improves the yield estimation in our data, whatever the categorical covariates used. This low 
improvement might be explained by the strong effect of the orchard on the tree yield variance, 
as shown by the ANCOVA result. When the effect of the random factor is too strong, it is known 
that LMM will reach the same performance as LM (Schielzeth and Nakagawa, 2013). A strong 
effect of the random variable will tend to produce non-normal distributions of group means 
that are not adapted to mixed-effect modelling. The high effect of the random effect might also 
explain the non-compliance with the assumption of normality and homoscedasticity of the 
residuals observed with the LMMs. 
Although the transformation of the dependent variable should be avoided if possible, it 
is sometimes necessary to obtain a model that better fits the data (Harrison et al., 2018). In 
our case, the square root transformation of the actual and detected counts gives a normal 
distribution of the residual and reduces the heteroscedasticity (Figure 1). Data 
transformation might be relevant to tree yields because these data frequently have a right-

 
 
 
 
 
353 
skewed distribution with a high number of low and zero values. It is specifically the case for 
mango trees in West Africa, for which yields are highly irregular and null to low yields are 
frequent in smallholder orchards (Sarron et al., 2018). 
While transformed LM with cropping system and region (LM4_sqrt) reached the best 
performances, the addition of region only (LM2_sqrt) still improved the prediction compared 
to the model with no categorical covariates (LM1_sqrt). This result suggested that only the 
region information is sufficient to reach good and stable model performances. An LM model 
using the orchard as a categorical covariate was also developed (data not shown) but 
performed less than the other LMs with the region or cropping system. Moreover, such a linear 
model is limited to the sample orchards and cannot be practically extended to new orchards. 
Our study showed that despite the calibration of the correction model being the most 
straightforward task when building a tree yield estimation tool, a minimum investment is 
required to investigate and choose the suitable model. This task is especially required when 
such a tool will be used in various contexts, including different orchards, cropping systems, 
and regions. In this study, we used data from only one cultivar, but the effect of the cultivar 
will be explored in further development by integrating data from various mango cultivars in 
West Africa. Similarly, the year effect was not explored as we assumed the data set to be 
sufficiently heterogeneous to cover all the fruit loads observed in the regions studied. The 
effect of year on the yield estimation of the same sample should be explored in order to know 
whether the correction model should be recalibrated each season or not. 
Other approaches have been developed for detection adjustment, including using an ML 
algorithm or contextual data extraction from the image. For instance, Cheng et al. (2017) 
trained an ML algorithm that extracted and used fruit and canopy features (number and area 
of fruit, area of fruit clusters, and leaf area per tree image) to predict apple yield. We chose to 
work only on the correction model, which required no further analysis or data extraction from 
the acquired image. The correction model has the advantages of simplifying and lightening 
the estimation tools and keeping the objective of developing frugal tools usable by West 
African smallholders. The use of correction models poses the constraint of verifying the 
assumptions on the residuals. This verification is not systematic in the previous studies. 
However, this study shows that compliance with the regression assumptions (i.e., normality 
of residuals and homoscedasticity) impacts the final model chosen. 
CONCLUSIONS 
This study focused on developing a tool for mango tree yield estimation based on image 
analysis. In particular, we discussed that although the training and validation of the image 
analysis algorithm is an important task, the development of correction models should not be 
neglected either. These models are essential to overcome the occlusion of the fruits and 
detection errors. 
On a set of mango tree images from West Africa, mangoes were detected by a specifically 
trained network (YOLOv5). We compared linear and linear mixed-effect models to correct the 
detected count and estimate the fruits’ actual count. We showed that assumptions on the 
normality of the residuals and homoscedasticity for some models were not respected, thus 
ruling out these models. Even if the LMM is an interesting model, it was not pertinent in our 
case, the variance of the grouping factor (orchard) being too high. The best performances were 
obtained with the linear model with square root transformation of continuous variables 
(detected and actual counts) and including region and cropping system as covariates 
(LM4_sqrt). The model using only the region information (LM2_sqrt) is also interesting if one 
wants to reduce the amount of information needed for the yield estimation. 
Beyond the development of a correction model, this study shows that checking the 
respect of the regression assumptions is critical. These results should motivate researchers to 
be more attentive when developing corrective models before their deployment into digital 
tools. Such results will be further used to improve fruit yield estimation under heterogeneous 
contexts of tree characteristics and orchard systems in West Africa.
