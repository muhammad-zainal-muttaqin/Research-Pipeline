Precise Yield Estimation
Hari Chandana Pichhika1 · Priyambada Subudhi2

Abstract
Accurate mango yield estimation is crucial for efﬁcient resource management and harvest planning. This study presents
a novel framework utilizing a modiﬁed version of MangoYOLO5, a custom YOLOv5 model for mango detection proposed
in our previous work. It includes an attentive feature fusion mechanism in the backbone, a convolutional block attention
module, dense connections in the neck, and an additional 10× 10 detection layer to enhance detection precision and reduce
false positives. Furthermore, we integrate the proposed detection model with an enhanced centroid tracking algorithm that
employs multi-frame references for occlusion handling and dynamic threshold adjustments to achieve consistent tracking
and accurate fruit counting. The system’s effectiveness is validated across varying light conditions and different trees,
demonstrating close alignment with ground truth data from manual labeling, achieving a mean average precision (mAP) of
93.6%. This work not only advances the state-of-the-art in mango detection and yield estimation but also provides insights
into developing robust computer vision solutions for agricultural applications.
Keywords Attention mechanism · Deep learning · Fruit counting · Precision agriculture · Object tracking
Introduction
Mangoes are highly valued in many tropical and subtropi-
cal areas worldwide due to their popularity and economic
importance (Delhi 2018). Efﬁcient mango farming requires
meticulous planning and management including the early
and precise prediction of yield, which is helpful in proactive
planning for storage, packing, and other logistical consider-
ations. Traditional manual counting methods are both labor-
intensive and time-consuming (Anderson et al. 2019), with
inherent inaccuracies arising from the repetitive counting.

To address these issues, the agricultural sector is increas-
ingly adopting computer vision techniques for automated
mango yield estimation. This involves collecting data in
the form of images or videos, upon which deep learning
(DL) models are trained. These models ﬁrst detect and
then count the detected mangoes. The accuracy of mango
detection can be inﬂuenced by several factors, including
changing lighting conditions and obstructions from ele-
ments such as fruits, leaves, or stems on the tree. In such
cases, detection can be precise; the counting process may
encounter issues, like counting the same mango multiple
times, especially when collecting video data from various
angles around the tree (Stein et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2019).
Initially, for determining the yield from image or video
datasets collected from mango farms, traditional feature-
based techniques were employed. These methods involve
extracting features such as color, shape, and texture using
approaches like edge detection, blob analysis, and the ran-
domized Hough transform (Neethi et al. 2023; Payne et al.
2013). However, these methods faced limitations, such as
sensitivity to variations in lighting, issues with occlusion
and overlap. As a result, the shift towards machine learning
(ML) methods, including support vector machines (SVM),
multi-layer perceptron, and random forests (Cengel et al.
2024; Neethi et al. 2021), offered improvements in detec-

tion and yield estimation accuracy. Despite their potential,
ML models faced challenges when confronted with sub-
stantial variations in input data, such as changes in mango
color, occlusion, and camera angles.
The introduction of DL techniques (Ergün 2024) has
marked signiﬁcant improvements in the ﬁeld of agriculture.
These methods automatically extract features from data,
overcoming the limitations of manual feature design and
showing superior performance under diverse conditions.
While DL algorithms have shown promising results, a sig-
niﬁcant limitation is that most of the previously proposed
methods have considered one side of the tree for yield es-
timation (Neethi et al. 2023). However, this approach may
not provide an accurate yield estimate, as mangoes can be
distributed all around the tree. Recent studies have aimed to
improve accuracy by considering multiple sides, employing
either DL-based tracking algorithms (Li et al. 2023; Li et al.
2024; Wang et al. 2019), 3D-based reconstruction meth-
ods (Stein et al. 2016), and transformer-based approaches
(Lin et al. 2021; Zhang and Tian 2023). Although track-
ing algorithms like SORT (Bewley et al. 2016) and Deep
SORT (Wojke et al. 2017) have proven effective for vari-
ous fruits like apples, citrus, and pineapples, applying these
methods to static objects of similar shape and size, such
as mangoes, presents unique challenges. These challenges
primarily arise from the limited variability in object fea-
tures, which reduces the effectiveness of the algorithms in
such scenarios. Moonrinta et al. (2010) developed an auto-
mated system for mapping and predicting pineapple crops
using video cameras, employing Speeded-Up Robust Fea-
tures (SURF) feature points for precise fruit detection. Sim-
ilarly, Chaivivatrakul et al. (2010) introduced a novel ap-
proach for pineapple detection and 3D reconstruction from
video sequences, utilizing Harris corner detection, Scale-
Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) keypoint descriptors,
and SVM for keypoint classiﬁcation, achieving signiﬁcant
accuracy in fruit dimension estimation.
In another study by Santos et al. (2020) focused on grape
yield prediction and automated harvesting using convolu-
tional neural networks (CNNs), achieving a high F1-score
of 0.91. Also, the authors introduces a public dataset with
annotated grape clusters and a novel annotation method-
ology for segmenting complex objects in natural images.
Parico and Ahamed (2021) introduce a real-time pear fruit
counter for mobile applications using You Only Look Once
version 4 (YOLOv4) and Deep SORT tracking, achieving
an F1 count of 87.85%, attributed to YOLOv4s low false
negatives. Later on, Junos et al. (2022) proposed an im-
proved You Only Look Once (YOLO) model for detecting
oil palm loose fruits from unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)
images. Key improvements include a densely connected
network for feature reuse, swish activation, multi-layer de-
tection for small targets, and prior box optimization for
accurate bounding boxes. Similarly, Prakash and Prakasam
(2023) introduced a CNN-based intelligent fruit classiﬁer
using bilinear pooling with heterogeneous streams, treat-
ing fruit classiﬁcation as a ﬁne-grained visual classiﬁca-
tion (FGVC) problem. Zhang et al. (2022) enhanced the
YOLOv3 network into OrangeYOLO for detecting small
citrus fruits at various scales, incorporating a channel and
spatial attention multi-scale fusion module. To address dou-
ble-counting of occluded fruits, they proposed the Orange-
Sort algorithm, which uses a tracking region counting strat-
egy and a tracking algorithm based on motion displace-
ment estimation. Wang et al. (2022) introduced a litchi
fruit detection method with an improved YOLOv5s model,
achieving a 62.77% mean average precision. In Li et al.
(2024), the authors developed a lightweight apple detec-
tion model by modifying YOLOv5s with a 3× 3 Conv and
ShufﬂeNetV2 backbone, removing the SPP and C3 layers,
using DWConv in the neck, and replacing PANet’s Concat
layers with Add layers, resulting in a more efﬁcient and
compact model. Similarly, Si et al. (2024) proposed a novel
registration algorithm for aligning thermal infrared and vis-
ible apple images by integrating feature point registration
with an active contour model, improving the accuracy of
multi-modal image alignment for agricultural applications.
Zhao et al. (2024) proposed YOLO-Granada, an enhanced
version of YOLOv5 designed for real-time pomegranate de-
tection. This model integrates ShufﬂeNetv2, grouped con-
volution, channel shufﬂe, and the Convolutional Block At-
tention Module (CBAM), achieving 92.2% accuracy and
a 17.3% improvement in detection speed. We have incorpo-
rated this technique in our comparative analysis to evaluate
its performance relative to other methods.
We have implemented an improved, fast, and compact
version of YOLOv5s (termed MangoYOLO 5) (Hari Chan-
dana et al. 2023) for efﬁcient detection in MangoNet Se-
mantic Dataset (Kestur et al. 2019). Despite its effective-
ness, it exhibits few limitations, including false positives
and inaccuracies in bounding box localization for partially
occluded mangoes. Among the approaches for yield esti-
mation, two primary methods are 3D reconstruction (Stein
et al. 2016) and tracking algorithms (Zhang et al. 2022). The
3D reconstruction method captures detailed spatial distribu-
tion and provides a comprehensive tree representation but
requires specialized equipment such as multi-camera setups
or Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) sensors, and in-
volves complex data processing, increasing both cost and
computational overhead, making it less practical for certain
conditions. In contrast, tracking algorithms are cost-effec-
tive, computationally efﬁcient, and rely on simple video-
based inputs. While they may face challenges with over-
lapping objects, they are well-suited for this study, which
involves static objects and simple conﬁgurations, enabling
effective tracking and yield estimation. Based on these con-

Data Collection and Pre-processing
A comprehensive video dataset was created by record-
ing eight Mangifera Indica (‘Banganapalle’) mango trees
in a 360° view using a handheld mobile camera with
a resolution of 1920× 1080 pixels. The recordings were
conducted during the daytime in the tropical summer
months of April–June 2022 at a mango orchard located
at the IIIT Sri City campus (latitude: 13.54951°N, lon-
gitude: 80.02749°E), Andhra Pradesh, India. The camera
was chosen for its accessibility and cost-effectiveness, with
imaging performed at a consistent distance of 1.5–2m to
balance the ﬁeld of view and resolution. The featured trees
are medium-sized ‘Banganapalle’ mango trees with an
average height of 2–3m and a canopy diameter of 1–2m,
containing approximately 50–60 mangoes per tree visible
under natural conditions. The orchard-style planting con-
ﬁguration, with a spacing of approximately 2m between
trees, facilitated optimal light penetration and smooth nav-
igation for video recording. Each tree was recorded by
walking around it in a 360° path, capturing an average
60–80s of video per tree to ensure comprehensive canopy
coverage under varying lighting conditions (sunny and
partially cloudy). To enhance diversity, the dataset initially
consisted of 704 image patches of size 256× 256 pixels,
extracted from three videos. Augmentation techniques,
including rotation (90°, 180°, 270°), ﬂipping (horizontal
and vertical), and scaling, were applied to each image,
generating six additional images per original. This resulted
in a total of 5000 image patches for training. Additionally,
around 400 high-resolution full-size images (1920× 1080)
were included for training and validation. Testing was per-
formed on ﬁve separate videos. Since the original dataset
lacked YOLO-compatible labels, all images were manually
annotated using the makesense.ai tool to generate ground

Proposed Detection Model
The primary objective of the enhanced MangoYOLO5
model is to increase the precision and accuracy of de-
tection, reducing false positives and addressing the key
challenges in yield estimation tasks. While the baseline
MangoYOLO5 model (Hari Chandana et al. 2023) sig-
niﬁcantly improves on the general YOLOv5s for mango
detection, certain limitations such as false positives and
inaccuracies in bounding box localization for occluded
mangoes were observed. To tackle these issues, we propose
a series of architectural enhancements, which are detailed
in the following subsections.
Attentive Feature Fusion in the Backbone
We integrated a unique Attentive Feature Fusion (AFF)
block (see Fig. 2) in the back-bone layer in our proposed
architecture, designed to selectively emphasize important
features during the feature extraction process in the back-
bone. The AFF block (Dai et al. 2021) consists of multiple
attention mechanisms that work synergistically to boost the
model’s focus on relevant regions, ensuring that key fea-
tures are effectively captured and utilized for detection.
The core components of the AFF block include:
 Channel Attention (CA): This submodule applies global
average pooling and channel-wise dependencies to recal-
ibrate features along the channel dimension, enhancing
feature representations based on their signiﬁcance.
Fig. 2 Enhanced MangoYOLO5 architecture. SPP Spatial Pyramid Pooling, CSP1 Cross Stage Partial Layer 1, Conv Convolution, Concat Con-
catenation
 Spatial Attention (SA): This component generates spatial
attention maps by leveraging both average pooling and
max pooling along the channel axis, which are then com-
bined to highlight important spatial regions in the feature
map.
 Cross-layer Feature Fusion (C2F): To enrich feature in-
formation, C2F combines features from different layers,
promoting the propagation of useful information across
layers, which is crucial for capturing details in small ob-
ject detection.
 Multi-scale Feature Aggregation (MSFA): The MSFA
mechanism aggregates features at different scales to cap-
ture both global and local context, improving detection
performance for objects of varying sizes.
 Identity: This acts as a direct skip connection, allowing
the unaltered input features to pass through and be com-
bined with the outputs of the other attention mechanisms,
ensuring the retention of original feature information for
better representation.
Each of these modules within the AFF block plays a role
in selectively enhancing feature maps, allowing the model
to focus on areas with occluded or clustered mangoes. The
output of the AFF block is subsequently passed through
convolutional layers, resulting in a more reﬁned representa-
tion that enhances the backbone’s ability to capture critical
features.
CBAM and Dense Connections in the Neck
In the next section, we introduce the CBAM (Woo et al.
2018) and dense connections to further enhance feature ﬂow
and localization precision. CBAM integrates both chan-
nel and spatial attention mechanisms, enabling the network
to adaptively emphasize informative regions in the feature

map. Its inclusion in the neck is essential because, after fea-
ture extraction in the backbone, the neck aggregates multi-

contributing to stronger feature representation without in-

Enhanced Detection Head
To enhance mango detection and address limitations in the
baseline model, an additional 10× 10 detection head has
been incorporated into the model’s head section. This de-
tection layer captures coarser features, making it particu-
larly effective for detecting larger mangoes. It is integrated
into a multiscale detection pipeline by fusing it with higher-
resolution layers (20× 20, 40× 40, and 80× 80). This hier-
archical fusion enables the model to utilize contextual in-
formation at multiple scales, improving detection accuracy
for mangoes of various sizes. The enhanced architecture
strengthens the semantic connections between shallow and
deep features, as illustrated in Fig. 2.
To further enhance detection accuracy, an intersection
over union (IoU)-based reﬁnement step is employed for
bounding boxes. By setting a lower IoU threshold (τIoU=
0.5) compared to the conventional threshold 0.7, the reﬁne-
ment reduces false positives and ensures tighter bounding
box localization.

troid tracking algorithm to accurately estimate the yield by
monitoring the movement of detected mangoes across suc-
cessive video frames. This process assigns a unique identi-
ﬁer (ID) to each mango for consistent tracking and incor-
porates several improvements, including multi-frame refer-
ence consideration, and dynamic threshold computation to
the traditional centroid tracking approach as explained in
the following subsections.

Discussion of Results and Experimental
Analysis
In this section, we present and examine the outcomes of
our developed system for on-tree detection and tracking of
mangoes by dividing the results obtained into two subsec-
tions: detection results and tracking results. Experiments
were conducted on a high performance computing (HPC)
facility graphics node with Intel® Xeon® processor (Intel
Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and an NVIDIA®
Tesla® graphics processing unit (GPU) (NVIDIA Corpo-
ration, Santa Clara, CA, USA), featuring 16 GB memory, a
base clock of approximately 1190 MHz, and 3584 CUDA
cores. The evaluation of the algorithms, based on detec-
tion and tracking, is presented in detail in the subsequent
subsections.

(Ultralytics, 2021), MangoNet (Kestur et al. 2019), the

advanced YOLO series models such as YOLOv8, YOLOv9,

Tracking Results
In the tracking algorithm, we applied an enhanced cen-
troid-based tracking approach with a dynamic threshold to
classify newly detected mangoes as either new or existing.
The results of the proposed detection model were processed
through widely adopted tracking algorithms such as SORT
(Bewley et al. 2016), Deep SORT (Wojke et al. 2017),
FairMOT (Y. Zhang et al. 2021), and the proposed track-

yield estimation across various crops. We have observed
that the SORT algorithm generates numerous false posi-
tives, counting the same mango multiple times and inﬂat-

a reference box for mango counting, challenges arise due
to camera movement, leading to inaccuracies and an over-
estimation of mangoes. In contrast, FairMOT (as shown in
Fig. 7a) improves mango tracking by assigning unique IDs
to mangoes, thereby reducing redundant counts. This im-
provement is evident in its ability to maintain consistent
ID assignment across speciﬁc frames. However, our pro-

Ablation Study
Impact of IoU Threshold on Detection Metrics
To select the best IoU threshold, we conducted an ex-
periment and analyzed its impact on detection results
through metrics like precision, recall, and mAP. As shown
in Table 4, lowering the IoU threshold from 0.7 to 0.5
signiﬁcantly improved recall (77.0%) by reducing false
negatives. While this adjustment slightly reduced preci-
sion (89.3%) due to an increase in false positives. Despite
this trade-off, the highest mAP (91.6%) was observed at
the 0.5 threshold, demonstrating a robust balance between
precision and recall. This ﬁnding suggests that a lower
IoU threshold is optimal for detecting partially occluded
objects or scenarios where higher recall is essential for
performance.

Impact of Additional Sub-module Components on the
Proposed Centroid Tracking Approach
We conducted an extensive ablation analysis to evaluate the
impact of different submodules in the proposed tracking ap-
proach, using centroid tracking with static thresholding as
the baseline. Table 7 highlights the performance improve-
ments achieved with each integrated submodule. For in-
stance, IoU-based bounding box reﬁnement enhances pre-
cision and MOTA but slightly reduces mAP due to tighter
bounding boxes missing some detections. Multi-frame ref-
erence tracking improves recall and robustness by leverag-
ing temporal information but increases computational com-
plexity. The combination of IoU reﬁnement and dynamic
threshold tracking boosts all metrics by adapting to vary-
ing object sizes, although it requires ﬁne-tuning threshold

grates these submodules, achieving the highest performance
(mAP of 93.6%), balancing precision, recall, and MOTA.
This analysis provides a clear understanding of the trade-
offs associated with each submodule.
Conclusions and Future Work
In this work, we present a system for detecting and tracking
mangoes to estimate the yield from video sequences cap-
tured using stand-alone cameras. The proposed framework
consists of an enhanced MangoYOLO5 model, which in-
corporates attentive feature fusion in the backbone, CBAM
and dense connections in the neck, and an additional 10×

10 detect layer to reduce false positives and ensure accu-
rate detection. The tracking module employs a modiﬁed
centroid tracking algorithm that uses multi-frame reference
for occlusion handling, and dynamic threshold selection
to uniquely track mangoes across frames. The proposed
model achieved an mAP of 93.6% when tested across mul-
tiple scenarios involving occlusions and varying lighting
conditions, demonstrating its effectiveness in the accurate
detection and tracking of mangoes. However, the model’s
performance in terms of MOTA is observed to be inferior,
ranging from a minimum of 76% to a maximum of 84%,
when affected by poor video quality factors such as low
resolution, blurriness, inconsistent lighting, as well as chal-
lenges like extreme occlusion or abrupt camera movements.
Future work will focus on incorporating advanced tracking
mechanisms and exploring varying imaging distances to
address the current limitations of the proposed model, en-
hancing its robustness and optimization for real-time, large-
scale monitoring.
