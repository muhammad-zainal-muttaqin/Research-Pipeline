Abstract—Almost all oil palm harvest management is still 
done manually today, especially in the monitoring stage where 
oil palm farmers assess the ripeness of oil palm fresh fruit 
bunches (FFB) in the plantation. The monitoring stage, which is 
a crucial step, requires re-checking the ripeness of fresh fruit 
bunches (FFB) at the collection point (TPH) to ensure that the 
bunches brought to the factory are ripe. This research focuses 
on creating an SOP to be implemented in the plantation so that 
the AI in the automated system, also created in this research, 
can be used to optimally detect, calculate, and classify the 
ripeness of the FFB. By using the YOLO8 algorithm based on 
deep learning and CNN, the dataset used consists of 4257 images 
taken directly from PTPN IV plantations. The results of this 
study showed good model performance, with an accuracy of 
96.56%. The application of this model is expected to increase the 
efficiency and accuracy of FFB quality monitoring, reduce 
losses, and support better data-based decision-making. 
Keywords—palm oil, ripeness classification, deep learning, 
operational efficiency, harvest management 
I. INTRODUCTION 
In Indonesia, palm oil is the largest supplier of vegetable 
oil [1], [2]. The important role of palm oil in the Indonesian 
economy requires a tight operational flow of palm oil [2]. One 
of the state-owned companies involved in oil palm plantations 
is PTPN, which has a number of operations for its own 
harvesting activities. PTPN’s harvesting operations are known 
as harvest management. 
The main stages of harvest management in PTPN include 
planning, organizing, implementing and monitoring. In gen- 
eral, harvest management involves a series of activities 
ranging from cutting ripe bunches according to criteria, 
collecting and gathering loose fruit, and arranging bunches at 
the collection point (TPH). Currently, all harvest management 
activities are still done manually by the Harvest Foreman and 
the Afdeling Assistant. The Harvest Foreman is in charge of 
making all the preparations before the harvest day, such as 
recording and esti- mating the resources needed, while the 
Afdeling Assistant has full control over the Afdeling’s 
harvesting activities according to the plan prepared. 
The monitoring phase is a crucial step. This step consists 
of calculating and rechecking the eligibility of the FFBs at the 
TPH. This verification is mandatory as it has a direct impact 
on the quality of the crude palm oil if any raw bunches are 
transported to the factory [1]-[6]. This step can take a long 
time. If there are problems, such as bad weather or human 
error, there will be delays in all the harvest management steps. 
These delays can result in block-by-block harvesting not being 
completed, time conflicts when FFB is transported from TPH 
multiple times, potential resting time, trucks having to wait 
and turn around until they are full, damaged roads due to being 
passed more than once in a harvesting round, idle capacity at 
the mill, and increased harvesting costs. 
After a series of activities at the TPH, the FFBs are loaded 
into the factory according to a predetermined schedule. 
Harvest management SOPs are of great importance, especially 
the criteria for the ripeness level of the FFB. This SOP has a 
direct impact on the processed crude oil because, in addition 
to preventing losses, the SOP on the ripeness level of the FFB 
affects the yield, quality and grade of crude palm oil (CPO) 
and palm kernel oil (PKO). Therefore, it is very important to 
classify the ripeness level of FFB and calculate the amount of 
FFB harvested at TPH before transportation to the palm oil 
mill to maintain the oil quality. 
In this research, the researchers propose the YOLO (You 
Only Look Once) algorithm model used in computer vision 
[4]-[8] because it is considered to fit the needs of the field with 
the ease and lightness of the model in execution. To improve 
the performance of the model, this research collects a larger 
and more complex data set in accordance with the conditions 
in the field, using internal data belonging to PTPN IV. The 
researcher will study the proposed model with techniques to 
prevent overfitting and ensure accuracy in counting objects 
[4], [5], [8]. The model will be tested by photographing the 
bunches at the TPH to create an SOP for photographing. The 
ability of the model to detect objects, classify objects, and 
count objects according to their class will be the main concern. 
The way to take pictures that maximizes object detection 
results will be used as the SOP for arranging and 
photographing bunches with smartphones in this study.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
A. Deep Learning 
Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning that uses 
artificial neural networks to analyze data and make decisions 
based on patterns found in the data. Deep learning is par- 
ticularly effective in image classification and object detection 
tasks due to its ability to learn complex and non-linear data 
representations [5], [15]. 
B. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) 
CNN is a neural network architecture specifically 
designed for image processing tasks. CNNs consist of a 
convolution layer that extracts features from the input image, 
followed by a pooling layer that reduces the dimensionality of 
the data, and a fully connected layer that performs 
classification [9]. 
C. YOLO Algorithm 
CNN is a neural network architecture specifically 
designed for image processing tasks. CNNs consist of a 
convolution layer that extracts features from the input image, 
followed by a pooling layer that reduces the dimensionality of 
the data, and a fully connected layer that performs 
classification [9]. 
 
Fig. 1. Comparison graph of the latest generation 5 to 8 YOLOs [18] 
 
YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a fast and efficient object 
detection algorithm capable of detecting objects in real time 
[13], [18]. The latest generation of YOLO, such as YOLOv8 
(see Fig. 1), offers significant improvements in detection 
accuracy and speed over previous versions, making it an ideal 
choice for real-time applications [13], [18]. 
 
Fig. 2. Performance comparison of YOLOv8 object detection models [13] 
 
Based on (see Fig. 1), the graph illustrates a clear trend 
where increasing the various scales also increases the accuracy 
of the model [18]. Each model includes different scales 
denoted by n (nano), s (small), m (medium), l (large), and 
x (extra-large). YOLOv8n (Nano) (see Fig. 2) is a smaller, 
lighter version of the YOLOv8 model, optimized for speed and 
efficiency in resource-constrained environments such as edge 
devices or mobile applications. In other words, YOLOv8n is 
designed to run faster because of its smaller architecture. The 
researchers chose this model because it is lightweight for real- 
time bunch detection, especially to compensate for the lack of 
mobile device specifications in the field. 
D. Transfer Learning 
 
Transfer learning (TL) is a technique in machine learning 
where a model trained for one task can be used to solve another 
task. This technique can speed up training time and increase 
efficiency so that the model’s performance on different tasks 
can improve [24]. The method used is pre-training, where 
researchers use a model that has been trained on the same 
or similar task as the new task, but then use the model as a 
starting point to build a new model from scratch. 
E. Object Detection in Agriculture 
 
In the palm oil industry, object detection technology can 
be used to automate the process of monitoring and grading 
FFB at the TPH. Companies can improve the accuracy of FFB 
ripeness classification, reduce losses and improve operational 
efficiency. Previous research shows that the use of this 
technol- ogy can provide significant benefits in improving the 
quality and quantity of oil palm production [3]-[6]. 
F. Image Annotation and Dataset Preparation 
 
The creation of a high quality dataset is an important step 
in the development of an object recognition model [3]. This 
process involves image annotation, where objects in the image 
are labeled and bounding boxes are used to train the model. A 
good dataset should cover a wide range of lighting conditions, 
viewing angles, and object variations to ensure that the model 
performs well in different real-world situations [3], [18]. 
G. Evaluation Metrics for Object Detection 
 
The performance evaluation of object detection models is 
usually done using metrics such as mean average precision 
(mAP), intersection over union (IoU), and inference time. 
These metrics help to measure the accuracy, precision, and 
speed of the model in detecting and classifying objects 
[7], [18]. The use of appropriate evaluation metrics is essential 
to ensure that the developed model can meet the needs of real- 
world applications.

This stage consists of cleaning, resizing, annotation, and 
augmentation. By resizing all images to the input size

C. Model Development 
The research continued by performing a transfer learning 
model from Ultralytics’ pre-trained weights to speed up the 
training process and improve performance. 
 
Fig. 6. Loads pre-trained weights ultralytics 
 
Using the pre-trained weights on Ultralytics, the model 
was retrained using new preprocessing data to match the 
specific task [24] of detecting and classifying FFB in images 
with a new dataset from PTPN IV by using Pre-trained 
Weights and Fine-tuning. 
 
Fig. 7. Hypertuning parameter settings 
Task: detect, sets the task for object detection. Mode: 
train, indicates that the model is being trained. Model: 
yolov8n.pt, specifies the nano version of the YOLOv8 model 
used. Data: Path to the data set configuration file (chip.yaml). 
Epochs: 200, sets the number of epochs for training. Workers: 
16, the number of workers used to load data. Batch: -1, use an 
automatic batch size. Imgsz: 800, sets the input image size to 
800x800 pixels. Save: True, saves the training results. Cache: 
ram, caches the data set in RAM for faster access. Verbose, 
Profile, Val, Plots: True, enables detailed log, profile, 
validation, and plotting of training results. 
 
These parameters are designed to optimize the training 
pro- cess of the YOLOv8 model, focusing on efficiency and 
quality of the recognition results. As with the Batch 
Processing set- ting, data is processed in batches for 
computational efficiency. Setting Batch Processing to -1 
allows the model to process multiple tasks at once, depending 
on the resource limits of the computer, speeding up training 
and taking advantage of parallelism on hardware such as a 
GPU.

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
A. Performance Metrics Deep Dive 
 
Fig. 10. YOLOv8n’s best set of training result graphs 
 
The process of training the YOLOv8 model using the 
collected and augmented data resulted in several important 
performance metrics. The YOLOv8 model has optimal 
perfor- mance in detecting and classifying oil palm FFBs. The 
training box loss, classification loss, and distance field loss 
(DFL) all decrease consistently, indicating the model’s 
improved ability to accurately predict the bounding box and 
correctly classify objects. The validation loss also follows the 
same trend, indicating that the model generalizes well and can 
classify unseen data well. The precision and recall metrics 
both show a steady increase and reach high values. This 
means that the model’s predictions are becoming more 
accurate and it is effectively detecting most of the relevant 
objects. The mAP50 metric shows a high and increasing 
trend, indicating good performance at the IoU threshold of 
50%. 
The precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 metrics all 
increase steadily, indicating the improved accuracy and 
perfor- mance of the model in detecting relevant objects at 
different IoU thresholds. Overall, the consistent decrease in 
all loss metrics during training and validation, along with the 
high precision, recall, and mAP values, indicate that the 
model performs well without overfitting. It effectively detects 
and classifies objects and maintains good performance on 
both training and unseen validation data. 
B. Confusion Matrix 
 
Fig. 11. Confusion matrix training results 
Accuracy is calculated by dividing the number of correct 
predictions by the total number of predictions. In this case, the 
model successfully predicted 96.56% of the samples correctly. 
Thus, the learning model’s ability to guess the bunch ripeness 
criteria in the image has achieved high accuracy and minimal 
errors. 
The background column in the table is the Binary Cross 
Entropy (BCE) or object loss in YOLO, which is calculated 
using the Binary Cross Entropy. It is intended to help the 
model determine whether a portion of the image contains an 
object or not. This is especially important in object detection 
tasks, where the model must not only detect the object, but 
also determine its location in the image. If the YOLO model 
is not trained to recognize the background, then any part of the 
image will be considered a potential object. This will lead to 
inaccurate predictions because the model cannot clearly 
distinguish relevant objects from noise or unimportant 
elements in the image, and the model will produce many false 
positives because it considers the background to be an object.

It can be concluded that distance, lighting and shooting 
angle affect the object detection results, especially in images 
with a large number of objects. For example, images (d) and 
(d) have many objects, but only image (e) has convincing 
prediction and detection results. The best Standard Operat- 
ing Procedure (SOP) for taking images is to have sufficient 
lighting, a shooting angle between 45◦ - 90◦, bunches below 
30, and a distance that is not too small or cropped. 
Based on (see Fig. 11), two features are created that can 
be modified to improve object prediction. The creation of 
these two features is done by differentiating the confidence 
level. If there are many objects, then the confidence used is 
40% and vice versa for 60%. This is done so that objects that 
look small can be detected. 
V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS 
Overall, the results show that the trained YOLOv8 model 
successfully improved its performance on both the training 
and validation data. The (see Fig. 10) and (see Fig. 11) 
confirm that the model is able to learn effectively and 
generalize well to previously unseen data. With these results, 
the trained YOLOv8 model can be implemented in a 
computer vision- based object detection and counting system 
at TPH PTPN IV. This implementation is expected to 
improve the efficiency and accuracy of FFB quality 
monitoring, as well as support better decision making based 
on accurate and real-time data. 
Future research could focus on improving the 
performance of the YOLOv8 model by incorporating larger 
and more diverse data sets, further physiological and 
contextual features, and optimized hyperparameters, or by 
using the latest version of the YOLO model. By discussing 
these research directions, this paper can advance the crop 
management process and develop more effective prevention, 
intervention, and support strategies to prevent yield loss. 
Ultimately, these efforts will contribute to a more optimized 
harvest that is safer and more inclusive to maximize company 
production.
