Abstract
The demand for intelligent agriculture is increasing due to the continuous impact of world 
food and environmental crises. Focusing on fruit detection, with the rapid development of 
object detection technology, it is now possible to achieve high efficiency and high accu-
racy in fruit detection systems. However, detecting fruit with high precision in unstructured 
orchard environments remains particularly challenging. Such environments, which are 
composed of varying illumination conditions and degrees of occlusion, can be mitigated by 
certain strategies. To our knowledge, this is the first time that optimization strategies used 
in fruit detection have been reviewed. This review aims to explore methods for improving 
fruit detection in complex environments. First, we describe the common types of complex 
backgrounds found in outdoor orchard environments. Subsequently, we divide the improve-
ment measures into two categories: optimization before and after image sampling. Next, 
we compare the test results obtained before and after the application of these improved 
methods. Finally, we describe the future development trends of fruit detection optimiza-
tion technology in complex backgrounds. We hope that this review will inspire researchers 
to design their optimization strategies and help explore lower-cost and more robust fruit 
detection systems.
Keywords  Intelligent agriculture · Fruit detection · Computer vision · Deep learning
Introduction
With the rapid growth in population and continuous urbanization, natural resources on 
which agriculture depends are becoming increasingly stressed (FAO, 2017). Meanwhile, 
environmental deterioration caused by climate change and human activities poses severe 
challenges to the growth of agricultural crop yields (Tebaldi & Lobell, 2008). In addition, 
the negative side effects of agricultural chemicals are becomingt increasingly apparent. 
However, the widespread use of agricultural chemicals has contributed significantly to the

spectacular growth of crop production in the past few decades (FAO, 2017). Therefore, 
innovative solutions to the drawbacks of traditional agriculture are required.
Currently, smart agriculture (Tyagi, 2016) has been gaining more importance since it 
can deal with the issues of sustainable agriculture caused by global climate change and 
human activities (Gebbers & Adamchuk, 2010). The use of artificial intelligence (AI) and 
robotics to replace technologies in traditional agriculture that can increase the yield per 
unit area but are environmentally hazardous (e.g., excessive use of pesticides and fertilizers 
and large-scale use of agricultural machinery) can meet the requirements of agricultural 
sustainability (Pretty, 2008). Moreover, the application of AI technology in agriculture is 
still in its infancy, but it has developed rapidly in recent years and has spread to many dif-
ferent fields in agricultural applications, such as crop management, livestock management, 
and water and soil management (Gongal et al., 2015; Kamilaris & Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018; 
Liakos et al., 2018; Koirala et al., 2019a, 2019b; Zhang et al., 2020).
Specific to the field of fruit detection, automatic fruit picking, and fruit yield estimation 
in smart orchards (Chen et al., 2021), with the booming development of object detection 
technology in the past two decades (Zou et al., 2019), it is possible to have a fruit detection 
and counting system that has both high efficiency and high accuracy. Based on this, the 
error between the predicted results of an automatic fruit production estimation technology 
and the actual yield should be lower than that of traditional manual visual production esti-
mation (Apolo-Apolo et al., 2020a). However, the background of collected images is more 
complicated in the actual wild orchard environment, which is mainly reflected through the 
occurrence of occlusion, overlap, and clustering of fruits in images; different light direc-
tions and light intensity; and the difficulty in distinguishing fruits and the background by 
visual appearance (Sa et al., 2016). Most studies have suggested that such complex back-
grounds are the major challenges limiting the accuracy of fruit detection as well as locali-
zation based on machine vision in the orchard environment (Gongal et al., 2015; Wang 
et al., 2019).
An image processing approach to deal with the influence of complex backgrounds is 
to manually extract features (such as color features, geometric features, and texture fea-
tures) that have large differences between the detection target and the complex background 
(Gongal et al., 2015) to separate the detection target from the complex background. The 
output picture is a binary image (the white pixel area is the detection target, and the black 
pixel area is the background). This method is used by various researchers and relies on 
traditional machine learning algorithms. For fruit detection, features such as Haar-like fea-
tures (Viola & Jones, 2001, 2004), histogram of oriented gradient (HOG) (Dalal & Triggs, 
2005), deformable part-based model (DPM) (Felzenszwalb et  al., 2008a, 2008b, 2009, 
2010), scales-invariant feature transformation (SIFT) (Lowe, 1999), and speeded up robust 
features (SURF) (Bay et al., 2008) are often used as the features to be extracted. These 
features are often combined with machine learning algorithms, including K-means clus-
tering (MacQueen, 1967), Bayesian classifier (Ripley, 2007), KNN clustering (Cover & 
Hart, 1967), artificial neural network (Rumelhart et al., 1985) (ANN), and support vector 
machine (SVM) (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995), to classify image pixels into detection targets 
and complex backgrounds to complete the task of target detection. Although the above 
method is feasible to a certain extent, in a field environment with increasingly complex 
factors, it is difficult to increase the precision of fruit detection technology based on tradi-
tional machine learning algorithms, and some defects are exposed. For example, a tradi-
tional machine learning algorithm usually detects several clustered fruits as a single fruit, 
making it difficult to separate them accurately (Wei et al., 2014). When a fruit is divided 
into two or more parts by branches or leaves, the conventional machine learning algorithm

will misjudge these sub-regions as different target fruits. Furthermore, the performance of 
traditional object detection technology depends more on the quality of feature engineering 
(i.e., the quality of artificially selected features), and the image processing experience of 
experimental personnel will have a greater impact on the final detection accuracy.
The performance of feature engineering, which relies on manual feature selection, tends 
to saturate; therefore, traditional object detection technology has reached a bottleneck. 
However, the emergence and development of deep learning (DL) (LeCun et al., 2015) has 
helped to address and overcome this bottleneck. In particular, convolutional neural net-
works (CNNs) (LeCun et al., 1998) have made significant progress in target detection tech-
nology. A deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) has powerful image feature learn-
ing ability and can automatically learn higher-level features of images, which cannot be 
extracted by traditional feature engineering. In recent years, many state-of-the-art (SOTA) 
deep learning frameworks have emerged in the field of image classification, detection, and 
segmentation, including AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al., 2012), VGGNet (Simonyan & Zis-
serman, 2014), GoogleNet (Szegedy et al., 2015), and ResNet (He et al., 2016) used in 
classification tasks, the R-CNN series (R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, and Faster R-CNN) (Girshick 
et al., 2014; Girshick, 2015; Ren et al., 2015), the YOLO series (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020; 
Li et al., 2022; Redmon & Farhadi, 2017, 2018; Redmon et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022a, 
2022b), SSD (Liu et al., 2016), FCN (Long et al., 2015), U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015), 
SegNet (Badrinarayanan et al., 2017), the DeepLab series (DeepLab v1, DeepLab v2, Dee-
pLab v3, and DeepLab v3+) (Chen et al., 2014, 2017a, 2017b, 2018), and Mask R-CNN 
(He et al., 2017) used in detection and segmentation tasks. Therefore, in response to the 
impact of complex environments, DCNN-based fruit detection models show better perfor-
mance than traditional fruit detection technology (Liakos et al., 2018), and fruit detection 
has also entered a new stage.
In the field of agriculture, deep learning is a promising technology that has contin-
ued to attract increased interest from researchers in related fields. Deep learning exhibits 
powerful performance and great potential for fruit detection, automatic fruit picking, and 
fruit yield estimation in a smart orchard. However, researchers currently face the problem 
that the detection performance of fruit detection models or vision systems in a real field 
is degraded compared with those obtained in experimental environments (indoor experi-
mental laboratory or outdoor test fields). This is because the current deep learning network 
architecture still has certain limitations when dealing with high-complexity scenes (Zou 
et al., 2019). Furthermore, the complex outdoor orchard environment will interfere with 
the performance of the machine vision equipment, making it difficult to achieve the desired 
effect. In our opinion, researchers should focus on the current problem of fruit detection 
and find an effective optimization strategy that can overcome or mitigate it while looking 
forward to the design of model architectures based on more advanced theories and with 
more powerful feature extraction and learning capabilities in the future. This is one of the 
motivations for this review.
Additionally, there are some existing reviews on the application of deep learning in 
agriculture (Kamilaris & Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018; Koirala et al., 2019a, 2019b; Zhang et al., 
2020). However, most of these reviews only briefly summarize the scenes of complex out-
door agricultural environments and the adverse effects they cause, or introduce some gen-
eral optimization strategies in the field of object detection. These reviews all lack detailed 
descriptions of the strategies currently employed to address these complex backgrounds. 
Nonetheless, images with complex backgrounds are becoming more common due to the 
increasing applicability of AI technology in the field of agriculture. Therefore, it is neces-
sary to review these existing optimization strategies. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the

This review article discusses fruit detection, a popular research field in the application 
of AI technology in the agricultural scene. The paper is structured as follows: Sect. Com-
plex background factors and negative effects provides a summary of the types of complex 
environments currently faced in fruit detection tasks and their impact on detection perfor-
mance. Section Complex backgrounds in outdoor orchard environments lists optimization 
methods for addressing these complex backgrounds, which can be divided into optimiza-
tion strategies before and after image collection, according to the stage in the entire task 
process. Section Meeting the challenges of complex backgrounds provides conclusions and 
presents future expectations in this domain.
Complex background factors and negative effects
This review divides the background types of visual detection objects into two categories 
based on the complexity of the backgrounds that enter the field of view of visual sensors in 
different agricultural application scenarios: clean and complex backgrounds. For example, 
a clean background in the case of an RGB camera refers to a solid color plane with an obvi-
ous color difference from the object to be detected, or only a few interference factors that 
have minimal effect on the detection performance and are easy to eliminate, such as the 
texture of the mechanical structure and regular lines on the conveyor belt. These situations 
are typically found in areas such as supermarket self-checkout or automated fruit and veg-
etable sorting, as shown in the left half of Fig. 1.
In contrast, a complex background is common in fields that require deployment in out-
door planting environments, such as outdoor fruit picking robots and automatic fruit yield 
estimation. Unlike a clean background, the factors that contribute to a complex background

Lighting conditions
Unstructured lighting conditions are common complex background factors that can affect 
fruit detection performance in the field environment. These conditions change dynamically 
over time and with changing weather conditions, such as changes in the direction of sun-
light throughout the day and differences in light intensity between sunny and cloudy days. 
These factors are reflected in the fruit image as follows: (1) changes in the brightness of 
the fruit image with light intensity; (2) oversaturated areas on the fruit surface due to direct 
sunlight; and (3) division of the fruit surface into shadow and non-shadow parts due to the 
phenomenon of light occlusion. This is illustrated in the upper right part of Fig. 1.
Although the human visual system can determine the color invariance of the surface 
of an object under changing light and imaging conditions, imaging devices do not have 
this color invariance capability (Tian et al., 2019a, 2019b). As a result, lighting conditions 
can significantly affect fruit detection performance (Zhou et al., 2022). Wang et al. (2020) 
noted that some segmentation algorithms based on conventional machine learning are sig-
nificantly affected by lighting in the natural environment. Furthermore, non-optimized deep 
learning models exhibit misrecognition in uneven lighting. Yan et al. (2021) compared an 
improved model optimized for complex backgrounds with four other mainstream object 
detection models and reported that non-optimized detection models may exhibit misiden-
tification or missed recognition under cloudy conditions. The detection performance of the 
same model under different lighting conditions will also vary. For instance, the green citrus 
detection method proposed by Wang et al. (2018a, 2018b) is more prone to false positives 
under front-lighting conditions than under back-lighting conditions, while the phenomenon 
of missed fruit is the opposite. In the kiwifruit detection task, the improved YOLOv3-tiny 
model proposed by Fu et al. (2021) yielded better detection performance in the afternoon 
than in the morning.
Occluded fruits
Occlusion is considered one of the most important complex background factors in object 
detection. While the human visual system cannot see through scene occluders to visual-
ize the complete picture of the occluded target, it can infer abstractly what the target is 
based on a small visible area. However, this capability is expensive for visual detection sys-
tems because better feature extraction and information inference capabilities usually mean 
a larger and more complex model architecture. The design of a model architecture is often 
limited by the hardware conditions of the deployment platform. Therefore, target occlusion 
poses a huge problem to detection performance.
In general, occlusion can be divided into two groups: inter-class occlusion and intra-
class occlusion. The former occurs when an object is occluded by objects of different cat-
egories, while the latter occurs when an object is occluded by objects of the same category 
(Wang et al., 2018a, 2018b). In fruit detection, fruit occlusion cases occur as follows: (1)

fruits are covered by branches and leaves of the fruit plant and (2) fruits of the same plant 
cover, overlap, and adhere to each other, as shown in the lower right part of Fig. 1.
Gongal et al. (2016) noted that a camera cannot capture most apples (approximately 60% 
of the fruits can be seen) from only one side of the apple crown because leaves, branches, 
and other apples shelter each other in an orchard, which has a significant effect on the 
fruit counting accuracy. Yu et al. (2019) used a Mask R-CNN model to detect and segment 
strawberries growing in an unstructured environment and locate their picking points. The 
overall accuracy and recall were 95.78 and 95.41, respectively. The detection errors can be 
attributed to the fact that the collected image’s features are not prominent enough due to 
the effect of light, occlusion, or the photograph angle when sampling the strawberry data-
set, leading to misjudgment. The research of Jia et al. (2020) on detecting and segmenting 
green apples demonstrated that the proposed recognition method yielded the best detec-
tion performance for unobstructed fruits. However, the detection performance significantly 
decreased for blocking fruits and further deteriorated for overlapping fruits.
Fruits with different degrees of maturity
Orchards typically harvest fruits when they are close to maturity to ensure that they are 
sold at the optimal time for consumption. At this point, the same fruit tree may have fruits 
at various stages of maturity. If the appearance of the fruit (mainly its color) shows notice-
able differences based on the degree of maturity (also see the lower right part of Fig. 1), it 
can interfere with robotic harvesting, which relies on accurate and dependable fruit detec-
tion. Additionally, if there is a need to monitor the growth stage of fruits or provide staged 
yield estimation through fruit detection technology, it is essential to improve the detection 
model’s robustness for fruits at different degrees of maturity.
Tian et al. (2019a, 2019b) proposed an apple detection model for different growth stages 
in an actual orchard environment. The researchers divided apple growth into three stages 
according to the period when the color characteristics changed significantly: young apples, 
expanding apples, and ripe apples. The experimental results showed that the detection per-
formance of ripe apples was the best, and that of young apples was the worst. The same 
experimental results were also obtained in the detection of litchi fruits (Wang et al., 2021a, 
2021b, 2021c) and cherry fruits (Gai et al., 2021). These three fruits share a similar char-
acteristic: the color difference between the fruit target and the surrounding foliage tends to 
become more apparent during the fruit’s development.
Complex backgrounds in outdoor orchard environments
Complex backgrounds in outdoor orchard environments are typically caused by multiple 
factors rather than a single one. The research reviewed above has shown that complex 
backgrounds can have significant negative effects, such as unacceptable levels of missing 
and wrong fruit recognition. This interference can lead to reduced efficiency and reliabil-
ity in robotic harvesting and yield estimation, which rely on accurate fruit detection. To 
address this issue, many researchers have proposed various optimization strategies to over-
come or mitigate the negative effects of complex backgrounds and improve fruit detec-
tion performance. In this paper, we will review recent optimization strategies used in fruit 
detection to address complex backgrounds.

Meeting the challenges of complex backgrounds
We reviewed and analyzed various studies in the field of fruit detection and yield esti-
mation in recent years. researchers have put a significant amount of effort into addressing 
the challenges posed by complex backgrounds. At present, the response solutions can be 
divided into two categories: optimization before and after image sampling.
Optimization strategies for fruit detection: pre‑image sampling
In the past few decades, deep learning was not commonly used in fruit detection, and 
most works were done using traditional machine learning methods. However, traditional 
machine learning methods have limitations as they cannot learn high-level features, and 
feature engineering requires high-quality datasets with minimal noise interference. To 
address this, the “optimization before sampling” approach aims to collect high-quality 
datasets with minimal noise interference. Although the powerful feature learning ability of 
deep learning can mitigate the impact of an unstructured environment to some extent, there 
is no substitute for improving the performance of the target detection model with a high-
quality dataset.
Artificial control of the unstructured environment
Unstructured environments are not completely uncontrollable. In recent years, cultivation 
technologies such as intelligent greenhouses have seen abundant growth, such as for grow-
ing mushrooms. An intelligent greenhouse is equipped with a large number of information 
sensors, including light sensors, to monitor the unstructured environment in the process of 
planting in real-time (Hemming et al., 2019). However, intelligent greenhouses are cur-
rently more suitable for high-value crops due to their high cost. For fruits with low added 
value and needing large-scale cultivation, such methods are too expensive and impractical 
to adopt.
Some researchers found that building a simple controllable artificial structured environ-
ment is feasible and effective in reducing the interference of outdoor environmental factors. 
Orchard standardized planting, appropriate pruning of crop branches and leaves, and the 
construction of a light shield before shooting are common ways to artificially control the 
unstructured environment. Although these methods are relatively simple, the effect is very 
noticeable. The strawberry dataset obtained by Yu et al. (2019) comes from a strawberry 
orchard with the earth-ridge strawberry planting pattern. This planting method allows 
strawberries to grow in a vertical downward direction and cling to the soil ridge, effectively 
reducing the occlusion of the leaves on the fruit (see Fig. 2a). Therefore, their subsequent 
detection and segmentation tasks only need to focus on solving the problem of mutual 
occlusion between fruits. In another instance, numerous researchers tend to collect data-
sets from studied orchards for apple detection tasks, and the apple trees in these orchards 
were trained to grow in tall spindle systems (Gené-Mola et al., 2020a, 2020b; Gongal et al., 
2016; Wang et al., 2013). The main characteristic of this type of tree form is that the crown 
is high and narrow. On the one hand, it is beneficial for the fruit trees to get sufficient 
light and get more well-distributed light (Robinson et al., 2008). On the other hand, a nar-
rower canopy means fewer branches and leaves occlusion, which can address the increase 
in detection error due to the fruit being hidden in the luxuriant canopy and the inability of 
the visual system to see it (see Fig. 2b).

To address the problem of lighting, we can use a light shield to create a controllable 
light source sampling platform. Additionally, the image acquisition equipment can capture 
high-quality images by incorporating additional light sources and adjusting the lighting 
conditions during the sampling process. This ensures that the sampling can be conducted 
seamlessly in a variety of natural lighting environments. The sampling platform with the 
controllable light source isolates the external natural light and creates a structured light-
ing environment, resulting in a dataset with standardized lighting conditions. Nguyen et al. 
(2016) proposed an algorithm based on color and shape features to detect and locate red 
and bicolored apples on trees in orchards. In both the data collection stage and the apple 
detection stage, the light shield is used to block direct sunlight. The algorithm achieves a 
100% detection accuracy for apples that are completely visible on the fruit tree and more 
than 80% for occluded apples, with respective false detection rates of 0% and 1%. Thus, the 
use of a light shield can effectively mitigate the impact of light. Gongal et al. (2016) con-
structed a controlled lighting environment using a tunnel structure that was built by cover-
ing the top and four sides of the platform with light-proof tarps and containing numerous 
LED lights (see Fig. 3). This environment can be used to detect apples on a tree and esti-
mate the crop load, with a detection accuracy of 79%. The tunnel structure eliminates the 
negative influence of variable sunlight factors on the accuracy of detection.
Most object detection, segmentation, or location algorithms are highly sensitive to 
imaging conditions. When performed at night, imaging conditions can be adjusted manu-
ally by using additional light sources. In addition, a controlled light environment at night 
can improve detection performance compared to the variable natural light environment. 
Many researchers choose to perform detection at night to improve performance and reduce 
errors (see Fig. 4). Common crops, including apple (Gongal et al., 2016; Linker, 2018; 
Linker & Kelman, 2015; Wang et al., 2013), mango (Koirala et al., 2019a, 2019b, 2021; 
Payne et al., 2014), litchi (Liang et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2018), grape (Aquino et al., 
2018; Font et al., 2014, 2015; Nuske et al., 2014), kiwifruit (Fu et al., 2021; Song et al., 
2019), citrus (Lu et al., 2018), and tomato (Xiang, 2018) have shown good performance in 
detection or robot picking tasks at night. Nighttime fruit detection is an important research 
area that aims to improve algorithm performance under low light conditions and expand 
the feasible working hours of orchard operations.

ence of uncontrollable environmental factors, but it will also lead to the problem of low

Choosing the appropriate visual sensor
The visual sensor serves as the “eye” of the intelligent fruit-picking robot. Gongal et al. 
(2015) and Tang et al. (2020) have investigated the applications of visual sensors and vis-
ual sensing technology in the fields of fruit detection, automatic fruit harvesting, and fruit 
yield estimation. They have also pointed out the advantages and disadvantages of various 
types of visual sensors and their related visual sensing technologies. Common visual sen-
sors in the field include single cameras, depth cameras, thermal cameras, multi-spectral or 
hyper-spectral cameras, and LiDAR sensors. These reviews have described in detail the 
imaging principles of various vision sensors and their relative merits. Therefore, this arti-
cle will not present unnecessary details on these devices.
Visual sensors should be selected based on the actual situation of the project. Using 
appropriate visual sensors to collect sample images can optimize the characteristics of vis-
ual sensors and visual sensor technology to obtain the optimal dataset most suitable for 
the algorithm model selected in this study. This is particularly helpful in reducing the dif-
ficulty of feature engineering in traditional machine learning or improving the performance 
of deep learning models.
Fu et al. (2020) provided a detailed review of the application of consumer RGB-D cam-
eras and corresponding image processing methods (structured light, time of flight, and 
active infrared stereo technique) in fruit detection and positioning. The authors considered 
that the RGB-D camera is a promising sensor in the field of intelligent agriculture. The

In early research, the RGB camera has been used as the main visual sensor. Therefore, 
various studies have been conducted considering color, shape, and texture as the main 
detection features. Many researchers have found that the surface temperature changes of 
some fruits under different light environments or weather conditions are significantly dif-
ferent from those of their leaves or branches, which provides a new approach for extracting 
features in fruit detection. The introduction of a thermal camera is effective in address-
ing the problem of insufficient detection performance caused by the similarity in the color 
of the fruit surface and branches and leaves, whether combined with traditional machine 
learning methods (Bulanon et al., 2009; Das et al., 2015; Feng et al., 2019; Stajnko et al., 
2004; Wachs et al., 2010) or deep learning methods (Gan et al., 2018, 2020).
A multi-spectral camera or hyper-spectral camera takes the spectral data obtained based 
on the reflectivity of a target object to different bands as the features to detect. Therefore, 
it is widely used in plant diseases and insect pests detection (Cabrera Ardila et al., 2020; 
Qin et al., 2012), crop defect detection (Kleynen et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2018), and crop 
maturity detection (Pourdarbani et al., 2020) in agriculture. Hung et al. (2013) used multi-
spectral features to automatically segment almonds in orchard scenes. The experimental 
results showed that the introduction of an additional infrared (IR) channel can improve 
the performance of the detection system and the segmentation accuracy. Senthilnath et al. 
(2016) used an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) equipped with a multi-spectral camera 
module to automatically detect tomato fruits in the field and compared the performance 
of different clustering algorithms in spectral clustering. Steinbrener et al. (2019) proposed 
a CNN-based spectral image classification method, which makes full use of the additional 
information brought by spectral data. The classification accuracy of fruit and vegetable 
classification in complex environments was improved by 4.08% compared with the classi-
cal RGB image classification. However, the study was performed in a dining table environ-
ment rather than an outdoor orchard environment. Therefore, the multi-spectral camera can 
potentially be applied in the field of fruit detection. Figure 5 shows the image information 
of some visual sensors.
The imaging characteristics of different visual sensors determine their respective appli-
cable scenes. The use of appropriate visual sensors can effectively mitigate negative effects 
in complex environments. Note that in an actual fruit detection scene, two or more visual 
sensors are usually combined to improve the robustness of the detection system to meet the 
needs of high-performance automatic fruit detection in different situations.
Appropriate camera pose
In the process of artificial fruit harvesting or artificial fruit yield estimation, professionals 
usually move constantly to find the best observation angle to avoid missing fruits covered 
by branches, leaves, or overlapping with other fruits in the front. This optimization strategy 
for fruit detection in complex environments involves finding an appropriate camera pose 
for the visual sensor based on the growth environment and plant morphology of the actual 
crop. To achieve this optimization, the pose and viewpoint of the camera during dataset 
collection and fruit detection must remain consistent.

Hemming et al. (2014) discussed the effects of different camera positions and view-
ing angles on the visibility and detectability of sweet pepper detection. After analyzing 
and comparing 14 camera positions, the best single-camera pose for sweet pepper detec-
tion was determined to be the front view, looking up at the zenith angle of 60°, as shown 
in Fig. 6a. This optimal camera pose for sweet pepper detection can also be useful for 
detecting other crops.
For instance, in the automatic picking of kiwifruit, which is usually grown on sturdy 
pergolas, Fu et al. (2019, 2021) used a different sampling method than previous studies. 
Instead of placing the camera close to the tree crown with its central axis parallel to the 
canopy, they positioned the camera under the tree crown with its central axis perpen-
dicular to the plane of the canopy, as shown in Fig. 6b. This approach for collecting 
kiwifruit datasets effectively avoids the phenomenon of branches and leaves covering 
the fruit body or fruit overlap. Although fruit adhesion remains an unavoidable issue 
for both traditional machine learning and deep learning methods, using this camera per-
spective significantly improves the detection performance of kiwifruit and the success 
rate of automatic picking.

However, optimizing the camera pose may not have a significant effect on the detection 
of macro phanerophyte fruit trees such as apple, citrus, and lychee. These types of trees 
have dense foliage, which means that the complexity of the fruit background remains the 
same regardless of the angle of the camera. This is a limitation of static monocular vision.
To overcome this limitation, one solution is to use a moving camera to sample fruit 
trees. The camera can take multiple images from different angles as it moves around the 
tree, using a predetermined strategy (such as moving equidistantly or randomly). These 
images provide more comprehensive information, allowing the detection of fruit that was 
previously hidden by branches or other fruits.
Using the structure from motion (SfM) method (Schonberger & Frahm, 2016), a set 
of consecutive images can be used to estimate the 3D structure of the entire fruit tree or 
a selected area. This improves the accuracy of fruit detection and provides the fruit pick-
ing robot with the 3D spatial positioning of the target fruit. Figure 7 shows an example of 
using the SfM method to obtain the 3D point cloud of a certain area from two adjacent 
image frames, which are then used to accurately segment the fruit using the Mask R-CNN 
model. In Fig. 7b, the result on the 3D point cloud shows that the fourth apple was hidden 
behind two apples in the first image, but was detected in the second image, demonstrating 
the advantage of using multiple images. Figure 7c shows the instance segmentation results 
projected onto the 3D point cloud, achieving accurate segmentation of occluded images.
It can judge whether the fruit is the same by analyzing whether there is an overlapping 
area between the previous image frame and the fruit area in the next image frame in a 
group of continuous image sequences and by recording the moving track of the fruit in the 
group of image sequences. It can avoid the problem of the same fruit being counted repeat-
edly due to multi-view detection and improve the reliability of fruit counting (Liu et al., 
2018, 2019; Stein et al., 2016). Figure 8 shows typical results on fruit image tracking.
Researchers have found that providing additional information through more views or a 
combination of multiple visual sensors achieves better detection performance. Therefore, 
large-scale sensor platforms with greater loads and flexible unmanned ground vehicles 
(UGV) have become the mainstream equipment for photographing datasets and field opera-
tions. However, designing and building these platforms is time-consuming, and the wheels 
or tracks will compact the soil during the operation of these platforms, which may lead to 
soil degradation (Apolo-Apolo et al., 2020a, 2020b).

With the increasing popularity of civil UAVs, many researchers have begun to use aerial 
imaging platforms (i.e., using UAVs to carry vision sensors). Compared with ground imag-
ing platforms, the UAV imaging platform can shoot with its unique looking-down angle 
(see Fig. 9) and can freely adjust the imaging angle and imaging distance in the air based 
on the actual operation conditions (i.e., sunlight irradiation direction or spatial distribu-

comprehensive image information. Chen et al. (2019) developed an automatic detection 
system for strawberry yield prediction based on deep learning. The system is composed 
of a small-sized UAV equipped with a high-resolution RGB camera to take aerial pho-
tos of strawberry soil ridges at a height of 2 and 3 m above the ground. In this study, the 
datasets used to train the deep learning model and the images used to verify the results are 
all derived from the orthophoto images established from the aerial images. The authors 
believe that the strong wind generated by the propeller of the UAV can blow away the 
dense leaves, exposing some flowers and fruits that were originally covered by the leaves to 
be fully captured by the camera, which is conducive to improving the detection accuracy. 
The results show that the average accuracy of the system in strawberry counting is 84.1%, 
which is better than manual counting. The deep learning algorithm used in estimating the 
number of apples per tree based on the UAV imaging platform (Apolo-Apolo et al., 2020b) 
has high accuracy for apple detection in the orthophoto of apple orchards. However, using 
standard linear regression to predict the number of fruits harvested is not applicable, and 
the accuracy is not as good as that of the manual yield estimation. Under natural light con-
ditions, Apolo-Apolo et al. (2020a) used a UAV to fly at low altitude among citrus trees to 
shoot the left and right sides of each fruit tree. Both fruit detection and yield prediction in 
this study used deep learning techniques, and the models used were faster R-CNN and long 
short-term memory network (Shi et al., 2015). Regarding actual yield counting and future 
yield prediction, the results show that the method proposed by the authors is superior to 
that of the traditional manual method.

small and lightweight sensors can be carried. This means that the sensors chosen must have 
high performance to capture clear aerial images, which can be expensive. However, we

Optimization strategies after image collection
Most researchers focus on optimizing after image collection because it has stronger gener-
alization ability, but it also has a higher technical difficulty. Optimization is mainly accom-
plished through two methods: (1) postprocessing of datasets and (2) designing and improv-
ing algorithms or network models.
Dataset preprocessing
The optimization measures introduced in Sect. 3.1 can improve the quality of collected 
images and reduce the interference of complex environmental noise to a certain extent. 
However, before entering the pipeline of a fruit detection system, high-quality datasets 
need to go through some preprocessing procedures, mainly at the pixel level.
Data augmentation techniques are one of the forms of dataset preprocessing. Image 
spatial transformation, including translation, rotation, mirroring, scaling, and cropping, 
is a common data augmentation technique, as shown in Fig. 10. Image transformation

augmentation technology compared to neural network training that uses only raw data 
(Bargoti & Underwood, 2017).
Another data augmentation method is to change the color space of the original 
image. Several researchers have found that changing the color space of the original 
image achieves better detection performance than using the commonly used RGB space 
in some scenes for fruit detection. Therefore, this technology is considered a common 
optimization method that uses the traditional machine learning strategy, which depends 
on color features to detect fruits in the natural environment (Dorj et al., 2017; Xiong 
et al., 2018; Zemmour et al., 2019). The combination of this data augmentation method 
and deep learning also improves the detection performance and robustness of the sys-
tem. Kirk et al. (2020) used nonlinear transformation to convert the RGB space of an 
original strawberry image into the CIELab space. The RGB and CIELab images were 
stacked into a 6-dimensional tensor according to depth and input into a deep neural net-

Training a high-precision object detection network model depends especially on a large 
number and various types of reliable datasets. For fruit detection in the natural environ-
ment, researchers must ensure that the collected datasets contain fruit photos taken under 
different lighting conditions, weather conditions, and degrees of occlusion to meet the 
requirements of a large number and diverse types of datasets. Sampling workers usually 
need to plan the sampling trip in advance because fruit crops have specific growth environ-
ments and growth cycles. If a specific fruit growth period (immature or mature) is missed, 
researchers will not be able to refresh the dataset at any time according to current test 
needs. In addition, sampling workers may face some uncontrollable risks in the natural 
environment, implying that the collection of the original dataset is a time-consuming and 
laborious process. Moreover, the image labeling of the collected dataset is also an expen-
sive process, and the human errors (missing or wrong labels) in the manual labeling pro-
cess cannot be avoided.
In recent years, data synthesis technology has been proposed to solve the problems men-
tioned above. Specifically, synthetic simulation data, similar to the characteristics of the 
original real data, are generated by a computer. The synthetic data are used only as train-
ing datasets for the deep learning model, while the validation and test sets are composed 
of real data collected on site. Rahnemoonfar & Sheppard et al. (2017) created a synthetic 
image by completely covering a blank image with green and brown circles to simulate the 
background and tomato plants in an outdoor planting environment. The image was then 
processed with Gaussian blur and randomly drawn with circles of various diameters and 
colors similar to those of real tomatoes. The circle distribution on the image reflects the 
positional relationship of mutual occlusion, overlap, and adhesion of uncropped tomatoes 
in a real image. The experimental results showed that the network can converge quickly, 
and the average detection accuracy reached 91% on the dataset containing synthetic images 
for training. Bresilla et al. (2019) found that the addition of synthetic images in the train-
ing set can slightly improve the IoU, although the impact on the F1 score is limited. This 
demonstrates that the use of synthetic images can be an easy approach to rapidly expand a 
dataset and improve the positioning accuracy of the detected target. Figure 11 shows the 
synthetic image examples mentioned above.
In addition to manually drawing synthetic images, researchers have proposed using deep 
learning to generate new images. In previous studies, generative adversarial nets (GANs) 
(Goodfellow et al., 2014) were used to synthesize pedestrian datasets. After mixing syn-
thetic data with real pedestrian images, the resulting dataset is used as the training set for 
the network (Zheng et  al., 2017). In recent years, cycle-consistent adversarial networks 
(CycleGANs) (Zhu et  al., 2017), derived from GANs, have performed well in various 
fields, including agriculture (Tian et al., 2019a, 2019b; Zhang et al., 2021). CycleGAN is a 
ring network composed of a pair of mirror-symmetrical GANs, which have strong image-
to-image translation abilities. Specifically, in the field of fruit detection, using CycleGAN 
to realize the mutual conversion of daytime and nighttime images, the conversion of fruit 
images with different maturity levels, and the conversion of fruits in the image to other 
fruits, is a very promising method of image augmentation.
Furthermore, GANs are widely used in the field of image inpainting (Elharrouss et al., 
2020). Removing undesirable objects from images without leaving any traces is an impor-
tant research direction (Jiao et al., 2019; Ju et al., 2022). We believe that image inpainting

Using the improved neural network model
The rapid development of computer vision technology has promoted industrial production 
to become intelligent and unmanned. Currently, some advanced target detection models 
based on deep learning have important applications in various practical industrial produc-
tion fields, including human face recognition (Taigman et al., 2014), vehicle and pedes-
trian detection in autonomous driving (Yurtsever et al., 2020), and medical image analysis 
(Litjens et al., 2017). However, due to a shortage of sufficient open-source datasets in the 
field of agriculture, most researchers use custom datasets generated by themselves. The 
detection models developed by researchers are optimized based on the characteristics of 
the dataset, sensor selection, and detection task requirements to enable the existing mod-
els to achieve high-performance detection results in agricultural applications. Based on the 
analysis of the studies we retrieved, the model improvement methods can be divided into 
two categories: (1) improving the backbone network structure and (2) improving the non-
backbone network structure.
In deep learning tasks, especially in the field of computer vision, the backbone network, 
also known as the feature extraction network, is used to extract the features of an input 
image. The powerful feature extraction capability of the backbone network can extract 
high-level abstract features of an input image to generate feature maps. The subsequent 
classification prediction networks or segmentation prediction networks rely on these fea-
ture maps. Mainstream backbone networks include the VGG series (Simonyan & Zis-
serman, 2014), ResNet series (He et al., 2016), MobileNet series (Howard et al., 2017), 
DenseNet (Huang et al., 2017), and DarkNet (Redmon & Farhadi, 2018).

In common and less complex computer vision tasks, these mainstream backbone net-
works meet the requirements of high precision and strong robustness. In outdoor agricul-
tural scenes with higher background complexity, direct application of these networks may 
be able to successfully detect some targets due to the powerful feature extraction capabili-
ties of these mainstream networks. However, it is necessary to consider the optimization of 
the backbone network to obtain better detection performance and adaptability to hardware 
devices. Common backbone network improvement strategies include the following: (1) 
adjusting the depth and width of the backbone network; (2) integrating with other back-
bone networks; (3) embedding other structural units.
The depth and width of the network are important variables that directly affect the 
performance of the overall network model. When designing the network architecture, we 
need to find out the depth-breadth tradeoff based on the actual detection scene. Wang et al. 
(2021a, 2021b, 2021c) proposed the YOLOv3-Litchi detection model to realize real-time 
detection of small and dense litchi fruits in large visual scenes. The model adopted the 
strategy of reducing the depth of the backbone network and increased the prediction scale 
to improve the detection ability of small litchi fruits. Fu et al. (2021) actively increased 
the depth of the model by adding two convolutional kernels of 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 to the fifth 
and sixth convolution layers of the YOLOV3-tiny model, respectively, so that the proposed 
model (Dy3TNet model) had the characteristics of small size, high efficiency, and meeting 
the real-time detection of kiwifruit in orchards.
The DenseNet architecture proposes a radical dense connection mechanism, where each 
layer accepts all previous layers as additional inputs. The structural design of DenseNet 
enhances feature propagation, effectively solving the problem of gradient disappearance, 
and improving the performance of neural networks in image classification. Tian et  al. 
(2019a, 2019b) proposed an improved model, YOLOV3-dense, to detect apples at different 
growth stages in the complex orchard environment. In this model, DenseNet replaces the 
original low-resolution transmission layer in YOLO-V3, thereby enhancing feature propa-
gation, promoting feature reuse, and improving the performance of the detection model. 
The detection results showed that the improved YOLO-V3 model achieved higher F1 score 
and IoU compared to the original model, without increasing the detection time. For more 
advanced target detection networks, DenseNet is used to improve the performance of the 
detection model. In YOLO-V4, Gai et al. (2021) used DenseNet to replace the CSPDar-
ketNet53 architecture and proposed the YOLO-V4-DenseNet network architecture, which 
achieved better detection performance when detecting cherry fruits in complex background 
scenes.
Embedding structural units into the backbone network of the detection model improves 
the training effect and performance of the detection model, although a single structural 
unit is not competent for the whole detection task independently. Li et al. (2021a, 2021b) 
introduced the attention mechanism (SE) (Hu et al., 2018) and the Mish activation function 
(Misra, 2019) to redesign the backbone network of the YOLOV4-tiny model, solving the 
problem of low recognition accuracy of grape picking robots for overlapping grape fruit 
(Wang et al., 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). This improved YOLO-Grape model achieved excel-
lent detection results on the occluded grape test set, with 7.32% and 6.96% improvement in 
F1 score and mAP, respectively, compared to the YOLOV4-tiny model. Yan et al. (2021) 
embedded the SE block into the backbone network of YOLOV5s, optimizing the abil-
ity of the network to learn apple target features under different conditions. The improved 
YOLOV5s model has better detection performance and accurately distinguishes occluded 
apples. Wang et al. (2020) introduced dilated convolutions (Yu & Koltun, 2015) into the 
backbone network of Mask R-CNN (ResNet module) to improve the sensing field of the

A complete object detection network not only needs superior feature extraction abil-
ity but also reasonable processing and utilization of features. Various researchers focused 
on the subsequent network architecture after the backbone network to make the detection 
model more robust in the complex orchard environment.
Chu et al. (2021) considered that the feature learning backbone of Mask R-CNN may 
learn wrong inference features like leaves with apple-like shapes, which leads to insuffi-
cient detection accuracy of the original model in the real orchard environment. To block 
non-apple image regions from entering the final classification layer, the authors connected 
a feature suppression network between the backbone network and the decision layer. This 
improved suppression Mask R-CNN model can reduce the frequency of wrong detec-
tions in real orchard backgrounds. Kuznetsova et al. (2020) discarded recognized objects 
whose ratio of the length and width of the bounding box was more than three to prevent 
the detection system (YOLO-V3) from falsely detecting the leaves and small gaps between 
the leaves of apples. The results showed that it effectively reduced the false detection rate. 
Behera et al. (2021) believed that the correct positioning of target detection depends on 
the regression of bounding boxes. The author focused on improving the design of IoU and 
proposed MIoU (it is a Modified IoU, not the common Mean IoU). The improved MIoU 
focused more on overlapping and occluded areas, overcame the limitation of the original 
Faster R-CNN, i.e., it cannot detect overlapping and severely occluded fruits, and enhanced 
detection accuracy.

In summary, fruit detection in complex orchard environments is an important and chal-
lenging task. Various deep learning models have been proposed for fruit detection, and 
the research direction has gradually shifted from improving feature extraction ability to 
optimizing network architecture and post-processing. These approaches have shown great 
potential in improving the detection accuracy and robustness of fruit detection systems.
To conclude, the comprehensive extraction of useful information from a dataset and 
the identification of ways to leverage this information determine the performance of the 
object detection task. Although deep learning-based object detection models typically have 
a more complex pipeline, they also provide the model designer with the possibility and 
freedom to optimize all aspects of the pipeline in order to design a model that can meet 
complex tasks.
In recent years, with the increase in research on hybrid models, there have been various 
studies on the application of natural language processing architectures in the field of com-
puter vision. Among these, the transformer architecture (Carion et al., 2020; Vaswani et al., 
2017) is of particular interest. Additionally, the field of fruit detection has been impacted 
by this emerging research, with some visual versions of fruit detection models based on 
transformers proposed (Han et  al., 2021; Wang et  al., 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). However, 
since these studies are not well documented, there are no detailed reviews of these articles. 
I believe that this is a very promising research direction.
Conclusion
This paper focuses on recent research progress in overcoming the environmental impact of 
complex orchards. We investigated fruit detection methods, including traditional machine 
learning methods and deep learning methods, but we focused more on deep learning meth-
ods in this study. Based on these surveys, readers can understand the complex environ-
ments that exist in the field of automatic fruit picking and the coping methods adopted in 
these complex environments. The optimization techniques mentioned in this review can 
help researchers analyze which optimization strategies should be used to deal with com-
plex environments in fruit detection tasks. It can be concluded that the test results have

For the future development of this field, we give the following prospects:
•	 It is necessary to rely on the improvement of horticultural technology to standardize the 
orchard planting environment and cultivation techniques to transform orchard planning 
and design from manual picking to robot automatic picking.
•	 With the continuous development of UAV technology, the endurance and load capacity 
of UAVs have gradually improved. In the future, UAVs will be equipped with high-
performance visual sensors to dynamically detect and track fruit targets from different 
visual angles to address the accuracy issues in identifying occluded targets from a fixed 
perspective. More advanced target tracking technology is needed to avoid the problem 
of repeated positioning in the process of motion tracking.
•	 Both traditional machine learning methods and popular deep learning methods are 
data-driven techniques. The quality of the dataset determines the maximum limit of 
fruit detection performance. Due to the characteristics of this field, it is difficult to 
obtain sufficient and diverse datasets compared to popular target detection fields such 
as vehicle, pedestrian recognition, and face recognition. Therefore, more advanced 
image augmentation and data expansion technologies are needed.
•	 Deep learning is still in the stage of rapid development. New algorithms using new 
network architectures are emerging consecutively, while classical object detection algo-
rithms are constantly updated and iterated. As future work, improving and redesigning 
the detection algorithm according to the distinguishing features of a target fruit will be 
of great research interest.
