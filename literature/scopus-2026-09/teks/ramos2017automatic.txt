In this article, a non-destructive method is proposed to count the number of fruits on a coffee branch by
using information from digital images of a single side of the branch and its growing fruits. In order to do
this, 1018 coffee branches at different ripening stages. They had different numbers of fruits, harvest dates,
were of different varieties, and were at different stages of coffee tree’s life. A Machine Vision System
(MVS) was constructed, which was capable of counting and identifying harvestable and not harvestable
fruits in a set of images corresponding to a speciﬁc coffee branch was constructed. This MVS consists of an
image acquisition system, based on mobile devices (it does not require to control of the environmental
conditions), and an image processing algorithm to classify and detect each one of the fruits in the
acquired images. After obtaining information regarding the number of fruits identiﬁed by the MVS, linear
estimation models were constructed between the detected fruits automatically and the ones observed on
the coffee branch. These models were calculated for fruits in three categories: harvestable, not har-
vestable, and fruits whose maturation stage were disregarded. These models link the fruits that are
counted automatically to the ones actually observed with an R2 higher than 0.93 one-to-one. Not only
is the MVS used to estimate the number of fruits on the branch but also to estimate their maturation per-
centage and weight. The MVS was validated in four Variedad Castillo coffee plots, in different stages of
development and with different densities. We found that MVS neither overestimates nor underestimates
the number of fruits and that it shows a correlation higher than 0.90 at early stages of crop development,
when tree fruits are still not harvestable. The information obtained in this research will spawn a new gen-
eration of tools for coffee growers to use. It is an efﬁcient, non-destructive, and low-cost method which
offers useful information for them to plan agricultural work and obtain economic beneﬁts from the cor-
rect administration of resources.

1. Introduction
The coffee trade is the second most proﬁtable industry world-
wide, followed by oil. In spite of its importance, and excepting
for some farms in Brazil, coffee is a type of crop for which growers
still do not employ all the available technology in agricultural
works, especially at harvest. As far as we know, said growers do
not have the type of technology which would allow them to obtain
information about coffee production in advance, and plan a better
use of their resources, namely labor. Normally, coffee growers can
make approximated calculations about their crops. Based on their
experience, they evaluate the ﬂowering intensity, fruition, the phy-
tosanitary condition of the plant, and also track previous produc-
tion cycles to predict whether their current harvest will be good,
average, or bad. Nevertheless, a coffee plantation may be nega-
tively inﬂuenced, by external or handling factors, which can lead
to ﬂower and/or fruit decay, and, therefore, alter the size of the
harvest.
Counting of the number of fruits on the trees and branches
would be the most reliable method to obtain production data.
However, the count must be performed when all the fruits of the
productive cycle are on the tree, that is to say, at the beginning
of the harvest cycle. This measurement would show the quantity
of fruits produced per tree, and the number of fruits per plot could
then be interpolated. With this information, a coffee grower could
be ready to sort out their needs for workers during the harvest,
prepare the facilities and conditions for the post-harvest, carry
out machine maintenance, ask for loans (when necessary), and
enter into marketing agreements more safely. However, most cof-
fee growers do not possess efﬁcient tools to obtain information
about the production of their farms in advance. Some of them carry

1.1. Related work
Knowledge of the productivity of a crop is a strategic matter for
the government, agro-industrial enterprises, farmers, and sellers.
This information is applied to the planning of trading strategies,
which are policies that decrease the economic risk of production
due to a lack/excess of the product. It is also applied to the plan-
ning of tasks on the farm such as harvest and post-harvest. The
productivity of a crop can be deﬁned as: the produced mass per
unit area within a given time, which can be measured or estimated
in the ﬁeld. Authors such as, Lobell et al. (2009), Montoya et al.
(2009), Agrawal (2005), Huddleston (1978), Cotter et al. (2010),
Basso et al. (2013), Vogel and Eaton (1985), Cilas et al. (2009),
Upreti et al. (1991), and Castro-Tanzi et al. (2014), report models
or statistical expressions for different crops, which relate historical
productions, econometric models, climate dynamics models, lack
or excess of a chemical element, soil characteristics, incidence of
plagues or diseases, and phenology of the plant.
In the speciﬁc case of a coffee crop, Cilas et al. (2009) and Upreti
et al. (1991) show coffee production estimations which are based
on the different production components of the tree. (Eq. (1)): num-
ber of stems per tree st, number of branches per stem bs, number of
glomeruli per branch gb, and quantity of fruits per glomeruli f g.
Additionally, they report stratiﬁed methods for variances in area,
height, soil, and area/ground and area/altitude relationships. This
estimation method is destructive; this means that the coffee fruits
must be picked from the trees to obtain the expression variables.

Models reported in direct measuring in ﬁeld are reported by
Vogel and Eaton (1985), Cilas et al. (2009), and Upreti et al.
(1991). In said models, they carry out a count of the vegetative-
productive structures on the plantation, such as fruits, glomeruli,
branches, and stems. These type of models offer a value that is very
close to the real production of the crop. The advantages are: (i) use
of the quantitative method; (ii) development on physiological
bases; (iii) elimination of harvest distribution problems when sam-
pling is strictly deﬁned; (iv) incorporation of climatic variability
and handling of the harvest; and (v) incorporation of expressions
of the plants in their ground-atmosphere relationship. The main
disadvantages are: (i) requirement of destructive sampling; (ii)
requirement of certiﬁed labor; and (iii) deﬁnition sampling
moments, as it is likely that the plant physically changes between
the moment the information is acquired and harvest.
Other authors, such as Castro-Tanzi et al. (2014), also use the
fruit count method with a random and stratiﬁed sampling,
employing empirical models, formulated by using dependent and
independent variables on a logarithmic scale. In the case of the
Caturra and Catuaí varieties of coffee, they used the number of pro-
ductive lateral branches per tree and the number of fruits per
branch. They found that, by counting the fruits that are present
in 8 or 9 branches per tree, it was possible to determine the total
number of fruits per tree, having using two stems for each of the
aforementioned varieties, with regression coefﬁcients between
0.73 and 0.92. The authors themselves point out the importance
of making estimations about the harvest season and highlight the
difﬁculty of such estimation, since the coffee plant is not phenolog-
ically synchronized and various ﬂowering stages may take place
throughout the year. Moreover, nutrition events and presence of
rain may alter production method.
The aforementioned methods use destructive and/or manual
counts of the fruits on the branches and tend to require a great deal
of labor. The increasing availability of technology has lead the
agro-industrial sector to develop innovative techniques to monitor
crops, carrying out non-destructive and tasks more efﬁciently. In
the state of the art, authors have used digital processing of images
to automatically estimate the production of crops such as apples,
grapes, mangoes, and oranges. Satellite, aerial, and land images
have been used to measure variables and estimate production.
Stajnko et al. (2009), Aggelopoulou et al. (2011), Wang et al.
(2013), and Nuske et al. (2011) report production estimation mod-
els for apples and grapes based on the measurement of the features
of fruits and ﬂowers in speciﬁc ﬁeld conditions. High resolution
cameras are set up in vehicles that move along the plantation fur-
rows. These cameras take pictures and the information is pro-
cessed in order to count fruits or ﬂowers and to determine
production using statistical expressions. In each one of these cases,
the performance was different: Stajnko et al. (2009) obtained a
detection efﬁcacy of 89% for apples. In the case of Nuske et al.
(2011), an overestimation of 10% was generated with grapevines
as well as a 60% correlation between what is real and what is
estimated.
1.2. The research problem and contributions of this study
Currently, coffee growers have no means for determining coffee
production in a non-destructive way and, therefore, obtain no
information in advance which would help them to program of agri-
cultural tasks or sell the product in advance. This research pro-
poses a system for obtaining information about the production of
coffee tree branches in a non-destructive way, based on partial
information collected with acquired images on a single visible side
of the branch. This system, which is based on a machine vision,
allows for detection and recording of the number of fruits on the
coffee branches and their weight, with no need for destructive
samplings and under uncontrolled lighting, branch contrast, and
occlusion of fruit conditions. The images in this research were
acquired with the main camera of a mobile device and, were simul-
taneously processed using an algorithm to detect and count coffee
fruits based on their maturation stages. This algorithm determined
the number of visible fruits on a single side of the branch and, with
this information, the total number of fruits on the branch were
estimated. The system was evaluated and validated using coffee
branches in different crop conditions and production stages.
The contributions of this research are the following: (i) to deter-
mine the number of fruits on a coffee branch, the maturation per-
centage of said branch, and the weight of detected fruits, with no
need for destructive samplings, based on partial information
obtained with mobile devices and image processing algorithms;
(ii) a masking technique that detects occluded and unoccluded
fruits in ﬁeld images and which classiﬁes them either as har-
vestable or not harvestable; (iii) estimation models that neither
overestimate nor underestimate the number of fruits per branch
and that achieve one-to-one relations, with determination coefﬁ-
cients higher than 0.93; and (iv) a measuring tool in the ﬁeld to
determine the number of fruits in different moments of the harvest
cycle.
The information from our research and of the system we pro-
pose is shown in Fig. 1. Both a manual and an automatic count
were carried out: the manual count by certiﬁed personnel, and
the automatic count as shown in the stages of Fig. 1, starting with
the acquisition and adaptation of the images, and following differ-
ent steps until obtaining the number of fruits per branch. The val-
ues of the manual and the automatic count were compared to one
another to obtain linear estimation models, which were later eval-
uated. Finally, a validation of the proposed system on branches in
four experimental parcels was performed.

2. Machine Vision System (MVS) for automatic count of fruits
We developed the machine vision system (MVS) in ﬁve stages
(Fig. 1): (i) Image acquisition and adjustment, where we deﬁned
the hardware used, the image acquisition conditions and carry
out a scaling of the images to homogenize them and achieve an
optimal processing, regardless of the size of the images; (ii) seg-
mentation of homogeneous regions. This step consisted of spotting
the zones that might have texture information; (iii) the detector of
outlines likely to be arcs of fruits consisted of analyzing each one of
the outlines generated in the previous step and determining
whether they belonged to a coffee fruit arc or not; (iv) adjustment
and selector of ellipses. This part consisted of adjusting the points
found in the previous step into an ellipse and evaluating whether it
was possible that these ellipses belonged to coffee fruits; and (v)
detection, classiﬁcation, and count of fruits. This ﬁnal step con-
sisted of placing the adjusted ellipse in the classiﬁed image for
each vegetative structure, detecting a single fruit per ellipse, and
counting the detected fruits according to their maturation stage.
In this section, we will explain the different steps required to
perform the automatic count. In the following section (Section 3),
we will present the evaluation used for the image processing
technique.
2.1. Acquisition and adjustment of images
The images were obtained with the main camera of a mobile
device (Samsung Galaxy S5 SM-G900M), located 8–15 cm from
the branch using a handle, with a built-in start/stop recording but-
ton (Fig. 2). The lighting conditions, contrast of the branches, and
occlusion of the fruits were not controlled and images were taken
without ﬂash. The obtained images show partial branch informa-
tion, since each image corresponds to sets of 3–4 glomeruli and,
in the images, only information from the upper part of the branch
can be registered. Overall, we obtained 3–5 images per branch so
as to register all of the glomeruli. We created a database of 1018
coffee branches, with 4300 total acquired images. The analyzed
coffee branches belong to the Coffea Arabica genus, Castillo and
Caturra varieties, and the images were acquired in December of
2013, from January to April of 2014, from July to November of
2015, and, ﬁnally, in July of 2016.

2.4. Adjustment and selection of ellipses corresponding to coffee fruits
A major part of the analyzed information showed sequence vec-
tors of circular arc candidates (CAC) with only 5 points. They gen-
erally represented occluded fruits. This was due to the glomerular-
like nature of coffee fruits on the branches. With the partial infor-
mation about each arc, we conﬁgured an ellipse using the
Fitzgibbon et al. (1999) algorithm. This algorithm requires at least
6 points to make the adjustment, but, where there are at least 7,
the adjusted ellipses resemble the ellipse of a coffee fruit. Thus,
it was necessary to estimate additional points for those CAC that
only had 5 or 6 points. If the CAC had 5 points, we estimated two
points: one at the beginning of the sequence and another at the
end; if the CAC had 6 points, we estimated one additional point
at the beginning of the sequence. This ensures that the resulting
ellipse is a bit shorter and avoids later adjustments to larger
ellipses that do not correspond to fruits.

2.5. Detection, classiﬁcation, and count of ellipses/fruits
If the ellipse meets the criteria shown in Fig. 8, it is used as a
mask for the homogeneous region generated in Section 2.2
(Fig. 9a). For the masked information, a vector with 42 features
of color is calculated; these colors correspond to the mean and
the standard deviation of each component of color in the
RGB; HSV; YCrCb; XYZ; CIELab; LUX, and LUV spaces. By means of
the Fisher linear discriminant analysis, the dimension of this speci-
ﬁc vector was reduced and, afterwards, three classiﬁers were
implemented: (i) a Bayes classiﬁer; (ii) a k-nearest neighbors clas-
siﬁer (KNN); and (iii) a support vector machine (SVM) classiﬁer.
The classiﬁed maturation stages or classes correspond to: (i) unripe
fruits; (ii) semi-ripe fruits-1; (iii) semi-ripe fruits-2; (iv) ripe and
overripe fruits. The aforementioned classes are shown in Table 1.
In order to select one of the three classiﬁers, we used 300 coffee
fruits (75 fruits per class) which were subsequently evaluated. For
this evaluation we used the confusion matrix criteria, the true pos-
itives rate (TPR, Eq. (10)), and the overall success rate (OSR, Eq. (9)).
The true positives rate was the relation between the true positives
and the sum of false negatives and true positives. The OSR value
corresponded to the sum of correctly classiﬁed instances in rela-
tion to the total number of classiﬁed instances.

3.2. Fruit count system in images
We compared the number of fruits counted manually in the
images with the number of fruits detected by the MVS in each mat-
uration stage and for all of the stages. In order to measure the func-
tionality of the count algorithm, we compared the measured values
with the ones observed in the images through linear regression. It
is to be expected that, when there are no fruit present on the
branch, the system detects no fruit, and that a strong correlation
exits between the values that are measured and the ones that are
observed. Given this, the algorithm acceptance criterion now is
that the intercepts of linear regressions can be statistically equal
to zero and that the determination coefﬁcients be greater than
0.70.
Table 4 shows the regression and determination coefﬁcients
and intercept of the stages’s regions: (1) ripe and overripe, (2)
semi-ripe, (3) unripe, and (4) all of the stages. In the section Inter-
cept Different than Zero (IDZ) in Table 4, we see that the semi-ripe
fruit count does not meet the stated criteria, since the R2 is 0.356
and the intercept of this stage cannot be zero according the t-test
at 1%. According to R2 criteria, the algorithm works correctly for
the other stages. This behavior is due to the dynamics of the
semi-ripe maturation stage, given that the average of visible fruits
in the images is not higher than 5 fruits per branch, and also,
due to occlusion, the visibility percentage is the lowest, with a
value of 76.81%. Due to this result, semi-ripe fruits are grouped
together with ripe and overripe fruits, thus forming a group called
harvestable fruits. Also, in section IDZ in Table 4, we observe that
this relation does meet the stated criteria for determination
coefﬁcients.
In the relations identiﬁed that meet the criteria, b0 was equal to
zero, and b1 and the determination coefﬁcients were estimated
once more. In the section Intercept Equal to Zero (IEZ) from Table 4
the value of these coefﬁcients is shown. In general, the automati-
cally generated count corroborates the manual count of visible
fruits in the images and conﬁrms that, when there is no fruits,
the system detects doesn’t detect any.
An average of the difference between the observed variable and
the measurement was within 5.53 and 19.87 fruits, with a percent-
age error between 18% and 40%. When analyzing the images that
generated this error, four problems were identiﬁed in the pro-
cessed images: (i) an unsuitable contrast between the branch
and background, be it for blurriness (Fig. 10a), or an excess or lack
of light on the branch (Fig. 10b); (ii) small fruits on branches or a
large distance among glomeruli (Fig. 10c); (iii) a branch not in
the center of the image (Fig. 10d); few fruits in the images, curva-
tures of branches, and leaves can be confused with outlines likely
to be arcs of fruits. In general, the images that do not have these
types of problems obtain an error below 6%.
The results shown in Fig. 10 indicate the system can be affected
by some environmental conditions. It should be had special care
during image acquisition, such as avoiding direct sun rays on the
branches and the shades generated by the leaves. In foggy days
or with low light conditions, the system works properly, however,
it should be veriﬁed the sharpness of the acquired images. In rainy
days the mobile device can have some inconveniences due to the
human interface is achieved throughout the touchscreen: in the
case a water drop falls on the screen, when set in the open camera
mode, the zoom or the whites balance could be unintentionally
modiﬁed or a photo can be accidentally shot. The method can be
applied to images acquired during daytime, taking into considera-
tion the mentioned facts.

detection of occluded fruits. In Fig. 11, are examples of the detec-
tion of these fruits. In Fig. 11a, we show the original analyzed sec-
tion and the adjusted ellipse of said section. In Fig. 11b, is the result
of putting the adjusted ellipse on top of the segmented zone as a
homogeneous region and, in Fig. 11c, the masked section which
is a result of putting together the ellipse with the homogeneous
zone on the classiﬁed information is illustrated. The way that the
ellipse estimates the hidden section of the fruit can be observed.
However, it only uses visible information to determine which class
it belongs to.
The masking technique developed uses both the resulting
homogeneous region from Section 2.2 and the adjusted ellipses
from Section 2.4. This is an advantage with respect to other types
of detection in that they only use seed growth for watershed
segmentation. In this case, simple marks from a few pixels are
not enough to generate a segmentation of individual fruits. In this
case, a mark with a larger area is required, as the images used
present a great variability in color intensity and grayscale for a
single fruit.

4. Results and validation
In this section, we present the evaluation and validation of the
linear relationship between the information obtained by the algo-

we present expressions used for the estimation of fruits per branch
according to the number of fruits detected by the MVS. Afterwards,
we show an evaluation of each of the constructed expressions.
Then, we present the maturation percentage measurement and
weight of the fruits. Finally, we present a validation of the count
of fruits on branches in 4 sown coffee plots.
4.1. Obtention of linear models for the estimation of the number of
fruits on branches

and semi-ripe fruits; (b) not harvestable: unripe fruits; and (c) all

4.3. Validation of the count estimation of unripe fruits
Once the linear estimation models of the number of fruits on a
branch were evaluated, a validation of the method was performed
in four experimental parcels with sown with Variedad Castillo,
with plants aged 2, 3, 4, and 5 years. The parcels had distributions
between 4444, 6667, and 10,000 trees per hectare. On average,
each parcel had 650 trees, from which 60 were randomly selected
and, simultaneously, four branches per tree were selected from a
third of the whole productive lot.
A manual count of the fruits was performed on the selected
branches and a mobile device was used to acquire the images, as
was shown in Section 2.1 (Fig. 3b), thus avoiding the problems
mentioned in Section 3.2, in the acquired images. Overall, we ana-
lyzed 940 coffee branches during July of 2016. At that time, all of
the coffee fruits on the branches were unripe and, therefore, clas-
siﬁed as not harvestable. The acquisition conditions varied on each
parcel due to tree morphology, terrain topography, and plantation
density. In general, dense parcels have self-shading problems,
similar to immature parcels, where the production happens in
the lowest part of the tree. For older parcels, production happens
above 1.5 m and chairs were needed to acquire the images.

5. Conclusions
The presented results support the possibility of estimating the
total number of fruits on a branch in a non-destructive way by
using processing algorithms and images. The fruits are counted,
regardless of their maturation stage, contrast between the branch
and the background, or lighting conditions. The problems pre-
sented in the acquired images generate count errors greater than
6%, a percentage which can be reduced by means of an appropriate
image acquisition. The fruits must be gathered in the images and
be appropriately focused, avoiding direct or ﬁltered sunlight, and
avoiding camera movement.
One of the major difﬁculties when identifying fruits in the ﬁeld
is the occlusion that occurs with branches, leaves, and other fruits.
In this research, we evinced that the problem of coffee fruit occlu-
sion can be overcome through the masking technique employed,
which guarantees that regions that show arcs or arc sections are
segmented by means of the ellipse that ﬁts the best. This is one
of the advantages presented by the MVS, since a large part of the
coffee fruits are occluded given the nature of the glomeruli on
branches.
The proposed system does not require high deﬁnition cameras,
sophisticated lightning systems, or vehicles to move across the
plantation. The system is carried by a person, who can easily walk
into the dense crop and, with the handling system and mobile
device, can acquire images at any hight of the tree, at different
heights, and under different lighting conditions, needing no exter-
nal lighting sources.
This development can be found in the state of art as a model of
direct measurement, which is capable of counting the total number
of fruits on a branch based on the automatic fruit count on a single
view of the branch. Compared to similar models that perform fruit
counts via images (Stajnko et al., 2009; Nuske et al., 2011), ours
present a greater determination coefﬁcient, higher than 0.935 with
respect to 0.60, and an average absolute difference of 13.8 fruits
per branch.
As future work it is expected to develop a mobile app to detect
the quality of the acquired images, control the acquisition of the
images on the branches and unable the touchscreen interface, in
order to avoid the problems of images acquisition in rainy days.
Further research should focus on improving the fruit detection
algorithm so as to reduce the value of the absolute differences as
well as ﬁnding a way to reduce the risk of problems generated
by branch curvature, blurry sections on the sides of images, and
blurriness due to movement. At the same time, this method should
be applied to estimate production per tree and per parcel, and
should generate a tool so that coffee growers can estimate the pro-
duction of their farms at any time of the year.
