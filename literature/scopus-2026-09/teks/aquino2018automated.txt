Title: Automated early yield prediction in vineyards from on-the-go image acquisition 
1 
Authors: Arturo Aquino, Borja Millan, Maria-Paz Diago, Javier Tardaguila 
2 
Instituto de Ciencias de la Vid y del Vino (University of La Rioja, CSIC, La Rioja Regional 
3

4 
Corresponding author: Javier Tardaguila (javier.tardaguila@unirioja.es). 
5 
Abstract 
6 
Early grapevine yield assessment provides information to viticulturists to help taking 
7 
management decisions to achieve the desired grape quality and yield amount. In previous works, 
8 
image analysis has been explored to this effect, but with systems performing either manually, on 
9 
a single variety or close to harvest-time, when there are few rectifiable agronomic aspects. This 
10 
study presents a solution based on image analysis for the non-invasive and in-field yield 
11 
prediction in vines of several varieties, at phenological stages previous to veraison, around 100 
12 
days from harvest. To this end, an all-terrain vehicle (ATV) was modified with equipment to 
13 
autonomously capture images of 30 vine segments of five different varieties at night-time. The 
14 
images were analysed with a new image analysis algorithm based on mathematical morphology 
15 
and pixel classification, which yielded overall average Recall and Precision values of 0.8764 and 
16 
0.9582, respectively. Finally, a model was calibrated to produce yield predictions from the 
17 
number of detected berries in images with a Root-Mean-Square-Error per vine of 0.16 kg. This 
18 
accuracy makes the proposed methodology ideal for early yield prediction as a very helpful tool 
19 
for the grape and wine industry. 
20 
Keywords: Grapevine early yield prediction; image analysis; non-invasive sensing; precision 
21 
viticulture.

1 Introduction 
28 
Among all collectable data from a vineyard, grapevine yield estimation outstands for its 
29 
economical relevance (Wolpert and Vilas, 1992; Martin et al., 2002; Dunn, 2010), and also for 
30 
being key to help optimizing plant growth and to improve fruit quality (Dunn and Martin, 2003). 
31 
Yield variability within a vineyard has been proved to be high (Bramley and Hamilton, 2004). 
32 
Classical yield estimation methods, which consist on manual collection and weighting of the crop 
33 
yield in a given and limited number of plants previous to harvest is tedious and insufficient to 
34 
obtain representative yield data. Consequently, non-invasive imaging-based methods are being 
35 
investigated to make possible the efficient and continuous capture of detailed information from 
36 
vines throughout their life cycle (Spalding and Miller, 2013; Li et al., 2014). 
37 
Grapevine yield is determined by the yield components, defined as the number of clusters, the 
38 
number of berries per cluster and the berry weight (Tardáguila et al., 2012). Alternatively, yield 
39 
can also be estimated from the total number of berries and the berry weight (Nuske et al., 2014). 
40 
Whatever the case, the number of berries is the most labile variable determining yield (Anderson 
41 
et al., 2008). It is highly influenced by the weather conditions during inflorescence development 
42 
and berry-set, when it gets fully established and remains mostly invariable until harvest (May, 
43 
2004). 
44 
Imaging-based developments aimed at yield estimation can be found in the literature under two 
45 
differentiated approaches. A first set of proposals is framed within the manual acquisition of 
46 
images, thereby focusing on the discrete analysis of samples from the vineyard. Contrary, the 
47 
second set studies yield estimation by means of on-the-go image acquisition using modified 
48 
human-driven, or autonomous vehicles, with the ambition of making possible the continuous and 
49 
massive analysis. Within the first approach, Chamelat et al. (2006) presented a method based on 
50 
pixel classification to detect grape-pixel aggregations in vine RGB images manually taken in the 
51 
field under daylight. Reis et al. (2011) also proposed the detection of grape-pixel aggregations in 
52 
outdoor RGB images that were taken using the camera flash. They employed colour analysis and 
53 
non-linear image processing to detect aggregations of white-grape pixels. The method was later 
54 

improved and extended to work with red grapes also (Reis et al., 2012). On the other hand, 
55 
Rahman and Hellicar (2014) studied the detection and classification of undeveloped and mature 
56 
clusters of white grapes. Their development consisted on the extraction of the clusters from the 
57 
background as a first step, to finally perform pixel classification by means of texture analysis. 
58 
Other approaches involved colour histogram classification, RGB thresholding and fuzzy 
59 
clustering to estimate cluster weight in vine images (Liu et al., 2013). A more complex capturing 
60 
device was developed by Fernández et al. (2013), which consisted on a sensor rig composed of a 
61 
CCD camera and a servo-controlled filter wheel. This device was used to collect multispectral 
62 
imagery, which was analysed employing a sequential masking algorithm based on k-means 
63 
clustering. Aquino et al. (2017) proposed a methodology based on mathematical morphology and 
64 
false positives filtering by means of supervised learning to count the number of berries in RGB 
65 
cluster images taken in the vineyard with a smartphone under natural sunlight. Very recently, 
66 
Mack et al. (2017) described a method based on 3D reconstruction of high-resolution laser range 
67 
data to model sampled grape clusters. 
68 
Regarding the methods for on-the-go vineyard monitoring, the literature is rather limited. Some 
69 
works involved image acquisition after veraison (Font et al. 2015; Rose et al. 2016), very close 
70 
to harvest, when the time window for cluster thinning, to either regulate the yield or to promote 
71 
increased allocation of carbohydrates to specific clusters (to improve fruit ripening) is very 
72 
limited. Font et al. (2015) equipped a ground vehicle with a reflex camera and artificial lighting 
73 
to capture vine images of a single table grape variety (Flame seedless) at night-time right before 
74 
harvest and reported yield prediction errors of 16%. More recently, a multi-view-stereo system 
75 
coupled to a LED external illumination system was mounted in an autonomous moving platform 
76 
(0.3 km/h), to acquire night-time images, and the estimation of the number of berries in the cluster 
77 
images was attempted (Rose et al., 2016). The choice of earlier yield estimation, before veraison, 
78 
was taken by several authors. Likewise, Nuske et al. (2014) used a tractor, which moved around 
79 
5 km/h, to mount RGB cameras and artificial illumination for image capture in the vineyard at 
80 
night time. They collected data from six grapevine varieties in several growing seasons and 
81 
vineyards, at various timings before harvest, and developed a supervised classifier for berry 
82 

detection fed with a wide set of descriptors (above 30) of texture, colour and shape. Average yield 
83 
estimation accuracy of 11% was only provided for Flame seedless, using images acquired at 
84 
harvest time (Nuske et al. 2014). A different approach was followed by Liu et al. (2017), who 
85 
developed a computer vision system for early grape yield estimation based on shoot detection 
86 
using videos acquired with a low-cost camera at daytime using a white background which moved 
87 
alongside the row.  
88 
In view of the missing factors and lessons learned from previous works, the goal of the present 
89 
study was the development of a comprehensive technological solution for automated early 
90 
grapevine yield prediction based on images acquired on-the-go at a speed similar or faster than 
91 
most agricultural vehicles. In contrast to other available tools focused mostly on yield estimation 
92 
of a single variety at pre-harvest, this proposal analyses vine images of tens of clusters of five 
93 
different grapevine varieties at earlier stages; concretely at phenological stages between berry-set 
94 
and cluster closure (around 100 and 120 days before harvest)  This earlier stage at which yield 
95 
information is acquired could significantly improve its impact, since grape-growers could perform 
96 
viticultural practices to rectify certain key parameters more effectively. Additionally, a secondary 
97 
goal of the present work was to simplify the classifier for berry detection and counting and to 
98 
evaluate the potential development of a unique solution involving multiple grapevine varieties.

2 Materials and methods 
100 
2.1 Experimental design 
101 
The trials were carried out during season 2015 in a commercial vineyard located in Falces (lat. 
102 
42º27’46.0’’N; long. 1º48’12.9’’W; Navarra, Spain). Five grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) varieties 
103 
(red and white) were considered for this study: Albariño, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, 
104 
Tempranillo and Viognier. The vines were planted in year 2009 on rootstock Richter 110 in N-S 
105 
orientation, trained to vertical shoot positioning (VSP) trellis system with 2 and 1 m inter-row 
106 
and intra-row distances, respectively. Vines were defoliated from node one to six only on their 
107 
east side after berry set. Depending on the variety, at the time of image acquisition in the field 
108 
(23rd June, 2015), the clusters were at phenological stages between K and L, according to the 
109 

scale proposed by Baggiolini (1952). Following this scale, phenological stage K refers to that at 
110 
which berries have the 50% of their final size; it is also denoted as pea-size stage. With regard to 
111 
phenological stage L, also called cluster closure, it is reached when berries have about 70% of 
112 
their final size, and they start to touch each other within the cluster. 
113 
2.2 Acquisition of reference data 
114 
For every cultivar, ten consecutive sampling segments composed of three adjacent vines each, 
115 
were labelled and delimited (5 𝑣𝑎𝑟𝑖𝑒𝑡𝑖𝑒𝑠 × 10 𝑠𝑒𝑔𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑠 × 3 𝑣𝑖𝑛𝑒𝑠); thirteen additional 
116 
segments of non-defoliated vines were also selected. Each three-vine segment constituted a 
117 
unique sampling point in which the produced yield was individually weighted and registered at 
118 
the end of the season using a hanging scale (Kern CH15K20, Kern & Sohn GmbH, Balingen, 
119 
Germany). 
120 
2.3 Image acquisition 
121 
The vine segments were photographed ‘on-the-go’ at night time without user intervention. To this 
122 
effect, a sensor-equipped all-terrain vehicle (ATV) was driven through the vineyard at 7 km/h. 
123 
Image capture automation was achieved by adapting the vehicle to incorporate the following 
124 
elements: 
125 
− A mirror-less RGB camera (Sony α7II, Sony, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a Zeiss 24/70mm 
126 
lens with optical stabilisation. This camera mounts a 24 Mpx CCD sensor, incorporates a 5-
127 
axis image stabilisation system and provides high shutter speed, quick image storage and low 
128 
noise generation (Figure 1-(a)). These features allowed to capture and store on-the-go three 
129 
images per wheel-spin at 7 km/h, producing high quality images despite the vibrations caused 
130 
by the ATV’s engine and the irregular ground’s surface. For the experiments, the camera was 
131 
set in manual mode, configuring the aperture in f/4, shutter speed in 1/2500 seconds, ISO 
132 
sensitivity in 5000 and focus in manual mode. 
133 
− A white-light LED panel (Figure 1-(a)) to provide controlled artificial illumination for 
134 
vineyard monitoring at night time. By means of illumination and camera parametrization, it 
135 

was possible to isolate in the image the vines under evaluation from those in the adjacent row. 
136 
Should image acquisition be conducted at day time, the sunlight would equally illuminate the 
137 
whole scene, producing images in which the vines under study were hardly distinguishable 
138 
from those at their back (this fact is illustrated in Figure 2). 
139 
− A modular and flexible structure built with commercial aluminium profiles for sensor 
140 
attachment. The structure consisted on an upfront and a rear tray, plus an adjustable arm to 
141 
be installed in the front tray (Figure 1-(a)). The arm was designed to mount the camera and 
142 
illumination system, making the combination adjustable to different vineyard heights and 
143 
widths. The arm was adjusted for the camera to be at around 1.5 meters from the canopy.  
144 
− An inductive sensor installed in the rear axle for camera triggering; the sensor produced three 
145 
activation pulses per wheel-spin (Figure 1-(b)). 
146 
− A GPS receiver (Leica Zeno 10 Global Positioning System; Heerbrug, St. Gallen, 
147 
Switzerland) for image georeferencing (Figure 1-(c)).  
148 
− A custom-built electronic control system for managing the signals generated by the installed 
149 
devices (GPS and inductive sensor) and triggering the camera using an isolated signal (Figure 
150 
1-(d)). The system also allowed for data storage in an SD-card and for showing capture-status 
151 
information in a 4.9” TFT screen.

2.4 Image analysis algorithm for berry detection in grapevine images 
 
165 
The methodology developed for berry segmentation in digital images acquired on-the-go can be 
166 
divided into three main steps. The first phase consisted on pre-processing the images to discard 
167 
redundant data; this is, those canopy segments captured in more than one image. Then, in a second 
168 
phase, a set of berry candidates was detected by means of morphological filtering. Finally, those 
169 
candidates not corresponding to berries, i.e. false positives, were discarded using a supervised-
170 
learning approach. The berry-segmentation algorithm was developed and tested using Matlab 
171 
(R2015a, Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA). Figure 3 includes a flow-chart diagram illustrating the 
172 
image analysis algorithm described below. 
173 
2.4.1 Image pre-processing 
174 
Three images per wheel-spin were acquired on-the-go with the sensing vehicle, which ensured a 
175 
full coverage of the canopy under evaluation. However, this approach also produced duplication 
176 
of information in images, as two consecutive images always contained a repeated vine segment 
177 
(Figure 4-(a)-(c)). To avoid the analysis of redundant data, images were matched using the ‘Auto 
178 
Blend’ tool provided by Adobe Photoshop CC 2015 (Adobe Systems Incorporated; San José, 
179 

California, USA) to this effect. By this way, matched images were created for being piecewise 
180 
analysed afterwards (Figure 4-(d)).

2.4.2 Extraction of berry candidates 
188

189

190

191 
luminosity independently. Contrary to the RGB scheme, the CIELAB space decouples the 
192 
illumination and colour information. Indeed, it provides a luminosity layer ‘L*’, and the 
193

194 
yellow axis, respectively. Thus, the colour information is represented in the ‘a*b*’ sub-space, 
195 
keeping the luminosity information isolated in the ‘L*’ plane.

3.2 Evaluation results of yield prediction  
426 
From the regressions shown in Figure 8-(a), the number of detected visible berries was a good 
427 
indicator of the actual yield. Based on this finding, a linear model for yield prediction was built. 
428 
First, 20 segments, four per variety, which accounted for the whole dataset variability were 
429 
selected for training. Then a predicted ‘visible’ yield was calculated for these segments by 
430 
multiplying the available average berry weight per variety (computed from historical data) by the 
431 
number of automatically detected berries. Thus, the weights corresponding to the berries visible 
432 
in the segments were estimated. Finally, these predicted ‘visible’ yield values were linearly 
433 
correlated to the actual yield per segment (reference data described in section 2.2) manually 
434 
harvested to build the model (Figure 8-(b)). Thus, the linear model obtained in Figure 8-(b) was 
435 
used to generate the yield predictions per variety detailed in Table 4 for the 30 segments kept for 
436 
external validation. According to the results considering all images together, an average square 
437 
error of 0.16 kg was made per vine, since the measured 𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸𝑣 of 0.48 kg referred to image 
438 
segments composed of three vines. On the other hand, taking into account the relative square error 
439 
(𝑆𝐸𝑣) per variety, the predictions ranged in the interval [8.74, 16.86] (expressed as %). This 
440 
variability can be explained by the different phenological stages at which images were taken 
441 

depending on the variety (ranging between K and L (Baggiolini, 1952)). Indeed, there was a great 
442 
variability in cluster compactness between varieties at both stages, which influenced the 
443 
percentage of visible berries. Therefore, according to the results and this factor, it can be inferred 
444 
that specific predictive models for the stages K and L may surely produce even better predictions. 
445 
Curiously, the obtained overall relative error (|𝐸𝑣|) was very close to 0, concretely 1.30. Contrary 
446 
to the 𝑆𝐸𝑣, errors are accumulated with their signs (positive or negative) in the |𝐸𝑣| calculation, 
447 
and errors by excess and defect neutralized each other in this case. 
448 
Compared to previous studies, Font et al. (2015) achieved a relative square error of 16.0 % when 
449 
all the 25 cluster images (acquired at harvest time) were analysed together. In comparison, the 
450 
presented methodology yielded 12.83 % of error when considering the 30 segments containing 
451 
90 vines. Therefore, besides being more accurate, the presented methodology provides a more 
452 
efficient tool for vineyard management, as it performs at earlier stages on images containing tens 
453 
of clusters. Nuske et al. (2014) linearly correlated the berry counts produced by their algorithm 
454 
with the actual harvest crop weights collected from five Vitis vinifera L. and one table-grape 
455 
varieties. Images were taken at different phenological stages, ranging from berry-set to pre-
456 
harvest. The measured correlation scores varied from 𝑅2 = 0.60 to 0.73 depending on the variety, 
457 
although results were not specified per phenological stage nor in terms of weight error. The 
458 
present work casted a 𝑅2 value of 0.7823 in yield prediction for the training set with five different 
459 
varieties, being this result specifically assessed at early phenological stage. In the study of Nuske 
460 
et al. (2014), yield prediction accuracy was evaluated along two seasons for a single table grape 
461 
cultivar (Flame Seedless) and yield prediction errors ranging from 6.5 to 11.7 % were reported. 
462 
These yield prediction accuracy values are similar to those obtained in the present work for five 
463 
grapevine varieties (Table 4). However, differently from this study in which yield prediction was 
464 
accomplished around 100 days before harvest, yield estimation was conducted at harvest time in 
465 
the work of Nuske et al. (2014). There are some studies in the literature facing yield prediction at 
466 
early stages in different crops, such as apple (Zhou et al., 2012) or mango (Payne et al., 2013). In 
467 
grapevine, early yield prediction (pre-veraison) provide outstanding advantages with respect to 
468 

pre-harvest yield estimation, when only limited corrective actions addressed to comply with 
469 
controlled yield regulations can be performed. In terms of the classifier’s parsimony, a simple 
470 
image classifier fed with 17 descriptors was used in the present study, while Nuske et al. (2014) 
471 
compared three sets of texture descriptors giving as result classifier configurations of more than 
472 
30 descriptors after applying PCA analysis for dimensionality reduction.

The presented methodology paves the way towards its implementation in all sort of agricultural 
489 
moving vehicles during a time window of two to three weeks, prior to veraison, to get an accurate 
490 
estimation of final yield. In addition to the competitive advantage of having such early yield 
491 
estimation, for logistic, economical, and vineyard management purposes, the developed image-
492 
based method can be used to generate maps representing the spatial yield variability in vineyards 
493 
expected at harvest time. To maximize the exploitation of these valuable data, these maps could 
494 
be available to viticulturists through smartphone or tablet applications, as well as through web 
495 
services, directly in the vineyard, and could also be used by the new generation of variable rate 
496 
machinery. Overall it constitutes a key tool for the grape and wine industry as it may help to 
497 
define in a proper way, viticultural operations for yield adjustments to either meet legal 
498 
regulations (as in many wine appellations), as well as to support decision making regarding 
499 
harvest planning and arrangements.

4 Conclusions 
501 

This study presents a new, comprehensive technological solution for non-invasive and automated 
502 
early grapevine yield prediction based on RGB images acquired on-the-go from a vehicle moving 
503 
at a speed similar or higher than most agricultural vehicles. The developed tool was able to 
504 
accurately predict yield by monitoring vines at phenological stages between berry-set and cluster-
505 
closure, that is around 100 days prior to grape harvest. Yield prediction models can be developed 
506 
for individual varieties, but a global, multi-varietal solution has also proved effective, although 
507 
larger training datasets acquired at similar working conditions should be added to maintain or 
508 
increase the performance of the global solution. According to the detailed results per variety, it 
509 
was found that even better outcomes could be achieved by developing prediction models 
510 
adaptable to the specific phenological stage of the vineyard under analysis. 
511 
This advanced yield prediction would be of great utility for viticulturists to better adopt grapevine 
512 
management operations, such as cluster thinning to regulate the yield in an efficient way, not only 
513 
to comply with yield regulations but also to promote improved ripening and quality of the 
514 
remaining fruit. Additionally, for the wine industry, early yield estimation can be also very 
515 
beneficial, as the supply chain from grapegrowing to wine marketing strongly depends on 
516 
anticipated yield estimation well before harvest. The presented solution enables the acquisition of 
517 
a large amount of georeferenced images in a short time, hence of the spatial variability of yield 
518 
prediction within a vineyard plot. This fact will favour the application of vineyard precision 
519 
management techniques as well as the adoption of the new generation of variable rate machinery.
