A B S T R A C T
The ripening stage of a fruit is a crucial determinant of its quality. Traditional assessment methods for 
fruit ripeness are often subjective, error-prone, and time-consuming. Therefore, numerous attempts are being 
made to establish rapid and reliable techniques for assessing the ripeness of fruits and vegetables. Innovative 
technologies, such as electronic noses, imaging technologies, and nondestructive firmness sensors, have been 
employed to evaluate fruit maturity based on various descriptive criteria. The most effective approaches 
are dominated by artificial intelligence (AI) strategies, namely, machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), 
and most recently explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). With the rapid evolution of automated ripeness 
classification, this review summarizes recent developments in sensors, algorithms, and real-world applications 
across fruits and vegetables. It also evaluates the main obstacles (environmental variability, generalizability, 
and data quality) and outlines promising avenues for feasible, scalable solutions. The findings of this study 
emphasize the need for standardized methodologies to enable reproducibility and outcome comparisons. 
Developing a common framework for evaluating various algorithms, sensing systems, and comparable datasets 
as well as standardized evaluation criteria could significantly advance automated fruit ripeness classification. 
Higher classification accuracy can be achieved using more complex deep learning algorithms, such as 
convolutional neural networks, despite their high accuracy, the lack of transparency and explainability limits 
the broader industrial adoption of such techniques. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) is emerging as a 
promising approach, enhancing transparency and trust by addressing the interpretability challenges inherent 
in complex AI-driven models. In conclusion, although automated ripeness classification has made significant 
advances in recent years, improvements in data processing, standardization, and accuracy are still needed 
to address current challenges. By integrating appropriate sensing technologies and selecting suitable feature 
data and algorithms, automatic classification of fruit ripeness can be continually enhanced, thereby improving 
classification accuracy. This progress will ultimately contribute to advancements in agricultural intelligence, 
supply chain management, higher food quality, and reduced waste generation.

1. Introduction
Fruits are rich in vitamins, minerals, carbohydrates, water, and 
phytochemicals, which help prevent and reduce the risk of chronic dis-
eases [1]. On the other hand, fruits have a shorter shelf life due to their 
high perishability, vulnerability to microbial spoiling, and physiological 
and biochemical decomposition. The Food and Agriculture Organiza-
tion (FAO) estimates that 14% of the world’s food is lost after harvest, 
with fruits and vegetables accounting for 40%–50% of this loss before 
reaching the shelves of retailers [2,3]. Therefore, fruit ripeness classifi-
cation is a critical task that aids in harvest management and subsequent 
food distribution through supply chain connections [4,5]. However, the 
wide variety of characteristics among fruits makes classification chal-
lenging [6]. These characteristics include comparable colors, shapes, 
and textures between different classes as well as irregularities within 
classes [7,8]. Currently, several methods are being used to assess fruit 
ripeness, namely subjective quality, conventional methods, invasive 
methods [9], machine learning (ML) models [10], and non-destructive 
techniques [11]. While traditional methods are accurate, they are 
often are labor-intensive, subjective processes, and rely heavily on 
human expertise, making them suboptimal and prone to errors [12]. 
Non-destructive techniques are non-invasive, chemical-free, and user-
friendly approaches to assess fruit ripeness. These strategies play a key 
role in implementing several United Nations Sustainable Development 
Goals (SDGs). Automated fruit ripeness classification supports several 
SDGs mainly by reducing waste, enhancing food security, improving 
nutrition, mitigating climate impacts, and fostering innovation. since it 
promotes sustainable agriculture, reduces food waste, and strengthens 
food security.
Machine learning (ML) and neural networks are evolving as valu-
able tools for collecting and processing data from a variety of sensors 
including image, aromatic, and tactile sensors [13]. They also facil-
itate the interpretation of collected data, providing insights into the 
ripening process. Recent years have witnessed a surge in the use 
of AI approaches for fruit ripeness classification within academic re-
search. Successful applications of ML pattern-recognition algorithms, 
such as decision trees and support vector regression (SVR), have been 
documented in the literature [7,13]. More recently, deep learning 
techniques, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), attention 
mechanisms, and long short-term memory (LSTM) recurrent neural 
networks (RNNs), have shown promising results in classifying fruit mat-
uration. Deep learning–based computer vision is now being applied to 
address a wide range of agricultural challenges, including the detection 
and categorization of fruits and vegetables [14]. However, traditional 
models often referred to as ‘‘black-box’’ models have several shortcom-
ings, mainly owing to their interpretability and complexity [15–17]. 
The emerging technology known as explainable artificial intelligence 
(XAI) improves trust and transparency by resolving issues with inter-
pretability in intricate AI-driven models [18,19]. Contrary to traditional 
black-box models, XAI offers insights into the variables affecting pre-
dictions, which is crucial for the construction of robust models where 
interpretability leads to accurate and significant outcomes.
Rapid advancements in automated fruit maturity classification ne-
cessitate regular, thorough evaluations. This study offers a compre-
hensive, up-to-date analysis of methods and tools for automatically 
determining the maturity of a variety of fruits and vegetables. We 
provide an overview of current methodological advancements, rang-
ing from new sensors and imaging modalities to machine-learning 
and deep-learning techniques, assessing their effectiveness and real-
world applications, and highlighting enduring issues like dataset bias, 
variability in growing and harvesting conditions, and real-world ro-
bustness. Finally, we pinpoint promising directions and application 
potential to direct future research and develop laboratory findings into 
reliable, field-ready technologies.

3. Fruit ripening process - an overview
Fruit ripening is a highly regulated, genetically programmed process 
that transforms immature fruit into mature fruit with an appealing, 
consumable appearance [20]. This process involves a series of irre-
versible biochemical, physiological, and sensory changes, including 
modifications in texture, color, and carbohydrate and sugar contents; 
formation and release of volatile aroma compounds; and variations 
in organic acid and phenolic compound profiles [21]. Fruits can be 
categorized as climacteric or non-climacteric based on their ripening 
characteristics. Climacteric fruits enter a new phase of maturation 
after being harvested [8]. During this phase, they experience increased 
respiration and ethylene release. Although these fruits are harvested 
while still green and firm, they are fully developed and ripe by the time 
they are consumed. Examples of climacteric fruits include cantaloupe, 
peach, apple, blueberry, banana, nectarine, mango, guava, papaya, 
passion fruit, pear, avocado, apricot, plum, and plantain [22]. Under 
controlled conditions, a small amount of ethylene is used to initiate 
the ripening process [23]. By contrast, non-climacteric fruits cease 
the ripening process after harvest and exhibit little-to-no change in 
respiration and ethylene metabolism [24]. Examples of non-climacteric 
fruits include strawberry, watermelon, orange, rambutan, mosambi, 
pomegranate, cashew, and grapes [25].
3.1. Respiration
Respiration is a critical process in which complex biological com-
pounds are broken into simpler molecules, producing energy and sup-
plying specific chemicals for various cellular processes [26]. During 
respiration, fruits utilize oxygen (O2) to metabolize carbohydrates and 
other substrates, including organic acids, lipids, and proteins, while 
producing carbon dioxide (CO2) [27]. Consequently, respiration gen-
erates the energy required for fruits to maintain their structure and 
quality throughout and after the ripening process.

3.2. Fruit color
Fruit color is a crucial characteristic for classifying ripeness and 
serves as an excellent indicator of freshness, quality, and maturation 
stage [28]. The first criteria that fruit buyers evaluate to determine the 
acceptability of fruit are its color and appearance [5]. For example, 
bananas and strawberries [7] demonstrate a significant correlation be-
tween their exterior color and maturity level, making visual assessment 
an effective method for estimating maturity. During maturation, fruits 
undergo various biochemical and physiological changes that alter their 
color [29]. These changes in color (Fig. 4) result from the formation 
and breakdown of pigments, such as chlorophyll (green), anthocyanins 
(blue and red), betlains (red), and carotenoids (red, orange, and yel-
low), leading to a dynamic shift from green to yellow to red as the 
fruit matures [28]. The genetic makeup of the plant, stage of fruit 
ripeness, and influences of biotic and abiotic factors significantly affect 
pigment formation. In addition, changes in fruit pigments are crucial 
for postharvest handling and optimal storage [28]. Alterations in fruit 
color can be measured using colorimeters as well as through image 
acquisition and processing.
3.3. Fruit firmness
The loss of fruit firmness is a key characteristic that indicates 
changes in fruit as it matures [30]. Firmness is crucial when as-
sessing the maturity of certain fruits, such as the ‘‘Hayward’’ green 
kiwifruit, ‘‘Granny Smith’’ green apple, and ‘‘Conference’’ pear, where 
color change does not provide a clear indication of the maturation 
level [31]. These fruits maintain their chlorophyll, yet the concentra-
tion of carotenoids decreases as they develop [32]. Therefore, strategies 
to obtain firmness information are essential. Furthermore, environ-
mental conditions, such as temperature, can affect normal changes 
in fruit color. However, firmness offers a more precise measurement 
of the ripeness of internal tissues and indicates changes in the inner 
components of fruit [31].
3.4. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)
VOCs contribute to the natural aroma of fruits, serving as key 
indicators of their freshness [33]. However, not all VOCs are involved 
in the formation of fruit odor; only those that exceed a certain threshold 
influence fruit aroma perception. The primary volatile compounds 
responsible for fruit aroma include esters, terpenes, aldehydes, ketones, 
and alcohols [34]. In addition, the type, concentration, and presence 
of unique components significantly affect the strength of the aroma. 
Even within similar species, fruits can have distinct volatile molecules, 
leading to significant variations in aroma profiles. The characteristics 
of fruit aroma are shaped by both external environmental factors and 
internal genetic factors [34]. Ethylene, one of the most crucial volatile 
organic molecules, serves as a primary indicator of climacteric fruit 
maturity, making it possible to determine the ripeness stage [24].
4. Methods for fruit ripeness classification
There are several approaches for classification of fruit ripeness, such 
as subjective quality, conventional methods, machine learning models, 
invasive and non-destructive techniques [9,11]. Although easy, the 
subjective approach is prone to human error, time-consuming, labor-
intensive, and requires specialist knowledge. Yet, it is still utilized as 
a reference point for other techniques. Invasive detection approaches, 
such as chemical and textural analysis, can improve classification accu-
racy but may cause harm and are not useful in many applications [11]. 
Therefore, innovative techniques such as non-destructive artificial in-
telligence (AI)-based approaches (Fig. 4) are essential for precise fruit 
ripeness classification in real-time.
4.1. Non-imaging techniques for fruit ripeness classification
4.1.1. Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy
NIR spectroscopy is one of the most commonly used nondestructive 
technologies, as it encompasses the spectral region associated with the 
vibrations of molecular bonds, including O–H and C–H bonds, which 
are characteristic of fruit macroconstituents [40]. NIR spectroscopy 
can penetrate fruits at deeper levels compared with other vibrational 
techniques due to the poor absorptivity of overtone characteristics. 
In particular, short-wave infrared radiation, falling between 750 and 
1100 nm, provides the most effective path lengths, making this spectral 
region ideal for predicting properties of intact fruit. Table 1 presents 
recent spectroscopy techniques used for fruit ripeness classification. For 
instance, Ricci et al. [35] used a portable NIR sensor (740–1070 nm) 
along with the PLSR algorithm to assess intact pomegranate fruits. 
They measured total soluble solids (TSSs) in fresh fruits over a 2-year 
period, achieving a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.25. Similarly, 
Sharma et al. [42] used NIR hyperspectral scanning (900–1600 nm) to 
classify the maturation stages of durian, with the linear discriminant 
analysis (LDA) algorithm demonstrating 100% accuracy in ripeness 
classification. Although NIR cameras are excellent at detecting invisible 
defects, they come with challenges such as high costs and more complex 
operations.

4.1.2. Visible–NIR (Vis–NIR)spectroscopy
Vis–NIR spectroscopy is a widely recognized method for postharvest 
assessment of fresh, intact fruit. As light reaches the fruit’s surface, 
it can be absorbed, re-emitted, or scattered in measurable ways [38]. 
The quantity of these interactions is influenced by the fruit’s chem-
ical components and physical characteristics, which also indicate its 
maturity [43]. Reflectance spectroscopy measures Vis–NIR light in the 
380–2500-nm range, which is highly dependent on the light absorption 
properties of the fruit’s major chemical constituents [43]. Srinivasagan 
et al. [38] used an inexpensive Vis–NIR spectral sensor with wave-
lengths of 410–940 nm to investigate the interior physicochemical 
properties of fresh datefruit (Table 1). Their portable spectral sensor, 
combined with a TinyML model, successfully predicted the storage life 
of fresh fruit at all ripening stages. This was experimentally confirmed 
by comparing the results to laboratory data, with the light-weight 
regression model achieving an accuracy of over 93% for all treated 
samples. According to its reported performance, this TinyML sensor 
could serve as a portable electronic device to monitor fruit freshness 
in real time. Olisah [36] explored the ripeness of blackberry fruits 
using a stereo sensor equipped with Vis–NIR spectral filters at 700 
and 770 nm wavelengths. The resulting images were used as inputs for 
a multi-input CNN-VGG16 ensemble classifier model, which achieved 
accuracies of 95.1% on unseen sets and 90.2% under field conditions.

fruit ripeness classification.
4.1.3. Volatile-based techniques
Fruits with low chlorophyll content exhibit reduced reflectance, 
rendering color detection ineffective. As an alternative, electrochem-
ical sensors can detect gases emitted by fruits at various stages of 
ripeness [44]. Recent advancements in sensor design have led to signifi-
cant improvements in electronic noses (‘‘e-noses’’). An e-nose comprises 
a gas sensor array that responds uniquely to certain volatile com-
pounds. Volatile molecules interact with the elements in gas sensors, 
causing permanent changes in electrical properties, such as conductiv-
ity [45]. By integrating signals from an e-nose system with pattern-
recognition algorithms or classifiers such as SVM, artificial neural 
networks (ANNs), and other ML classifiers, identifying different sam-
ples by clustering similar emissions that correspond to specific volatile 
compounds is possible [46]. The e-nose is not only cost-effective and 
time-efficient but also yields reliable results across systems and is less 
biased compared with sensory panels.

Table 2 summarizes recent applications of electronic nose techniques 
for evaluating fruit ripening. Rivai et al. [49] developed a system 
that employed gas sensors (MQ-3, ME3-C2H4, and Alphasense H2S-B4) 
and neural networks to differentiate between various ripeness levels 
of durian based on their volatile components. This system successfully 
classified ripeness stages with a 91% accuracy rate.
Trebar et al. [50] used four MOS sensors (MQ3, MQ135, MQ136, 
and MQ138) to obtain measurements from ‘‘Golden Delicious’’ apples. 
They employed K-means clustering and principal component analysis 
(PCA) to identify three ripening phases: low ripe, ripe, and overripe. 
Their results indicated effective apple sorting for specific datasets using 
the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) model, achieving an accuracy exceeding 
75%. Remarkably, 100% accuracy was recorded for the dataset that 
included observations from all trials on particular testing sets and for 
the assessment set from completely new and separate experiments. 
Furthermore, PCA indicated that using just two or three sensors could 
yield successful results. This underscores the importance of analyzing 
data from multiple tests conducted over an extended period following 
apple harvest. Variations and similarities were observed in the VOCs 
(such as esters, ethylene, aldehydes, and alcohols) among more or less 
ripe apples tested in the fall.
In another investigation, Sun et al. [51] introduced an e-nose based 
on arrays of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) for the rapid and 
accurate measurement of ethylene. Using the QCM technology, they 
synthesized and assembled six zirconium-based MOFs with systemat-
ically varied pore diameters and 𝜋–𝜋 binding sites into a sensor array. 
The complementary characteristics of these six MOF sensors allowed 
for selective detection of ethylene. The system demonstrated high 
selectivity and stability, with a stability rate of 98.29% and a detection 
limit of 0.27 ± 0.02 ppm. After data were fed into a ML algorithm 
(i.e., KNN), the e-nose system was able to distinguish ethylene from 
gas blends, achieving overall qualitative and quantitative accuracies of 
90.30% and 98.89%, respectively. This system can be used to assess the 
quality of fruits by accurately detecting ethylene produced during fruit 
ripening.
In addition, the study highlights the intriguing potential for creating 
MOF-based e-nose devices that can monitor and detect challenging 
target VOCs. Table 2 lists recent applications of e-nose sensors in 
fruit ripening classification. Garcia et al. [45] employed an e-nose 
system to collect volatile compounds and analyze changes in them 
during the growth and ripening stages of three grape varieties (Niagara 
Rosada, BRS Vitória, and Bord˘o). All models demonstrated a balance of 
accuracy, with over 85%, 92%, and 93% for the cultivars BRS Vitória, 
Niagara, and Bord˘o, respectively. KNN and random forest (RF) models 
outperformed extreme learning machine (ELM) and SVM models.
Research indicates that although olfactory sensors can be used to 
categorize fruits in certain situations, their utilization faces specific 
limitations and challenges. The aromas of fruits are often complex 
mixtures of multiple compounds [51]. Thus, aroma sensors struggle 
to differentiate among various odor elements and accurately associate 
them with specific fruit species in categorization tasks [54]. The accu-
racy of fruit categorization is compromised by the inability of olfactory 
sensors to detect subtle odor variations, as they are either responsive 
only to certain concentrations of aromas or can only detect specific odor 
constituents [55].
In addition, the performance of olfactory sensors can be influenced 
by ambient conditions such as humidity and temperature as well as the 
presence of other gases in the environment, including air pollution or 
other odors, which may interfere with the sensors [56]. In response 
to these challenges, the field of monitoring VOCs has significantly 
advanced, focusing on the development of portable, low-cost sensors 
that can detect various types of volatile molecules. However, to achieve 
highly selective and precise VOC detection, key challenges must be 
addressed, specifically regarding the selective capture of VOCs and 
accurate interpretation of results. The use of ML to recognize patterns 
from a range of gas sensors can help overcome these limitations.
4.1.4. Firmness-based techniques
In the fruit sector, handheld penetrometers are commonly used for 
assessing firmness [57]; however, this method does not reliably indicate 
the right maturity level for all fruits. Its widespread commercial use is 
limited due to its damaging nature and labor-intensive procedure [30]. 
Recently, nondestructive techniques for evaluating fruit firmness have 
gained popularity, allowing for quality assessments without causing 
harm. Firmness, as a key internal or external quality feature, has been 
inferred using various nondestructive assessment techniques that rely 
on electromagnetic signal processing, such as HSI [58] and Vis–NIR 
spectroscopy [59]. Although selected signals at different frequencies 
have effectively identified specific internal attributes, such as total acid-
ity, TSS content, weight, and size [60,61], their associations with fruit 
firmness remain unclear, complicating the development of mechanistic 
models. By contrast, empirical models can be constructed using only 
statistical features, but their accuracy is often subpar, and their trans-
ferability is limited [62]. Furthermore, evaluating firmness continues 
to be challenging due to its tactile nature, which exhibits inconsistent 
correlations with these signals. Table 2 reviews recent applications of 
tactile sensors in fruit ripening classification. Lin et al. [31] introduced 
a soft gripper that combines vision-based tactile sensing technology 
with soft robotics, enabling gripping, sensing, and classification. A 
CNN–LSTM framework was employed to predict fruit firmness based 
on visual data derived from finger deformation and image sequences,

Nondestructive fruit firmness sensors, such as piezoelectric beam 
sensors, acoustic vibration sensors, and multisensor systems, have the 
potential to effectively and accurately assess fruit quality and ripeness. 
Both contact and noncontact [63,64] acoustic signals have been ex-

because tactile sensors classify fruits based on hardness, shape, and 
surface roughness [64], but they cannot capture other characteristics 
such as color and flavor. Although tactile sensors can perceive certain 
physical properties of materials, they may struggle to differentiate 
between complex fruit classification features as the differences in char-
acteristics such as firmness and shape are often minimal across various 
fruit types.
4.2. Color- and imaging-based techniques for fruit ripeness classification
Color assessment of fruit ripeness is considered one of the most 
important nondestructive techniques, offering several advantages over 
traditional destructive methods. These advantages include the ability 
to conduct multiple concurrent measurements, assess high volumes, 
and make timely decisions [56]. The nondestructive evaluation of 
fruit ripeness began over 50 years ago with the introduction of light 
transmittance methods [66]. Since then, numerous techniques have 
been developed, including colorimetry, visible imaging, multispectral 
imaging, HSI, and fluorescence imaging.
Image sensors can capture the multidimensional characteristics of 
fruit, such as color, size, texture, and shape, which are essential for 
categorization and differentiation [67]. Consequently, these sensors 
provide comprehensive information on various features, facilitating 
more accurate fruit classification. They enable subtle distinctions to 
be made between fruits, as they typically possess high resolution and 
can capture detailed visual data [56]. The use of image processing 
technology for automatic fruit categorization offers numerous benefits, 
including the ability to process large-scale data, access rich feature 
information, and achieve high-resolution visualizations [68]. In ad-
dition, advancements in optical sensor technology have enabled ex-
tensive image datasets to be more readily obtained. These datasets 
enhance classification accuracy and robustness by training classifiers 
to deliver more precise representations of features and categorization 
patterns [69]. Here, we focused on the most frequently referenced 
techniques for fruit evaluation found in scientific literature.
4.2.1. Colorimetry
Traditional devices known as colorimeters are widely used in the 
fruit industry to assess the color of fruits. These instruments provide 
more accurate measurements than human visual evaluation, utilizing 
the CIELAB color space to comply with established standards [70]. 
In the Lab* color space, the L* value indicates lightness or darkness,
a* values represent the red-to-green spectrum, and b* values indicate 
the blue-to-yellow spectrum [70]. Nowadays, portable colorimeters are 
commercially available and can be used to collect data in the field. 
For instance, Tabassum et al. [71] employed a Hunter Lab Colorimeter 
to analyze the color properties of fresh-cut papaya samples, including 
storage time (in days) and color values (L, a, and b), chroma, and 
color change (𝛥E). The results were then used to train ML algorithms 
(ANN and SVR), which demonstrated a strong ability to adapt to 
the experimental findings (R2 > 0.99). However, due to the small 
sampling area relative to the size of the fruit, colorimeters may not 
always produce reliable color results [44]. Furthermore, certain fruits 
are known to exhibit inconsistent colors at different stages of ripeness, 
making quality assessment through colorimetry more complex.
4.2.2. Conventional imaging
Conventional imaging systems can extract useful properties from 
samples, including color, surface texture, and morphological traits, by 
imitating the functions of human eyes [68]. By converting photons 
reflected from the skin of fruits into electrical impulses and transmitting 
these signals to a camera equipped with complementary metal oxide 
semiconductor (CMOS) or charge-coupled device (CCD) sensors, 2D 
color imaging overcomes the limitations of colorimeters. Typically, 
the sensor captures light and filters it into three channels: red (R), 
green (G), and blue (B). The intensity values are influenced by the 
lighting conditions, fruit samples, and intrinsic features of the camera. 
Fruit ripeness can be analyzed in the RGB color space, similar to the
Lab* color space [66]. The advantages of image-based automated fruit 
categorization technologies include the extraction of deep feature in-
formation, high resolution, ability to process massive amounts of data, 
and cost-effectiveness [56]. Thus, in comparison to other approaches, 
this technology allows for the inexpensive evaluation of changes in the 
exterior properties of fruits, simplifying the determination of ripeness. 
Digital cameras outperform more conventional techniques such as col-
orimeters when it comes to fruits ripeness assessment.
Currently, RGB imaging is widely used in fruit classification, as it 
requires significantly less processing power compared to other imaging 
systems [56]. Tables 3 and 5 review recent applications of conventional 
imaging techniques for fruit ripening classification. To classify custard 
apples (Cv. Balanagar) based on variations in their imaging, physical, 
and chemical characteristics, such as the color of fruit areole skin, 
Wakchaure et al. [72] presented a prototype system that detects matu-
rity phases using image processing and clustering algorithms (SVM and 
K-means). Statistical analysis revealed significant variations in R, G, 
and TSS between the maturation phases. Ultimately, the classification 
of fruit at different stages of maturation achieved 100% accuracy. 
However, visible imaging presents some challenges. For instance, dis-
tinguishing between the background and the fruit in an image can be 
difficult. In addition, the RGB values obtained can vary depending on 
the technology used. In general, the sensor receives light, filtering it 
into red, green, and blue channels, with intensity values depending on 
the camera’s built-in properties and the actual illumination conditions.
4.2.3. Hyperspectral imaging (HIS)
Hyperspectral imaging is recognized as one of the most effective 
methods for assessing fruit quality [58]. HSI captures images at a broad 
range of continuous wavelengths, creating a three-dimensional imaging 
cube [80]. It collects spectral information from each pixel in a 2D 
data array, resulting in 3D data sets of spatial and spectral information 
known as hypercubes, which consist of one spectral dimension and 
two spatial dimensions [80]. These characteristics enable HSI to detect 
chemical composition and interior structures, making it a reliable tool 
among imaging methods. Table 4 reviews recent applications of HSI 
techniques for fruit ripening classification. In their research, Benelli 
et al. [32] combined HSI with chemometric methods (partial least 
squares (PLS) and PLS-DA) to estimate the flesh firmness and TSS 
content of kiwifruit and categorize samples based on ripeness. They 
found that reducing the number of wavelengths did not significantly 
affect classification and prediction results; thus, a less costly multispec-
tral camera operating in the 400–1000-nm range could yield similar 
outcomes.

applied hyperspectral techniques to predict strawberry quality. They 
preprocessed the spectral data using SG smoothing and employed PCC 
for data reduction to extract feature bands. Their findings indicated 
that using HSI technology to assess the quality of strawberries is viable. 
Similarly, Raj et al. [82] used two narrow hyperspectral bands adjusted 
at 674 and 698 nm to develop a model for categorizing ripeness. Their 
trials showed that using the entire spectrum, rather than just a few

4.2.4. Multispectral imaging
Instead of scanning the entire wavelength range, multispectral imag-
ing, a type of HSI that collects data at specific wavelengths [80], is 
typically achieved using a frame scanning imaging system that incor-
porates a CCD or CMOS sensor along with a liquid crystal tunable filter. 
The objective of multispectral integration is to comprehensively assess 
fruit quality by combining data from various spectral bands, including 
visible, hyperspectral, and near-infrared bands. Mohammed et al. [39] 
introduced an affordable and rapid multispectral sensor that captures 
reflectance data across wavelengths of 410–940 nm for stored dates. 
They employed a TinyML model to estimate the overall quality and 
shelf life of packed dates based on reflectance spectroscopy, achiev-
ing a high accuracy with an R2 value of 0.951. Similarly, Tamayo-
Monsalve et al. [83] developed a calibrated and validated multispectral 
system operating within the 400–1000-nm spectral range for matu-
rity classification of coffee. Five models were employed, including 
Inception-V3, Inception-ResNet-V2, DenseNet201, VGG16, and VGG19, 
with DenseNet201 yielding the best results and achieving 100% accu-
racy in cross-validation and 98% accuracy on the dataset  (see Table 
4).
4.3. Data preprocessing
Visual sensors often capture images affected by distortion and noise, 
which limit their suitability for direct use in image processing and com-
puter vision tasks. Consequently, significant preprocessing is required 
to reduce noise and correct distortions. Median filtering, for instance, is 
widely employed to preserve image boundaries while smoothing homo-
geneous regions, effectively addressing issues such as salt-and-pepper 
noise [87].
The readability, interpretability, and overall quality of fruit images 
can be enhanced through various image enhancement methods applied 
in the transformation, frequency, and spatial domains. Techniques such 
as contrast stretching, histogram equalization, and diverse filtering 
strategies improve feature visibility and discrimination. Addressing 
color-related challenges is equally important; approaches including 
color transformation, calibration, and white balance correction ensure 
color accuracy and enhance application compatibility.
Advanced denoising methods such as Self2Self NN, DFT-Net, and 
DnCNNs have demonstrated strong performance in noise reduction. 
However, limitations such as hyperparameter sensitivity and potential 
loss of fine details remain [88]. For image enhancement, techniques 
like Novel RetinexDIP, Unsharp Masking, and LE-Net can significantly 
improve visual quality but often face difficulties in handling complex 
scenarios while preserving image integrity. Segmentation methods such 
as Mask-RCNN and PSPNet provide accurate object separation [89], 
though challenges persist with overlapping objects and maintaining 
robustness. Finally, feature extraction approaches ranging from con-
ventional CNNs to hybrid architectures such as LSTM-augmented CNNs 
reveal critical image characteristics while balancing adaptability and 
computational efficiency.
4.4. Selecting classifiers
The selection of a classifier primarily depends on several fac-
tors: type of dataset, total number of datasets, number of attributes 
per dataset, desired accuracy, available computational resources, and 
whether the learning process is supervised. In general, unsupervised 
methods such as hierarchical clustering and K-means clustering aim 
to identify previously unknown but potentially valuable object classes
[80]. Due to the complexity of features, hybrid techniques such as 
PCA combined with ANN are frequently employed [47]. In many 
cases, supervised ML classifiers are applied, requiring labeled data 
for training. The model learns the spectral properties of each class 
and based on spectral similarity, assigns additional pixels to the most 
similar class [90].
Traditional ML methods perform well with datasets containing nu-
merical or extracted characteristics (such as chemical measurements, 
texture features, or color histograms) [47]. SVMs are well-suited for 
problems with distinct features, whereas RF excels in handling non-
linear data relationships across various characteristics. The KNN algo-
rithm provides a straightforward classification method based on the 
proximity of neighbors in feature space. Logistic regression is typically 
employed for simpler classification issues with linearly separable fea-
tures. For hyperspectral data analysis, RFs, SVMs, and neural networks 
are popular supervised methods for classifying data [80]. Despite their 
computational efficiency, these techniques often struggle to capture the 
intricate characteristics that differentiate distinct fruit varieties.

More advanced algorithms, such as deep learning models, may be 
required for processing larger datasets due to their ability to handle 
vast amounts of information and extract complex features. By contrast, 
simpler models tend to perform better with smaller datasets, helping 
to prevent overfitting. To select the most suitable algorithm, the fruit’s 
physical characteristics, such as its size, color, texture, and shape, must 
be considered. For image processing, color-based techniques may be 
used to classify fruits with noticeable color differences. Algorithms such 
as CNNs and ANNs require substantial computing power for training 
and prediction, which might not be practical for many tasks. In these 
cases, less resource-intensive algorithms such as SVMs and KNN may be 
more appropriate. CNNs are excellent choices for imaging data [88] and 
are frequently used in classifying ripeness [91]. They can discern hier-
archical features from images, including textures, edges, and complex 
patterns related to maturity. Simple CNN architectures (e.g., MaxPool-
ing2D, fully connected layers, and Conv2D) can be trained to categorize 
fruits based on their appearance [92].
Pretrained models such as VGG16, EfficientNet, and ResNet can be 
advantageous [93]. These models have been trained on large datasets 
(such as ImageNet) and can be fine-tuned for classifying fruit maturity 
with specific datasets [94]. The technique of transfer learning involves 
adapting a pretrained model, typically developed on massive image 
datasets, to classify fruit ripeness [91]. This approach can save sig-
nificant time and computing resources [89]. Popular transfer learning 
models for fruit categorization include VGG16, ResNet50, InceptionV3, 
and MobileNet. Additional advanced techniques, such as RNNs and at-
tention mechanisms may also be applied. RNNs or LSTM networks can 
be particularly beneficial for applications involving time-series data, 
such as tracking the ripening of fruit over time [5]. To evaluate the 
quality and consistency of the results, classification metrics, including 
F1-score, accuracy, recall, precision, receiver operating characteristic 
curve, and confusion matrix are used. Simultaneously, computational 
metrics such as training time, memory usage, testing time, and power 
consumption, are used to assess the speed and resource efficiency of 
the classification algorithms. 
4.5. Statistical and ML models for fruit ripeness classification
The most commonly used statistical models for fruit classifica-
tion are ANNs, LDA, PCA, and PLS. Other statistical methods can be 
employed to process images. Classical ML techniques, such as SVM, 
KSVM, KNN, RF, DTC, MLP, and gradient boosting regression trees, 
are typically used to categorize the ripeness stages of fruits based on 
texture and color characteristics. This discussion specifically focused 
on the most frequently cited methods for fruit assessment in scientific 
literature. Table 3 summarizes recent studies that have classified fruit 
ripening using statistical and ML methods. For instance, Fan et al. [81] 
used RF, SVM, PLS, and Gaussian process regression models to predict 
strawberry quality using hyperspectral data. Their findings indicated 
that the SVM model was the most effective for predicting strawberry 
soluble solids, anthocyanins, and hardness, achieving R2 and RMSE 
values of 0.89, 0.81, 0.87, and 0.72%, 0.04 mg/g, and 0.33 kg/cm2, 
respectively. The PLS model provided the most accurate and robust 
prediction of titratable acids, with R2 and RMSE values of 0.85 and 
0.03%, respectively.
Worasawate et al. [95] explored the effectiveness of ML models in 
classifying mangoes based on physical characteristics, such as average 
color and weight, as well as electrical measurements such as capaci-
tance. The feed-forward ANN achieved the best performance, with a 
93.6% accuracy rate in a two-class classification (unripe vs. ripe) trial 
and an 89.6% accuracy rate in a three-class classification (unripe, ripe, 
and overripe) experiment. For avocados, firmness and maturity were 
assessed by extracting color values (Lab*) from images of the fruit. 
The SVR model yielded an RMSE of 7.54 following training [79]. PCA 
was employed to identify key features from strawberry images captured 
across 19 spectral bands, ranging from 405 to 970 nm. Following train-
ing with various ML methods, the SVM classification model achieved 
100% accuracy based on the extracted features.
Further review of the data in Table 3 reveals several issues and 
challenges associated with ML techniques for fruit classification. The 
difference in accuracy among various fruit varieties and classification 
platforms presents a significant drawback. Due to the inherent com-
plexity of some visual characteristics of fruits or limitations of the 
algorithms used, certain combinations achieve near-perfect accuracy, 
whereas others fall short. Although classification efficiency can be 
markedly improved through refinement and technique improvement, 
traditional image recognition technologies have their disadvantages. 
For instance, extensive image preprocessing is often required, including 
tasks such as picture segmentation and edge detection. In addition, 
the implementation of these methods relies on numerous images with 
accurate labels, which is a labor-intensive and challenging process.

rapid advancements in image recognition systems [88]. This approach 
utilizes pretrained weights and factors and processes data for feature 
extraction through multilayer networks [94]. Current research indi-
cates that deep learning–based methods, such as CNNs, RNNs, and 
LSTM networks, are highly effective in classifying fruits, significantly 
reducing classification errors [93].

deep learning methods for fruit categorization. A significant issue is 
the variation in accuracy across different classification algorithms and 
fruit varieties, which may stem from the inherent complexity of specific 
fruit visual features or the limitations of the algorithms themselves. In 
fruit ripeness classification, CNN approaches combined with transfer 
learning have generally outperformed traditional ML methods. How-

pretability remains a concern, as understanding the decision-making 
processes of deep learning algorithms can be difficult.
4.7. Hybrid models and multimodels
The accuracy of classification can be improved by merging sensor 
data, such as hardness and sugar content, with image data [114]. 
Using multiple data modalities, a hybrid model that accepts both image 
data and sensor measurements as input may achieve reliable perfor-
mance [115]. For example, traditional ML models such as SVMs or RF 
can process sensor data, whereas CNNs are capable of analyzing image 
data to improve accuracy of predictions. This combination improves 
prediction accuracy and mitigates overfitting problems commonly asso-
ciated with traditional methods. Sinha and Dhanalakshmi [6] proposed 
a multifused CNN technique that integrates three advanced deep learn-
ing models, namely, EfficientNet-B0, ResNet50V2, and MobileNetV2, 
into a single workflow. Using the Fruit-360 dataset, both small and 
complete versions, the proposed multifused CNN model demonstrated 
exceptional performance, accurately classifying fruit types across 24 
and 131 categories and achieving impressive accuracy rates of 99.32% 
and 97.15%, respectively.
A fruit classification technique achieving 96.08% accuracy was 
developed by Gill et al. [116] using deep learning models such as CNN,

fiers, and KNN. Zhu and Spachos [118] developed a two-layer classifier 
for banana classification. The first layer employs an SVM as the classi-
fier, whereas the second-layer classifier utilizes YOLOv3. The first layer 
receives a feature vector that includes retrieved texture and color data, 
and its output is then fed into the second-layer classifier. The proposed 
system achieved an overall classification accuracy of 96.4%.
4.8. Attention mechanism models
Attention mechanism models can be directed toward specific areas 
of an image (e.g., fruit sections) that are more indicative of maturity. 
For example, Min et al. [119] developed a multiscale attention network 
called MSANet that integrates visual features at multiple levels to 
provide a comprehensive representation of each image. The attention-
based MSANet aims to improve fruit image recognition by combining 
multiscale visual characteristics that emphasize the key elements of 
images. Moreover, Bu et al. [109] created a deep learning model for 
classifying citrus ripeness by training YOLOv5, YOLOX, YOLOv7, and 
YOLOv8 on a citrus fruit dataset, categorizing ripeness into unripe, 
half-ripe, and ripe. Their results revealed that the recognition model 
trained with YOLOv5 achieved the highest recognition rates, with mean 
average precision (mAP) @0.5 and mAP@0.7 approaching 94.73% 
and 93.73%, respectively. They enhanced recognition performance by 
incorporating five attention mechanisms (CBAM, EVC, SimAM, CA, 
and SE) into the YOLOv5 model. Their findings revealed that the 
best recognition accuracy was obtained by combining YOLOv5s with 
the CBAM attention mechanism, achieving 95.40% on mAP@0.7 and 
96.07% on mAP@0.5.
4.9. Infield assessment of fruit ripeness
Real-time imaging systems enable immediate information process-
ing and decision making. The integration of edge computing and ML 
algorithms is crucial for rapid evaluation, thereby reducing processing 
time and boosting overall efficiency. To achieve real-time detection 
of strawberries in field environments, Zhang et al. [120] proposed a 
novel deep neural network called RTSD-Net. This system relies on the 
newly developed light-weight YOLOv4-tiny, which consists of fewer 
layers and has a modified topology. The accuracy difference between 
RTSD-Net and YOLOv4-tiny is only 0.62%; however, RTSD-Net sig-
nificantly improves detection speed. To detect ripe green tomatoes, 
Wang et al. [121] employed an autonomous image collection method 
using a Mask R-CNN algorithm, achieving an F1 score of 92.0% for 
both the bounding box and mask region. For precise identification 
and classification of peaches in real orchard conditions, Zhao [89] 
introduced a one-stage attention-based peach instance segmentation 
technique.
This new model outperformed the advanced Mask R-CNN, with 
an average precision of 72.12% compared with 69.91% and pro-
duced smoother and more accurate boundary predictions. The proposed 
model eliminates the need for anchor generator design and region 
proposal networks while utilizing both spatial and channel attention to 
enhance perception capabilities. In addition, the same study introduced 
NinePeach, a substantial and notable peach dataset, which includes 
images of nine peach varieties at various stages of maturity under 
natural light.
5. Challenges and future research
Reliable assessment of fruit maturity is essential for improving food 
quality and safety, extending shelf life to reduce food waste, and 
optimizing production and marketing. The fruit sector is expected to 
benefit greatly from the integration of AI and sensor technology. How-
ever, the variability among different fruit types presents challenges, 
as different fruits ripen differently, and even closely related varieties 
may ripen differently depending on environmental conditions. Factors 
such as lighting and the surrounding environment, including changes 
in weather or background noise, affect the accuracy of image-based 
classification.
Thus, the calibration process must involve careful adjustments to 
ambient conditions and application parameters, including illumina-
tion, resolution, background, and speed. Although adopting advanced 
tools such as deep learning and HIS may incur additional costs, they 
offer valuable opportunities to enhance fruit classification accuracy. 
Concerns regarding the consistency and reliability of machine vision 
systems arise due to the lack of standardized techniques for image 
gathering, processing, and analysis. Real-time processing remains a 
significant challenge, particularly for large-scale systems such as au-
tomated harvesting robots, as developing systems that can classify fruit 
ripeness accurately in real time is complex. Innovative approaches 
such as dynamic lighting strategies and multicamera technologies can 
improve performance but may also increase the overall cost of the 
machine vision system.
Image sensors can capture multidimensional fruit characteristics, 
including texture, color, size, and shape, that are crucial for differ-
entiating and classifying fruits. By contrast, aroma sensors focus on 
fruit odor, whereas firmness sensors measure tactile qualities. The 
integration of data from various sensing modalities, combining visual 
and nonvisual data (e.g., chemical or tactile sensors), can enhance 
classification accuracy. Effective strategies for data fusion are vital in 
this process, as they synthesize information from multiple aspects to 
provide a comprehensive assessment of the fruit.
These methods operate at different levels, including feature-,
decision-, and model-level fusion. Furthermore, an important area of 
study involves enhancing the abilities of cameras to see through fruit 
surfaces to detect internal features non-invasively. This research entails 
exploring novel techniques that leverage sensor technologies or diverse 
imaging methods.
Despite AI’s immense promise, numerous issues remain unresolved. 
Classical ML models have faced significant limitations, particularly due 
to their challenges with image processing, which complicates automa-
tion. By contrast, deep learning models, particularly CNNs, are gaining 
more attention than other ML methods in fruit classification studies.
6. Conclusion
This comprehensive review has brought to light the significant de-
velopments and approaches in automated fruit ripeness classification, 
as well as their methodologies and utilization in the agriculture field 
and fruit industry. DL and ML models, and imaging or non-imaging 
methodologies have all been shown to be successful in delivering

precise and real-time ripeness classification for fruits and vegetables. 
Most recently, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has emerged 
as a promising paradigm aimed at enhancing transparency and trust 
by mitigating the interpretability challenges associated with complex 
AI-driven models. The benefits of these methods are also outlined in 
this investigation, shedding insight into the difficulties and potential 
directions for automated fruit ripeness classification growth in the fruit 
industry. Yet, the insights gained emphasize that there are a number of 
obstacles hinder the application of these cutting-edge methods in prac-
tical situations. To deal with these issues, we advise (1) the necessity 
for a consistent approach to facilitate comparison and reproducibility of 
results. (2) Developing a common framework for assessing different al-
gorithms and sensing systems, similar datasets, and uniform evaluation 
criteria could significantly advance the field. (3) CNN-based techniques 
have produced outstanding results; however, determining the extent to 
which one CNN architecture outperforms others remains challenging. 
Furthermore, details of distinct models designed for specific purposes 
are lacking. (4) To address the issue of resilience, advanced algorithms 
are required to mitigate the effects of noise, distortion, obstruction, 
and system failures on image quality. (5) Through constant training 
and refinement of the algorithm, an automated fruit classification 
system can be tailored to accommodate various fruit types as well as 
differing classification standards and requirements, enabling it to adapt 
to changing agricultural conditions and markets.
