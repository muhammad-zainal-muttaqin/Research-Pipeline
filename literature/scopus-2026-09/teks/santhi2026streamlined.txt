AbstractAccurate vineyard yield estimation is crucial mainly
for harvesting, logistics, and production planning. Manual esti-
mation of yield is time-consuming, labour-intensive, and error-
prone. Many vision-based systems were proposed to overcome the
limitations due to the manual mechanisms; therefore, complex
systems that depend on costly sensors have been adapted.
However, despite the use of expensive hardware, these systems
failed to deliver promising results, also limiting their practical
use.
This paper proposes a pipeline that focuses on maintaining
simplicity, eliminating the use of overpriced hardware, and
also succeeds in delivering accurate yield estimation. This was
achieved using the Wine Grape Instance Segmentation Dataset
(WGISD). The WGISD dataset undergoes proper data prepro-
cessing using CLAHE and denoising to improve image quality
under varying illumination, which is then followed by grape
bunch detection using the Faster R-CNN, where berry counts
were refined using a CNN-based regression model optimized with
Huber loss, achieving an accuracy of 94.7%. A YOLOv8 was
also evaluated, which achieved an mAP@50 of 89.1%, showing
reduced robustness under occlusion.
In addition, a MINN model was introduced, combining visual
and cluster-level features, which achieved the best overall perfor-
mance, gaining an accuracy of 95.8%, proving that a simplified
model, with proper data preprocessing, can deliver reliable yield
estimation for real-world vineyard applications.
I. INTRODUCTION
Accurate grape yield estimation is a crucial factor in
vineyard management as it affects harvest planning, labour
allocation, and post-harvest logistics. Accurate estimation
helps reduce uncertainty and also provides clear insights into
production planning. However, estimation under real vineyard
conditions poses a challenge due to factors such as dense
foliage, variable lighting, and overlapping grape clusters.
In earlier times, yield estimation was done manually, that
is, through manual field sampling, where workers inspected
each grape cluster. While the traditional methods seem theoret-
ically simple, they are time-consuming, labour-intensive, and

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
Early work on vineyard yield estimation relied on classi-
cal image processing and sensing techniques. Hand-crafted
features such as colour, shape, and texture were commonly

used to identify grape clusters. Some studies also explored
non-visual sensing approaches, including microwave antennas
and millimetre-wave radar, to estimate yield indirectly [4], [6].
While these methods provided initial insights into automated
yield monitoring, they often produced high error rates under
real vineyard conditions and required costly hardware, limiting
their scalability.
With advances in machine learning, more flexible ap-
proaches were introduced. Early learning-based methods com-
bined handcrafted features with classifiers such as Support
Vector Machines, achieving moderate accuracy but limited
robustness in complex vineyard environments [4]. Subsequent
research shifted toward deep learning, particularly Convolu-
tional Neural Networks (CNNs) and object detection frame-
works. Models such as Mask R-CNN demonstrated improved
grape cluster detection and temporal consistency when applied
to vineyard imagery [7]. Similarly, video-based inflorescence
detection and counting approaches reported yield prediction
errors between 4-11% [8], highlighting the potential of deep
learning to reduce manual effort.
Later studies focused on yield estimation closer to harvest,
using robotic platforms equipped with RGB-D cameras to
measure grape cluster dimensions [9]. Although accurate,
these systems relied on specialised sensors and robotic hard-
ware, making them less practical for large-scale deployment.
Alternative sensing techniques, such as radar-based systems,
were also explored to address occlusion challenges [5], [6], but
their operational complexity and cost further limited adoption.
More recently, end-to-end object detectors such as the
YOLO family have been applied to vineyard monitoring tasks
[11]. YOLO models enable real-time detection and are suit-
able for field deployment. However, despite improvements in
YOLOv8, challenges remain in handling dense grape clusters
and fine-grained berry-level details under occlusion. In con-
trast, multi-stage detectors like Faster R-CNN provide better
localisation accuracy, though at a higher computational cost.
Overall, existing methods either rely on expensive sens-
ing equipment or struggle under dense canopy conditions.
Moreover, most studies focus on cluster-level detection, with
limited emphasis on berry-level yield estimation. Very few
works explore combining visual features with cluster-level
numerical information. To address these gaps, the present work
proposes a simplified vision-based framework that compares
Faster R-CNN for reliable cluster detection, YOLOv8 for real-
time evaluation, and a Multi-Input Neural Network (MINN)
for more stable and accurate berry-level yield estimation.
III. METHODOLOGY
The proposed framework follows a streamlined pipeline,
which consists of image preprocessing and data augmentation,
followed by grape cluster detection, tracking, berry counting,
and yield refinement. The design ensures that the limitations
of prior work, such as minimal preprocessing or complex
architectures, are overcome.
A. Dataset and Preprocessing
The experiments in this work were conducted on the Wine
Grape Instance Segmentation Dataset (WGISD), which con-
tains RGB vineyard images along with grape cluster annota-
tions in JSON format.
To overcome illumination variation and background noise
that is commonly observed in vineyard scenes, Contrast Lim-
ited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation (CLAHE) was used
to enhance local contrast, followed by Non-Local Means
denoising to reduce noise while preserving important image
details. Along with these, the images were then resized to
512x512 pixels to maintain consistent input dimensions. These
steps improved the visibility of grape clusters and supported
more reliable detection and counting.

C. Cluster Detection and Tracking
Grape cluster detection was carried out using a Faster R-
CNN model with a ResNet-50 backbone and Feature Pyramid
Network (FPN). The model was initialised with COCO pre-
trained weights and fine-tuned on the WGISD dataset to learn
grape-specific features. Detected regions were classified into
two classes: grape clusters and background.
To ensure consistent cluster identification across frames
and avoid duplicate counting, the Simple Online and Real-
time Tracking (SORT) algorithm was employed. SORT uses
Kalman filtering and IoU-based matching to assign persistent
IDs to detected clusters, improving the reliability of yield
estimation.
For comparison, YOLOv8 was also evaluated as a single-
stage detector. Unlike Faster R-CNN, YOLOv8 performs de-
tection in a single forward pass, resulting in faster inference
and lower computational cost. The model was trained using the
same preprocessing and augmentation pipeline to ensure a fair
comparison. While YOLOv8 demonstrated strong real-time
performance, its precision was reduced in scenes with dense
clusters and occlusion, highlighting the trade-off between
speed and localisation accuracy.
D. Berry Counting Using CNN Regression
To estimate yield at the berry level, a CNN-based regression
model was used. Instead of detecting individual berries, which
is difficult due to heavy occlusion and overlapping berries, the
problem was treated as a regression task. Cropped images of
detected grape clusters were resized and provided as input
to the CNN, which learned visual patterns related to berry
density and texture. The model directly predicted the total
number of berries per cluster, avoiding the complexity of
instance-level berry detection. This approach resulted in more
stable predictions and improved yield estimation compared to
detection-based counting methods.
E. Robust Yield Estimation Using Huber Regression
To further improve berry count predictions, Huber regres-
sion was applied as a refinement step. The Huber loss com-
bines the properties of mean squared error and mean absolute
error, making it less sensitive to outliers than standard regres-
sion losses. This helps reduce the impact of noisy predictions
caused by variations in berry size, lighting conditions, and
partial occlusions.
By limiting the influence of extreme errors, the use of
Huber loss produced more stable and consistent berry count
estimates, particularly in challenging vineyard scenes.
F. Berry Counting Using Multi-Input Neural Network (MINN)
To further improve berry-level yield estimation, a Multi-
Input Neural Network (MINN) was employed to combine
visual and numerical information. One input branch processes
cropped grape cluster images using a CNN to extract visual
features related to berry density and texture. The second
branch uses numerical features obtained from the detection
stage, namely the estimated berry count from the CNN-based
regression model and the bounding box area of the detected
cluster.
The outputs from both branches are concatenated and
passed through fully connected layers to learn the relationship
between visual appearance and cluster-level attributes. This
fusion helps the model produce more stable and consistent
predictions, particularly under dense occlusion where visual
cues alone may be insufficient.
MINN achieved the highest overall accuracy of 95.8%,
outperforming Faster R-CNN with Huber refinement and
YOLOv8, demonstrating that combining multiple input
sources leads to more reliable vineyard yield estimation.
G. Training and Testing
All experiments were conducted on the WGISD dataset.
Images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels and split into training,
validation, and test sets using an 80:20 ratio. Preprocessing and
Faster R-CNN with a ResNet-5O FPN backbone was ini-
tialised using COCO pre-trained weights and fine-tuned on
vineyard images. Detected clusters were passed to a CNN-
based regression model with Huber loss, followed by evalua-
tion using the proposed MINN.
Models were trained using mini-batch optimisation and
evaluated on a held-out test set. Identical training conditions
were maintained across all models to ensure fair comparison.

VI. CONCLUSION
This work presents a deep learning framework for vineyard
yield estimation that involves image preprocessing, grape
cluster detection, and regression-based berry counting. The
proposed pipeline addresses issues concerning occlusion, light-
ing variations, and dense grape clusters.
Experiments conducted on the WGISD dataset showed
strong results across all the evaluated models. Faster R-CNN
achieved an accuracy of 94.7%, while YOLOv8, used for real-
time inference, achieved an mAP@50 of 89.1%. The proposed
Multi-Input Neural Network (MINN) outperformed the pre-
ceding models, achieving the best overall performance with
an accuracy of 95.8% and stable berry-level yield prediction.
These results demonstrate that combining lightweight prepro-
cessing with multi-input learning offers a reliable and practical
alternative to complex pipelines with expensive hardware for
yield estimation.
VII. LIMITATIONS
The experiments conducted in this study were solely done
on the WGISD dataset, which covers limited grape varieties,
canopy types, and seasons. As a result, generalisation to other
regions, such as different lighting, vineyard layouts, or varying
environmental conditions, may be reduced. Evaluating the
framework on more diverse datasets could help in providing
deeper insights into its robustness and adaptability.
Although the proposed framework relies only on RGB
images and lightweight preprocessing, making it cost-effective
and scalable, the design may still limit performance under
extreme lighting or occlusion scenarios compared to multi-
sensor systems.
VIII. FUTURE WORK
Although the proposed framework showed promising re-
sults, the work can be further explored by training and evaluat-
ing the models on larger and more diverse datasets that include
different grape varieties, seasonal conditions, and vineyard
structures, overall improving the generalisation ability across
varying conditions. Another extension is exploring time-series
yield estimation using UAV or in-field images captured at
multiple growth stages, which enables yield trend prediction
over an entire season.
Future research may also include optimising models for
real-time deployment, reducing computational cost for edge
devices or vineyard robots. Additionally, combining vision-
based methods with sensing modalities such as LiDAR or
hyperspectral imaging could help handle extreme occlusion
and illumination. Finally, the proposed approach may be
adapted to other crops that exhibit similar challenges related
to occlusion and dense clustering, extending its applicability
beyond viticulture.
