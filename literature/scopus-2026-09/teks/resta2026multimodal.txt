ABSTRACT
This work presents a multimodal radar-vision framework for non-destructive ripeness
classification of oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) under field conditions. A continuous wave 2.4 GHz radar
and a digital scale capture backscattered power and bunch mass as internal cues related to dielectric properties
and growth, while RGB images provide external color and texture information. Radar-weight features
(power, weight, and power-to-weight ratio) are modeled using an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)
classifier, and a YOLOv8-Nano detector is trained using an expert-annotated RGB dataset to localize and
classify bunches into four maturity stages (Unripe, Underripe, Ripe, Overripe). Decision-level late fusion
combines the class-probability outputs of both branches through mean, weighted, and stacking schemes.
Experiments on radar-weight data (255 samples per class) acquired at a commercial loading ramp show that
the unimodal radar-weight and vision models reach 89.9% and 94.3% accuracy, respectively, with YOLOv8-
Nano achieving mAP@0.5 of 0.99. The best fusion configuration, based on stacking, attains 96.9% accuracy
and 97.3% F1-macro, demonstrating that radar-weight information provides complementary evidence that
stabilizes vision-based grading and supports more consistent harvest decisions.

I. INTRODUCTION
Indonesia possesses a well-established palm oil industry, with
crude palm oil (CPO) serving as one of the nation’s most
significant export commodities and a substantial contributor
to the national economy [1]. However, the overall productiv-
ity and quality of palm oil are strongly influenced by how
accurately the ripeness of oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs)
is assessed before harvest. In many plantations, workers
still determine harvest readiness by visually inspecting
the color of the fruitlets and counting the loose fruits
that have fallen to the ground. This manual method is

labor-intensive and highly dependent on the worker’s expe-
rience. As a result, grading can be inconsistent and may
lead to suboptimal harvest timing and reduced oil extraction

The free fatty acid (FFA) content and the amount of oil
obtained from palm fruit are strongly influenced by maturity
stage and post-harvest handling [3]. Bunches harvested too
early tend to produce less oil, whereas overripe bunches
are more prone to elevated FFA levels due to enzymatic
degradation in the mesocarp [4], [5]. A method that can
estimate FFB ripeness more objectively and rapidly is,
therefore, needed to help keep oil quality within specification
and to support gradual automation of harvesting and grading
operations [6].

Image-based approaches have been widely explored to
address this need. Early studies relied on RGB image
processing and simple statistical features to perform color-
based grading of bunches [7], [8]. Later, convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) and object-detection frameworks such as
YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 were applied to detect and classify
fruits directly from field imagery in near real-time [9], [10],
[11]. These methods can reach high accuracy and have
been implemented on mobile or embedded platforms for
autonomous fruit monitoring. However, their performance
is still sensitive to changes in illumination, occlusions, and
background clutter. Tan et al. [12] conducted their imaging
experiments in a dedicated chamber with carefully controlled
lighting to ensure a consistent visual appearance of the
fruit. This reliance on controlled illumination indicates that
vision-based approaches can be strongly affected by lighting
variability, so their predictions may become less stable under
typical outdoor plantation conditions.
Microwave and radar-based sensing offer a complementary
way to assess the ripeness of oil palm fruit. During
ripening, the mesocarp composition changes as the relative
fractions of oil, water, and kernel evolve with maturity [13].
At microwave frequencies, liquid water exhibits a much
higher relative permittivity and dielectric loss than vegetable
oils such as palm oil [14], [15]. Prior work has shown that
free-space microwave measurements can capture ripeness-
dependent variations in the transmission coefficient of oil
palm fruits that are associated with changes in moisture
content, oil content, and free fatty acid levels [16]. These
observations suggest that radar measurements can be used as
a non-contact dielectric probe for internal characterization of
fruit maturity, while remaining insensitive to ambient lighting
conditions.
Based on these insights, this work proposes a multimodal
ripeness classification framework that combines radar-
derived features with computer-vision outputs. A compact
microwave radar is used to capture non-contact measure-
ments related to the internal dielectric response and mass
of each bunch, while RGB images are processed to capture
external visual cues. The two information sources are then
combined through late fusion strategies to improve the
reliability of FFB ripeness classification into four maturity
classes (Unripe, Underripe, Ripe, and Overripe). The result-
ing framework is intended as a step toward practical tools
for precision agriculture and semi-autonomous harvesting
in oil palm plantations. The contributions of this paper lie
in the end-to-end design and experimental validation of a
practical multimodal ripeness-assessment system, leveraging
established radar sensing, imaging, and machine-learning
components in a realistic plantation setting.
II. RELATED WORK
A range of non-destructive sensing and machine-learning
techniques has been proposed for automated grading of
oil palm FFBs. Representative methods are summarized
in Table 1. Existing studies can be grouped into three
main categories: vision-based approaches, dielectric-based
approaches, and multi-sensor fusion. Each group offers
particular strengths but also exhibits limitations, which
motivates the use of hybrid multimodal systems.
A. VISION-BASED CLASSIFICATION
Vision-based approaches infer FFB maturity by analyzing
surface color and texture patterns. Earlier studies predom-
inantly employed handcrafted descriptors computed from
RGB images. For example, Pamornnak et al. [8] proposed
a mobile image-analysis application that calculates color
moments to approximate oil content, offering a compact
feature-based solution that can be deployed on portable
devices.
With the advancement of deep learning, object detection
frameworks based on the YOLO family have become increas-
ingly popular for agricultural inspection tasks. Suharjito et al.
[9] implemented a smartphone-based grading system using a
modified YOLOv4 detector and reported mean average pre-
cision (mAP) above 95% under daylight conditions, demon-
strating the feasibility of deploying lightweight detection
models on resource-constrained mobile platforms. Beyond
application-specific implementations, recent methodological
developments have focused on enhancing YOLO architec-
tures through attention mechanisms and transformer-based
modules while maintaining computational efficiency. For
instance, TGC-YOLOv5 integrates transformer blocks and
coordinate attention modules to improve detection accu-
racy without significantly increasing complexity [17]. Such
efforts reflect a broader research trend toward balancing
accuracy and efficiency in embedded visual detection
systems.
Other vision-based ripeness assessment methods have also
been explored. Hazir et al. [18] used fluorescence measure-
ments to derive anthocyanin and flavonoid indices, followed
by stochastic gradient boosting classification, achieving a
maximum accuracy of 87.7%. Rosbi et al. [19] applied fuzzy
C-means segmentation and texture feature extraction prior
to conventional classification, reporting 93.68% accuracy on
outdoor images.
Overall, vision-based techniques can perform well when
lighting and viewpoint are reasonably controlled. However,
their accuracy remains sensitive to changes in illumination,
occlusions, and heterogeneous fruit surface appearance.
In addition, RGB images mainly capture external visual cues
and do not directly reflect the internal moisture and oil
composition that determine ripeness.
B. DIELECTRIC-BASED CLASSIFICATION
Dielectric and microwave-based methods aim to probe
internal fruit composition by measuring the electromagnetic
properties of the mesocarp and related tissues. Earlier studies
reported strong relationships between electrical parameters
such as resistivity and permittivity and the moisture content

Rahmawati et al. [16] proposed a non-destructive free-

vector network analyzer operated in the 9-12 GHz band. Their
experiments showed that the S21 transmission responses
differed systematically between unripe, ripe, and overripe
fruits, which they attributed to shifts in internal moisture, oil
content, and free fatty acid formation. Similar trends have
been reported in other microwave studies: materials related
to palm oil generally behave as relatively low-loss dielectrics,
whereas liquid water exhibits much higher permittivity and
dielectric loss [14], [20]. The difference between water-
rich and oil-rich regions explains why microwave and radar
measurements can be used to track changes in moisture and
oil content during ripening.
From a radar sensing perspective, the received backscat-
tered power is governed by the reflection coefficient at
the air-fruit interface, whose magnitude depends on the
effective relative permittivity of the fruit tissue. The dielectric
contrast between air and fruit determines the strength of this
reflection. At microwave frequencies around 2.4 GHz, water-
rich biological tissues exhibit high relative permittivity due
to dipolar relaxation of water molecules [21], whereas oil-
dominated materials exhibit substantially lower permittivity
values, typically in the range of 2-3 [14].
During ripening, the gradual decrease in moisture con-
tent and corresponding increase in oil content reduce
the effective permittivity of the mesocarp. This reduction
decreases the dielectric contrast at the air-fruit interface and
consequently lowers the reflected radar power. Although
absolute dielectric parameters are not directly measured in
this study, this sensitivity-based interpretation helps explain
the general trend in reflected power observed across ripeness
stages.
It is important to note that dielectric properties of
biological tissues are inherently frequency-dependent [21]
The present study employs a single-frequency 2.4 GHz
continuous-wave radar and therefore does not explicitly
characterize dielectric dispersion across bands. However,
within the microwave regime, water remains the dom-
inant contributor to dielectric contrast relative to lipid
components, which exhibit comparatively low permittivity
values [14]. Consequently, while the absolute magnitude
and sensitivity of the backscattered response may vary
with operating frequency, the qualitative monotonic trend
associated with the moisture-to-oil transition during ripening
is expected to remain preserved across typical microwave
bands.
Dielectric studies operating in the 9-12 GHz range
are primarily intended for laboratory-scale characterization
and typically involve small samples, thin fruit sections,
or controlled free-space measurement setups. At higher
microwave frequencies, increased dielectric loss in moisture-
rich biological tissues leads to stronger electromagnetic
attenuation and reduced penetration depth, causing the
measured response to be dominated by near-surface lay-
ers [21]. While this characteristic is advantageous for precise
dielectric spectroscopy, it limits the suitability of such
frequencies for sensing the bulk internal properties of large
and heterogeneous targets such as intact oil palm fresh fruit
bunches. For whole-bunch sensing under field conditions,
lower microwave frequencies provide improved volumetric
penetration into the fruit and yield responses that are more

C. MULTI-SENSOR AND FUSION APPROACHES
An increasing number of studies has investigated multimodal
sensing to make fruit grading more robust under varying
field conditions. Pamornnak et al. [22] designed a fast
grading system that jointly extracts features from RGB,
near-infrared (NIR), and depth images to assess oil palm
fruit quality and ripeness, showing how multi-channel visual
information can enhance discrimination. Building on this
idea, Pipitsunthonsan et al. [23] introduced a multi-input
convolutional neural network (CNN) that fuses RGB camera
images, infrared sensor signals, and load-cell measurements;
their model achieved an accuracy of about 90.26% on field
data, highlighting the benefit of combining color, texture,
infrared response, and mass. On the dataset side, Goh et al.
[24] released a multimodal field dataset comprising RGB,
depth, and point-cloud measurements for FFB classifica-
tion and localization, which supports systematic evaluation
of both early- and late-fusion strategies under realistic
variability.

While vision-based methods provide strong surface-level
discrimination, they remain sensitive to illumination variabil-
ity, occlusion, and appearance ambiguity near transitional
ripeness stages. In contrast, dielectric and microwave-
based approaches directly probe internal physicochemical
properties related to moisture and oil content but often
lack spatial and contextual information and are typically
evaluated under laboratory-scale conditions. Recent multi-
modal systems attempt to combine complementary sensing
modalities to enhance robustness; however, such approaches
may introduce increased hardware complexity, higher com-
putational demand, or limited portability in field deploy-
ment. These trade-offs highlight the need for a practically
deployable multimodal framework that balances internal
sensing capability, visual discrimination, and embedded
feasibility.
Building on prior FFB grading studies, we present
a radar–vision sensing prototype and late-fusion pipeline
for four-class ripeness assessment, and evaluate it under
plantation-style acquisition conditions.
III. METHODOLOGY
This section presents the design of the multimodal ripeness-
classification system for oil palm FFBs. The system has
two branches: a radar-weight branch modeled with extreme
gradient boosting (XGBoost) and a vision branch that
applies YOLOv8-Nano to RGB images. Each branch outputs
class probabilities for the four ripeness levels, and these
probabilities are later combined using late-fusion to obtain
the final prediction. Figure 1 shows the overall workflow,
including the sensor modules, processing steps, learning
models, and decision stage.
A. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
The proposed system is divided into four parts: sensing,
processing, modeling, and fusion. In the sensing part,
an SDR-based radar, a digital scale, and a camera collect
measurements from each FFB. The processing part converts
the radar and weight readings into tabular features, and
the modeling part uses XGBoost and YOLOv8-Nano to
produce class probabilities for both the radar-weight and
vision branches. In the final step, the fusion part combines
these probabilities to obtain a single ripeness label for each
sample.
1) SENSING STAGE
The hardware platform comprises three input sources: (i) a
continuous wave radar module operating at 2.4 GHz, (ii) a
digital scale, and (iii) an RGB camera (webcam). The radar
measures the backscattered signal from the FFB and provides
information related to its internal dielectric properties. The
digital scale records the mass of each bunch, and the camera
captures external visual appearance such as fruitlet color and
surface texture.
2) PROCESSING STAGE
Data from the radar and digital scale are transformed
into tabular features, namely the average radar power
magnitude in decibel (dB), the bunch weight in kilograms,
and a normalized power-to-weight ratio. These features
form the input to the XGBoost classifier. The RGB images
are preprocessed and directly fed to the YOLOv8-Nano
model, which learns visual patterns related to ripeness,
such as changes in fruitlet color and the presence of loose
fruits.

3) MODELING STAGE
The tabular and visual features are handled by two separate
classifiers. The radar-weight features are modeled using
XGBoost, which outputs a probability distribution over the
four ripeness classes for each sample. In parallel, YOLOv8-
Nano processes each image and returns bounding boxes,
class labels, and associated probabilities for the same set
of classes.
4) FUSION AND DECISION STAGE
At the decision stage, three decision-level late-fusion strate-
gies are investigated to combine the class-probability vectors
from the radar-weight and vision branches. In mean and
weighted averaging, the two probability vectors are merged
directly, with the weighted scheme assigning different
importance to each modality. In the stacking approach, the
concatenated probability vectors are used as inputs to a
meta-classifier that learns how to exploit complementary
evidence from both branches. In all cases, the fused
probabilities are finally used to assign the ripeness label for
each FFB.

C. RADAR AND WEIGHT FEATURE EXTRACTION
The radar-sensing unit employs an ADALM-Pluto SDR
covering 325 MHz-3.8 GHz. In the experimental configura-
tion, the device operates at a center frequency of 2.4 GHz
with a 1 MS/s sampling rate and a buffer size of 1024.
A continuous-wave excitation is directed toward the FFB, and
the backscattered response is sampled at baseband, encoding
variations related to the internal moisture and oil content of
the bunch.
For each measurement, the radar module records the
backscattered signal from a single fruit bunch. This time-
domain sequence is transformed to the frequency domain
using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) [27]. The magnitude of
the resulting spectrum around the transmitted carrier is then
converted to a logarithmic scale and expressed in decibels
(dB) [28]. The mean value of these spectral power samples
is used as the radar feature, because it summarizes the overall
backscattered power and changes with the dielectric contrast
of the fruit.
Each FFB was also weighed using a digital scale to obtain
its mass in kilograms. Because ripening affects fruit density
and oil concentration, the weight provides a complementary
physical attribute to the radar measurements. To reduce the
influence of size variation among bunches, a normalized
feature was derived by computing the ratio between the
average radar power magnitude and the corresponding bunch
weight, as expressed in (5):

E. DATA FUSION STRATEGY
To integrate complementary information from the radar-
weight and vision branches, fusion is performed at the
probability level. Each modality independently produces a
four dimensional class-probability vector corresponding to
the four ripeness categories. Three decision-level late fusion
schemes are considered: mean fusion, weighted fusion, and
stacking fusion.
In mean fusion, the fused probability vector is obtained
by averaging the radar-weight and vision probability vectors
element-wise, assigning equal importance to both modal-
ities. In weighted fusion, the class-posterior vectors from
the radar-weight and vision branches are combined using
modality-specific weights. The radar-weight coefficient,
αradar, is selected in a data-driven and reproducible manner
via a validation-based grid search performed independently
in each outer cross-validation fold. Specifically, αradar is
swept from 0 to 1 with a step size of 0.01, and the optimal
value is chosen by minimizing a validation objective on the
fold’s tuning subset from the non-held-out folds, with macro-
averaged F1-score used as a tie-breaker. The vision weight is
defined as 1 −αradar. The sensitivity of weighted fusion to
αradar is examined in Sec. V.
For stacking fusion, a more flexible meta-classifier is
trained on paired class-probability vectors from the radar-
weight and vision branches. For each paired sample, the
radar-weight and vision probability vectors are concatenated
to form a single feature vector, which is then fed to a
logistic regression classifier that produces the final fused
probabilities, as in (6) [30]:
yfusion = softmax (WZ + b)
(6)
where Z represents the concatenated probability vector, W is
a learnable weight matrix that determines the contribution of
each modality and class, and b is a bias vector that adjusts
the decision boundary. The softmax function ensures that
the resulting vector yfusion forms a normalized probability
distribution across all ripeness classes. This stacking scheme
allows the meta-classifier to exploit systematic patterns in
how the two modalities agree or disagree, beyond what can
be captured by simple averaging.
To mitigate information leakage in stacking fusion, the
meta-classifier was evaluated using stratified five-fold cross-
validation with an explicit out-of-fold (OOF) protocol. The
vision branch (YOLOv8-Nano) was trained once using the
fixed train/validation/test split and then kept fixed, serving
as a fixed probability generator during fusion evaluation,
while the radar-weight branch (XGBoost) provides fold-wise
probability outputs under stratified five-fold cross-validation.
For each fold in the fusion evaluation, the meta-classifier
was trained exclusively on the four training folds using
concatenated class-probability outputs from both branches,
and fusion predictions were generated only for the held-out
fold. This ensures that each fusion prediction is produced by
a meta-classifier that has not been trained on that sample,
preventing information leakage at the fusion stage.
Although feature-level early fusion and model-level
middle fusion represent alternative multimodal integration
strategies, they were not adopted in this study due to
structural and data-related considerations. Early fusion would
require concatenating low-dimensional radar-weight features
with high-dimensional visual representations, necessitating
additional cross-modal normalization and joint retraining
of the vision network, which would substantially increase
model complexity. Model-level middle fusion would require
defining a shared intermediate representation and performing
tightly coupled end-to-end training between radar and
vision encoders. Given the limited dataset size and the
heterogeneous nature of the two modalities, such tightly
integrated approaches may introduce optimization instability
and overfitting risks. In contrast, decision-level late fusion
provides a modular and robust integration mechanism by
combining modality outputs within a common probability
space [31]. This choice is consistent with prior comparative
analyses indicating that late fusion tends to offer more stable
performance than early fusion when modalities differ sub-
stantially in feature dimensionality and training requirements.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
This section describes the experimental configuration used
to validate the proposed multimodal ripeness classification
system. It describes the sensing hardware, the FFB data
collection procedure, and the resulting dataset composition.
Because the system was developed in multiple stages,
a distinction is made between the initial setup used for dataset
acquisition and the final integrated prototype.
A. HARDWARE SETUP
The hardware system was designed to capture three types
of data from each FFB: radar backscatter, RGB images, and
bunch weight. A continuous wave radar module operating at
2.4 GHz measured the backscattered signal from the bunch to
sense internal dielectric changes, a camera unit captured RGB
images to record external visual cues, such as fruitlet color
and surface texture, and a digital scale recorded the weight of
each bunch as an indicator of its mass and overall size. In the
subsequent analysis, the radar backscatter and bunch weight
are treated jointly as a radar-weight modality, since they are
always acquired together and processed by the same tabular
classifier, whereas the RGB images constitute the vision
modality. The frequency selection is guided by application
constraints for whole-bunch sensing, and the trade-offs are
discussed in Section II-B.

For the primary Tenera dataset, FFB samples were collected
at a commercial loading ramp that receives bunches from
multiple estates using a continuous-wave radar, a digital

a quiet corner of the ramp to reduce motion and clutter
from nearby activity while still representing normal field
operation. The radar operated at 2.4 GHz with separate
transmit and receive antennas connected to the SDR. Both
antennas were mounted side by side and aimed toward the
bunch at a stand-off distance of approximately 10 cm from
the front surface of the fruit, and this configuration was kept
fixed for all measurements.
FIGURE 4. Multimodal data acquisition workflow for each oil palm fresh

Before recording the FFB measurements, a background-
based calibration of the radar channel was performed to
mitigate static reflections and constant receiver baseline
offsets. With no bunch placed in front of the antennas,
a short segment of the baseband I-signal was recorded
and processed using the same FFT-based pipeline as in
the subsequent measurements. The resulting spectral power
over the frequency bins of interest was then converted to
the dB scale to obtain a background reference level. For
each FFB measurement, the spectral power was computed
and converted to dB in the same manner, and background
compensation was applied by subtracting the background
reference in the dB domain. Accordingly, the compen-
sated radar feature represents the relative (background-
suppressed) scattering contribution of the fruit bunch, while
reducing fixed clutter from the environment and receiver
offsets.
This calibration procedure mitigates the influence of static
environmental factors, such as nearby metallic structures,
antenna coupling, and stationary reflectors that remain
constant throughout the measurement session. During data
collection, ambient temperature variations remained within
typical outdoor ranges and are not expected to introduce
significant variation in SDR front-end response or tissue
dielectric behavior within the duration of each measurement
session. Surface moisture was observed to influence the
absolute magnitude of the backscattered signal; however,
this effect predominantly introduces a scale shift and does
not alter the relative separation trends between ripeness
classes. Furthermore, the use of background subtraction and
relative power-based features reduces sensitivity to residual
environmental variations.
The three sensing measurements were then acquired
sequentially within a single handling session. First, the bunch
was placed on a digital scale and its mass (kg) was recorded.
Next, an RGB image of the same FFB was captured using
a fixed camera arrangement under natural lighting. Finally,
a single radar measurement was acquired for each FFB; each
recording lasted approximately 5 s, which was sufficient to
obtain a stable estimate of the backscattered power while
keeping the acquisition time per sample practical for offline
processing. The overall acquisition workflow is summarized
in Figure 4, which illustrates how radar, weight, and image
measurements are collected for each FFB in an integrated
manner.
During data collection, trained field technicians visually
graded each FFB into one of four ripeness classes using
surface color and fruitlet condition as the main criteria.
The assigned label was recorded together with the corre-
sponding radar, weight, and image measurements for that
bunch.

information about the ripeness state.

D. TRAINING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS
All models were evaluated under a consistent study design,
with modality-specific dataset partitions and reporting pro-
tocols described below. Each modality was first trained
separately to evaluate its ability to discriminate between
ripeness classes before multimodal fusion was applied. The
next subsections summarize the training settings, dataset
splits, and evaluation metrics used for each model. The
datasets were partitioned in a way that matched the prediction
output of each model.
1) RADAR-WEIGHT (XGBOOST)
The tabular radar-weight dataset was evaluated using strati-
fied five-fold cross-validation. In each fold, the model was
trained on four folds (80% of the samples) and evaluated on
the remaining fold (20%). The input feature vector consisted
of (i) average radar power magnitude (dB), (ii) bunch weight
(kg), and (iii) normalized power-to-weight ratio (dB/kg).
Hyperparameters were tuned using stratified five-fold
cross-validation on the radar-weight dataset via a grid search
over maximum tree depth {3, 5, 7}, learning rate {0.1,
0.2}, number of estimators {100, 200}, and subsample
ratio {0.7, 0.9}. In each split, the model was trained on
four folds and evaluated on the held-out fold. This process
was repeated such that each fold served once as the held-
out fold. The configuration with the highest mean macro
F1-score across the five held-out folds was selected. All
reported accuracy and F1-score values for the radar-weight
branch were computed by aggregating the out-of-fold predic-
tions from the held-out folds, ensuring that each sample is
predicted exactly once by a model that was not trained on
that sample. The overall confusion matrix was constructed
by aggregating these held-out (out-of-fold) predictions across
all splits.
2) VISION (YOLOV8-NANO).
The image dataset was partitioned at the image level into
70% training, 10% validation, and 20% testing subsets.
Each image was assigned exclusively to one subset to
prevent overlap between training, validation, and testing data.
Model performance was evaluated solely on the held-out
test set.
All images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels. During
training, online data augmentation was applied using standard
geometric and photometric transforms (random horizon-
tal flips, rotation, scaling, shear, and brightness/saturation
adjustments) to improve robustness to viewpoint and illu-
mination variation at the loading ramp. YOLOv8-Nano was
trained for 50 epochs with batch size 16 using the Adam
optimizer and a cosine learning-rate schedule initialized at
0.01. Cross-validation was not applied to the visual model
due to its higher computational cost and common practice
in object detection benchmarks, where fixed train-validation-
test splits are typically adopted.
3) EVALUATION METRICS
For classification outputs from the radar-weight branch and
the late fusion models, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and
macro-averaged F1-score are reported. The macro-averaged
scores assign equal importance to each ripeness category,
providing a balanced view of performance across classes.
For the vision model, mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 are
additionally reported to quantify detection and localization
performance. For the radar-weight and fusion models, all
metrics correspond to averages over the validation folds
in the stratified 5-fold cross-validation. For the vision
model, all metrics are computed on the held-out test
set. The experimental results for the unimodal baselines
and the proposed late fusion strategies are presented
in Section V.
V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
This section presents the experimental results obtained
with the proposed multimodal framework. The analysis is
divided into four parts: (i) preliminary validation of the
radar response using reference materials, (ii) unimodal radar-
weight classification with XGBoost, (iii) unimodal vision-
based classification with YOLOv8-Nano, and (iv) late fusion
of both modalities. Unless stated otherwise, performance
figures for the radar-weight and fusion models correspond to
averages over the stratified 5-fold cross-validation, whereas

B. UNIMODAL RADAR-WEIGHT RESULTS
The unimodal radar-weight experiment evaluates how effec-
tively the combination of radar power magnitude and bunch
weight can represent the ripeness level of FFB. Radar
power reflects the dielectric response linked to moisture
and oil content, while bunch weight describes the physical
development of the bunch. Both evolve during maturation,
so together they form a compact description of the internal
state of the fruit.
The analysis is presented in two steps. First, the distribu-
tions of the radar-weight features are examined to understand
their physical behavior across ripeness classes. Second,
several classifiers are trained to quantify how well these
features can separate the four categories.
The results indicate that radar-weight information already
carries clear ripeness-related patterns. This single-modality
performance later serves as a baseline and physical reference
for the multimodal fusion experiments.

2) INFERENCE LATENCY AND COMPUTATIONAL
COMPLEXITY
To assess the deployment performance of the proposed
multimodal system, inference latency was measured on a
Raspberry Pi 5 equipped with a Quad-core Arm Cortex-A76
CPU (2.4 GHz) and 8 GB RAM using a headless python
deployment script. All measurements were conducted using
pretrained models, and sensor acquisition was excluded to
isolate computational overhead.
The radar-weight branch based on XGBoost incurs a mean
inference latency of 3.42 ms per sample. The stacking-based
fusion stage introduces a negligible additional overhead of
0.37 ms. In contrast, the vision branch implemented using
YOLOv8-Nano constitutes the dominant computational cost,
with a mean inference time of 333.4 ms per image. These
results indicate that the fusion stage does not represent a
computational bottleneck, and that overall system latency is
primarily determined by visual processing.
From a computational complexity perspective, both the
radar-weight and fusion branches operate on fixed-size, low-
dimensional feature vectors, resulting in bounded inference
cost independent of input resolution or environmental condi-
tions. The vision branch employs a lightweight convolutional
neural network with fixed input resolution, leading to
constant per-sample complexity with higher computational
demand. Overall, the proposed system remains compatible
with near-real-time decision making on embedded platforms
under practical deployment constraints.
From an operational standpoint, the proposed system
targets single-bunch, decision-based inspection rather than
continuous high-frame-rate video processing. In typical
plantation loading-ramp scenarios, fresh fruit bunches
are static or semi-static, and per-sample inference on
the order of hundreds of milliseconds is operationally
acceptable.
Deployment experiments on a Raspberry Pi-class platform
confirm the computational feasibility of the multimodal
algorithm at the inference level. The radar-weight, vision,
and fusion modules are independently trained and interact

3) PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS
The experimental results suggest that the multimodal radar-
vision system can be used to assist grading of oil palm
fresh fruit bunches at loading ramps or mill intake points.
By combining radar-weight measurements and RGB images,
the fusion model produces ripeness estimates that are more
stable than those from any single sensor, so fewer underripe
and overripe bunches are likely to enter the production line.
Fewer grading errors can help keep oil quality more uniform
and improve extraction efficiency, while also reducing the
dependence on fully manual visual checks. The use of
low-cost SDR hardware and a compact YOLOv8-based
detector means that the system is compatible with embedded
platforms and can be adapted for near real-time operation in
plantation or mill environments.
E. CROSS-VARIETAL AND ENVIRONMENT ROBUSTNESS
ANALYSIS
To provide preliminary insight into cross-varietal robustness,
we performed an auxiliary external evaluation on Dura
FFBs collected from a different plantation environment. The
radar-weight XGBoost model was trained and tuned on
the Tenera dataset, and then applied to the Dura samples
without retraining. On this external Dura evaluation set,
the model achieved an accuracy of 0.857 and an F1-macro
of 0.858. Relative to the Tenera results, this indicates a
moderate performance degradation, consistent with increased
inter-class overlap induced by varietal and plantation
differences.
Environmental robustness was further examined under
wet and dry surface conditions using representative Tenera
samples. While surface moisture introduced shifts in the
absolute magnitude of radar backscattered power, the relative
ordering and separability of ripeness classes remained con-
sistent. This suggests that environmental moisture primarily
affects signal scale rather than the underlying discriminative
trend.
These findings indicate that while radar-weight features
exhibit reasonable robustness across varieties and envi-
ronmental variations, some degradation may occur under
heterogeneous deployment scenarios. This further motivates
the use of multimodal fusion to enhance generalization across
plantation conditions.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a multimodal ripeness classification
framework for oil palm FFBs that combines radar-weight
measurements and vision-based sensing through decision-
level late fusion. The radar branch uses backscattered
power and bunch weight to capture changes in internal
dielectric properties and mass, while the vision branch relies
on YOLOv8-Nano to extract external color and texture
cues. Together, these two views provide a more complete
description of fruit maturity under field conditions.
Experimental results show that both unimodal branches
already reach competitive performance. The radar-weight
classifier attains an accuracy of 89.9%, and the YOLOv8-
Nano model reaches 94.3% accuracy on the test set. When
the outputs are combined through late fusion schemes, all
multimodal configurations outperform their single-modality
counterparts. The best fusion setting achieves 96.9% accu-
racy and an F1-macro of 97.3%. These results indicate that
combining internal radar-weight information with external
visual evidence yields more reliable and physically inter-
pretable ripeness decisions, especially in situations where
illumination, occlusion, or fruit variability can limit purely
optical methods. The proposed framework therefore offers
a practical step toward smart-harvesting and inline quality-
control tools for the palm-oil industry and illustrates how
multimodal sensing can be applied to other agricultural
monitoring tasks.
Despite these promising results, several aspects still merit
further work. First, the number of samples, in particular for
the intermediate ripeness classes, is modest and may limit
the generalization of the fusion model. Second, the radar
front-end was operated at a single frequency of 2.4 GHz;
extending the study to multi-band or polarimetric configu-
rations could reveal additional dielectric contrast related to
internal moisture and oil gradients. Third, while inference
latency was benchmarked on an embedded device (Rasp-
berry Pi 5), all classification experiments were conducted
offline using recorded data. As a next step, the proposed
system should be integrated into a real-time pipeline with
synchronized radar and camera acquisition and evaluated in
conveyor-based or field-mounted setups to assess end-to-end
throughput, latency, and robustness under practical operating
conditions. Addressing these points would help translate
the current proof-of-concept into a more mature multi-
modal ripeness-assessment system with stronger practical
impact.
