Abstract
AI-driven imaging is becoming central to crop monitoring, with proximal and unmanned
aerial vehicle (UAV) platforms now routinely used for disease and stress detection, yield
estimation, canopy structure, and fruit counting. Yet, as these models move from plots to
farms, the main bottleneck is no longer raw accuracy but robustness under distribution
shift. Systems trained in one field, season, cultivar, or sensor often fail when the scene,
sensor, protocol, or timing changes in realistic ways. This review synthesizes recent
advances on robustness and transferability in proximal and UAV imaging, drawing on
a corpus of 42 core studies across field crops, orchards, greenhouse environments, and
multi-platform phenotyping. Shift types are organized into four axes, namely scene,
sensor, protocol, and time. The article also maps the empirical evidence on when RGB
imaging alone is sufficient and when multispectral, hyperspectral, or thermal modalities
can potentially improve robustness. This serves as a basis to synthesize acquisition and
evaluation practices that often matter more than architectural tweaks, which include
phenology-aware flight planning, radiometric standardization, metadata logging, and
leave-one-field/season-out splits. Adaptation options are consolidated into a practical
symptom/remedy roadmap, ranging from lightweight normalization and small target-set
fine-tuning to feature alignment, unsupervised domain adaptation, style translation, and
test-time updates. Finally, a benchmark and dataset agenda are outlined with emphasis on
object-oriented splits, cross-sensor and cross-scale collections, and longitudinal datasets
where the same fields are followed across seasons under different management regimes.
The goal is to outline practices and evaluation protocols that support progress toward
deployable and auditable systems, noting that such claims require standardized out-of-
distribution testing and transparent reporting as emphasized in the benchmark specification
and experiment suite proposed here.
Keywords: machine learning; deep learning; digital agriculture; datasets; domain shift
1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision (CV) are increasingly expected to
guide decisions over the agricultural season, from seedling emergence and weed control
to nutrient diagnostics, yield forecasting, canopy health, and fruit detection. Fulfilling
this promise requires large, high-quality datasets that reflect real operational variability
(seasons, sites, cultivars, management, and sensors). This is hard to achieve in agricul-
ture, as uncontrolled and unstructured conditions introduce substantial variation into any
measurement. In practice, data distributions can shift sharply across locations, sensors,
dates, management regimes, and growth stages. As a result, the central obstacle to reliable

deployment is achieving robustness under distribution shift [1–3]. In practice, models
trained in one field, season, cultivar, sensor, or management regime often fail under new
illumination, phenology, background structure, or scale [4–8]. Recent studies have begun
to quantify these gaps and demonstrate practical solutions across crops, traits, and imaging
setups [9–12]. Accordingly, the practical question is not whether proximal/UAV models
can reach high accuracy in-domain but how reliably they carry that performance across the
routine changes that occur between plots, farms, sensors, and seasons.
This article focuses on in-field data acquisition with proximal and unmanned aerial
vehicle (UAV) imaging, including red–green–blue (RGB), multispectral (MS), hyperspec-
tral (HS), and thermal modalities. In practice, robustness is often won or lost upstream,
before any model is trained. These platforms directly expose models to the operational
variability that most often breaks transferability (site, cultivar, management, illumination,
and phenology) and therefore provide the most informative setting for studying robustness
under distribution shift. Throughout, satellite imagery is not treated as a primary sens-
ing modality but rather is discussed only when it is explicitly coupled to proximal/UAV
data in cross-scale transfer scenarios (e.g., UAV-to-satellite mapping) or when it provides
methodological context.
In this review, we use the following terms consistently: generalization refers to per-
formance on new samples drawn from the same data-generating process as training (in-
distribution); robustness under distribution shift refers to maintaining acceptable performance
when the test distribution differs from training due to realistic changes in scene, sensor,
protocol, or time (out-of-distribution robustness) and is the primary deployment objective
here; transfer learning (TL) denotes reusing knowledge from a source dataset or task to
improve a target crop-imaging task, typically with at least some target labels; and domain
adaptation (DA) is a TL setting where the task is unchanged but the input domain shifts,
with supervised or unsupervised (UDA) variants depending on target-label availability.
Throughout, we use transferability to mean the empirical degree to which a trained model
maintains performance across domains with zero or minimal updating.
On the model side, transfer learning (TL) and physics-guided pretraining form a
second pillar of robustness. A practical strategy is to start from a broadly trained reference,
such as an ImageNet-style or self-supervised RGB model that captures generic texture and
structure cues, and then to fine-tune on a smaller dataset of field images to adapt to local
crops and conditions [10,13,14]. When spectral or physiological traits are central, using
physics to simulate how crops would reflect light under different leaf area index (LAI),
chlorophyll, water content, soil backgrounds, and sun/sensor angles, thus generating better
pretraining signals, can further prepare the network before field fine-tuning [15–18]. To
bridge site and sensor gaps in practice, input data can be stabilized with color constancy
and exposure/white-balance control for RGB or via band selection and reweighting that
emphasize invariant spectral cues for MS and HS data [9,19–21]. Representations can also
be aligned so that features from different cameras and systems become comparable, for
example, using spectral standardization paired with latent-space or transfer component
analysis (TCA [22]) alignment for hyperspectral data [23,24]. Finally, fusing complementary
geometry or thermal cues helps regularize predictions under changing illumination or
canopy structure, improving transfer to new fields and seasons for water-related and stress
traits [25]. These ideas reappear in Section 6 as part of the adaptation roadmap from
low-cost updates to more involved alignment and updating strategies.
In deployment, conditions often shift faster than labels can be collected, so a practi-
cal sequence is to start with a small, spatially diverse target set and perform fine-tuning
with roughly 10 to 30% of target samples. This is often enough to adapt the model to
the new region, cultivar, or management regime without retraining from scratch [4,10,20].

Such simplified fine-tuning has been effective for head and bunch detection, stress and
yield mapping, and nitrogen-related trait detection across cereals, orchards, and soybean
breeding trials [7,10,14]. When labels are scarce or unavailable, domain adaptation (DA)
and unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) provide additional options. First, shallow
feature alignment methods (e.g., TCA or spectral-response-aware band transforms) align
feature distributions and band spaces across sites and sensors, stabilizing classifiers with
minimal supervision [23,24,26]. Next, teacher–student consistency schemes and related
UDA strategies can be applied so a source-trained teacher guides a student on unlabeled
target imagery, a pattern explored for multisensor nitrogen and disease mapping [27,28].
Finally, appearance differences can be addressed directly with pixel-level style transla-
tion that makes source images “look like” the target domain (e.g., CycleGAN [29] and
CUT-style transforms [30,31]) while preserving geometric content for detection and seg-
mentation [32–34]. These techniques can be paired with classic cross-sensor band correction
and multisensor fusion, which reduce input drift and emphasize the most informative
traits [20,26].
Crucially, none of these remedies is meaningful unless evaluation protocols actually
match the intended deployment gap. Evaluation design dictates whether reported accuracy
reflects deployable performance. Studies that compare random splits with spatial or leave-
one-field-out validation show that naive pixel- or plant-level splits artificially improve
accuracy by leaking near-identical backgrounds and illumination into both training and test
sets, thus overestimating transferability [6,7]. In practice, spatially aware protocols tend to
yield more conservative and realistic results, especially when test parcels differ in soil, man-
agement, or phenology from the training set [10,35]. Open benchmarks make these gaps
visible at scale. For example, the Global Wheat Head Detection (GWHD) dataset frames
cross-site wheat head detection with standardized train/test farms for out-of-domain gen-
eralization [8], and MinneApple emphasizes fruit detection under heterogeneous orchards,
cultivars, and lighting to probe robustness beyond random splits [7]. Targeted domain-
shift evaluation sets go further by isolating specific factors. Grape-bunch datasets with
controlled temporal, viewpoint, and variety changes reveal how performance decays with
camera pose or cultivar shifts and which adaptations recover it [14]. Weed, disease, and
weather-driven benchmarks quantify how illumination, management, and season interact
in segmentation and grading tasks [12,27]. Cross-site root and soil imaging benchmarks
separate lab/field and location effects to test whether models truly travel across acquisition
setups [36,37]. Together, these designs help identify failure modes early and guide the
choice of adaptation tactics that work for real deployments [6,35].
The remainder of the paper proceeds from defining shift types and how to test them, to
summarizing empirical evidence by modality, and then to providing acquisition/evaluation
guidance and an adaptation roadmap that links common failure modes to practical reme-
dies. This review synthesizes robust evidence for proximal and UAV imaging across
agriculture, including field crops, orchards, and protected environments. Considering the
four axes of scene, sensor, protocol, and time, the contributions are as follows:

Practical definition of robustness under distribution shift for agricultural imaging and

proximal and UAV data (Section 3);
•
Mapping of empirical evidence across RGB, MS, HS, and thermal sensors for field
crops, orchards, and protected environments, highlighting when additional modalities
consistently improve robustness (Section 4);
•

from a loosely stated aspiration to a set of concrete acquisition, evaluation, and adaptation
practices that can be adopted in real-world agricultural imaging pipelines.
This article focuses on robustness under domain and distribution shift for proximal
and UAV crop imaging pipelines. We do not attempt exhaustive coverage of purely satellite-
based remote sensing, non-imaging sensor streams (e.g., point spectroscopy/IoT without a
CV component), or non-crop domains such as livestock and forestry. Our emphasis is on
practices and methods supported by field-relevant evidence under shift-aware evaluation.
In this review, UAV imaging refers to aerial acquisition from drone platforms (typically
nadir or oblique mosaics), while proximal imaging refers to ground-based close-range
sensing (handheld, rig, and phenotyping platforms). When recommendations are platform-
specific, they are labeled explicitly as UAV- or proximal-oriented.
2. Literature Search and Article Selection
To ensure transparency and reproducibility, this review followed a structured proce-
dure to identify, screen, and characterize the literature on robustness under domain and
distribution shift in proximal and UAV imaging for crop monitoring. The methodology
comprised three main steps: a systematic search across major academic databases, screening
and application of explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, and structured characterization
of the final corpus of studies. The searches were executed on 8 October 2025 and covered
records indexed from database inception through the search date, with no publication-year
filter applied.
The search strategy was designed to identify work at the intersection of domain or
distribution shift, transfer learning, and related robustness concepts, and proximal or
UAV imaging for major field crops and orchards using RGB and multisensor data. The
database query was constructed in two layers. First, the “title” field was required to
contain at least one term directly related to robustness and transfer, namely “domain
shift”, “distribution shift”, “domain adaptation”, “transfer learning”, “model transfer”,
“calibration transfer”, “transferability”, “OOD”, or “out of distribution”. Second, the
“title–abstract–keywords” fields were required to jointly mention at least one target crop
(wheat, rice, maize/corn, soybean, apple, citrus, mango, tomato, grape, or pear), at least
one imaging or vision-related term (image, computer vision, remote sensing, hyperspectral,
multispectral, RGB, RGB-D, LiDAR, or thermal), and at least one proximal or near-field
platform term (UAV/UAS/drone, gantry or rail system, proximal or handheld imaging,
ground robot/UGV, tractor or sprayer, or greenhouse/in-field phenotyping). Only records
satisfying both layers were retrieved.
This query restricts the “title” field to core robustness terms (e.g., domain adaptation,
transfer learning, calibration transfer, transferability, and out of distribution) so that domain
or distribution shift is central to the article, while the “title–abstract–keywords” fields
constrain crop types, sensing modalities, and platforms. An equivalent query, with the

same logical structure, was implemented in Web of Science (WoS). The Scopus query

initial pool of 106 unique articles was obtained.
The 106 records were then screened using explicit eligibility criteria based on title, ab-
stract, and, when necessary, full text. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) written in English;
(ii) focused explicitly on domain or distribution shift (or closely related transfer/robustness
concepts) in the context of crop imaging; and (iii) used proximal or UAV imaging for the
targeted crop types and sensing modalities defined by the query. Works were excluded
when robustness or transferability was only mentioned tangentially, when the imaging
component was not proximal or UAV (such as purely satellite-based studies or non-imaging
sensors without a CV component), or when the main emphasis was unrelated to distribu-
tion shift (e.g., generic yield mapping without cross-site or cross-sensor evaluation). After
applying these criteria, 42 articles remained and were retained as the core corpus for the
review. All synthesis in Sections 3–7 is grounded primarily in these 42 studies.
To reduce the risk of missing relevant work, the reference lists of all 42 core articles
were examined (backward snowballing). Each cited paper was checked against the same
inclusion criteria. Because the database query constrains crops and imaging modalities,
the resulting core set is treated as a focused corpus (major field crops and orchards under
proximal/UAV imaging) rather than an exhaustive census of all agricultural sensing stud-
ies. No additional article satisfied these criteria beyond the initial 42. It is worth pointing
out that, throughout the thematic sections, additional references are cited when they
provide relevant context or methodological detail (for example, agronomic background,
related modeling strategies, or sensor-specific discussions). These context papers are
not counted as part of the core set and are not used in quantitative tallies of robustness
evidence, but they help situate the core findings within the broader agricultural AI and
phenotyping literature.
Each of the 42 core articles was coded using a structured template capturing the
following: crop or domain, primary trait or task, sensor configuration, acquisition platform,
and the main shift axes and methods addressed. These variables are summarized in Table 1,
which provides a compact overview of the corpus and supports cross-study comparisons.
The characterization is descriptive rather than statistical, reflecting the heterogeneity of
crops, sensors, and tasks represented, but it provides a quantitative backbone for the
thematic synthesis. It underpins, for example, the breakdown of robustness evidence
across RGB and multisensor imaging (Section 4), the mapping between shift axes and
acquisition or evaluation choices (Section 5), and the organization of adaptation strategies
into a practical decision aid (Section 6).
Because the query targets specific major crops and explicitly filters for imaging and in-
field or near-field platforms, the resulting corpus is naturally richer in cereals and orchards.
Studies on related domains, such as root and soil imaging or greenhouse phenotyping,
were retained in the core set when they satisfied the search criteria and contributed directly
to the analysis of domain or distribution shift (for example, calibration transfer between
hyperspectral platforms or cross-environment disease recognition). By contrast, works
outside this scope are used only when they offer methodological insights and are explicitly
treated as contextual rather than core evidence. This distinction between the 42-paper
core corpus and additional contextual references ensures clarity about the evidential basis
for the robustness claims synthesized in Sections 3–7 while still allowing methodological
advances from the broader agricultural machine learning (ML) literature to inform the
proposed taxonomy, protocols, and experimental agenda.

3. Robustness Under Distribution Shift: Definition and a Field-
Focused Taxonomy
In proximal and UAV agricultural imaging, robustness under distribution shift means
that a model’s accuracy and error structure remain within a small, predictable margin when
conditions change in realistic ways. Four real-world factors are particularly relevant in
this context (Figure 1): the scene, which includes canopy geometry, variety, netting, and
background clutter; the sensor, which includes spectral response, optics, radiometry, and
resolution; the acquisition protocol, which includes UAV flight altitude and timing, expo-
sure and white balance, and sampling layout; and time, which includes phenology, season
or year, and weather. To ground this taxonomy empirically, Table 2 summarizes recurring
patterns observed across the 42-study core corpus, pairing each axis with representative fail-
ure modes, evaluation designs, and robustness levers (with concrete examples). In practice,
robustness is assessed by testing models on independent fields, orchards, or seasons that
differ along one or more of these axes, checking whether performance degrades smoothly
rather than collapsing, and whether acceptable accuracy can be maintained without ex-
haustive relabeling or full retraining [7,8,10,35,36]. In plant pathology, continual-learning
formulations provide a practical way to update models as new domains and categories
arrive, while explicitly mitigating catastrophic forgetting [49]. This sets a clear deployment
target in which models should hold up across farms and years, not only across random
splits within a single field.

Conventional random cross-validation often mixes pixels or plants from the same
rows and illumination pockets across the training and test sets, inflating transfer capability
claims by leaking micro-context. This concern is especially well-documented in plant
disease recognition, where limited dataset representativeness and capture-condition bias
are repeatedly identified as primary barriers to field-ready deep learning systems [50,51].
This leakage is repeatedly documented in UAV yield-prediction and phenotyping studies,
where random cross-validation overestimates transfer relative to spatial or field-level
splits [6,35]. When evaluations instead hold out entire fields, orchards, or seasons, accuracy
typically drops and error modes shift toward missed detections under occlusion, canopy
shadows, or altered color and texture [8,35]. Fruit and ear-counting datasets corroborate
this, as tests with independent orchards, varieties, or fields reveal failures that are missed
under random splits, underscoring that independence between training and test sets
in agriculture must be defined spatially and temporally, not only at the pixel or patch
level [7,13,14].
Scene changes alter the distribution of geometries and textures. Leaf angles and
densities, row spacing, under-canopy darkness, reflective soil or mulch, and the presence of
nets or bags are all relevant factors in this context. For example, grape-bunch segmentation
across varieties and viewpoints shows that models trained in one vineyard can become
ineffective when bunch size, leaf glare, and occlusions change, even if the camera and
protocol are fixed [14]. In apples, MinneApple and robotic-harvesting studies emphasize
similar sensitivity to canopy structure, occlusion, and background clutter [7,43]. Category-
level UAV benchmarks in cereals and weeds likewise report accuracy degradation when

evaluation sites, highlighting scene shift as a major source of error in counting, localization,

Changing cameras or lenses alters pixel statistics and feature salience, with factors like
band placement, dynamic range, bit depth, lens distortion, and effective ground sampling

that even similar plant-phenotyping systems can require explicit spectral-space or latent-

effective GSD cause systematic shifts in vegetation indices and texture features, affecting
model transferability across devices and platforms [5,9]. These results underscore that
sensor metadata and calibration are essential to reproducibility and to any credible claim
of robustness, even when a similar RGB or multispectral setup is used.
UAV flight altitude and airspeed affect motion blur and effective GSD, while sun angle
and cloud cover reshape shadows and color. In addition, sampling layouts (random tiles,
row-following, and stratified designs) change the mix of backgrounds and phenological
states. Empirical studies comparing different flight altitudes, overlaps, and acquisition
timings show that holding GSD, sun elevation, and observation dates near phenologi-
cal peaks often stabilizes performance across sites more than adding model complexity
alone [5,35]. Field detection and segmentation studies also note that stabilizing motion,
using sufficient overlap, and avoiding extreme insolation angles reduce false negatives in
shaded or partially occluded targets, especially for small objects [12,13].
Phenology, season, and year introduce systematic appearance changes and alter clutter
(weeds, residues, and soil moisture). Cross-season yield-prediction and nitrogen-content
models in wheat, rice, and potato consistently report larger OOD gaps than within-season
splits, so leave-one-season-out and independent-year tests provide the most unbiased
estimate of deployable performance [4,6,9,11,35]. Similar temporal gaps are also observed in
UAV disease mapping when models are evaluated on truly independent acquisition periods;
for example, multi-site wheat disease segmentation datasets collected across consecutive
seasons/years (e.g., 2022–2023) require explicit cross-site folds and highlight the difficulty
of transferring across time and location [27]. Temporal shift further interacts with scene
and protocol; the same altitude and exposure that work at heading may underperform
at senescence due to color/contrast changes and increased occlusion from lodging and
senescent leaves [4,35].
It is worth pointing out that shifts rarely occur in isolation. A farm adopting different
trellising (scene) may mount a different camera (sensor) and fly at a new altitude (protocol)
at a later phenological stage (time). Fruit datasets that take into account variety and
viewpoint show that the angle of image capture can amplify occlusion under dense canopies
and interact with pruning regime and canopy density [7,14]. Root phenotyping and soil
structure imaging, as well as weed segmentation studies, similarly show that temporal shifts
occur with cross-site differences in background and illumination, compounding domain
shift for segmentation models [34,36,37]. Wheat head and ear counting studies reveal
that small resolution drifts and changes in observation distance worsen head separation
precisely when phenology changes head shape and contrast [5,8,13]. Treating the four
axes explicitly helps plan acquisitions (which combinations to cover) and decide which
adaptation strategies to prioritize first in each deployment.

independent field or independent season test aligned with the intended deployment
gap [4,6,9,35]. They should specify sensor details (bands, bit depth, lens, nominal GSD, and
radiometric treatment) and protocol settings (altitude, overlap, exposure and white-balance

enumerating farm-level factors (soil background, inter-row cover, irrigation, and manage-

A practical view is to define robustness thresholds across the four axes, that is, how
much GSD drift, exposure variance, variety change, or phenological gap the model can toler-

make this explicit by plotting accuracy or error against controlled deviations in altitude,
year, or viewpoint, or against different cross-validation schemes [5,6,12,35,36]. Simple
sanity checks and illumination diagnostics, per-field resolution checks, basic spectral cali-
bration comparisons, and occlusion profiling help distinguish sensor and protocol issues
from scene and time effects before resorting to more complex adaptation or retraining [23].
The taxonomy thus guides both evidence synthesis and method selection. In crops
and orchards, scene and time shifts dominate counting and detection errors, so evaluation
should employ leave-one-field-out and independent season tests, as done in wheat head,
ear counting, fruit, and weed benchmarks [7,8,12,14,27]. In addition, sensor and protocol
standardization or explicit calibration-transfer procedures should be reported alongside
accuracy to clarify whether performance is more related to the model architecture or to the
acquisition and preprocessing pipeline [5,9,23]. The following sections address which adap-
tations (normalization and augmentation, small target-set fine-tuning, feature alignment, or
style translation) are the most effective for each type of shift, with benchmark datasets that
stage variety, viewpoint, site, sensor, and season changes providing appropriate reference
points to compare remedies across tasks [10,12,27,36].
4. Empirical Evidence Across RGB and Multisensor Imaging
Robustness under distribution shift depends as much on what is measured as on
how and where it is measured. Across the reviewed studies, RGB emerges as the practical
baseline for in-field sensing because of its ubiquity, cost, and relative ease of standardiza-
tion. When acquisition protocols are controlled, and evaluation is spatially aware, RGB
models already transfer better than expected in several tasks, particularly object-level
detection (heads, fruit, organs) and canopy-level counting or segmentation in daylight
conditions [7,12,13,32]. In orchards, detectors trained on RGB imagery with block-wise
or row-wise splits generalize across rows and blocks more reliably than models trained
and evaluated with random patch splits, which tend to inflate accuracy and mask sensitiv-
ity to background, cultivar, and illumination changes [7,43]. For field crops, RGB works
well when flight height, ground sampling distance (GSD), and view geometry are held
consistent, as shown in wheat head, ear counting, and weed segmentation studies that
emphasize careful protocol design and spatially structured validation [8,13]. However, per-
formance degrades under oblique views, long shadows, high residue, or different sowing
densities, unless the training set or augmentation strategy explicitly covers these shifts,
as documented in cereal head and seedling detection and synthetic data soybean seed
phenotyping [12,44].
The empirical evidence favors multispectral, hyperspectral, and thermal modalities in
regimes where RGB-only cues are less stable under shift—namely when the target signal is
genuinely spectral or physiological (for example, stress, nutrient status, chlorophyll content,
leaf water content, or canopy temperature) or when lighting and background are unstable

and spectral features provide information that RGB lacks. In the subset of studies that
report explicit modality ablations or paired baselines, adding red-edge/NIR (and, where
available, thermal) consistently reduced cross-date and cross-field degradation for vigor-,
chlorophyll-, and nitrogen-related traits compared with RGB-only baselines, especially
when soil brightness, residue, and illumination differ across sites and seasons [5,9,10,19].
In field crops, red-edge and near-infrared (NIR) bands systematically serve as nutrient
and vigor proxies across dates and sun angles, improving transfer to new parcels and
years with different soil brightness, residue levels, or management practices, for both
handcrafted indices and learned representations [4,5,9,10,19]. Hyperspectral and multi-
spectral approaches that couple radiative transfer models with deep networks or recurrent
architectures demonstrate that narrowband cues can maintain chlorophyll, nitrogen, and
biomass estimation accuracy under significant changes in acquisition date, geometry, and
site for wheat, soybean, maize, and rice [15–18]. In dense canopies, paddies, and pro-
tected environments, such narrowband or physics-guided cues mitigate saturation and
mixed-pixel issues that challenge RGB classifiers, especially for early stress detection and
subtle disease symptoms where color and texture changes are weak [23]. Thermal and
water-content-oriented imagery adds robustness for water-related traits under midday
illumination changes by anchoring canopy temperature or moisture indices relative to air
and soil conditions, although models still benefit from co-registered RGB or multispectral
imagery to provide structure and context for segmentation and detection [17,27].
At the same time, extra bands do not automatically improve robustness. Several
studies show that simply adding multispectral, hyperspectral, or thermal channels can
degrade transfer if radiometric handling, band selection, or cross-sensor alignment is
weak. Under domain shift, noise and sensor-specific artifacts quickly propagate through
deep models, and redundant or unstable bands make models more sensitive to hardware
and protocol changes [5,9,23,24]. As a result, a smaller set of more informative bands
often outperforms the full spectral set, especially when combined with band selection,
spectral compression, or physics-guided feature construction [15,46]. Practical gains arise
when pipelines compress or regularize spectra to reduce sensor-specific idiosyncrasies,
harmonize exposure, white balance, and irradiance across acquisition sessions, and fuse
modalities at the feature level with explicit normalization rather than raw concatenation
of channels [9,23,24]. In orchards, multispectral and hyperspectral data are most helpful
for maturity, quality estimation, and subtle disease or health signals, whereas counting
and detection tasks usually employ RGB images unless illumination is highly variable
or fruit color closely matches the background [7,39]. In those cases, adding a red-edge
band or simple vegetation indices can improve model transfer without major architectural
changes [9,20].
Across tasks and crop types, consistent patterns emerge. For counting and detection
problems, RGB with strong acquisition control and spatially aware evaluation is typically
the most efficient choice. Multisensor gains appear mainly when canopy texture and target
color overlap (for example, green fruit against foliage), when severe specular reflections
and shadows are recurrent across sites, or when class boundaries are subtly spectral rather
than purely geometric [7,12]. For stress, nutrient, and disease grading, multispectral, hy-
perspectral, and thermal modalities more consistently improve robustness, provided band
handling and calibration are explicit and stable over time [5,26,46]. Disease and stress stud-
ies under greenhouse or semi-controlled environments also show that spectral cues tend to
be disproportionately useful because artificial lighting and engineered backgrounds create
shifts that spectral normalization and calibration-transfer methods can dampen [23,24].
Even in these settings, however, geometry control (camera distance and angle) remains
critical for transfer, as reflection effects and occlusion patterns still cause errors [32]. Models

usually generalize better across seasons when they exploit multi-date context or temporal
smoothing, since aggregating predictions over several acquisition sessions makes them less
sensitive to sporadic lighting or management anomalies [4,10,35].
Cross-platform and cross-sensor experiments further characterize the role of additional
modalities. For UAV-to-UAV transfer (different cameras or lenses), models trained with
explicit radiometric standardization and sensor alignment perform better than those relying
on raw reflectance estimates alone. Even simple per-band scaling based on reference panels
or pseudo-invariant features reduces drop-off on new hardware and between different
multispectral cameras [5,9,23]. For UAV-to-proximal and proximal-to-UAV transfer, domain
gaps in view geometry and reflectance-distribution effects dominate. Studies on wheat
yield, biomass, and nitrogen status show that combining shallow structure cues from RGB
with a few stable spectral features often outperforms either modality alone, particularly
when UAV flight altitudes vary across sites or when canopy architecture differs between
ground and aerial views [4,26]. Thermal transfer improves when canopy temperature
is normalized by ambient conditions, wind, and reference surfaces, not just absolute
temperatures, highlighting that protocol metadata (such as air temperature and wind speed)
are relevant and must be logged to ensure reproducible robustness [12,26]. Physics-guided
sim-to-real strategies that pretrain on radiative-transfer simulations and then adapt to real
data also help bridge sensor and platform gaps for chlorophyll and biomass traits [15–18].
In summary, the empirical evidence suggests four practical guidelines for choosing
modalities under a distribution shift. First, start from a robust RGB baseline evaluated un-
der spatially aware splits, thus establishing a realistic reference for transferability [7,8,13,35].
Second, add red-edge or NIR when the trait of interest is physiological or when illumi-
nation and soil/background conditions vary substantially over time or across fields, and
consider hyperspectral or physics-guided spectral features when chlorophyll and nitrogen
are primary targets [5,9,15,17,46]. Third, consider thermal for water stress assessment,
irrigation timing, and heat-related risks, but normalize canopy temperature by ambient
conditions and wind, and combine it with cues extracted from RGB or multispectral
images [12,26]. Fourth, prioritize band handling, alignment, and protocol standardization
before increasing spectral depth, because under domain shift, how data from different
flight missions and sensors are made comparable often matters more than how many bands
are available [5,9,23,24]. These patterns hold across field crops, orchards, and controlled
environments in the reviewed studies, and they connect directly to the acquisition and
evaluation choices discussed in the following sections.
5. Acquisition, Standardization, and Evaluation Under Distribution Shift
Robustness in proximal and UAV imaging starts with how data are acquired and
standardized. Many of the gaps observed in cross-field and cross-season tests can be
traced to choices made before any model is trained [35]. This section consolidates practical
guidance from the reviewed studies into a coherent workflow, from flight planning and
camera configuration to radiometric handling and evaluation design.
As a guiding framework, Figure 2 summarizes how shifts along the four axes (scene,
sensor, protocol, time) translate into observable effects in model behavior and how these
symptoms can be addressed through a graduated set of remedies, from low-cost normaliza-
tion and augmentation to more involved domain-adaptation and test-time strategies.
Acquisition should be planned around agronomic phases that maximize target visibil-
ity while minimizing confounders. Good moments for acquisition include when heads are
visible and not yet lodged for cereals, when bunches and fruits are not yet subject to heavy
occlusion or bagging in orchards, and when canopies are dry and reasonably stable under
wind [7,8]. Many studies recommend constraining flights to a solar window (for example,

15 of 32
late morning to early afternoon) or, when this is not possible, deliberately sampling multi-
ple times of the day and documenting them, because sun elevation and cloud cover have
a strong influence on shadows, specular reflections, and apparent color [4]. Relatedly, in
multi-temporal UAV campaigns, logging sky conditions (haze, aerosol, variable overcast)
and filtering acquisitions that violate illumination constraints helps stabilize sequential
inputs across dates [52]. Maintaining a roughly constant ground sampling distance (GSD)
across fields and dates also emerges as a simple but powerful control. When this is not
feasible, acquiring at two nearby altitudes and reporting model sensitivity to different
GSDs (such as 1–2 cm versus 3–5 cm) helps quantify how resolution interacts with domain
shift [12]. Recording cloud cover, wind, and leaf wetness is equally important, since wind
increases motion blur and parallax errors, while wet foliage changes reflectance and tem-
perature patterns, and soil moisture and salinity strongly modulate spectral and thermal
responses in water-limited environments [12,21].

For RGB imaging, locking exposure, ISO, and white balance and, where possible,
recording RAW images reduces variability due to in-camera tone mapping and automatic
gain control [35]. Calibrating lens distortion and vignetting and checking focus at the
operating altitude are basic but often ignored steps. For multispectral and hyperspectral
sensors, good practice includes capturing dark frames, sensor temperature, and spectral
response metadata. For thermal cameras, this involves recording emissivity settings,
ambient air temperature, and wind information, ideally with at least one reference object
of known emissivity when materials differ in the scene [26]. View geometry should be
kept consistent (for example, predominantly nadir flights with limited roll and pitch),
or, if off-nadir views are intentional, they should be sampled in a controlled way, and
performance according to view angle should be reported [44].
Several works show that radiometric treatment is central to transferability. Panel-
based normalization, or the use of pseudo-invariant features (PIFs), reduces cross-date and
cross-camera drift [53,54] more reliably than histogram matching alone [5]. For multispec-

tral and hyperspectral sensors, conversion to reflectance using an incident light sensor is
preferred when available. Otherwise, panel-based scaling should be applied and residual
variability documented [16]. When transferring between cameras, aligning bands by their
spectral response functions and, in some cases, learning a compact band space, such as
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or latent-space representation, helps reduce sensi-
tivity to sensor-specific characteristics [23,24]. Recent multisensor nitrogen and Nitrogen
Nutrition Index (NNI) studies further illustrate how such calibration-transfer and latent-
space harmonization steps improve cross-camera robustness [26]. For thermal imaging,
normalizing canopy temperature by ambient air temperature and wind (for example, using
∆T = Tc −Ta or similar indices) improves stability across microclimates and seasons and
is a recurring recommendation in irrigation and stress-monitoring studies [26].
Geometric alignment matters both for mosaics and for multimodal fusion. High
forward and side overlap (on the order of 70–90 % in each direction), combined with
RTK/PPK or well-distributed ground control points, reduces between-mosaic warping
that can otherwise cause label noise and degrade performance when tiles from the same
field are split across folds [9,16]. For proximal imaging, fixing standoff distance and view
angle with physical objects limits BRDF-related variation and makes repeated scans more
comparable [16]. When fusing RGB with multispectral, hyperspectral, or thermal data,
extrinsic calibration and per-band alignment should be verified along salient edges before
feature-level fusion is attempted. Several studies report that misregistration, rather than
model choice, dominates errors in naïve multimodal pipelines [26].
The reviewed evidence favors simple, auditable preprocessing controls that generalize,
including locking white balance and exposure where feasible, applying panel- or PIF-based
linear scaling, using light dehazing or color-constancy corrections only when acquisition
conditions justify them, and adopting band selection guided by agronomic priors or physics-
based simulations rather than unconstrained channel concatenation [5,9,16]. Any transform
that could entangle training and test domains (e.g., tiling strategy, CLAHE, denoising)
should be documented and kept identical across splits [6]. Finally, core metadata should be
logged, since even lightweight models using a small set of metadata cues can generalize
better across sites and seasons than more complex models that ignore these factors [10,35].
Acquisition and standardization alone are not sufficient, and evaluation must be
structured so that robustness claims truly reflect shifted distribution behavior. A recurrent
recommendation is to treat the field block, management unit, or farm as the fundamental
independence unit and never tile a single mosaic into both training and test folds [7,8,35].
For detection and segmentation, image footprints should not cross split boundaries, and
all dates from a given field or block should stay within the same split to avoid temporal
leakage. Most studies that explicitly target robustness report three families of splits: an
in-domain (ID) random-by-unit split within a site as a sanity baseline; a spatially structured
cross-validation (for example, block-wise or leave-one-field-out) to quantify within-region
transfer; and at least one leave-one-site, leave-one-season, or leave-one-sensor-out con-
figuration that matches the intended deployment gap and serves as the main robustness
indicator (Figure 3) [6,9,35].

tests (e.g., altitude/GSD, view angle, acquisition window, or exposure policy). These tallies
are conservative because many papers describe the shift qualitatively or mix several axes
without releasing split files or reporting matched in-domain (ID) and OOD scores. Where

6. Adaptation and Updating Under Distribution Shift: A Practical
Decision Aid
Robustness in real deployments rarely comes from a single technique. Instead, it
depends on matching specific shift symptoms to lightweight solutions that respect field
constraints such as limited labels, tight compute, and changing illumination and canopy
structure. This section consolidates adaptation options, from simple normalizations to
unsupervised domain adaptation, into deployability-oriented guidelines for proximal and
UAV imaging across RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, and thermal data. Many first-order
robustness gains come from the acquisition and preprocessing controls summarized in
Section 5 (e.g., exposure/WB policy, radiometric scaling, and protocol standardization).
Here, the focus is on model-side updating strategies that remain after those controls are in
place, organized by label availability and by the dominant shift axis.
When a modest fraction of the target site can be labeled, typically on the order of
10–30 %, small target-set fine-tuning becomes a powerful next step. In practice, this often
means retraining the task head while freezing most of the backbone, followed by cautious
fine-tuning of upper layers with discriminative learning rates so that early features remain
generic rather than adapted to local quirks. Class-balanced and difficulty-aware sampling,
deliberately including occluded, overexposed, or otherwise challenging examples, allows
the small labeled set to “teach” the critical gaps. Early stopping on a spatially independent
validation block, instead of random tiles, prevents excessive optimism caused by leakage
and preserves a realistic view of transferability [7,35]. This strategy is effective for RGB
baselines in disease, fruit, and head detection [7,8,14], as well as for multispectral and
hyperspectral trait mapping when spectral shifts are moderate [15,17,26]. Several multi-
year yield and nitrogen studies show that such targeted fine-tuning on a few representative
fields can close a large fraction of the cross-season gap [4,6,35].
When labels in the target domain are scarce or unavailable, feature-alignment tech-
niques based on shallow statistics offer label-free or label-light options. Classical color
and histogram matching, applied per field and after panel-based scaling, can bring RGB
mosaics from different missions into closer correspondence, provided that overcorrection is
avoided [35]. In feature space, alignment approaches such as transfer component analysis
(TCA) equalize means and covariances of feature distributions between source and target
domains and can be inserted as lightweight modules or offline transforms. They pair well
with small target label sets and provide quick robustness gains [20,23,24]. For multispectral
and hyperspectral data, band transforms that map sensors into a common latent basis
help decouple model behavior from the particularities of individual cameras [9]. These
methods are computationally inexpensive, auditable, and suitable for on-farm updates
where resources are limited.
For larger domain gaps under zero or very limited labeling capabilities, unsuper-
vised domain adaptation (UDA) becomes attractive. Mean-teacher or exponential-moving-
average consistency schemes enforce prediction stability under realistic augmentations
on target images, with a teacher network smoothing updates and reducing overfitting.
Related consistency and domain-adaptation strategies have been applied to multisensor
chlorophyll and nitrogen mapping across seasons and sites [27]. Adversarial feature align-

ment, where a domain discriminator pushes features toward domain invariance [56], can
further reduce appearance-related discrepancies, although care must be taken to avoid
class-conditional misalignment (for example, confusing leaves with background under
new lighting conditions). Pseudo-labeling with confidence filtering, sometimes combined
with spatial priors such as row geometry, can progressively label easy target examples and
increase effective supervision. Open-set and closed-set UDA variants for plant disease
recognition in greenhouses illustrate this progression [28,31]. Overall, UDA is most useful
when acquisition differences dominate the gap (lighting, soil background, sky conditions,
sensor characteristics) and class taxonomies are the same across domains [27,28].
When inter-sensor or weather effects dominate pixel statistics, style or appearance
translation can be used to bring source images closer to the target “look”. CycleGAN, CUT,
and related frameworks translate source imagery into the target style (illumination, color,
texture) without target labels, after which standard training or fine-tuning is performed
on these translated pairs [31–34]. Content-preserving constraints that emphasize edges
and structure are critical in yield-relevant applications so that objects such as panicles,
bunches, and fruits are not hallucinated or distorted [32]. Style translation is particularly
useful when color and contrast shifts are the main failure mode (for example, weather-
induced appearance changes or cross-camera color differences). It is less appropriate when
structural differences, such as canopy architecture or trellis type, are the dominant source
of variation [12,36].
Several techniques operate directly at test time to provide plug-in robustness during
deployment. Batch-normalization recalibration, where statistics are updated per field or per
mission, can adapt feature distributions without labels. This can be combined with entropy
minimization on target inputs to sharpen predictions [57,58]. Normalization schemes that
explicitly condition on metadata such as solar elevation, GSD, or temperature (for example,
through conditional batch normalization or metadata-conditioned heads) further reduce
shift by tailoring affine parameters or decision thresholds to acquisition conditions [26].
These methods are fast and label-free but must be monitored for drift, with a clean rollback
path to a stable baseline model.
Because labels are costly in the field, active learning and data selection strategies
can make each annotation count [59]. Query strategies that target spatial novelty and
failure sources (regions of low confidence, high entropy, or uncommon backgrounds) help
select the most informative patches for labeling. Selecting entire blocks or representative
tiles across field heterogeneity, rather than random tiles, improves coverage of key condi-
tions with fewer labels [6,35]. Some studies implicitly follow this logic when constructing
multi-year training sets or when adding targeted labels on crops, cultivars, or management
regimes that initially show the largest generalization gaps [10]. While formal data-valuation
methods remain rare in proximal and UAV phenotyping, their underlying principle, identi-
fying redundant imagery and highlighting rare, high-leverage situations, aligns well with
these practices.
Uncertainty estimation and calibration add another layer of robustness. Shallow
ensembles, using just two or three independently trained seeds or backbones, often deliver
observable cross-site gains with modest computational overhead and provide dispersion
estimates that highlight failure modes [6]. Simple post-hoc calibration, such as temperature
scaling on a spatially independent validation block, improves the reliability of confidence
scores and thus the thresholds used for actions like spraying or harvesting, especially when
shifts alter the distribution of output scores. In irrigation, stress mapping, and nitrogen
management contexts, calibration is particularly important because small changes in score
distributions can translate into discrete management decisions and costly interventions [55].

In practical deployments, it is useful to think in terms of a symptom–remedy cor-
respondence rather than a fixed recipe. If a model performs well in controlled tests but
fails under harsh sun or overcast conditions, the first line of response is to strengthen
illumination and color augmentation, enforce panel or PIF normalization, and consider
batch-normalization recalibration or entropy-based test time adaptation. If color statistics
remain the main issue, style translation may be justified [12,32]. When moving to a new
site with different soil, residue, or background, shallow feature alignment (e.g., TCA)
combined with small target-set fine-tuning, and possibly geometry-aware priors, tends
to provide quick improvements [23,26]. For sensor changes, such as a new UAV camera
with a different spectral response, spectrally aware band mapping, or compact band spaces,
combined with minimal fine-tuning, can mitigate mismatch. If thermal imaging is involved,
normalizing canopy–air temperature differences by ambient conditions and wind is essen-
tial [26]. Seasonal and phenological drift often requires curated target sets that consider
key stages, augmented by teacher–student UDA for unlabeled data and geometry-aware
augmentations or modest ensembling [35]. When the deployment shift is driven by evolv-
ing class sets (e.g., new varieties/market categories), adaptive class-incremental updating
combined with domain adaptation can reduce the need for full retraining while controlling
forgetting [60]. When dealing with sparse, rare classes (early disease symptoms, small
fruit), active learning focused on uncertain or rare patches and class-balanced fine-tuning
are generally preferable to heavy UDA, which can dilute minority signals [59].
A recurring caveat across these adaptation families is that robustness gains are not
monotonic. Negative transfer can occur when class priors change, when the target domain
contains unseen sub-classes, or when alignment enforces invariances that erase discrimina-
tive cues [28,31,56]. Pseudo-labeling and teacher–student UDA are particularly sensitive
to confirmation bias under phenological mismatch and under sensor noise or radiomet-
ric instability, which can cause drift if updates are not constrained and monitored [6,35].
Likewise, style translation can inadvertently “fix” a domain gap by altering agronomically
meaningful structure (geometry/texture), so content-preservation checks and conservative
use are essential [32]. Finally, test-time adaptation can silently degrade without safeguards,
so this should be treated as a monitored, reversible component (with stable baselines, cali-
bration checks, and truly held-out field/season tests) rather than a substitute for shift-aware
evaluation [6,26,58].
In practical deployments, it is useful to think in terms of a symptom–remedy cor-
respondence rather than a fixed recipe. When performance drops are dominated by
appearance/protocol changes, prioritize augmentation and lightweight updates (e.g., nor-
malization or test-time adaptation) before heavier pipelines [6,26]. When the dominant
gap is a scene or site change, small target-set fine-tuning and shallow alignment are often
the fastest reliable options. When sensor mismatch is primary, spectrally aware band
mapping (and, if needed, limited fine-tuning) should be used before introducing complex
UDA [31,56]. For seasonal drift, measures include curating small target sets spanning key
stages and comparing lightweight updating against UDA under the same OOD split [61].

While the literature surveyed here is heterogeneous in terms of crops, tasks, and

lightweight updates (normalization, small-set fine-tuning, shallow alignment) are typically
easier to document and roll back than heavier UDA or image-to-image translation pipelines.

7. Benchmarks and Targeted Gaps: A Practical Agenda for Datasets
and Experiments
Robustness under distribution shift is only as credible as the evaluation settings used
to measure it. In practice, this depends on which public benchmarks and domain-shift
suites are being used, how they are split and analyzed to produce spatially and temporally
realistic estimates of performance, and whether newer datasets are designed explicitly for
out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation rather than only for in-distribution accuracy.
Two public benchmarks are particularly relevant in the context of this work. The
Global Wheat Head Detection (GWHD) dataset provides diverse, multi-country UAV and
ground imagery for wheat head detection and includes explicit cross-site splits, making
it suitable to study scene and time shifts in cereal canopies [8,35]. MinneApple offers
orchard imagery for fruit detection and counting with substantial variability in illumination,
occlusion, and background, which is useful to probe appearance and protocol shifts in
tree crops [7]. Related apple datasets for robotic harvesting extend this setting to semantic
segmentation and domain adaptation under changing canopy structure and lighting [43].
In addition, several works curate targeted domain-shift benchmarks, such as grape-bunch
datasets organized by season, viewpoint, and variety, or cross-site root and soil imaging
where texture and moisture vary markedly, as well as multi-site weed and disease datasets
that vary in weather and management regimes across fields [14,27,36]. These emphasize
combinations of sensor, protocol, scene, and time shifts beyond the random splits used in
many earlier studies. Together, these resources support more than in-distribution testing;
they allow leave-one-field, leave-one-site, or leave-one-season-out evaluation that reveals
transfer gaps, which random tile or patch splits systematically hide [6,7].
To obtain realistic robustness estimates from these datasets, spatially aware partitions
or explicit leave-one-site/season-out protocols should be preferred over random splits.
Reporting both in-site and OOD scores makes it possible to separate memorization from
true transferability [7,8,35]. When metadata permit, results should be reported separately
by altitude or GSD and by sun geometry to quantify protocol sensitivity and accompanied
by simple calibration checks (such as temperature scaling or binned reliability plots) so
that action thresholds remain trustworthy across sites and years [6,26]. For adaptation
studies, it is important to fix a reference source model and compare lightweight updates
(normalization, small target-set fine-tuning, feature alignment, teacher–student UDA, or
style translation) under the same OOD split. This isolates the contribution of the adaptation
method from artifacts caused by different split definitions [28,31]. Benchmarks such as
GWHD, MinneApple, grape-bunch benchmarks, and multi-site disease datasets already
support this design, but many published adaptation papers still rely only on in-domain
random splits.
Despite recent progress, several gaps remain and are particularly valuable to fill. First,
grain crops beyond wheat are underrepresented in publicly released UAV and proximal
RGB datasets with explicit cross-farm and cross-season partitions. Maize, soybean, rice,
barley, and sorghum remain especially scarce compared with orchards and wheat, even
though several closed or partially released datasets exist for yield, biomass, nitrogen, and
suitability estimation [4,9,10,25,38]. Second, there is a lack of cross-sensor benchmarks
acquired over the same fields with RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, and thermal cameras,
with measured spectral response functions and radiometric references. Such datasets are
needed to study sensor replacement, fusion, and band-compression strategies without
confounding changes in the scene, building on calibration-transfer studies that currently

rely on smaller or proprietary collections [23,24]. Third, longitudinal sequences (multi-

to quantify temporal drift and to compare small periodic updates against heavier UDA

such as standardized panel or PIF frames, exposure and white-balance logs, and wind

datasets immediately useful for robustness studies:
1.
Design for OOD: Include at least three distinct farms or sites and at least one held-

conventional random splits [7,8,27].
2.

3.

enable alignment, calibration-transfer, and band-compression studies [23,24,26].
5.
Reporting template: Encourage or require spatially aware metrics, explicit OOD scores,
calibration plots, and a short description of any adaptation steps so that results remain
comparable across studies and across adaptation methods [6,12,35].

8. Conclusions
The key conclusions of this work can be summarized as follows:
•
Robustness failures in proximal and UAV crop imaging can be interpreted along four
shift axes (scene, sensor, protocol, and time), making failure modes diagnosable rather
than generic.
•
Acquisition and standardization choices (solar windows, timing, exposure/white
balance, radiometry, GSD/overlap, and metadata) often dominate apparent gains
from model changes.
•
Credible robustness claims require spatially and temporally aware evaluation (e.g.,
field/farm/site and season/year hold-outs); random splits are mainly in-distribution
references.
•
Adaptation is most effective when staged and symptom-driven, ranging from
lightweight normalization/augmentation to fine-tuning and alignment matched to
the dominant gap.
Robustness under distribution shift is emerging as a central obstacle to deploying
proximal and UAV imaging models in real agricultural settings. Across the 42 core studies
reviewed here, a consistent picture emerges in which models do not fail “in general”, but
in specific, diagnosable ways along four axes: scene, sensor, protocol, and time. Scene
changes alter canopy geometry, background clutter, and management regimes; sensor
changes bring different spectral response functions, resolutions, and noise properties;
protocol differences reshape view geometry, illumination, and radiometry; and temporal
drift across phenological stages, seasons, and years compounds these effects. Treating these
axes explicitly turns robustness from a vague objective into a concrete design space for
acquisition, evaluation, and adaptation.
This review also complements and extends existing surveys on UAV and proximal
phenotyping, crop monitoring, and agricultural machine learning. Prior reviews have
mainly organized the literature by trait (for example, disease detection [62], yield pre-
diction [63], or nitrogen status [64]), by sensor or platform (e.g., broad proximal/remote
phenomics overviews [65], UAV [66] versus proximal systems [50]), or by model family
(such as convolutional networks [67] or transformers [68] for crop imaging) and have
typically evaluated methods under in-distribution settings. Complementary perspective
papers in plant pathology argue that many reported gains do not translate to deployment
because training datasets are often not representative of real field variability (backgrounds,
devices, cultivars, growth stages and lighting), which inflates apparent performance and
obscures generalization limits [50,51]. Likewise, generic surveys on domain adaptation or
transfer learning in remote sensing seldom focus on field-scale crop monitoring or on the

concrete acquisition and evaluation constraints faced in agronomic experiments. Related
system-level reviews in precision weeding and directed-energy robotics emphasize that
real-world performance depends on an end-to-end perception–decision–actuation stack
(not just model accuracy), with practical constraints such as field capacity, automated navi-
gation, energy efficiency, affordability, and safety standards shaping what is deployable.
We cite these deployment-oriented lessons as complementary context for why our bench-
mark agenda stresses protocol disclosure, shift-aware splits, and reproducible reporting
for proximal and UAV crop imaging [69]. By contrast, the present review takes robustness
under distribution shift as its primary organizing principle and proposes a field-focused
taxonomy along four axes (scene, sensor, protocol, and time). It then uses a systematically
defined corpus of 42 studies to link this taxonomy to acquisition protocols, evaluation
designs, adaptation and updating strategies, and benchmark specifications. The goal is not
to catalog every phenotyping pipeline but to provide a coherent framework and a practical
decision aid for researchers and practitioners who need models that continue to work as
farms, sensors, and seasons change.
The empirical evidence summarized in Sections 3 and 4 shows that a well-designed
RGB baseline, acquired and evaluated carefully, already generalizes more broadly than
many practitioners expect. When ground sampling distance, view geometry, and illu-
mination are controlled, RGB models for head, fruit, and stress detection can maintain
performance across fields and seasons. Multispectral, hyperspectral, and thermal modali-
ties add robustness primarily when the trait of interest is explicitly physiological (nitrogen
status, chlorophyll, fuel moisture, water stress) or when lighting and background are
unstable in ways that RGB alone cannot compensate. In those settings, red-edge and
NIR bands, physics-guided spectral features, and canopy temperature relative to ambient
conditions dampen appearance shifts and stabilize trait estimation across sites, sensors,
and years. However, additional bands only improve robustness when radiometry, band
handling, and cross-sensor alignment are explicit; otherwise, they simply add new avenues
for domain shift.
Sections 5 and 6 highlight that robustness is often won or lost before the first model
is trained. Acquisition and standardization choices (solar windows, phenological timing,
exposure and white-balance control, panel or pseudo-invariant-feature use, GSD and over-
lap targets, metadata logging) frequently deliver larger gains than changing architectures.
Evaluation design is equally fundamental, as random splits at pixel or patch level systemat-
ically overestimate transferability by leaking micro-context across folds, whereas spatially
aware and leave-one-field/season/site/sensor-out protocols reveal the real generalization
gap and provide a realistic baseline for adaptation. On the model side, an incremental
adaptation plan, summarized in the roadmap of Figure 2, is more effective than any single
technique. Lightweight normalization and augmentation are low-cost first steps that can be
deployed even without target labels. When a modest fraction of a new site can be labeled,
small, spatially diverse target sets combined with careful fine-tuning of upper layers re-
anchor models to new cultivars, backgrounds, or management regimes without retraining
from scratch. Shallow feature alignment and calibration-transfer methods stabilize repre-
sentations across sensors and phenotyping platforms, while teacher–student consistency
schemes and related UDA strategies [61] provide label-free improvements when acqui-
sition differences dominate the gap. Style translation can bridge pure appearance shifts,
provided content is preserved, and test-time normalization, simple ensembles, and explicit
calibration further stabilize predictions and make confidence scores more trustworthy for
decision thresholds. Crucially, these techniques are most effective when they are matched
to observable symptoms rather than applied as generic recipes.

Section 7 shows that public benchmarks already offer many of the ingredients needed
for shift-aware evaluation. GWHD and MinneApple, together with emerging domain-shift
benchmarks in grapes, weeds, diseases, and root/soil imaging, support leave-one-site and
leave-one-season protocols and explicit cross-sensor or cross-platform tests. At the same
time, important gaps remain: grain crops beyond wheat are underrepresented in UAV
and proximal datasets with explicit cross-farm and cross-season partitions; cross-sensor
suites acquired over the same plots with RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, and thermal
cameras are rare; and longitudinal multi-season datasets with consistent protocols are still
the exception rather than the rule. The minimal benchmark specification proposed here
is intended as a compact checklist for future dataset releases so that robustness claims
become more comparable and reproducible across crops, sites, sensors, and management
regimes. The terms “deployability” and “auditability” are framed here as operational
goals. The purpose of this specification is to make such claims testable and comparable
via shared OOD splits, protocol disclosure, and paired ID/OOD reporting rather than
anecdotal demonstrations.
This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the core corpus
is restricted to proximal and UAV imaging studies retrieved by a specific query over a
defined set of crops (wheat, rice, maize, soybean, apple, citrus, mango, tomato, grape, and
pear) and sensing terms, and only articles written in English were considered. Relevant
work in other languages, crops outside this list, or studies indexed under different termi-
nology may therefore have been missed. Second, the focus on title-level robustness terms
(e.g., “domain adaptation”, “transfer learning”, “calibration transfer”, “out of distribu-
tion”) deliberately concentrates on studies where distribution shift is central but necessarily
excludes many otherwise high-quality sensing or modeling articles that do not explicitly
highlight these concepts. Third, the included studies are methodologically heterogeneous
in tasks, sensors, and evaluation protocols, which precludes any formal meta-analysis of
effect sizes. The synthesis here is descriptive and conceptual rather than statistical. Fourth,
purely satellite-based works, non-imaging sensor networks, and broader digital-agriculture
systems are only touched upon when they inform proximal/UAV pipelines, and thus
the conclusions should not be directly extrapolated to those domains. Finally, while the
search and coding protocol aimed to be systematic and consistent, it was implemented by a
limited number of raters and inevitably reflects judgment calls in borderline cases. These
limitations do not undermine the main messages of the review, but they highlight that the
taxonomy and recommendations proposed here should be refined and extended as the
empirical base on robustness under distribution shift in agricultural imaging continues
to grow.
In summary, robustness under distribution shift in proximal and UAV agricultural
imaging is less about discovering a single “universal” architecture and more about design-
ing sensing, evaluation, and adaptation pipelines that acknowledge how and why data
change across farms and seasons. By making shift axes explicit, standardizing acquisition
and evaluation, and matching adaptation tools to observable symptoms, the community
can move from ad hoc fixes toward more deployable and auditable pipelines, provided that
robustness claims are supported by standardized OOD splits and reporting (as summarized
by the benchmark specification and minimal experiment suite).
9. Future Research Directions
Looking ahead, several lines of work appear promising. First, future datasets would
benefit from being designed explicitly for out-of-distribution evaluation, with built-in
leave-one-out splits, multiple farms or management units, and at least one held-out season,
accompanied by protocol data documenting flight timing, radiometry, sensor settings,
https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy16030364

28 of 32
and ground-truth procedures (including released split files or deterministic seeds, plus
baseline evaluation scripts for direct comparability). Second, cross-sensor and cross-scale
benchmarks, where the same plots are imaged with multiple cameras (RGB, MS, HS,
thermal) and at multiple scales (proximal, UAV, satellite), would enable systematic studies
of sensor replacement, fusion, and band compression without confounding scene changes
(ideally with band centers/FWHM or spectral response information and a consistent
radiometric workflow). Third, longitudinal and multi-management datasets spanning
rotations, management changes, and climate anomalies are needed to compare lightweight
periodic updates, heavier UDA pipelines, and purely physics-guided approaches, and to
quantify how often models need to be recalibrated or fine-tuned in practice (including
explicit update schedules, e.g., per season or per management change, so “how often
to update” becomes measurable). Fourth, systematic label-efficiency curves and active-
learning strategies under shared OOD splits would help practitioners plan labeling and
flight campaigns with clear trade-offs (using standardized target-label budgets such as
0/5/10/20% under the same OOD split). Finally, embedding decision-aware robustness
metrics, including calibration, uncertainty, and cost- or risk-based summaries that reflect
management actions, would better align technical progress with agronomic and economic
value (including simple decision-threshold reporting with recommended operating points
when models trigger management actions).
