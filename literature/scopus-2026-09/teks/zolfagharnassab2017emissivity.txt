Emissivity determination of oil palm fresh fruit 
ripeness using a thermal imaging technique 
S. Zolfagharnassab1, A.R. Mohamed Shariff1,a and R. Ehsani2 
1Depatment of Biological and Agricultural Engineering, University Putra Malaysia, Malaysia; 2Citrus Research and 
Education Center, University of Florida, USA. 
Abstract 
Thermal imaging is a technique to transform the invisible radiation pattern of 
an object into visible images for feature extraction and analysis. Infrared thermal 
imaging has a wide application in several fields such as engineering, aerospace, 
agriculture, civil, medicine, and veterinary. Emissivity is one of the important 
parameters used to determine the surface temperature of agricultural products and 
also to manage some agricultural engineering procedures. The purpose of this paper 
is to determine total emissivity as an essential parameter of non-destructive 
thermometry for oil palm fresh fruit bunches in different stage of ripeness (hard 
bunch, under-ripe, ripe, over-ripe and rotten bunch). Moreover, in this research, the 
influence of surface temperature of oil palm fresh fruit bunches on the ripeness was 
investigated whereby the fresh fruit bunches were heated to 100°C. Then, a thermal 
imaging camera and contact thermometer were used to determine the surface 
temperature of the bunches. The results show that emissivity of hard bunches is 94%, 
the emissivity of under-ripe, ripe and over-ripe is 95%, and the emissivity of rotten 
bunches is 98%. At these percentages, the thermal camera and contact thermometer 
demonstrate the same temperature. 
Keywords: oil palm, thermal imaging, emissivity, ripeness, FLIR, infrared 
INTRODUCTION 
Oil palm is the most productive vegetable oil crop, yielding several times more oil ha-1 
than any other major vegetable oil source and contributes 33.3% to the total world 
vegetable oil production and fats (United Stated Department of Agriculture Foreign 
Agricultural Service, 2011). Malaysia produces up to 18,400 thousand metric tons and is the 
second largest producer of palm oil which comprises 86% of worldwide exports (United 
Stated Department of Agriculture Foreign Agricultural Service, 2011). 
One of the challenges in processing of fruits during oil production is the grading of oil 
palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) for their ripeness (Saeed et al., 2012). In conventional 
methods, an oil palm FFB is harvested manually by observing number of loose fruits. The 
number of loose fruits per bunch or the number of hollow cartridges on the FFB is the 
present indicator of degree of ripeness (Fadilah et al., 2012). This manual harvesting method 
is inaccurate, time consuming, and costly. Therefore, there is a need for rapid, reliable, and 
accurate techniques to quantify the oil palm fruit ripeness and oil content so that the harvest 
is timely and cost effective (Saeed et al., 2012). There have been several studies on potential 
methods for determining the ripeness of palm fruits and thus, indirectly the oil content of 
palm fruits without the need for laborious oil extraction and analysis. 
Thermometry is one of the scientific techniques used in agricultural industry. 
Temperature measurements in the agricultural and food industries have generally been 
done by conventional devices such as thermometers, thermocouples, and thermistors (Nott 
and Hall, 1999). These instruments require close interaction with materials, and 
determining the temperature is done only at the contact point. Conversely, thermal imaging 
is a non-destructive and non-interactive technique that can determine the temperature 
distribution of any object in short period of time. Therefore, the use of infrared thermal

imaging is widely increasing in fields that are involved with thermometry. Thermal imaging 
has vast applications in agriculture and the food industry specifically in the postharvesting 
stage (Farid et al., 2014a) in tasks such as grading, quality inspection (Farid et al., 2014b;

The basic principle of thermal imaging is based on the fact that all objects above 0 K 
(-273.15°C) emit infrared radiation which is part of the electromagnetic spectrum 
(Manickavasagan et al., 2005). Thermal imaging records temperature by measuring infrared 
radiation emitted by a surface (Arora et al., 2008). It utilizes the radiation emitted to 
produce a pseudo image of the thermal distribution of a body’s surface (Gowen et al., 2010). 
The temperature of an object under investigation cannot be determined by measuring the 
total radiant remittance alone, as the emissivity (ɛ) must also be known (Kölzer et al., 1996). 
Emissivity is defined as the ratio of energy emitted from an object to that of a black body at 
the same temperature, and it can vary from 0 (perfect white body) to 1 (perfect black body) 
(Gowen et al., 2010). Equation 1 shows the basic principle of the thermal imaging technique 
in 8-14 wavelength (Gowen et al., 2010). 
W=ơ ɛ T4  
(1) 
where: W = total amount of energy emitted from the target (W m-2); ơ = Stefan-Boltzmann 
constant (5.67×10-8 Wm-2 K-4); ɛ = the emissivity of the target; T = the temperature of the 
target (K). 
Based on Equation 1, the thermal camera can calculate the total amount of energy that 
emitted from the target (W) if the emissivity of the material is known. Emissivity is the 
measure of how much radiation is emitted from the object, compared to that from a perfect 
blackbody at the same temperature. Emissivity indicates the amount of lost or earn in the 
thermal radiation by a target (Anonymous, 2011). There are two sorts of emissivity; spectral 
emissivity and hemispherical or total emissivity. It is important to know the spectral 
emissivity for non-interactive determination of the material temperature. There, it is 
assumed that wavelength has no effect on spectral emissivity of the surface, so the emissivity 
is expected to be a constant. However, to determine the heat transfer, the total emissivity 
should be measured (Anonymous, 2011; Kheiralipour et al., 2012). 
Emissivity tables are provided for different materials in the literature, but no 
emissivity measurement for oil palm is available to date. Therefore, in this paper, we present 
an investigation of total emissivity of oil palm FFB in different stages of its maturity. 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
All data collection was performed at United Plantation Research and Development in 
Teluk Intan (UPRD), Perak, Malaysia. 
Materials 
Oil palm fresh fruit bunches of ‘Nigresens’ cultivars were examined according to five 
categories. Hard bunch, under-ripe, ripe, over-ripe and rotten bunch. For each category ten 
samples were evaluated. FLIR E60 and FLIR T440 thermal imaging cameras (FLIR thermal 
imaging cameras - FLIR Infrared, 2013) were used for non-destructive thermometry of oil 
palm, whereas for contact thermometry, thermometer was used. 
Emissivity determination 
The surface emissivity of bunches were measured by comparing an infrared thermal 
image with one from the standard contact thermometer at the same temperature. For this 
purpose, in the first step, samples were heated to 100°C, and then the temperature of one 
random point on the bunch was measured by the contact thermometer. In the next step the 
thermal camera was focused as close as possible to the same spot. The emissivity was

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
The emissivity of oil palm bunches for hard bunch, under-ripe, ripe, over-ripe and 
rotten bunch are demonstrated in Figure 2a-e, respectively. As Figure 2a shows, for a hard 
bunch, the recorded temperature by thermal camera in 94% emissivity is close to that 
recoded by the contact thermometer. As seen in Figure 2b-d for under ripe, ripe, and over 
ripe, the recorded temperature by thermal camera in 95% emissivity are close to that 
recorded by the contact thermometer. As shown in Figure 2a, the same temperature for the 
thermal camera and contact thermometer was recorded as 94% emissivity. The reason is 
that the emissivity was affected by some parameters such as color, roughness, and other 
physical and chemical charactrestics of materials. These characteristics for under-ripe, ripe, 
and overripe oil palm banches are close, hence the emissivity of these three stages of ripness 
are similar (95%). 
Figure 3 shows that there is a difference between the emissivity of hard bunches and 
rotten bunches and the other stages of oil palm maturity. As mentioned above, the emissivity 
is the ratio of radiated energy to that of black body at the same temperature, and also, the 
emissivity value depends on other parameters such as thickness, color, and roughness. 
Thinner materials have lower emissivity (Kheiralipour et al., 2012) and it can be seen here in 
general, the emissivity of oil palm bunches are high (more than 90%) due to the hardness 
and thickness of the fruits. 
Comparing the different stages, hard bunches have a lower emissivity (94%) due to 
their raw fruit structure. Upon the commencement of the ripening process, the fruit 
structure changes and therefore, the emissivity increases to 95%. In under-ripe, ripe, and 
over-ripe stages, because of low alteration and constant physical characteristics, the 
emissivity did not change considerably and it was fixed around 95%. In rotten bunches, the 
physical characteristics of the fruits completely change, and the emissivity value increases 
significantly, reaching 98%.

CONCLUSIONS 
The following conclusions can be drawn from the study: 
- In this research, a measurement method was developed to obtain the emissivity of 
oil palm fresh fruit bunches as an important parameter for thermal imaging. 
- The results show that the emissivity of oil palm in three important stages of 
ripeness; under ripe, ripe, and over ripe is 95%. 
- Hence, in all application of infrared thermal camera to quantify the oil palm fresh 
fruit ripeness and oil content, the emissivity can be set as this value.
