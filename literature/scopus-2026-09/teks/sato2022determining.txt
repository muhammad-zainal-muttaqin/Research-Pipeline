Abstract— Decreasing numbers of farmers and an aging 
farming population pose challenges for Japanese agriculture. 
This has led to issues such as difficulties maintaining crop 
quality and ensuring productivity. In addition, the cultivation of 
fruit trees, such as apples requires maintenance labor for tasks 
such as thinning. These tasks are difficult to quantify and 
therefore are mainly performed by experienced agricultural 
workers based on their intuition. In this study, we used a general 
object detection algorithm and an RGB-D camera to visualize 
fruit cultivation work and determine the fruiting density of 
apple trees.  
Keywords—YOLOX, Object detection, RGB-D camera, 
Fruiting management 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
In recent years, Japan's agricultural industry has suffered 
from a labor shortage due to a numerically declining and aging 
workforce; this effect has also been evident in Akita Prefecture 
[1]. 
In the case of horticultural crops such as apples, which are 
grown mostly in the inland areas of Akita Prefecture, flower 
and fruit picking are essential to obtain high-quality fruit. 
However, many of these operations are dependent on the 
experience and intuition of workers who have cultivated their 
skills over many years; the tasks require careful thinning while 
considering fruiting density and branch length as well as the 
distance between adjacent fruits. Considerable differences are 
often observed in agricultural techniques. Therefore, variations 
in fruiting management techniques and the transfer of 
cultivation techniques to new farmers pose difficult challenges. 
The objective of this study was to develop a system that 
simplifies the process of flower and fruit picking by 
recognizing the fruiting density and spatial arrangement of fruit 
trees, and by visualizing cultivation techniques at an 
experimental fruit tree station through information and image 
processing. 
II. 
PRIOR ART 
A. Generic Object Detection 
General object detection algorithms based on deep 
learning are designed recognize learned objects in videos 
based on supervised data. They can be broadly classified into 
two main categories based on process used to recognize items; 
two-stage object detectors, such as faster region-based 
convolutional neural network (Faster R-CNN) models, 
perform object region detection and region candidate category 
identification sequentially. In contrast, You Only Look Once 
(YOLO) [2] and single-shot detector (SSD) [3] methods are 
classified as single-stage object detectors, which perform 
region extraction and category identification simultaneously. 
In this study, we used a method called YOLOX [4], which has 
exhibited significantly improved detection accuracy compared 
to conventional methods such as YOLOv4 [5]. 
III. 
TEST METHOD 
A. Outline 
An action camera (GoPro) attached to a worker's head 
recorded images and videos while they picked apples from 
apple trees grown at the Akita Fruit-Tree Experiment Station 
(Yokote City, Akita Pref.), to create a discriminator for buds, 
flowers, and young fruits. A general object detection 
algorithm was used to create the discriminator. In addition, 
the data were recorded with an RGB-D camera capable of 
capturing depth information, and these data were combined to 
analyze the spacing between neighboring fruits and the 
fruiting density. 
B. Flower and Fruit Recognition Methods 
A general object-detection algorithm was used to 
recognize flowers and young fruits. The following procedure 
was performed on the images. 
1) Creation of training data: Still images were collected 
every few frames from the video footage of the work recorded 
at the Akita Fruit-Tree Experiment Station. A total of 1,492 
still images were created, 102 of which were obtained from 
the video captured on May 3, 2020, 815 of which were taken 
on June 3, 2020, and 575 of which were taken on June 4, 
2020. In terms of the relationship between the time of the year 
and flowering and fruiting of apple trees, the images taken on 
May 3 contained many buds and flowers, whereas the images 
taken on June 3 and 4 mainly recorded young apples. For 
these images, rectangular regions were set using the 
annotation tool “labelImg,” and three types of tag 
information, “Bud,” Flower,” and “YoungApple,” were 
assigned. Table I presents a breakdown of the tags assigned 
to all 1,492 pieces of data.  
2) Creating Learning Models: The data was used 
construct training and evaluation set at a ratio of 8:2, with 
1,194 pieces of training data and 298 pieces of evaluation 
data. A breakdown of the tags assigned to each is presented 
in Table II. We trained a YOLOX model on these data.

C. Spatial Understanding of Apple Trees 
The spatial understanding of apple trees was achieved by 
recognizing flowers and young fruits from video images and 
acquiring depth information. 
The mentioned discriminator was constructed based on the 
video images collected from the action camera. However, 
apple trees must also be measured three-dimensionally to 
calculate their fruiting density. Therefore, in this study, we 
also used an RGB-D camera capable of acquiring depth 
information.

B. Results of Obtaining Depth Information 
To obtain depth information, we used a RealSense-brand 
RGB-D camera manufactured by Intel Corporation. In 
addition to the normal color image, depth information 
indicating the distance from the camera can be acquired by this 
device as well. The depth information at the center coordinates 
of the detected object rectangles was acquired, and the 3D 
Euclidean distance between them was calculated to obtain the 
density of observed fruit. Some depth information was missing 
due to shadows, etc., and was complemented by filter 
processing [6]. 
The RGB-D camera was mounted on a monopod and 
connected to a laptop computer, and aligned with the line of 
sight roughly equivalent to that of an agricultural worker 
picking fruit on a stepladder. Examples of the acquired RGB 
and depth images are shown in Figs. 1 and 2, respectively.

V. 
CONCLUSION 
In this study, we developed a discriminator to identify buds, 
flowers, and young fruits of apple trees from video images. 
We also conducted a basic experimental evaluation of a 
system designed to use depth information for recognizing the 
density of sets of fruit on a tree as well as the spatial 
arrangement of fruit trees. 
We have developed a learning model that can identify each 
object from videos of actual fruit tree management operations. 
In addition, by acquiring depth information and combining it 
with image recognition results, we have developed a method 
to calculate the Euclidean distance between fruits in three 
dimensions. 
In future research, we plan to improve the accuracy and 
recognition speed of the object recognition method and 
evaluate the accuracy of the acquired depth information.
