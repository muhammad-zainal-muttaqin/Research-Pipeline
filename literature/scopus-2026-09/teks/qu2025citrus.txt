A B S T R A C T
Accurate and efficient measurement of citrus fruit size is essential for managing tree form and estimating yields. 
Conventional manual methods are reliable but highly labour-intensive, while existing machine vision solutions 
often require specialised setups (e.g., distance calibration or 3D sensors). In this study, a low-cost, monocular- 
based framework that uses mature and healthy leaves as natural reference objects was proposed, eliminating the 
need for manual markers or complex camera parameter calibration. By compiling an offline leaf-size distribution 
from multiple citrus varieties, this method automatically converts fruit pixels to real-world diameters using the 
largest near-frontal leaf in each image. Further, the work integrates the deformable convolution (DNCv2) and 
shuffle attention (SA) into a YOLOv8 detector to improve occlusion handling, ensuring robust detection even 
when fruits are partially obscured by foliage. Extensive validation on three different citrus cultivars shows that 
leaf-size variability contributes less than 3.2% relative error in diameter estimation, while the overall approach 
achieves 93.14% accuracy and R2 = 0.76. Key contributions include: (1) a novel monocular technique leveraging 
inherent orchard elements (leaves) as references, (2) advanced detection modules to tackle partial occlusion, (3) 
cross-variety validation demonstrating consistent performance, and (4) a fast, user-friendly workflow suitable for 
real-world orchard applications. Future work will explore multi-frame or multi-view strategies to further refine 
diameter measurement under heavy occlusion.
1. Introduction
Citrus is one of the fruits with the largest cultivated area, highest 
yield, and greatest consumption worldwide (Joshi et al., 2023). 
Measuring fruit size and estimating the distribution of fruit diameter in 
citrus trees are valuable for orchard management and yield estimation 
(Islam et al., 2022). Traditional manual measurement methods not only 
require operators to minimise measurement errors but are also 
cumbersome and time-consuming (Neupane et al., 2023). With the 
advancement of science and technology, these tasks have been partially 
automated. Computer vision-based techniques for fruit diameter mea­
surement have gradually attracted widespread attention from re­
searchers (Mohimont et al., 2022; Neupane et al., 2023).
Several methods based on 2D and 3D technologies have achieved 
notable success in fruit diameter measurement (Neupane et al., 2023). 
Some studies have utilised traditional image processing and machine 
learning techniques, such as fitting algorithms for ellipsoids and spheres, 
to extract the size of fruits from images (Akin et al., 2012; Behera et al., 
2021; Chen et al., 2021; Gen´e-Mola et al., 2023; Neupane et al., 2022). 
Other studies have employed colour threshold-based methods for esti­
mating fruit size (Huynh et al., 2022). However, these methods are 
negatively influenced by the quality of image segmentation and fruit 
occlusion (Ferrer-Ferrer et al., 2023), and typically require manual 
placement of reference objects (Wang et al., 2018). In addition, some 
studies have estimated fruit size by pre-determining the distance be­
tween the camera and the fruit, thereby eliminating the need for refer­
ence objects (Huynh et al., 2020; Li et al., 2018; Ponce et al., 2019; Rizzo 
et al., 2023). Shao et al. (2022) measured tree diameter at breast height 
(DBH) using a smartphone, although a laser rangefinder was employed 
for distance measurement.
However, these methods require transporting the fruit to a desig­
nated location for measurement or necessitate prior measurement of 
distance information, which presents significant limitations. To address 
these challenges, researchers have explored 3D methods for fruit 
diameter measurement, such as depth camera and point cloud mea­
surement technologies (Neupane et al., 2022). These approaches utilise 
depth cameras to acquire depth information for fruit size estimation (Fu 
et al., 2020; Zaenker et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2022). Related studies 
suggest that the Kinect v2 ToF camera is preferable over the ZED stereo

However, depth measurements are influenced by lighting conditions 
(Kuan et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2017). Some methods utilise point cloud 
data of the fruit and its surrounding environment for 3D reconstruction 
to estimate size (Hartley et al., 2021; Wang & Chen, 2020; Yu et al.,

3D-based fruit diameter measurement methods have shown some suc­

their widespread application in practical scenarios.
Although a few monocular cameras, such as certain iPhone models, 
are capable of automatically measuring the sizes of objects in different 
spatial planes, they are equipped with an integrated LiDAR sensor 
(Tatsumi et al., 2023). Most monocular cameras (e.g., smartphones) do 
not capture depth information in their images, which makes it chal­
lenging to accurately measure the sizes of fruits located at varying 
spatial positions within a field taken image. Therefore, addressing the 
challenge of inconsistent distances between tree-hanging fruits and the 
monocular camera is essential for overcoming these limitations.
The size of mature leaves is relative stable and measurable statisti­
cally, leveraging this information to infer the size of nearby fruit is 
helpful. However, leaves are usually tilted away from camera and fruits 
are likely to be partially occluded in the fields. An increasing number of 
researchers are beginning to employ deep learning algorithms to address 
fruit detection and segmentation tasks (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2023; Tian 
et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022; Wang & He, 2022). Lu et al. (2022)
measured apple sizes using an improved YOLOv4 model; Hu et al. 
(2023) estimated crop internode lengths with an enhanced YOLOv5 
model; Nenavath and Perumal (2024) used a YOLOv7 model to measure 
mango sizes. Sapkota et al. (2024) achieved excellent results in 
measuring the diameter of unripe apples using the YOLOv8 model, with 
a mAP@0.5 value of 0.94. Compared to other models in the YOLO series, 
YOLOv8 offers significant advantages in terms of speed, prediction ac­
curacy, and robustness. Compared to other models in the YOLO series, 
YOLOv8 offers significant advantages in terms of speed, prediction ac­
curacy, and robustness. However, in field conditions with various leaf 
density and possibly occludes with each other, accurately detecting, 
segmenting and measuring leaf and fruit is difficult.
Gim´enez-Gallego et al. (2024) attempted to address the occlusion 
issue by elliptically fitting the pomegranate using a custom segmenta­
tion method. While this approach is effective in handling occlusion, it is 
difficult to eliminate the dependence on parameters. In their setup, they 
extracted the fruit mask from the full mask, leaving a 30-pixel margin on 
each side, and then scaled the cropped mask by a factor of four. The use 
of custom methods to solve real-world problems can lead to varying 
results depending on different parameter settings. This is particularly 
important to field applications, where impact factors are multiple and 
usually interacted with each other. For instance, if using nearby leaves 
as a reference to fruit size, how to choose cropping box size in each 
image could significantly affect the number of available leaves and 
consequently the statistical plausible inference to fruit size.
In summary, automatic measuring citrus fruit size in the field faces 
several critical challenges. While traditional 2D methods often need 
external markers or measured reference objects before applying, 3D 
methods usually lead to high cost and complex calibration. In field 
conditions, fruit can be partially hidden by leaves, branches, or other 
fruits, resulting in missed detections or incorrect size estimates. There­
fore, this research proposed a novel monocular-based computer vision 
framework that streamlines fruit diameter measurement by leveraging 
readily available orchard elements (leaves), employing a robust detec­
tion model for occlusions, and avoiding costly or labour-intensive cali­
bration procedures. With thorough and additional tests on model 
parameters, such as the effect of leaf density, sizes of cropping boxes, 
etc., this work aims to provide a fast, user-friendly workflow suitable for 
real-world orchard applications. The key features of the method are 
four-fold: 
1) Leveraging mature and healthy leaves as “built-in” references, which 
removes the need to place and measure physical objects around each 
fruit;
2) Cropping image around each fruit (and its nearby leaves) to reduce 
large portion of differences in fruit-leaf distance to the camera, which 
significantly mitigate the issues of inhomogeneous distances be­
tween tree-hanging fruits and the monocular camera;
3) Bypassing explicit calibration by using statistics of an offline leaf-size 
distribution to scale fruit pixels to real-world measurements, enables 
handling cross-variety differences without separate calibrations in 
the field;
4) Integrating deformable convolution (DNCv2) and shuffle attention 
(SA) within YOLOv8 improves detection robustness, helping the 
system locate partially occluded fruits and enhance robustness.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Overview
This study aimed to develop a deep learning model for measuring 
fruit diameters on citrus trees in real-world scenarios, which will be 
eventually used to estimate the distribution of fruit size of the entire 
tree. Initially, images of citrus fruits at various stages of maturity were 
collected and annotated. Subsequently, the actual lengths of the leaves 
surrounding the fruits from three common citrus varieties were recor­
ded, and a leaf dimension dataset was then established and the best 
statistical distribution was fitted to determine the size of the reference 
objects. The estimation of fruit diameters is carried out in three stages. In 
the first stage, the positioning and cropping of the fruits were achieved 
using object detection algorithms. In the second stage, the reference 
objects were further positioned by identifying the leaves within the 
cropping boxes around each target fruit, with a set of strategies filtering 
out uncharacteristically large blob or irregular leaf shape. In the third 
stage, the fruit diameters were estimated using triangulation algorithms 
based on the positioned reference leaf. Finally, the measured fruit di­
ameters across the entire tree were statistically analysed, and the 
diameter distribution was fitted. The accuracy of the measurements was 
then evaluated. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

2.3. Data labelling
This step prepares data annotations for subsequent steps such as the 
fruit positioning and cropping stage, in which the aim is to locate and 
crop the fruit within the images. To this end, a dataset of 700 citrus 
images was selected, and bounding boxes were manually annotated for 
each citrus fruit in the images. In the subsequent reference object 
positioning stage, in addition to continuing the identification of the 
fruits, it is also necessary to locate leaves surrounding the fruits. Based 
on the detection results from the previous stage, 600 cropped images 
were selected for further annotation, with all fruits and their sur­
rounding leaves manually labelled in the images.
2.4. Reference statistics
Our key insight is to exploit mature and healthy citrus leaves as 
natural reference objects, effectively turning the orchard’s own foliage 
into scale markers. Specifically, leaves around each fruit were measured, 
then compiled an offline distribution of all leaf lengths from multiple 
citrus varieties, allowing any sufficiently large, near-frontal leaf in an 
image to serve as a standard measurement unit.
To reduce the discrepancy between the offline leaf length data and 
the actual field conditions, the method for collecting leaf length data was 
optimised. Specifically, during the offline data collection, only six leaves 
near the fruit were measured, while those further from the fruit were not 
included. Additionally, measurements were taken only for nearby leaves 
that appeared larger, healthy, intact, and fully expanded. To avoid 
dehydration and curling of the leaves due to harvesting, the larger, 
easily accessible, fully expanded leaves on the tree were directly 
measured using callipers. In total, 485 leaves directly on the citrus trees 
were measured. Because the measurement process involved aligning the 
calliper tip from leaf base to apex, slight differences in angle or curva­
ture were effectively “flattened out” for length measurements. In other 
words, the true physical length of each leaf rather was measured than its 
apparent length from a particular camera perspective. This ensures that 
the 485 samples capture the biological variability of real-world leaves.
Based on the 485 leaves measured from three different citrus vari­
eties: Chun Jian, Navel Oranges, and Blood Oranges, the distribution for 
the combined dataset was fitted and also for each variety separately 
(using statistical analysis tools JMP Pro based on the goodness-of-fit), 
reflecting observed morphological differences (Fig. 3). The combined 
dataset is best fitted as a normal distribution with mean = 7.48 cm and 
standard deviation = 0.98 cm (model fit: AICc = 1363.09 and BIC =
1371.44, see Fig. 3A).

diameter. However, each variety’s true leaf-size distribution can differ 
from that single mean, either being larger, smaller, or more/less vari­
able, which may pose negative effects to the final estimation, so it will be 
further tested and discussed.
2.5. Three stages of fruit diameter estimation
The fundamental principle of citrus fruit diameter estimation in­
volves that based on the combined offline statistical data on the lengths 
of three common citrus leaves, converts fruit pixels to real-world di­
ameters using the largest near-frontal leaf adjacent to the fruit. The 
process consists of three stages: the fruit positioning and cropping, the 
reference positioning, and the fruit diameter estimation (Fig. 4). The 
entire process was implemented based on the YOLOv8 model.
2.5.1. Network preparing and optimising
This research implements fruit diameter estimation functionality 
within the YOLOv8 framework. Furthermore, to enhance the detection 
accuracy of fruits and leaves within the cropping boxes and improve 
performance under occlusion, the model’s ability to learn input features 
has been strengthened by introducing Deformable Convolution (DNCv2) 
with module Shuffle Attention (SA). The network architecture of DS- 
YOLOv8 is illustrated in Fig. 5.

the standard convolution kernels in the C2f module limits the network’s 
receptive field, restricting it to capturing only local information about 
objects and making it more susceptible to occlusion and other factors

Deformable Convolutions enhance the adaptability of convolutional 
neural networks to spatial transformations in images by introducing 
learnable offsets (Eq. (1)), making them suitable for addressing occlu­
sion issues in citrus images. Under occlusion, traditional convolutions 
with fixed sampling positions often fail to capture the complete features 
of occluded objects, negatively impacting recognition and classification 
accuracy. In contrast, deformable convolution can dynamically adjust 
the sampling positions of the convolutional kernels to "bypass" occluded 
areas, focusing on capturing information-rich regions that are not 
occluded, thereby enhancing the ability to predict fruit bounding boxes. 
Additionally, DNCv2 incorporates a modulation mechanism that adjusts 
the amplitude (intensity) of the input features, controlling the signal 
strength received from different spatial locations, which further im­
proves its ability to handle occlusion (Zhu et al., 2019). This adaptive 
receptive field adjustment enables the model to more accurately locate 
and identify partially occluded fruits or leaves, improving the network’s 
robustness in complex environments.

2.5.2. Fruit positioning and cropping
In this stage, the fruits within the images are located and cropped 
using YOLOv8 (Fig. 4a). By enlarging the fruit detection boxes by a 
constant factor (e.g., 2x, 2.5x, or 3x) and cropping the expanded 
detection boxes, this method ensures a high chance that the resulting 
cropped images contain several complete leaves adjacent to the fruits. 
This cropping approach allows only nearby leaves can be considered as 
the reference to the target fruit, addressing the measurement challenges 
arising from the inhomogeneous distances between the hanging fruits 
and the monocular camera. The effectiveness of this effort has been 
confirmed by our previous experiments, which showed that when the 
distance between the leaf and the fruit is relatively small, the distance 
from the leaf to the camera can be considered equivalent to the distance 
from the fruit to the camera. Different sizes of cropping boxes might 
yield varying measurement results. This uncertainty will be tested and 
analysed in Section 2.6.2.
2.5.3. Reference positioning
After images around each fruit are cropped, in addition to re- 
detecting the fruits, it is necessary to locate and choose suitable leaves 
around as the candidate of reference and obtain the pixel values of both 
the fruits and the reference leaves.
Leaf pose, maturity and position in field conditions can significantly 
affect the detected dimension. Leaves around a fruit might be tilted 
away from camera, or resemble a single leaf which often have an un­
characteristically large blob or irregular shape. A leaf that is tilted away 
from the camera or partially occluded can appear smaller in the image, 
causing potential underestimation if used directly as a reference. Vari­
ability in leaf growth and conditions can also have potential effect on 
dimensions of the detected leaves, because differences in cultivar,

To solve these issues, a three-step solution is utilised to filter out 
irregular shapes and choose suitable leaf as reference. Firstly, the leaf 
shape is validated by analysing candidate regions for typical citrus leaf 
profiles, e.g., bounding box dimension, aspect ratio and contour 
smoothness. Heavily tilted, overlapped and immature leaves often form 
irregular shapes or dimension that depart from these expected metrics, 
so they are filtered out. The expected metrics are established from the 
dataset of actual citrus leaf lengths and shapes under various orchard 
conditions. In the filtering inference, any candidate “leaf” whose pixel 
dimensions exceeds the known maximum or fall outside a plausible 
aspect ratio is flagged as invalid. This process treats invalid leaves as 
outliers that are two standard deviations away from the mean of the 
offline leaf-size distribution. Finally, the largest valid leaf within each 
cropped image is selected. In addition to the maximum leaf length, the 
average length of the valid leaves is also considered as comparison, 
which has been demonstrated as part of the experiments.

actual length to its pixel length into the measured fruit diameter. This 
approach eliminates the reliance on depth information and camera pa­
rameters, thus achieving the goal of fruit diameter estimation. Finally, 
all calculated fruit diameter values across the entire tree are statistically 
analysed to generate a diameter distribution plot.

2.6.2. Parameter selection
During the process of fruit diameter estimation, the use of cropping 
boxes with different sizes and the selection of maximum or average of 
candidate leaves as reference objects may both influence the accuracy of 
the estimation results.
Directly using the detection bounding boxes of the fruits to crop often 
excludes leaves, which are crucial reference objects for estimating fruit 
diameter. Therefore, it becomes necessary to expand the size of the 
cropping box. In this experiment, three different expansion ratios, spe­
cifically, 2 times, 2.5 times, and 3 times the size of the fruit prediction 
box, were selected as the tentative size of the cropping box. For instance, 
if the prediction box of the fruit is 100 × 100 pixels, the corresponding 
cropping box sizes should be 200 × 200 pixels, 250 × 250 pixels, and 
300 × 300 pixels, respectively. This approach aims to investigate the 
impact of different cropping box sizes on the accuracy of fruit diameter 
estimation.
Subsequently, the method outlined in Section 2.5.3 was employed to 
determine the pixel length of the valid leaves. These data were used in 
two distinct calculations: the calculation of the maximum pixel length 
and the calculation of the average pixel length. The leaf with the 
maximum pixel length and the leaf with the average pixel length were 
identified by calculating the pixel length of the diagonals of all leaf 
detection boxes. This study explored the accuracy of fruit diameter 
estimation using leaves with these two distinct features as references, 
respectively.
2.6.3. Handling leaf sparsity and occlusion
When using leaves as reference objects to estimate fruit diameter, 
high leaf density may cause occlusion, while low leaf density might 
cause reference unavailability and consequently affect the positioning of 
the reference objects. This experiment tested the effect of high leaf 
density (Fig. 7A) and low leaf density (Fig. 7B) on the accuracy of fruit 
diameter measurement.
In addition, an ablation experiment was also included in this section 
to examine the accuracy of the DS-YOLOv8 model under occlusion. The 
performance of the DS-YOLOv8 model was compared with that of the 
original model, the model with only deformable convolutions added, 
and the model with only the shuffle attention mechanism added, to 
analyse the effectiveness of DS-YOLOv8 under occlusion.
2.6.4. Accuracy and robustness
To validate the accuracy and robustness of the model, three citrus 
varieties with distinct appearances were selected for fruit diameter 
measurement: Chun Jian (Citrus reticulata ’Chun Jian’), Navel Oranges 
(Citrus sinensis var. navel), and Blood Oranges (Citrus sinensis var. 
sanguinea).

3.2. Parameter selection
In this experiment, fruit diameter estimation was conducted to test 
different size of cropping boxes and selecting maximum or average 
leaves as reference objects. A relative error plot (Fig. 9) and a summary

The fruit diameter estimation is most accurate when the cropping 
box is 2.5 times the size of the prediction box and the leaf with the 
maximum pixel length is used as a reference object, achieving R2 = 0.75 
(Table 1).
When the cropping box size is equal to that of the prediction box, the 
method often becomes ineffective as it may fail to include any leaves. 
When the cropping box is 2x or 3x the size of the prediction box, the 
estimation accuracy is affected due to either an insufficient or excessive 
number of leaves in the cropped image, resulting in the failure to detect 
a complete leaf with the maximum size or the detected leaf being too 
distant from the fruit. Using leaves with average pixel length as refer­

reduced performance. In contrast, using leaves with the maximum pixel 
length as reference objects is less affected by external factors such as

mation accuracy.

gathered from multiple varieties, the need for external calibration ob­

model’s advantages are analysed and its limitations for directing future

4.1. Model advantages
In the framework proposed in this work, manual references or 
camera parameters are no longer needed. Previous methods often placed 
rulers or known objects in the scene, or required knowledge of smart­
phone/camera parameters for scaling. Apolo-Apolo et al. (2020)
measured citrus sizes by placing a wooden ruler of known dimensions. 
Zhao et al. (2023) used a multi-camera smartphone to predict the phe­
notypes of an apple tree, but it is necessary to know the parameters of 
the phone, such as focal length, etc. Our method sidesteps these re­
quirements by using mature and healthy leaves as natural references. 
This not only removes the need for manual setup but also simplifies fruit 
diameter measurement, since focal length or other camera parameters 
become unnecessary.
A a computer vision solution with minimal cost and fast runtime is 
provided. Unlike approaches relying on depth sensors or LiDAR (e.g., 
Kinect, time-of-flight, or laser scanning), this study requires no speci­
alised equipment beyond a basic camera, thereby eliminating extra costs 
and logistics. Wang et al. (2017) utilised a Kinect RGB-D depth camera 
to obtain depth information between the camera and the fruits, applying 
the thin lens formula to measure the diameters of mangoes in an or­
chard. The RMSE values of mangoes’ width and length were 4.3 mm and 
4.9 mm, respectively. Gongal et al. (2018) used a time-of-flight (ToF) 
camera to measure the distance between apples and the camera and 
estimated apples diameters based on pixel measurements, achieving an 
accuracy of 84.8% (MAPE = 15.2%). Moreover, past work with 3D 
scanning has often been time-consuming, requiring hours to process 
each orchard section. M´endez et al. (2019) utilised LiDAR to obtain 
point cloud data and combined it with the K-Means algorithm to esti­
mate citrus fruit diameters. However, acquiring point cloud data with 
laser scanning is time-consuming, requiring an average of 8 h to measure 
each study area as reported. Here, the diameters of all fruits on a single 
tree can be measured in about a minute, which is a significant 
improvement in efficiency.
This method improved performance by handling occlusion and depth 
variation. The experiments confirm that the proposed method accu­
rately measures citrus fruit diameters in real-world orchard settings. By 
embedding deformable convolution (DNCv2) and shuffle attention (SA) 
into YOLOv8, detection performance under partial occlusion improves 
over the original network. This allows the system to detect and measure 
more fruits, which are often obscured by foliage, while keeping costs and 
complexity low. In practical terms, the method achieves overall high 
measurement accuracy cross all three varieties (MAPE = 6.86%) using 
only a monocular camera, making it accessible for large-scale orchard 
deployments without additional hardware. Unlike other studies that 
largely ignore occluded fruits, our DNCv2 + SA-enhanced YOLOv8 helps 
detect partially hidden citrus. Ferrer-Ferrer et al. (2023) used instance 
segmentation and size regression methods to calculate apple sizes, 
achieving MAE = 5.64 mm. However, as they mentioned, they did not 
account for measuring occluded fruits. To further reduce errors, images 
of fruit trees are taken from multiple angles, consolidating diameter 
measurements across different viewpoints. This mitigates cases where 
leaves and fruit are at very different depths, which can skew the 
pixel-to-real-world ratio if relying on a single vantage point.
4.2. Limitations and future directions
Although this study achieved high accuracy in estimating citrus fruit 
diameters, it has two main limitations. First, difficulties in some extreme 
conditions. In some scenarios, measurement errors arise from sparse leaf 
coverage or large depth gaps between the chosen reference leaf and the 
fruit, as shown in Fig. 15. By contrast, the method excels in frames where 
the fruit is clearly visible, leaves are well aligned to the camera, and 
distances are relatively uniform. This highlights the importance of leaf 
positioning and distribution: when leaves are oriented toward the 
camera and near the fruit, measurement accuracy is at its best. The 
difficulties can be low density of leaves or high chance of heavy occlu­
sion, high tilt angles, obvious deformation, or multi-leaf resembling, 
where less or even no suitable leaf can be found in a certain angle 
(image) as candidate of reference.
This issue might be addressed by a multi-frame approach that could 
further reduce confusion in situations where overlapping leaves appear 
in one frame. Therefore, for future work it is suggested adding a Multi- 
frame Enhancement approach to mitigate this issue. If a particular frame 
lacks any viable reference leaf (e.g., only partially visible leaves or none 
at all), that frame’s measurement is skipped. In video clips with a 
consequence of frames, it is highly likely that a subsequent frame with a 
clearer view of the fruit and its surrounding leaves will appear. Ana­
lysing additional frames could find a clearer view of the same area. 
Assuming an implementation captures multiple frames (e.g., in a video), 
we can filter out leaves that show extreme angles or shapes in some 
frames, but find clear and suitable leaves in others, ensuring that only 
representative (near-frontal) leaves are used for reference. This 
enhancement is not yet implemented but is a promising avenue for 
boosting robustness, especially when a single frame has substantial oc­
clusion or overlapping foliage.
Second, leaf and fruit dimension varying across varieties could be a 
major source of accuracy decline. In our implementation, a single 
average leaf length (e.g., ~7.48 cm from the combined sample distri­
bution) was used to estimate fruit diameter. However, each variety’s 
true leaf-size distribution can differ from that single mean, either being 
larger, smaller, or more/less variable. Although the unified mean 
approach keeps overall errors acceptable (under about 9% MAPE), sta­
tistically difference in each variety’s leaf size distribution can degrade 
prediction accuracy. For the least accurate prediction, e.g., Blood Or­
anges, the relatively high shape parameter (9.92) suggests the Weibull 
distribution is quite peaked around its scale (~6.9 cm). However, the

5. Conclusion
This research underscores the potential of leveraging natural orchard 
elements to simplify and reduce the cost of fruit diameter measurement. 
By combining a leaf-based reference approach with an advanced 
detection model with input from a monocular camera, a scalable, ac­
curate, and user-friendly solution can be provided that can be readily 
adopted in commercial citrus orchards. As machine vision continues to 
evolve, refining this method through additional viewpoints and so­
phisticated occlusion-handling mechanisms will further advance auto­
mated yield estimation and precision agriculture practices.
