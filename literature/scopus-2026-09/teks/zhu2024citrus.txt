A B S T R A C T
Accurately estimating the yield of citrus fruit on individual trees is essential for precise orchard management and 
the income of producers. However, estimating the yield of citrus fruit from images of trees remains challenging 
among different processes of tree pruning and image acquisition. This study adopted a deep learning based 
detection model to count fruit in tree images and machine learning models to estimate the yield of individual 
trees from the fruit count. Trees under four levels of pruning intensity (no pruning, 0–5 %, 5–10 %, and 10–15 % 
of new sprouts pruned) and imaged from three different views (two, four, and six images per tree) to determine 
the optimal conditions for yield estimation. The variables considered for yield estimation included fruit count, 
pruning intensity and image views. Dataset containing 1200 tree images were used to train and test four machine 
learning models: random forest, support vector machine, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and generalized 
linear model. The XGBoost model achieved the lowest errors in both training and testing. The optimal yield 
estimation occurs when there are two, four, and six image views and trees that have been pruned >10 %, 5–10 %, 
and ≤5 %, respectively. The findings can enhance the accuracy of image based citrus fruit yield estimation for 
individual trees and reveal the influences of pruning and image views.
1. Introduction
Citrus is the world’s most commonly eaten fruit and one of the most 
important economic fruit crops, with yield and cultivation area 
increasing every year. Increasing fruit yield and having an accurate yield 
estimation is essential to generating income for the producers (Costa 
et al., 2022). A detailed estimation of the number of fruit on individual 
trees would also promote precision management in citrus orchards (Xin 
et al., 2022). For this specific task, deep learning-based image target 
detection models offer more efficient and accurate assistance in the field 
of harvest of citrus fruit and the estimation of orchard yields (Ojo et al., 
2021). These models have considerably helped the management of cit­
rus orchards, but the accuracy of estimating fruit yield still varies widely 
(Chen et al., 2024). Thus, the challenge with image-based fruit yield 
estimating is to comprehend the relationship between the fruit 
recognized from the tree images and the actual fruit on the trees 
(Apolo-Apolo et al., 2020). In this regard, research has suggested that 
only 70–85 % of the fruit is present in the tree canopy images and this 
proportion could be influenced by canopy architecture, pruning, and 
camera views (Gongal et al., 2015). Therefore, it is important to study 
the influence on the recognition of image-based citrus fruit, so the fruit 
presented in images can be accurately detected.
Pruning is a significant factor that influences the estimation of citrus 
fruit yield based on images. Pruning usually take place in the winter and 
spring to promote the new growth of leaves and twigs, ensuring optimal 
nutrient distribution to newly emerging flowers and fruits (Liu et al., 
2020). Pruning with appropriate intensity also removes diseased/weak 
shoots and increases canopy light use efficiency (Budiarto et al., 2022). 
However, different pruning intensities alter the proportion of occluded 
fruits in images, posing a significant challenge for fruit yield estimation

based on images (Wang and He, 2021). Therefore, fruit yield estimation 
for individual trees based on images should address the influence of 
different pruning intensities.
The image acquisition process also affects the accuracy of citrus fruit 
yield estimation, particularly when detailed into individual trees. There 
are mainly two types of image acquisition methods: aerial imaging 
(Huang et al., 2022) and ground imaging (Zhang, Wang et al., 2022). 
Aerial imaging, usually based on unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV), is 
faster than ground imaging and applicable to both flat ground and 
mountain orchards (Moriya et al., 2021). Ground imaging, on the other 
hand, acquire images from more perspectives and locate the view of 
individual trees (Radcliffe et al., 2018). It is also applicable to orchard 
blocks with greenhouses, where aerial imaging may face occlusion is­
sues. The estimation of citrus fruit yield based on both image acquisition 
methods relies on deep learning target detection models, such as Faster 
R-CNN (region-convolutional neural network) (Gao et al., 2020) and 
YOLO (you only look once) (Zheng et al., 2021). In terms of fruit 
counting and yield estimation, Faster R-CNN tends to be more precise 
and suitable for detection with complex background (Mai et al., 2020), 
while YOLO is lightweight and responsive with acceptable accuracy (Xu 
et al., 2023).
The processes of pruning and acquiring images to estimate the yield 
of citrus fruit of individual trees by counting fruit remains a challenging 
task. Deep learning-based methods, particularly convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs), have been widely adopted for various agricultural 
applications because of its intuition and accuracy (Khattak et al., 2021). 
For instance, Mazzia et al. (2020) employed a CNN for estimating apple 
yield, achieving significant accuracy improvements over traditional 
methods. Similarly, Karydas et al. (2020) applied deep learning tech­
niques to estimate cherry yield, highlighting the model’s ability to 
handle varying lighting conditions and occlusions in images. Zeeshan 
et al. (2023) demonstrated the effectiveness of deep learning models in 
detecting citrus fruits, emphasizing the importance of model robustness 
across different growth stages and environments. de Luna et al. (2020)
focused on tomato yield estimation using a combination of image pro­
cessing and deep learning, underscoring the challenges posed by dense 
foliage and fruit clustering.
Despite the unique non-linear feature extraction approach makes 
deep learning models promising in fruit detection (Chen et al., 2021), 
several challenges remain in the domain of deep learning-based fruit 
detection and yield estimation. These challenges include handling oc­
clusion and overlapping fruits in the images (Lee et al., 2020), as well as 
addressing the impact of various orchard management practices, such as 
pruning. These impact on the accuracy of deep learning models has not 
been thoroughly investigated (Matias et al., 2023).
Therefore, in this study, a YOLOv6 based model to detect citrus fruit 
was trained and tested on a self-collected citrus trees image dataset with 
four different levels of pruning intensity and three different imaging 
views. Machine learning models were constructed to correlate image- 
based detections with actual fruit presence on trees by validating the 
output against the manually counted fruits (ground truth). Finally, we 
proposed the optimal machine learning model to estimate the yield of 
citrus fruit for individual trees. The innovation of our proposed method 
lies in the comprehensive approach to improve robustness against oc­
clusion and overlapping fruits by integrating YOLOv6 with machine 
learning models and optimizing image acquisition strategies. Addition­
ally, we systematically evaluate the impact of pruning intensity on 
model performance, providing actionable insights for orchard manage­
ment practices.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Citrus trees and study location
Citrus cultivar Miyagawa (Citrus unshiu Marc. ‘Miyagawa wase’) was 
selected as the dataset. This cultivar is widely cultivated in southern 
China. All the citrus trees were trimmed into similar branch architecture 
and cultivated in the orchard of the Institute of Citrus Fruit, Zhejiang 
Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Taizhou, China) (28.640 N, 121.165 
E). The citrus trees for different pruning treatments were planted in 
separate orchard blocks more than five years ago. These orchard blocks 
were more than 50 m apart. The tree height and width were measured 
and recorded for each tree.
2.2. Pruning treatment and fruit counting
Pruning was conducted in the spring after the first flush of growth. 
The intensity of pruning treatments was designed as follows: 0 (no 
pruning), 0–5 %, 5–10 % and 10–15 % of the new sprouts of each tree 
were pruned. Each level of pruning intensity was performed on 25 citrus 
trees in the same orchard block as replicates. Thus, 100 trees were 
involved in the whole experiment.
Since citrus trees are planted in rows as shown in Fig. 1 A, a ground 
imaging system is usually used to acquire images of each tree. Thus, 
three types of image views (Fig. 1 A) were designed to test the optimal 
image acquisition strategy, which aims to maximize the visibility of 
fruits and minimizes occlusion.
(1) Two views: This is the most common approach utilized by aerial 
and ground imaging. Two images were taken from the opposite 
side (180◦rotation) of each citrus tree, perpendicular to the row 
direction;
(2) Four views: An image was taken at every 90◦rotation for four 
images from each citrus tree, two of which had angle views 
perpendicular to the row direction;
(3) Six views: An image was taken at each 60◦rotation for six images 
from each citrus tree, two of which had angle views perpendic­
ular to the row direction.
The ground sampling distance (distance between the camera and the 
tree canopy) was kept the same (approximately 0.75 m) in all experi­
ments, so that the field of view (FOV) and image size were also kept the 
same (approximately 77.38◦and 1216 × 2160 pixels, respectively). The 
image direction was kept portrait to avoid covering fruits of other trees 
in the image. The percentages of tree width in the images under different 
pruning treatments were calculated and summarized in Table 1. Per­
centages greater than 100 % indicated the tree width covered the whole 
view.
A total of 1200 citrus tree images (taken from trees among 4 pruning 
intensity × 25 trees × (2+4+6) images per tree) were selected to form a 
dataset of fruit images. In most cases the fruits in the background were 
quite smaller than the fruits on the foreground tree. Thus, only the fruits 
on the foreground tree were labeled, which would prompt the fruit 
detection model less possible to detect fruits in the background. Easy­
DAM_V2 (Zhang, Chen et al., 2022) was used to label the images of citrus 
fruit and conduct data augmentation when building the dataset of fruit 
images. The dataset was then randomly divided into a training set 
(70 %), and testing set (30 %). Data augmentation was conducted on the 
training set, including rotation of images by 90◦, 180◦, and 270◦; hori­
zontal and vertical mirroring; resizing 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 2 and 4 times with 
bilinear interpolation; and Gaussian blurry with 5 × 5 and 10 × 10 
kernel. After augmentation, the training set contained 100,800 images 
with labels.
The Faster R-CNN based fruit detection model was trained and tested 
on our dataset mentioned above. An FPN (feature pyramid network) was 
implemented to encode color and texture features (Apolo-Apolo et al., 
2020). An improved RPN (region proposal network) was utilized to 
generate regional proposals based on the features and align the RoIs 
(Region of Interest). The model was then constructed by feeding the RoIs 
to the convolution layers for detection (Zhu, Gu et al., 2022). A total of 
88 epochs were trained on the dataset of fruit images with batch sizes of 
two and the sum of bounding box regression loss as the loss function.

YOLOv5 and YOLOv6 based fruit detection model was trained and

ensure the models maintained a compact footprint suitable for deploy­

detection performance, the smallest variant of YOLOv5 and YOLOv6 
was utilized and the model parameters (number of iterations, batch size, 
loss function, etc.) were initialized the same as those of Faster R-CNN.

2.3. Yield estimation using machine learning
The detected fruit counts in each type of image views were summed 
as the fruit count of an individual tree (Fig. 1B). Since missed and 
overcounting might have occurred during fruit detection, and factors 
like pruning, image views, tree height and width could make influences, 
the yield of citrus fruit and the fruit count of individual trees by the fruit 
detection model are not consistent with a generalized linear model. 
Therefore, the citrus fruit yield of individual trees was estimated using 
three supervised machine learning models, including random forest (RF) 
(Zhu, Chen et al., 2022), support vector machine (SVM) (Tu et al., 2018) 
and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) (Liu et al., 2021). A general­
ized linear model (GLM) was applied as the benchmark due to its 
simplicity and interpretability. It implemented maximum likelihood 
estimation to estimate parameters for the linear regression model. The 
performance improvements of machine learning models in yield esti­
mation can be effectively evaluated by establishing a baseline with GLM 
(Niku et al., 2019). All three models are appropriate for modeling data 
regression with potential non-linear correlations (Guo et al., 2021).
The Boruta algorithm (Kursa and Rudnicki, 2010) was first utilized to 
test the importance of each independent variable, including pruning 
intensity, image views, tree height and width. It is an iterative feature

3. Results
3.1. Influences on fruit detection
In the fruit detection model evaluation, a threshold is often used to 
determine if a prediction is correct or not. As F1-score and time spent on 
prediction of three deep learning models under 10 levels of confidence 
threshold listed in Table 3, data augmentation has improved F1-score by 
16.48 %, 13.74 % and 9.99 % on Faster R-CNN, YOLOv5 and YOLOv6, 
respectively. This implies the importance of data augmentation in 
improving model robustness and accuracy. The YOLOv6 based citrus 
fruit detection model has a highest F1-score of 0.958 in testing set when 
the threshold is 0.8, while the Faster R-CNN based model has a highest 
F1-score of 0.947 with the same threshold, higher than that of YOLOv5. 
This indicates that the YOLOv6 based model is the most accurate for 
citrus fruit detection, confirming a citrus fruit only if the model predicts 
a probability above 0.8. Also, YOLOv6 based model is faster than Faster 
R-CNN when detecting fruit from images, according to the average time 
spent on prediction (Table 3).
The fruit count of the YOLOv6 based detection model is compared 
with the actual number of fruit on each citrus tree, and the results are 
shown in Fig. 2A. This suggests that image views and pruning have a 
significant influence on the detection of fruit. When two images were 
taken per tree, the accuracy of fruit count decreased with the increase in 
actual fruit number, regardless of the pruning intensity. Most fruit 
counts are fewer than the actual fruit number. When four images were 
taken per tree, the fruit count was quite accurate for 0–10 % pruning 
trees with R2 > 0.94 and SMAPE <0.15. Yet the fruit count error for non- 
pruned trees was still considerable. When six images were taken per 
tree, the results of fruit count still had substantial error (SMAPE >0.24). 
Most of the trees pruned by 0–15 % had fruit count larger than the actual 
number fruit of each tree, indicating that the use of six image views may 
lead to overcounting. Fruit counting errors under different image views 
were significantly different according to ANOVA (p < 0.01). Fig. 2B also 
shows the same results and suggests that 5–10 % pruning with four 
image views had the minimum PE (closest to zero).
The influence of pruning was also considerable. Fruit counting errors 
under different pruning intensities were significantly different according 
to ANOVA (p < 0.01). Citrus trees that had not been pruned tended to 
generate large errors in the counts of fruit. Pruning >10 % also resulted 
in considerable SMAPE. Pruning 0–5 % and 5–10 % with four and six 
image views had R2 > 0.92 and SMAPE <0.3. The fruit count of pruning 
0–15 % with four image views had the lowest SMAPE, indicating four 
image views were appropriate to estimate the yield on individual trees 
using only the fruit detection model. No significant differences in terms 
of fruit counting error were identified between pruning 0–15 % with 
four image views.
Tree height and width had relatively smaller influences than pruning 
and image views. No significant differences were identified between 
fruit counting error and tree height or width according to ANOVA (p <
0.01). However, the interactions of pruning × tree height and pruning ×
tree width were identified significant. As shown in Fig. 2B, a positive PE 
indicated that the count of citrus fruit was larger than the actual fruit of

3.2. Importance of the variables used to estimate fruit yield
The Boruta algorithm confirmed that all the variables presented 
above contributed to the estimation of fruit yield. Variable importance 
was then measured as the percentage decrease in accuracy when a 
variable was omitted from a standard GLM model. A higher percentage 
decrease in accuracy indicated that the variable was more important. As 
shown in Fig. 3, the error bars indicate standard deviation in the results 
from iterations in the algorithm. Shorter error bars indicate that the 
results among iterations vary less. Therefore, the importance of each 
variable is highly convincing. The variable importance (percentage 
decrease in accuracy) of the five independent variables suggested that 
the fruit count is the most important factor for yield estimation, followed 
by pruning and image views. Tree height and width were relatively 
unimportant. Fruit count, pruning and image views were the most 
important variables. Fruit count was the most crucial and was respon­
sible for 27.94 % importance because it directly correlates with fruit 
yield. Moreover, pruning, responsible for 24.93 % importance, was 
highly important for the estimation of fruit yield, suggesting that 
pruning could have influenced the occlusion of branches and leaves in 
images. Image views, responsible for 22.12 % importance, were also 
important because they determined the perspective of investigating a 
tree. Tree height and width had importance of 5.39 % and 4.93 %, 
respectively, suggesting they had little impact on fruit counting or the 
estimation of yield.
3.3. Performance of the model used to estimate fruit yield
Four machine learning models were trained and utilized to estimate 
the yield of citrus fruit using three variables (fruit count, pruning and 
image views). Optimizing the parameters is essential for model perfor­
mance. Thus, the parameters of each machine learning model were grid 
tuned with five repeats of 10-fold cross-validations for overall estima­
tion error. The overall estimation error of each machine learning model 
and the corresponding optimal values of parameters are shown in 
Table 4. The MAPE and SMAPE of the machine learning model training 
and testing were calculated and evaluated to determine the performance 
of yield estimation and find the optimal model. SMAPE was utilized to 
avoid the influence when the actual fruit number is extremely small, 
which results in an excessively high MAPE value. The process involves 
>50 iterations of 10-fold cross-validations for each combination of pa­
rameters. XGBoost was the optimal machine learning model in terms of 
the overall MAPE and SMAPE values during both training and testing. 
The RF errors were small during training, but they were larger than 
those of XGBoost during testing. SVM models with different kernel

4. Discussion
The effects of pruning and imaging views were examined through 
various treatments. Our dataset of citrus tree images, including different 
pruning intensities and multiple angles, allows us to enhance the 
model’s robustness and accuracy in fruit detection. Thus, the influences 
of pruning and imaging views have been examined by different treat­
ments. Additionally, we train machine learning models to assess the 
relationship between the predicted fruit counts and the actual fruit 
counts. By systematically validating the model’s outputs against the 
manual counts, we ensure that the recognized fruits from the images 
accurately reflected the real-world fruit yield. This approach addresses 
the challenge of correlating image-based detections with actual fruit 
presence on trees, thereby enhancing the reliability of yield estimation.
Ground imaging systems in citrus orchards are crucial for fruit 
detection, typically imaging from two opposite sides (Zhuang et al., 
2018). This image view offers adequate accuracy in fruit detection, 
especially when the focus is on overall orchard yield. Trees are often 
pruned to expose the inner canopy, which aligns with findings that a 
10–15 % pruning level minimizes estimation errors under two image 
views. However, pruning primarily aims to boost yield and prevent 
disease, not specifically to enhance yield estimation. Over-pruning can 
actually reduce fruit yield (Chueca et al., 2021). To improve yield esti­
mation, more combinations of image views and pruning intensity are 
tested. The results suggest that four and six image views per tree are 
optimal for estimating the fruit yield of trees pruned by ≤10 % and those 
not pruned, respectively. This strategy ensures more accurate fruit yield 
estimation without compromising tree health or yield.
Pruning impacts fruit yield estimation potentially due to changes in 
canopy architecture (Huang et al., 2022). Consequently, the study ex­
amines how pruning influences the accuracy of yield estimation. The 
fruit counting result without machine learning models (RF, SVM, and 
XGBoost) and the yield estimation of these models suggest that increased 
pruning intensity reduces the number of image views needed for optimal 
yield estimation. This is likely because more severe pruning results in 
fewer branches and leaves, making fruits more visible (as shown in 
Fig. 5). Moreover, as pruning intensity and the number of image views 
increase, particularly at six image views, the fruit count may exceed the 
actual count (Fig. 2A). This suggests that overcounting could occur with 
more than four image views or when trees are pruned by >10 %, in 
consistent with additional research (Dorj et al., 2017).
Tree height and width decrease as the PE increases among all levels 
of pruning intensity (Fig. 2B) due to less severe pruning allowing more 
new shoots to grow, some of which develop into vertical upward 
branches. Despite these changes, the camera’s adjusted height and 
coverage of the canopy largely negate the influence of tree height and 
width on detection results. The Boruta analysis, which evaluates and 
ranks the variables in the order of importance (Koreˇn et al., 2021), has 
indicated that tree height and width contribute only about 5 % to the 
yield estimation model. Such conclusion has rarely presented in previ­
ous studies because they have primarily focused on the fruit yield of an 
area or a block in the orchard (Lee et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2023), 
instead of the yield of individual trees or plants.
Machine learning models are gaining popularity for estimating or 
predicting fruit yield, but no single model has been found universally 
applicable to all cases of citrus trees (Maheswari et al., 2021; He et al., 
2022). The SVM(RBF) model is noted for its robustness, with the 
smallest estimation error gaps between training and testing. However, 
both RF and XGBoost models have lower estimation errors, with 
XGBoost showing the lowest errors under specific pruning and imaging 
conditions. Nevertheless, the XGBoost model outperformed others, 
which aligns with previous research (Obsie et al., 2020).
The combination of the YOLOv6 for fruit detection and XGBoost for 
yield estimation has been deemed effective for individual citrus trees. 
This accords with previous research suggesting combination of machine 
learning models tends to outperform single models (Elavarasan and 
Durai Raj Vincent, 2021). The optimal number of ground-based image 
views recommended are two for trees pruned >10 %, four for 5–10 % 
pruning, and six for ≤5 % pruning. For most cases with 5–10 % pruning, 
both two and four image views are acceptable for fruit yield estimation.
In current citrus cultivation practices, pruning aims to make citrus 
fruits more visible on the canopy, making foliage and canopy structure 
the primary influence on fruit detection in productive scenarios (Matias 
et al., 2023). Also, orchard block layouts with compact rows and col­
umns also pose challenges for ground imaging systems, necessitating the 
use of wide-angle lenses. While this study demonstrates the potential of 
combining deep learning and machine learning for citrus yield estima­
tion, its generalizability may be limited due to the specific cultivar and 
location used, reliance on ground-based imaging, and the representa­
tiveness of the datasets. Furthermore, other influential factors such as 
soil quality and pest pressures were not considered. Despite these 
challenges, the results in this study suggest that the proposed methods 
are viable for estimating citrus fruit yield for individual trees, offering a 
promising approach to enhance yield estimation accuracy and efficiency 
in citrus cultivation.

5. Conclusions
This study explored the potential of a method that combined deep 
learning, machine learning and a ground imaging system to estimate the 
citrus yields of individual trees. A YOLOv6 based detection model has 
been built to accurately detect the fruit on citrus tree images and out­
performs the benchmark, which is in accord with previous studies 
(Apolo-Apolo et al., 2020). Our analysis revealed that fruit count, 
pruning intensity and image views significantly influence fruit yield 
estimation, while tree height and width have negligible impact. Spe­
cifically, the XGBoost model demonstrated the lowest estimation errors 
under optimal conditions: two, four, and six image views for trees 
pruned by >10 %, 5–10 %, and ≤5 %, respectively. The MAPE and 
SMAPE values indicated that these combinations provided the most 
accurate yield estimates. The results demonstrate the applicability of 
this approach to accurately estimate citrus fruit yield on individual trees, 
with the potential to enhance precision orchard management and opti­
mize resource allocation based on reliable yield predictions.
