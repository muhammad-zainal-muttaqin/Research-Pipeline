INTRODUCTION
Oil palm cultivation forms the agricultural backbone of 
Southeast Asia, with the region’s two largest producers, 
Malaysia and Indonesia, commanding roughly 85% of 
global palm oil production [1]. Together with Thailand, 
these three countries accounted for nearly 86% of the 
world’s oil palm fruit output in 2022, underscoring 
the region’s central role in global edible oil supply 
chains and food security, according to FAOSTAT/UN 
data. In Malaysia, exports of palm oil and palm-based 
products reached RM 94.95 billion (US$20.8 billion) 
in 2023 [2]. Meanwhile, in Indonesia, the marketing 
year 2022/23 saw production estimated at 46.0 million 
metric tons, with smallholder plantations accounting 
for 40% of the cultivated area, equivalent to about 
6.21 million hectares [3].
Such a scale underscores both the opportunity and the 
urgency: the industry faces significant challenges that 
range from labor shortages and sustainability scrutiny 
to the need for precision measurement and digital 
instrumentation [4]. Traditionally, yield estimation 
and fruit ripeness assessments relied on manual 
inspection, which is labor-intensive, error-prone, and 
not scalable [5]. The emergence of instrumentation-
integrated drone systems and deep learning models 
now offers transformational potential for real-time 
monitoring of palm fruit growth, health status, and 
yield metrics [6],[7].

EVOLUTION OF MEASUREMENT AND 
MONITORING IN PALM CULTIVATION
Measurement and monitoring practices in oil palm 
agriculture have undergone significant transformations 
over the past five decades. The earliest phase, beginning 
in the 1970s, relied on manual field inspections, in 
which trained assessors visually identified fruit 
color and counted bunches to estimate yield. While 
practical for smallholder farms, these human-based 
measurements were time-consuming, subjective, and 
often inconsistent across plantations [5]. As regional 
demand for palm oil surged, rising from less than 5 
million tons in 1980 to more than 70 million tons by 
2023 [2], the need for scalable, objective measurement 
systems became evident.
The second era, spanning from the late 1990s to the 
2010s, witnessed the integration of remote sensing and 
geographic information systems (GIS) into plantation 
management. Early Landsat and SPOT satellite 
imagery provided regional land-use mapping but 
lacked the resolution needed to monitor individual 
trees [8]. Researchers in Malaysia and Indonesia began 
experimenting with multispectral and hyperspectral 
sensors to identify canopy stress, soil moisture, and 
nutrient deficiencies [9],[10]. These systems introduced 
quantitative metrics to what had previously been a 
qualitative discipline, yet their cost and complexity 
limited adoption among smallholders.
A pivotal shift occurred in the 2010s, driven by the 
rise of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and the 
miniaturization of sensors. Drones enable centimeter-
level spatial resolution at a fraction of the cost of satellite 
imaging. UAV-mounted cameras generated dense point 
clouds and orthomosaics, enabling detection of canopy 
gaps, missing palms, and disease symptoms invisible to 
the naked eye [6]. In parallel, the availability of open-
source flight controllers and multispectral modules 
democratized access to aerial measurement. Thailand 
and Malaysia rapidly became regional testbeds for 
these affordable platforms, marking the transition from 
coarse mapping to precision monitoring [7].
The deep-learning era, beginning around 2018, redefined 
measurement accuracy. Convolutional neural networks 
(CNNs) and object-detection models, such as YOLO and 
Mask R-CNN, have learned to recognize fruit bunches, 
crowns, and disease symptoms directly from UAV 
imagery. Studies in Malaysia and Indonesia achieved 
detection accuracies exceeding 95% for palm crowns 
and fruit clusters [11],[12]. Deep learning eliminated 
the need for handcrafted image features and enabled 
the automated processing of thousands of hectares in 
near real-time. The combination of drones, edge AI 
processors, and wireless telemetry has transformed 
plantations into sensor-driven ecosystems.
Despite this progress, the region continues to face 
key measurement challenges. The tropical canopy 
complicates radiometric calibration due to shadows and 
humidity; cloud cover and rainfall disrupt UAV flights; 
and limited labeled datasets hinder the training of 
robust models [4]. Socioeconomic constraints compound 
these technical barriers, as many smallholders lack the 
capital or digital literacy necessary to deploy sensor 
networks [13]. Addressing these gaps calls for open 
datasets, cross-border collaboration, and modular 
instrumentation architectures that scale from small 
plots to industrial estates.
The historical trajectory of measurement in palm 
cultivation thus mirrors the region’s broader 
technological evolution: from manual judgment to 
AI-driven precision. What began as farmers gauging 
ripeness by sight has become a sophisticated interplay 
of imaging physics, embedded computing, and data 
science, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Yet the story remains 
human at its core: empowering millions of growers with 
accessible measurement tools is central to ensuring the 
sustainability of Southeast Asia’s most vital agricultural 
commodity.

DETECTION
The measurement revolution in palm cultivation 
accelerated with the advent of deep learning (DL) in 
agricultural imaging. Unlike classical machine-vision 
systems that relied on handcrafted color or texture 
features, DL models learn directly from visual data,

This shift addressed chronic challenges in tropical 
field conditions, where lighting, viewing angle, and

The earliest experiments, between 2017 and 2019, 
applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) such 
as AlexNet and VGG-16 to classify fresh-fruit-bunch 
(FFB) ripeness based on hue distribution. Accuracy 
rates exceeded 90% under controlled lighting but 
degraded sharply in field scenarios due to shadowing 
and occlusion [9]. Subsequent research in Malaysia 
and Thailand incorporated transfer learning, enabling 
models trained on large generic datasets to adapt to 
limited plantation imagery [7]. This breakthrough 
significantly reduced the need for expensive annotation 
campaigns and paved the way for practical deployment 
in the smallholder context of Southeast Asia.
A second wave began around 2020 with object-detection 
architectures such as You Only Look Once (YOLO) and 
Faster R-CNN. In oil palm applications, these models 
have been used for fruit bunch ripeness classification, 
palm tree counting, and crown-related detection tasks 
[11]–[14]. The appeal of single-stage detectors lies in 
their operational simplicity and fast inference, whereas 
two-stage models remain relevant when more detailed 
localization or segmentation is required. This shift 
made drone-based analytics increasingly feasible for 
plantation workflows, while also highlighting the 
importance of dataset quality, validation design, and 
deployment context.
More recently, the focus has shifted toward data fusion 
and model compactness. Multi-modal frameworks 
combine RGB, multispectral, and thermal imagery 
to infer physiological stress and disease onset before 
visible symptoms appear [6],[10]. At the same time, 
pruning and quantization techniques have enabled 
DL models to run on low-power edge devices attached 
to drones, tractors, or handheld scanners [15]. These 
innovations transform palm plantations into living 
laboratories, where each flight generates measurable 
indicators of yield potential, ripeness progression, and 
disease probability.
Yet, as in every revolution, open challenges persist. 
Tropical illumination changes rapidly, requiring 
dynamic exposure compensation and domain-
adaptive learning [16]. Limited labeled datasets hinder 
generalization across regions; a model trained in Sabah 
may underperform in Sumatra due to differences in 
canopy structure. There is also the ethical imperative 
to ensure inclusivity: if AI tools remain confined to 
industrial estates, smallholders, who manage 40% of 
regional plantations, could be left behind [4].
Despite these hurdles, the evidence is compelling: 
deep learning has moved palm agriculture from 
subjective judgment to quantifiable intelligence. From 
detecting a single fruit’s ripeness to orchestrating 
fleet-wide drone analytics, DL models now underpin 
a new measurement paradigm, one where each pixel 
tells a story about growth, health, and sustainability.

detection backbone is a measurement-design decision: it 
affects detection latency, computational budget, and the 
bias–variance behavior of fruit-count and ripeness-class

that dataset composition, class structure, software stack,

draw Table 1 from a single controlled study by the

Three measurement-relevant patterns emerge from 
Table 1. First, mAP@0.5 saturates near unity across 
all variants on this task, so detector capacity is most 
meaningfully compared using the stricter mAP@0.5–
0.95 metric. Second, performance peaks at YOLOv8m 
(mAP@0.5–0.95 = 0.927, 25.9 M parameters) and does 
not improve further at the larger l and x variants, in 
which both deliver 0.921 despite carrying 1.7× and 2.6× 
more parameters, and in the case of YOLOv8x, require 
roughly 10× the training time. This is a measurement-
design lesson rather than a leaderboard finding: scaling 
beyond the m tier on this dataset buys no detectable 
accuracy and incurs substantial computational cost. 
Third, YOLOv8m’s reported inference time of 11.27 
ms (≈89 FPS on RTX 4080) is well within real-time 
UAV processing constraints, supporting its selection 
as the operational sweet spot for palm-fruit ripeness 
measurement workflows. These mAP@0.5–0.95 values 
also bound the detector-miscount component u(N) of 
the uncertainty budget developed in the Uncertainty 
Budgets section.
The broader oil palm literature includes both single-
stage detectors (YOLO family) and two-stage detectors 
such as Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN. However, 
published studies differ substantially in their target 
definitions, dataset compositions, annotation protocols, 
IoU thresholds, and hardware. For this reason, this 
review does not treat the broader literature as a uniform 
benchmark. Instead, it interprets cross-family results 
in terms of detector trade-offs: single-stage detectors 
are favored when throughput, edge deployment, and 
operational simplicity are priorities, while two-stage 
detectors remain relevant when fine localization 
or instance segmentation is required [13],[16]. The 
controlled YOLOv8 evidence in Table 1 should therefore 
be read as a within-family case study, while the cross-
family literature provides comparative design context.
DRONE-BASED MEASUREMENT 
SYSTEM
The convergence of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) 
and precision instrumentation has revolutionized 
the monitoring, measurement, and management of 
palm plantations. Drones provide near-real-time data 
across large areas that once required days of manual 
surveying. Their integration with imaging sensors, such 
as RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, and LiDAR, has 
transformed yield estimation, canopy health evaluation, 
and disease detection into quantitative, reproducible 
processes [6],[17].

2012 were largely experimental and focused on aerial 
mapping to delineate plantation boundaries. With the 
introduction of miniaturized multispectral sensors (e.g., 
Parrot Sequoia, MicaSense RedEdge), UAVs evolved 
from photography tools into measurement platforms 
that can compute vegetation indices such as NDVI 
and NDRE [9]. These indices quantify photosynthetic

SENSOR PAYLOADS AND CALIBRATION
Accurate measurement demands meticulous sensor 
calibration. UAVs operating under the tropical canopy 
face fluctuating illumination, humidity, and vibration 
that can distort radiometric readings. Standard 
practice now includes reflectance-panel calibration 
before each flight and radiometric correction during 
post-processing [17]. LiDAR payloads, although more 
expensive, provide centimeter-scale canopy-height 
models, which are crucial for detecting missing 
palms and estimating biomass. In Malaysian Borneo, 
repeated airborne LiDAR mapping between 2015 and 
2016 detected individual-tree growth patterns linked 
to the El Niño drought cycle [17]. These quantitative 
measurements enable both environmental monitoring 
and yield forecasting at unprecedented resolution.
DATA INTEGRATION AND EDGE 
PROCESSING
Modern systems integrate UAVs with edge-AI 
computing and cloud-based analytics. Instead of 
transferring gigabytes of raw imagery to remote 
servers, lightweight processors, such as NVIDIA Jetson 
or Coral TPU modules, run detection models (e.g., 
YOLOv8, Mask R-CNN) directly onboard. This reduces 
latency from hours to seconds and enables real-time 
feedback for field operators [11],[16]. Some prototypes, 
developed in Malaysia and Indonesia, combine drone 
imagery with Internet of Things (IoT) ground sensors 
that capture soil moisture and microclimate data [18]. 
Such hybrid architectures close the measurement loop: 
drones observe the canopy, sensors monitor the ground, 
and AI fuses both data streams to generate actionable 
plantation-health maps.
SOCIOECONOMIC AND SUSTAINABILITY 
IMPACT
Beyond technological metrics, UAV systems are 
reshaping field operations and labor efficiency. One 
drone equipped with automated flight planning 
can replace up to ten field surveyors, significantly 
reducing operational costs while enhancing safety 
in remote terrain [4]. For smallholders, cooperative 
UAV service models have emerged in which village-
level entrepreneurs offer periodic aerial assessments 
at affordable rates. Environmentally, these systems 
help identify fertilizer over-application zones to 
reduce runoff and support Malaysia’s and Indonesia’s 
commitments to sustainable palm-oil certification [2].
THE TRANSITION TOWARD AUTONOMOUS 
FLEETS
Recent advances are steering the industry toward 
autonomous multi-drone fleets capable of collaborative 
mapping, obstacle avoidance, and adaptive mission re-
planning. Experimental setups in Thailand demonstrate 
swarm-drone coordination for simultaneous fruit 
detection and canopy-height estimation [7]. Combined 
with 5G communication and low-power GNSS 
correction, these fleets pave the way for continuous, 
self-calibrating measurement systems. The shift from 
human-piloted drones to semi-autonomous aerial 
networks marks a critical milestone toward scalable, 
sustainable instrumentation in palm agriculture.

RGB and multispectral sensors remain dominant 
due to affordability and ease of processing, while 
hyperspectral and LiDAR systems offer advanced 
analytics for disease detection and biomass estimation. 
Emerging edge-AI platforms combine sensing and 
onboard computation, enabling real-time detection 
and measurement across vast plantations.

CALIBRATION TRACEABILITY AND 
REFERENCE STANDARDS
Traceability of UAV–DL measurements may be 
established along three parallel chains. First, in the 
radiometric chain, factory-calibrated reflectance panels 
propagate a known reflectance reference to canopy-
level spectral indices. Second, in the geometric chain, 
GNSS/RTK positioning is linked to surveyed ground 
control points or an equivalent reference network. 
Third, in the mass chain, the bunch-mass values used 
to estimate m R
( ) are obtained using calibrated platform 
scales whose measurement practices should align with 
recognized weighing requirements, such as OIML R 76 
[21]. Explicit documentation of these links transforms 
an ad hoc field workflow into an auditable measurement 
chain and is conceptually consistent with ISO/IEC 
17025-style quality assurance [20].
REPEATABILITY, REPRODUCIBILITY, AND 
ERROR PROPAGATION
Following ISO 5725 [22], repeatability may be defined 
as the dispersion of measurement results obtained on 
the same plot under the same operating conditions, 
including the same UAV platform, sensor, operator, 
detector configuration, and measurement day. 
Reproducibility refers to the dispersion observed when 
one or more of these conditions change, such as the 
operator, drone unit, time of day, or detector retraining. 
In field settings, reproducibility is often materially 
worse than repeatability, indicating that operator 
protocol, platform setup, and environmental variation 
can dominate within-flight noise. This highlights the 
importance of standard operating procedures and 
calibration for improving measurement quality.

METROLOGICAL VALIDATION OF AI 
OUTPUTS AND GROUND-TRUTH SAMPLING
Confusion-matrix metrics (precision, recall, mAP) 
characterize the detector but do not by themselves 
establish agreement with the underlying physical 
measurand. Metrological validation, therefore, requires 
comparing UAV-DL estimates against an independent 
reference, treating the detector as a measuring 
instrument under test. Bland–Altman analysis can be 
used to assess bias and limits of agreement [23], while 
Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient can summarize 
agreement on a single application-relevant scale [24]. 
Orthogonal-regression-style analysis can also help 
reveal proportional bias when detector outputs are 
compared with reference counts or weights.
The reference itself must be drawn by a sampling design 
that controls its own uncertainty. We recommend 
stratified random sampling of ground-truth quadrats 
across replanting age (young, mature, old), terrain 
(flat, undulating, peat), and management class (estate, 
smallholder), with at least 30 quadrats per stratum 
to bound the standard error of the reference mean 
below 2%. Quadrats should be georeferenced with RTK-
grade GNSS so that the detector and reference share a 
common coordinate frame within the centimetric error 
budget assumed. Reference counts and bunch weights 
should be reported with their own uncertainty so that 
detector validation is performed instrument-against-
instrument, not detector-against-perfect-truth.
Embedding this metrological layer addresses a 
critical asymmetry in current practice: deep-learning 
models are reported with software metrics, while 
their downstream yield estimates are then quoted to 
certification bodies as instrumentation outputs. Closing 
that gap is the central methodological contribution that 
the instrumentation and measurement community can 
make to precision palm cultivation.

REGIONAL CASE STUDIES
The application of drone-based measurement and deep 
learning in palm cultivation has evolved unevenly 
across Southeast Asia, reflecting differences in industry 
maturity, infrastructure, and policy support. Malaysia 
leads with large-scale adoption and integration of 
certified plantation management. At the same time, 
Indonesia demonstrates the world’s greatest deployment 
potential but faces challenges in data infrastructure 
and in the inclusion of smallholders. Thailand, though 
smaller in production scale, has become a testbed 
for low-cost, research-driven innovations. Together, 
these three countries exemplify the regional trajectory 
from pilot research to the practical implementation of 
instrumentation and measurement systems that are 
transforming the quantification of palm fruit yield, 
health, and sustainability in the field.
MALAYSIA
Malaysia has been a pioneer and reference model 
for integrating drone-based and deep-learning 
measurement systems into plantation management. 
National research institutes, such as the Malaysian 
Palm Oil Board (MPOB), have led coordinated programs 
to evaluate UAV payloads and establish calibration 
protocols to ensure consistent data acquisition under 
tropical conditions [2].
Several

advanced applications of instrumentation, including 
hyperspectral-based detection of Ganoderma disease 
[6] and embedded-AI approaches for palm-related 
counting tasks [11]. Collectively, these studies show that 
UAV and AI workflows can shorten field assessment 
cycles and improve measurement consistency, although 
the magnitude of the gain depends strongly on the 
task definition, validation design, and field conditions. 
Malaysia’s case highlights how public–private 
collaboration can help translate academic prototypes 
into operational measurement systems. The growing 
emphasis on sustainability certification (MSPO) further 
encourages the adoption of more standardized I&M 
practices, offering a useful model for the rest of the 
region.
INDONESIA
Transitioning from Malaysia’s structured institutional 
ecosystem, Indonesia reveals how scale and diversity 
reshape the instrumentation challenge. As the world’s 
largest palm oil producer, Indonesia manages over 16 
million hectares of plantations, 40 percent of which 
are owned by smallholders [3]. This diverse landscape 
introduces significant variability in soil type, canopy 
density, and management practices, making accurate 
measurement particularly challenging.
UAV and deep learning initiatives in Indonesia have 
focused on disease detection and biomass estimation. 
For instance, studies in Indonesia have explored UAV-
based remote sensing and AI-assisted analytics for 
disease detection and plantation assessment [6],[17]. 
These efforts indicate the potential of aerial sensing to 
improve early detection and spatial decision support, 
although reported performance remains dependent 
on local data quality, sensor choice, and the specific 
validation protocol used.
However, the country’s vast scale demands cost-
effective, decentralized measurement architectures. 
Local universities and start-ups are experimenting 
with cooperative drone-sharing models and cloud-
based AI analytics to address infrastructure gaps [4]. 
These bottom-up innovations complement Malaysia’s 
top-down framework, illustrating how context shapes 
the adoption of instrumentation and measurement.
THAILAND
As Indonesia grapples with large-scale deployment, 
Thailand provides an instructive example of agility and 
focused experimentation on smaller, yet technologically 
advanced, plantations.
Thailand’s palm sector, although smaller, is rapidly 
modernizing and has leveraged UAV and AI tools 
primarily through research–industry collaborations. 
The country’s approach emphasizes integration and 
visualization rather than scale.
A national project led by Prince of Songkla University 
developed a drone-enabled geospatial verification 
platform that integrates object detection, yield 
prediction, and mapping dashboards for plantation 
certification [7]. Thai studies also explore stress 
detection using multispectral and thermal data, linking 
canopy temperature anomalies to water deficiency 
with R2 values above 0.8 [7]. Government-backed 
incentives for digital agriculture have accelerated 
these pilot programs, positioning Thailand as a 
regional innovation developer of low-cost, modular 
measurement systems.
Together, the experiences of Malaysia, Indonesia, 
and Thailand demonstrate complementary strengths: 
institutional integration, vast deployment potential, 
and agile innovation. This forms a regional ecosystem

CHALLENGES AND GAPS
Despite notable progress in UAV- and AI-enabled 
measurement systems across Southeast Asia, 
several persistent challenges limit their scalability, 
standardization, and equitable adoption. These 
challenges fall into three major dimensions: technical, 
economic, and policy/inclusivity, as shown in Fig. 2. 
Addressing them holistically is essential to realizing 
the full potential of data-driven palm cultivation.
TECHNICAL CHALLENGES: TOWARD 
RELIABLE AND STANDARDIZED 
MEASUREMENT
While UAV-based systems have revolutionized field 
data collection, sensor calibration and data consistency 
remain critical bottlenecks. Radiometric variations 
due to cloud cover, humidity, and illumination 
shifts reduce measurement reliability, particularly in 
equatorial climates [17]. Many deep learning models 
also struggle with domain generalization; a model 
trained in Malaysia may underperform when applied in 
Indonesia due to differences in lighting, soil reflectance, 
and fruit color profiles [16].
Another technical gap involves dataset standardization. 
Each research group often curates small, independent 
image collections with inconsistent labeling protocols. 
Without open, annotated regional datasets, reproducibility 
and cross-validation are limited [13]. Finally, sensor 
interoperability across UAV platforms remains limited; 
proprietary interfaces and closed ecosystems hinder 
multi-sensor fusion, preventing holistic field assessment 
that combines RGB, hyperspectral, and LiDAR data.

ECONOMIC CHALLENGES: COST, 
SCALABILITY, AND DIGITAL READINESS
High equipment costs, especially for multispectral 
and LiDAR sensors, remain a significant barrier for 
smallholders, who represent approximately 40% of 
Southeast Asia’s palm producers [3]. Even where drone 
hardware is affordable, recurring expenses such as 
maintenance, cloud storage, and model retraining limit 
adoption [2].
A second economic constraint is digital readiness. 
While industrial estates have access to skilled operators 
and reliable connectivity, smallholders often lack the 
digital infrastructure to process and interpret UAV 
data [4]. Cooperative UAV service models in Indonesia 
and Thailand show promise, but their financial 
sustainability depends on government incentives or 
cooperative financing schemes [18]. Building local 
capacity through technician training and accessible 
analytics tools remains essential for equitable adoption.
POLICY AND INCLUSIVITY GAPS: BRIDGING 
INNOVATION AND ACCESS
At the policy level, measurement innovation often 
advances faster than regulatory and extension frameworks. 
Few national programs have formally integrated UAV- 
and AI-based monitoring into certification or sustainability 
reporting mechanisms [1]. As a result, data governance 
and privacy standards lag, particularly concerning image 
data collected over private estates.
Furthermore, inclusivity gaps persist between industrial 
and smallholder operations. Without targeted incentives 
or open-access platforms, advanced measurement tools 
risk deepening digital divides rather than narrowing 
them. Programs led by the Malaysian Palm Oil Board 
and Thailand’s Ministry of Agriculture demonstrate 
how public–private partnerships can bridge this gap by 
embedding UAV-based measurement into sustainability 
auditing workflows [2],[7].
FUTURE ROADMAP
The next decade presents a pivotal window to transform 
UAV- and AI-based measurement systems for palm 
cultivation from experimental prototypes into integrated, 
standardized, and inclusive operational tools. Building 
on the challenges outlined above, this section proposes 
a three-phase roadmap encompassing short-, medium-, 
and long-term objectives that addresses technical 
maturity, economic viability, and social inclusivity.
The roadmap envisions 2025–2035 as the period 
when Southeast Asia can consolidate its leadership in 
smart agricultural measurement through harmonized 
instrumentation frameworks, open data ecosystems, 
and accessible deep learning models tailored to tropical 
environments.

STANDARDIZATION
The immediate priority is technical standardization, 
including establishing common calibration procedures, 
metadata structures, and benchmark datasets. Malaysia

(IoT) ground sensors and meteorological feeds should 
enable the creation of multi-source fusion frameworks 
that allow for comprehensive ecosystem monitoring 
by linking canopy measurements, soil health, and 
microclimate parameters.

AND POLICY INTEGRATION

autonomous measurement networks, including swarms 
of drones communicating over 5G or satellite IoT, 
capable of self-calibration and automated reporting. 
The integration of measurement data into national 
sustainability certification and carbon accounting 
systems will be crucial for policy legitimacy [1],[2].
Open-access regional data hubs, supported by 
ASEAN collaboration, could serve as repositories for 
anonymized imagery, models, and metrics, ensuring 
transparency and equitable access. Ultimately, the 
goal is not only technological sophistication but also 
measurement equity: empowering smallholders and 
cooperatives, with the same diagnostic precision as 
industrial estates, to close the digital divide that persists 
in tropical agriculture.

By 2035, Southeast Asia is poised to become the 
global benchmark for sustainable, data-driven palm 
agriculture, where every pixel, spectrum, and sensor

measurement evolves beyond a technical activity into

CONCLUSIONS
This review highlights that the evolution of palm-
fruit measurement in Southeast Asia is more than a 
technological shift: it represents a paradigm shift in 
how data, sensing, and sustainability converge. From 
manual inspection to deep learning and autonomous 
UAV systems, the region now stands at the forefront 
of agricultural instrumentation, linking precision 
measurement to environmental stewardship and 
socioeconomic inclusion. Malaysia’s institutional 
strength, Indonesia’s scale, and Thailand’s innovation 
collectively form a blueprint for an integrated 
measurement ecosystem that is grounded in accuracy, 
transparency, and accessibility. The roadmap outlined 
in this paper will position Southeast Asia not only as 
a regional leader but as a global exemplar of climate-
smart agriculture, where calibrated instruments, 
intelligent analytics, and inclusive policies coalesce 
to redefine productivity and sustainability. Ultimately, 
the future of palm cultivation will be determined not 
by the volume of production but by the precision and 
integrity of its measurement, a principle that stands 
at the very core of modern instrumentation and 
measurement science.
