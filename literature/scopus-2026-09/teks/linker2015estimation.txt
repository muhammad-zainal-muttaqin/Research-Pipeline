Estimation of apple orchard yield using night time imaging
R. Linker*, E. Kelman and O. Cohen
Faculty of Civil and Environmental Engineering, Technion – Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa 32000, 
Israel; linkerr@tx.technion.ac.il
Abstract
This work investigates the potential of night time imaging for estimating apple orchard yield. Forty 
two trees were photographed from two sides with cameras mounted at three heights. Each image 
was analyzed and the results of the six images associated with each tree were summed up to provide 
a ‘tree count’. Fourteen trees were selected randomly in order to calibrate a relationship between the 
‘tree count’ estimate and the actual tree yield. This relationship was then applied to the ‘tree count’ 
results of the remaining trees. Although the yield estimate error for a single tree was sometimes 
large, the overall yield estimate was within 10% of the actual yield.
Keywords: artificial vision, fruit detection, image processing
Introduction
Automated estimation of the expected yield is still a major challenge in orchards. Currently, yield 
estimation is based on visual inspection of some trees by the farmer, which is both time consuming 
and prone to errors. At first glance, it may seem that this task could be easily automated using 
computer vision. However, the unstructured and cluttered environment of the tree canopy makes 
this task very challenging, especially when the fruits are featureless and with a color similar to 
that of the canopy (e.g. Kurtulmus et al., 2011; Linker et al., 2012; Payne et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 
2012; Bansal et al., 2013; Kelman and Linker, 2014). Furthermore, uneven daylight illumination 
complicates the task and this has led a number of researchers to investigate the use of night time 
imaging (e.g. Sites and Delwiche, 1988; Payne et al., 2014; Cohen et al., 2014; Linker and Kelman, 
2015). Encouraging results were reported in the recent studies of Cohen et al. (2014) and Linker and 
Kelman (2015), which both focused on the detection of green mature apples in night time images. 
Both studies reported strong linear relationships between the number of objects identified as apples 
and the number of apples counted by visual inspection of the images. However, in neither study, 
was an attempt made to correlate the results of the image analysis with the actual yield at the tree 
level. In the present study, both methods were applied to a dataset containing over 250 pictures of 
42 trees and the suitability of each approach for estimating tree yield was investigated.
Materials and methods
Data collection
The imaging system consisted of three identical cameras (Canon PowerShot G7. Resolution 
3,648×2,736 pixels) mounted on a vertical pole at 110, 180 and 240 cm from the ground and two 
additional poles positioned at 60 cm from the camera pole, on which LED lights were mounted at 
heights of 130 and 250 cm. The whole assembly was mounted on a trailer which was driven in the 
middle of the tree rows. Each tree was photographed from two sides, resulting in six images per 
tree. The imaging system was used to record images of 42 Golden Delicious trees at the Matityahu 
Research Station in Northern Israel on July 17, 2014. The trees were picked individually at the end 
of the season in order to obtain ground truth yield data. In addition, the number of apples visible 
in each image was determined by visual inspection of the images.

Data analysis
The images were processed with two methods. The first method (labeled Approach #1 below), 
described in Linker et al. (2012) and Cohen et al. (2014), uses color-and-texture analysis followed 
by comparison of the contours of the obtained blobs with a simple model (circle). This method 
includes four main steps: (1) pixel classification based on color and texture; (2) detection of blobs 
of connected pixels and extension of these blobs to neighboring pixels with similar properties; 
(3) segmentation of the contours of the blobs into arcs; and (4) grouping of arcs into objects and 
comparison with the reference model (circle). The second method (Approach #2), described in 
Linker and Kelman (2015), relies on analysis of the spatial distribution of light around specular 
highlights (bright spots) that occur (mostly but not only) on apples when artificial illumination is 
used. This procedure is based on the observation that although such highlights also occur on leaves, 
because of the spherical shape of apples, the light distribution around highlights located on apples 
is such that pixels with intensity above some threshold form a circle or part of a circle. Accordingly, 
detection of apples is achieved via three main steps: (1) identification of a local maximum of the light 
intensity and extraction of a sub-image centered at this point; (2) formation of regions of connected 
pixels (so-called light patches) corresponding to decreasing light intensities; and (3) analysis of the 
geometrical properties of the sequence of patches obtained with decreasing intensity thresholds.
Although both methods are expected to detect the same objects, detection is achieved via completely 
different features of the ‘apple’ object: in Approach #1, detection relies primarily on pixel color and 
texture and on the assumption that the circular contour on the apple is at least partially visible. In 
Approach #2, color is not considered at all and apples are detected solely via their shape, assuming 
that the visible region of the apple is sufficiently large to enable analysis of the light distribution 
on its surface.
Both methods were calibrated using 12 images (four images at each height) selected randomly. The 
calibrated procedures were applied to all the images and, for each approach, the number of objects 
detected in the six images of a tree were summed up to produce a ‘tree count’. Fourteen trees were 
selected randomly in order to derive a relationship between the ‘tree counts’ and the actual tree yield. 
This relationship was then applied to all the ‘tree count’ results to produce an estimate of the yield of 
each tree. In order to investigate the influence of the choice of the trees used for calibration on the 
results, this last step was repeated ten times, each time with a different subset of trees for calibration.
Results
Calibration
The parameter values reported in Linker et al. (2012) (Dataset#1) and Linker and Kelman (2015) 
were used as default values for the respective algorithms and the number of parameter adjustments 
was kept at a minimum.
Approach #1 includes a color-and-texture classifier which is not generic since the apparent color 
of the apples depends not only on the apple variety and their developmental stage but also on the 
illumination. Accordingly, about half of the apples were marked manually in the 12 calibrations 
images (the remaining apples were masked out), creating ‘apple’ and ‘non-apple’ regions from 
which random pixels were sampled to train the classifier. The only parameters which were adjusted 
were the ones related to the apple size: minimum radius: 30 pixels, expected radius: 60 pixels, and 
maximum radius 150 pixels. Figure 1 shows the detection results obtained with these values for a 
typical image. Figure 2 (left) summarizes the results for the 12 calibration images and shows the 
relationship which was obtained between the number of detected objects and the number of apples 
according to visual inspection. This relationship was used to convert the number of detected objects 
into the estimated number of apples in all 252 images and the results are shown in the right frame 
of Figure 3. It can be seen that there is a good agreement between the two.

Yield estimation
As mentioned above, the number of objects detected in the six images of a tree were summed up to 
produce a ‘tree count’ and fourteen trees were selected randomly in order to derive a relationship 
between the ‘tree counts’ and the actual tree yield. This relationship was then used to produce 
an estimate of the yield of each tree. This analysis was repeated 10 times using different trees for 
calibration.

Conclusions
Night time imaging appears to be a promising technique for estimating apple yield, at least at the 
row scale. The two approaches investigated in this study led to overall estimates which were within 
10% of the actual yield using less than 15 trees for calibration. Although these results need to be 
confirmed using a larger dataset, one can envision a self-calibrating system which would require 
the farmer to provide fruit counts for 15-20 trees. This information would be used to establish the
