A B S T R A C T
Blueberry growers currently rely on manual or subjective approaches to harvest decision-making and yield 
estimation. Canopy image-based, pre-harvest blueberry detection provides a promising means for automated 
harvest maturity assessment, fruit counting, and yield estimation to optimize crop management and reduce labor 
costs. Data-driven deep-learning-based object detectors, especially the YOLO (You Only Look Once) series 
models, have emerged as a powerful tool for a diversity of computer vision tasks in agriculture. However, the 
detection of such small, densely populated objects as blueberries in unstructured scenes can pose great chal­
lenges, especially given the lack of dedicated datasets for model development. This study, therefore, presents the 
first publicly available dataset of blueberry canopy images with 17,955 ripe and unripe blueberries, which were 
captured in diverse orchard conditions, and performance evaluation of YOLOv8l (large) and YOLOv9-c 
(compact) with comparable complexity for blueberry detection and whereby fruit counting and ripe fruit per­
centage estimation. At the input image resolution of 2560 × 2560 pixels, both models performed similarly in 
terms of detection accuracy with around 91 % mAP@50, except that YOLOv9-c was far more time-consuming to 
train. Trained with the input of higher resolution images of 3520 × 3520 pixels, YOLOv8l achieved an overall 
mAP@50 of nearly 93 %, and an RMSEs (root-mean-square errors) of 10.4 in fruit counting and 3.62 % in 
estimating the percentage of ripe fruit of each image. Both the blueberry dataset1 and software programs2 of this 
study have been or will be made publicly available, which are expected to be useful for promoting greater 
community efforts for further algorithm development.
1. Introduction
Blueberries are an economically important fruit crop widely culti­
vated worldwide. As the world’s top blueberry producer, the United 
States (U.S.) has seen substantially expanded blueberry production since 
2000 [1]. Blueberries are commercially grown in 26 states in the U.S., 
with 98 % concentrated in 10 states [2], such as Washington, Georgia, 
Michigan, and Oregon. In 2023, the U.S. harvested 648 million pounds 
of blueberries from 103,000 acres, with a farmgate value exceeding $1 
billion [3]. Three types of blueberries are economically important, 
including the highbush (Vaccinium corymbosum), lowbush or wild 
(Vaccinium angustifolium), and rabbiteye blueberries (Vaccinium ashei), 
among which the highbush blueberry dominates blueberry production 
in North America [4].
Blueberries do not ripen simultaneously and have large maturity 
variations, even within a single blueberry plant [5]. With a shrinking 
farm labor supply and rising labor costs, once-over mechanical har­
vesters are being increasingly used [6], replacing or complementing 
manual labor for harvesting fresh-market blueberries. Determining 
appropriate harvesting timing is of utmost importance to ensuring the 
harvesting of the greatest number of high-quality fruit and maximizing 
production profits. Harvesting too early would result in a large per­
centage of unripe or unmarkable fruit (e.g., green, pink, and reddish 
blueberries), entailing postharvest sorting to remove the low-quality 
fruit [7]. On the other hand, late harvest of overripe berries increases 
the risk of fruit damage, short storage life, and postharvest diseases [8, 
9]. Either situation may reduce pack-out rates by more than 20 % [10], 
translating into financial losses of millions of dollars for the fruit

close attention to yield because it directly impacts production revenue 
and provides valuable feedback on how the crop responds to manage­

better schedule the field crews and harvesters, improve management 
practices, and optimize resource inputs for subsequent seasons [11].
Manual, visual assessment of fruit maturity based on the fruit skin 
color is the current practice for blueberry harvest decision-making. The 
color of blueberry fruit is green in the early developmental stage, 
gradually transitions to pink, red, and eventually turns deep blue or 
black as blueberries reach full maturity. For hand harvesting, the first 
picking event commonly takes place when about 20 % of the fruit on a 
bush is blue [8], and repeated picking will happen as the fruit maturity 
advances, while the first machine harvest is recommended when 50–60 
% of the crop is blue [6]. Determining the fruit color percentage on the 
bush requires manually counting the fruit in different colors (e.g., green, 
blue). This is an extremely time-consuming, labor-intensive process, 
which can only be performed for a small number of selected branches 
and may not yield reliable maturity estimation for the whole canopy and 
the orchard. Currently, there are no appropriate in-season yield esti­
mation methods or tools for blueberry growers, although growers often 
guess the crop yield based on the yield data from previous seasons. 
Hence, it would be economically attractive and beneficial for blueberry 
growers if automated or high-throughput methods could be developed 
for blueberry detection and maturity assessment to facilitate harvest 
decision-making as well as fruit counting for yield estimation.
In recent years, computer vision technology combined with 
advanced machine learning techniques, especially deep learning (DL), 
has gained significant interest as a promising method for automated fruit 
detection, counting, and yield estimation [12–15]. Various sensing 
systems and DL approaches to fruit counting and yield estimation have 
been reported for tree fruit crops, such as apples, citrus, mango, avo­
cado, etc. However, only a handful of studies were conducted on 
developing vision systems for small fruit crops such as blueberries. 
Image-based blueberry detection and other downstream tasks (e.g., 
maturity assessment, counting, and yield estimation) present challenges 
due to the small size of blueberries that are often densely packed and 
sometimes clustered, and the occlusion caused by leaves and branches as 
well as other factors (e.g., lighting, soil background) in the unstructured 
fields. Li et al. [16] investigated color component analysis and machine 
learning methods (e.g., K-nearest neighbor and supervised K-means) for 
identifying blueberries of different maturity levels, achieving pixel-level 
classification accuracies of around 90 %. Tan et al. [17] built machine 
learning models using the features of histogram-oriented gradients as 
well as color features for identifying blueberry maturity, achieving 
overall accuracies ranging from 84 % to 96 %. In the aformentioned two 
studies, only a small set of images was acquired for partial or localized 
branches of blueberries instead of the larger bush canopies, and 
hand-crafted features extracted from images were required for model 
development.
As one-stage DL-based object detection models, YOLO (You Only 
Look Once) detectors have gained tremendous popularity over the last 
five years, especially for real-time computer vision tasks. Suchmann 
et al. [18] applied four versions of YOLOv3 [19] to detect wild blue­
berries of three levels of maturity, achieving the best accuracy of 85.3 % 
mAP@50 with inference times of 28 ms. In an extended study on six 
YOLO detectors for blueberry detection [20], YOLOv4 [21] produced 
the highest mAP@50 of 79.8 % and 88.1 % in detecting 2-class and 
3-class blueberries, respectively, and the lowest mean absolute error of 
24.1 % in fruit detection-based yield estimation. Based on YOLOv5x 
[22], BlueberryYOLO [23] employed an improved MobileNetv3 as the 
backbone and integrated a multi-scale fused attention module into its 
head for enhanced small object detection, achieving a mAP@50 of 78.3 
% in detecting three ripeness levels of blueberries at 47 FPS (frame per 
second) on an edge computing unit. Despite these previous studies, most 
of them experimented with a small number of images captured for 
localized blueberry bushes, which may partially explain their mediocre 
detection accuracy, and replicated testing was not conducted, under­
mining the credibility of model performance. Additionally, datasets re­
ported in these studies are not publicly available.
Improved detection models can enhance the accuracy of fruit 
counting, which is a crucial step for fruit yield estimation. Li et al. [24] 
applied YOLOv8 for detecting blueberries from multi-view images (i.e., 
top, left, and right views), obtaining a mAP@50 of 77.3 % based on 
YOLOv8x. By synthesizing the detection results from different views, a 
linear regression model was built to predict the total number of blue­
berries per bush, leading to a percentage counting error of 24.6 %. This 
study encountered difficulty in stitching different images for each 
blueberry bush to generate a holistic view of the plant, which would help 
improve counting accuracy, and it did not differentiate blueberries of 
different levels of maturity in image annotation and modeling. Zhai et al. 
[25] integrated attention mechanisms into the YOLOv5s model for 
blueberry detection, enhancing the model’s ability to handle occluded 
blueberry clusters. The YOLOv5s-CA (Coordinate Attention) variant 
achieved a 91.1 % mAP@50, representing a 4.7 % improvement over the 
accuracy by the original YOLOv5s. Additionally, a counting algorithm 
was embedded into the YOLOv5 framework, enabling accurate blue­
berry fruit counting.
To assist in computer vision-based harvest-decision marking and 
yield estimation of blueberries, this study aimed to develop and evaluate 
advanced YOLO object detectors including YOLOv8 [26] and YOLOv9 
[27], for blueberry detection and whereby fruit counting and harvest 
maturity estimation using canopy images. Specific objectives were thus 
to: 
1) present the first publicly available, annotated dataset of blueberry 
canopy images acquired in diverse orchard conditions.
2) evaluate the efficacy of YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 models for detecting 
ripe and unripe blueberries using canopy images.
3) predict the blueberry count and the percentage of ripe fruits in each 
image based on fruit detection.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Dataset
The dataset in this study consisted of 140 color images of blueberry 
canopies captured using smartphone cameras (iPhone SE and iPhone 13 
Pro), among which 134 images were collected for rabbiteye blueberries 
of mixed cultivars on a horticulture farm of Mississippi State University 
(Beaumont, MS) and a commercial blueberry orchard (Richton, Mis­
sissippi) in May of 2022 and the remaining was collected for highbush 
blueberries of “Bluecrop” in a research farm of Michigan State Univer­
sity (Holt, MI) in August of 2022. These images from Mississippi were 
captured before the peak picking season and exhibited more variations 
in fruit maturity. Images were acquired at varied spatial scales for bush 
canopies and branches under diverse, natural field light conditions, 
which would be desirable for building robust blueberry detection 
models. Various viewpoints and scenes were considered in the image 
collection, ranging from localized branches to an entire bush canopy. 
Fig. 1 shows example images acquired from blueberry bushes.
The acquired images were annotated by a trained crew following a 
working protocol,3 where the VGG Image Annotator (VIA) (version 
2.0.12) [28] was used to perform bounding box annotations. To simplify 
maturity estimation while minimizing annotation errors, blueberry in­
stances were annotated as “Blue” and “Unblue” for the two classes of 
ripe and unripe fruit based on the fruit color, although fruit color varied 
from green to dark blue. To ensure annotation accuracy, each image

unripe fruits, and hence separate evaluations for both ripe and unripe

Fig. 2 shows the histogram of the number of annotated fruit instances of

with an average of 128; particularly, about 44 % of the dataset contained 
over 100 blueberry instances, and over 16 % of the dataset had over 200 
blueberries. On the other hand, the ripe fruit percentage ranged from 
0 % to 96 %, with an average of 35 %, and over 80 % of images had a ripe 
fruit percentage lower than 50 %, as the majority of the image data were 
captured during the relatively early stages of blueberry maturity.
2.2. Blueberry detection
2.2.1. YOLO models
YOLO series models have become the most popular real-time object 
detectors, among which YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 are at the forefront of 
YOLO models. As one-stage detectors, YOLOs directly predict bounding 
boxes and class probabilities in a single neural network run, delivering 
remarkable inference efficiency while attaining decent detection accu­
racy. To handle the perceived challenge of detecting densely packed 
blueberries, advanced YOLO models such YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 are 
preferred to earlier versions. Since its introduction in January of 2023, 
YOLOv8 [31] has already demonstrated remarkable performance in 
high-speed object detection for computer vision tasks [32]. Inheriting 
the advantage of YOLO series in achieving a balanced trade-off between 
detection accuracy and speed, YOLOv8 is featured with new notable 
improvements, such as a modified CSPDarknet53 backbone, anchor-free 
detection head, the use of distribution focal loss (DFL) to handle class 
imbalance, the one-stage task-aligned assigner for aligning classification 
and localization tasks, among others. In this study, YOLOv8 in a large 
configuration (YOLOv8l) was chosen for blueberry detection given its 
good trade-off between accuracy and speed.
Among the latest versions of the YOLO series, YOLOv9 [27] has 
shown performance surpassing previous YOLO versions with compara­
ble or even fewer parameters. YOLOv9 implements a novel concept of 
Programmable Gradient Information (PGI) to propagate multi-level 
gradient information through an auxiliary reversible branch. It also in­
corporates a newly designed generalized efficient layer aggregation 
network (GELAN) for improved parameter utilization efficiency. This 
expands the applicability of YOLOv9 to a wide range of inference

2.2.2. Experimentation
Fig. 3 depicts the modeling pipeline for blueberry detection and 
counting. The image dataset was randomly split into train, validation, 
and test subsets, according to a partition ratio of 75 %–5 %–20 %, 
respectively. To facilitate model training, pre-trained weights learned on 
the COCO dataset were leveraged to train YOLOv8l and YOLOv9-c 
models. The aforementioned train-from-scratch strategy [27] was uti­
lized for training the YOLOv9-c model for comparison. The size of each 
image was calculated as the square root of the product of its height and 
width. Given the average original image resolution of 1474 pixels and an 
average bounding box size of 43 pixels. In this study, images with sizes 
ranging from 412 to 3680 pixels showed that high image resolutions are 
required during training to effectively detect small objects in images 
with original high resolutions. Here for blueberry detection, the ac­
quired images were resized to 2560 × 2560 pixels as in the input of 
YOLO models. This resizing provides a balance between retaining suf­
ficient details in high-resolution images and ensuring training efficiency. 
Training YOLOv9 was much more time-consuming than YOLOv8 due to 
the added computation in PGI of YOLOv9 [27]. Hence, for enhanced 
detection accuracy, YOLOv8 was also trained and tested with 
higher-resolution images resized to 3520 × 3520 pixels. This resolution 
allows most of the images to retain their original size and maximize the 
available details. Only two images exceeded this resolution, with orig­
inal sizes of 3527 and 3680 pixels. Image resizing was done by rescaling 
the long side of each acquired image to the target size and padding the 
short side to maintain the original image aspect ratio. The image reso­
lutions were empirically determined given the computing hardware 
available for model training and the size of raw images.
Data augmentation has been shown to enhance model performance 
in object detection [34]. Both YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 have an active data 
augmentation preprocess of input images. In this study, mosaic [21], 
which is to increase image diversity by combining regions of interest 
from multiple images into one image, was implemented in combination 
with geometric/color transformations (e.g., image flip, image trans­
lation, and image hue-saturation-value adjustment), to increase data 
diversity. Other data augmentation options are also available for both 
YOLOv8 and YOLOv9, such as mix-up [35] and copy-paste. However, 
our preliminary experiments showed these techniques increased the 
complexity of images with blueberry clusters and confounded the 
detection model in the learning process and hence were not considered 
in the formal modeling.
Because of the uncertainty of dataset partitioning, training and 
testing models for one specific dataset split may be insufficient for 
objectively evaluating model performance, since the distributions of 
training set and test set can vary with dataset partition. More optimistic 
results can be attained by chance if the specific partition happens to be 
favorable, wherein the distribution of the training set closely matches 
that of the test set. Hence, a Monte Carlo cross-validation procedure 
[36], with three replications, was employed in this study to obtain a 
reliable estimate of model performance. This involved repeating the 
processes of dataset partitioning, model training, and testing three times 
with different random seeds. The mean performance metrics on the 
testing data, which are described in the subsequent section below, were 
computed and used for model evaluation.
The deep learning framework of PyTorch (version 1.13) with the 
Ultralytics library5 was utilized to train YOLO models for a maximum of 
200 training epochs with a batch size of 1 (to accommodate the large 
image resolution). All other hyperparameters were set to their default 
values as specified in the official implementations of YOLOv8.6 Both 
YOLOv8l and YOLOv9-c were trained based on the models pre-trained 
on the COCO dataset. Model training was conducted on a workstation 
computer with an Intel i9-10900 × 10-core processor, 256 GB random- 
access memory, and a graphical processing unit (GPU) of NVIDIA A6000 
(48 GB GDDR6X memory). With the input resolution of 2560 × 2560 
pixels, YOLOv8l requires 12.9 GB of GPU memory, while YOLOv9-c 
starts at 20.9 GB and continues to increase, eventually occupying all 
available GPU memory (48 GB in our case). At a resolution of 3520 ×
3520 pixels, YOLOv8l requires 23.7 GB of GPU memory.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Blueberry detection
Table 1 summarizes the mAP@50 of YOLOv8l, and YOLOv9-c tested 
with the input of images of 2560 × 2560 pixels. Both models attained an 
overall mAP@50 exceeding 90 %. Although YOLOv9-c outperformed 
YOLOv8l by 0.9 %, their difference over three modeling replications was 
found statistically insignificant based on a paired t-test (at a significant 
level of 0.05). Though, YOLOv9-c was far more time-consuming to train 
than YOLOv8l. The training times (for a single modeling replication) on 
a workstation computer with a 48-GB GPU were 5.5 and 30.1 hours for 
YOLOv8l and YOLOv9-c, respectively. The slow training speed of 
YOLv9-c is probably attributed to the fact that the additional auxiliary 
reversible branch and the calculation of multi-level auxiliary informa­
tion are implemented to alleviate the information bottleneck caused by 
the deepening of neural networks and aggregate all-level gradient in­
formation, respectively. These added modules were only utilized during 
the training phase to facilitate gradient propagation across different 
semantic levels and removed in the inference stage. Despite the lengthy 
training of YOLOv9-c, both models appeared to be on par with each 
other in terms of inference speeds, based on testing on different 
computing devices as shown in Table 2. YOLOv8l offers a better trade- 
off between training time and performance. Considering the superior 
training efficiency of YOLOv8l, especially for high-resolution images, 
while achieving the accuracy and inference speed comparable to 
YOLOv9-c, YOLOv8l was hence chosen for further experiments with the 
input of higher-resolution images for enhanced detection accuracy.
Trained with the image input of 3520 × 3520 pixels, YOLOv8l 
yielded slightly better detection accuracies. Table 3 summarizes the 
resultant performance metrics. An overall mAP@50 of 92.7 % was ob­
tained, representing a 1.7 % improvement (compared to that in Table 2). 
This confirmed the importance of high-resolution images for detecting 
blueberries which are small objects in canopy images. The accuracy 
obtained here compared favorably to those reported in previous blue­
berry detection studies with mAP@50 less than 90 % [20] and even less 
than 80 % [24]. The better detection of unripe than ripe fruit was 
observed in terms of all metrics (precision, recall, and mAP@50), with a 
mAP@50 of 93.3 % for unripe blueberries and a mAP@50 of 92.1 % for 
ripe fruits. The models trained with the reduced image resolution 
(Table 2) also performed better in detecting unripe fruits. This is likely 
due to the greater number of annotated unripe blueberries in the dataset

Figs. 4 and 5 illustrate the errors in detecting the ripe and unripe fruit 
in a canopy image, respectively, including close-up views that highlight 
typical precision and recall errors. Despite the densely packed fruit in 
this image, the YOLOv8l model achieved decedent results with 88.8 % 
precision, 87.0 % recall, and 91.3 % mAP@50 for ripe fruit, and 84.1 % 
precision, 89.0 % recall, and 91.5 % mAP@50 for unripe fruit. Yet, there 
still existed many blueberries that remained undetected (recall error) or 
wrongly classified (precision error). In general, those blueberries that 
are well exposed without significant occlusion in the scene were accu­
rately detected, while errors occurred in detecting the fruit that is 
visually blurry or occluded in the image. The blueberries heavily 
occluded by leaves, branches, or other berries, especially those in shaded 
areas, were notably prone to misclassification or omission. The fruit 
appearing in a dense cluster could also be a source of error. Overall, the 
presence of out-of-focus blueberries, dense clusters, and occlusions due 
to various factors, poses great challenges to the detection model. 
Moreover, the detection of unripe blueberries in the advanced maturity 
stage with transitional or ambiguous colors similar to ripe blueberries 
was also challenging, which could lead to precision errors. Annotating 
these blueberries could be prone to error as well due to the recognition 
subjectivity.
3.2. Blueberry counting and maturity estimation
Fig. 6 shows the scatter plots for blueberry counting and ripe fruit 
percentage estimation directly based on fruit detection by YOLOv8l 
(Table 3). These plots accumulate all the predictions for test images over 
three modeling replications. For the counting task, the predicted fruit 
counts and the ground truth agreed well, with an R2 = 0.996 and RMSE 
= 10.39. The relative counting error (calculated as the ratio of RMSE to 
the mean fruit count) was 6.67 %. The ripe fruit percentage estimation 
resulted in an R2 = 0.973 and RMSE = 3.62 %, with the corresponding 
relative error of 9.65 %. Overall, these results are practically respectable 
given their relative percentage accuracy exceeding 90 %, confirming the 
utility of YOLOv8l for the two tasks. Better counting and maturity 
estimation would be attained with improved fruit detection models.
3.3. Discussion
Research on deep-learning-based blueberry detection and down­
stream tasks (e.g., counting, maturity and yield estimation, and selective 
harvesting) has been scant in the literature, although early studies were 
conducted on yield estimation of wild blueberries based on the blue 
pixel percentage calculation through traditional imaging processing

YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 represent two recent generations of YOLO 
series for real-time object detection. Their utility for detecting small 
fruits in dense scenes has remained to be fully investigated. Previous 
studies on YOLOs for blueberry detection [20,24] experimented pri­
marily with the image input of relatively lower resolution, which could 
restrict model accuracy. Generally, small objects in low-resolution im­
ages are difficult to detect due to indistinguishable features and limited 
context information [40,41], especially with complex, unstructured 
backgrounds. MacEachern et al. [20] trained YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 
models with images of 1280 × 720 pixels and obtained the best accuracy 
of mAP@50 = 88.12 % in detecting unripe and ripe blueberries. How­
ever, higher-resolution inputs, although more computing power is 
demanded, would be beneficial for enhanced blueberry detection. Li 
et al. [24] evaluated the different inputs of 640 × 640 pixels and 1280 ×
1280 pixels for blueberry detection, in which the higher-resolution im­
ages yielded 10–17 % accuracy improvements over the lower-resolution 
input. In our study, the images at 2560 × 2560 and 3520 × 3520 pixels 
that preserved the appearance details of blueberries, were used for 
modeling, which may partially explain the obtained decent mAP50 of 
nearly 93 %, the highest accuracy in the context of literature [20,40,41]. 
Though, a trade-off should be considered between accuracy and effi­
ciency given constraints in computing resources in practice. Model 
pruning or compression techniques [42,43] are hence worthy of inves­
tigation for accelerated inference while gaining accuracy benefits from 
high-resolution inputs.

ments. The blueberry dataset was acquired using handheld imaging 
devices rather than an autonomous mobile platform. The imagery ac­
quired by mobile platforms would be more relevant to developing ma­

estimation of blueberry counts and maturity estimation, it would be 
necessary to acquire images from different views of the same blueberry 
canopy. However, it remains a technical challenge to stitch multi-view 
canopy images and generate a holistic view of specific bushes. Li et al. 
[24] acquired blueberry canopy images from three views (top, left, and 
right) but failed to generate satisfactory image stitching due to 
inadequate image overlaps.
Canopy-level blueberry detection face particular challenges with 
small fruit size and occlusions. Techniques for enhancing small object 
detection while handling occlusions should be considered in future 
research. For instance, Zhai et al. [25] evaluated the integration of four 
attention mechanisms, i.e., convolutional block attention module 
(CBAM) [44], SimAM [45], coordinate attention (CA) [44], and squeeze 
and excitation networks (SENet) [46] into YOLOv5s model for blueberry 
detection. While each attention mechanism offers specific advantages (e. 
g., feature representation enhancement, spatial correlation awareness, 
and channel-wise augmentation), their experiments explored the 
mechanism for improved model ability to detect small and occluded 
blueberries. To expand practical usage of models in video scans, Zhou 
et al. [47] enhanced YOLOv5s with attention and transformer modules 
for strawberry detection. Occlusion handling was achieved by 
enhancing and comparing tracking-by-detection algorithms (i.e. Deep­
SORT, ByteTrack, and StrongSORT) with a multi-object mapping algo­
rithm for precise multi-class counting based on consecutive frames. 
These approaches can be a useful reference for improving model 
robustness against occlusions in practical applications.
Moving forward, blueberry yield estimation based on fruit counting 
is to be accomplished in our future work. Research is ongoing to conduct 
a more comprehensive performance analysis of a large suite of YOLO 
models at varied complexity scales, including YOLOv10 [27] and 
YOLOv11 [48] as well as other advanced architectures, on a substan­
tially expanded dataset, to assist in informed model selection for prac­
tical deployment. The blueberry detection models of this study have yet 
to be field tested by deploying them on a machine vision-based mobile 
system. Furthermore, a smartphone application integrating YOLO 
models is being built for real-time usage by blueberry growers [49].
4. Conclusion
This study presents the first publicly available image dataset of 
blueberry canopies acquired under diverse orchard conditions, con­
taining 17,809 annotated ripe (blue) and unripe fruit instances for 
blueberry detection. YOLOv8l and YOLOv9-c were evaluated on the 
dataset, and trained with the input images of 2560 × 2560 pixels, both 
the models achieved comparable performance with an overall mAP@50 
of 91.0 % and 91.7 %, respectively, and similar inference speeds on 
different computing hardware. However, YOLOv9-c was far more time- 
consuming to train than YOLOv8l. The input of higher-resolution images 
of 3520 × 3520 pixels for YOLOv8l yielded improved accuracy with an
