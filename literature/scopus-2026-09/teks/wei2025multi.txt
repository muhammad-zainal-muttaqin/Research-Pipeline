Abstract—Quantitative fruit counting at the individual tree
level is a fundamental requirement in data-driven precision agri-
culture and a critical input for yield estimation models. However,
achieving consistent tracking and accurate detection of fruits
in unstructured outdoor environments remains a challenging
task due to factors such as occlusion, varying illumination and
sensor noise. Moreover, conventional 2D image-based methods
are prone to scale ambiguity and duplicate detections from
multiple viewpoints. To overcome these challenges, this work
presents a 3D fruit counting framework based on stereo cameras
in which each detection is localized within a georeferenced spatial
coordinate system using GPS and visual odometry. The fruits are
detected using a YOLOv8x model to identify object positions
within individual images. To enable tracking across multiple
consecutive frames, two state-of-the-art algorithms, OC-SORT
and ByteTrack, are benchmarked against each other. OC-SORT,
with incorporated optical flow, achieved the best performance
on the validation dataset, with HOTA = 0.59, MOTA = 0.52,
and IDF1 = 0.67, while the detection model reached a mAP0.5:0.95
= 0.56. The proposed fruit counting framework demonstrated
promising performance on Elstar trees, yielding an estimated
total counting error of 20 apples, corresponding to a RMSE of
6.24 % and an R2 score of 0.96. Given that fruit count is a
key parameter to yield prediction, these results support accurate
yield mapping and informed precision orchard management.
Index Terms—Fruit Counting, Multi-Object Tracking, Visual
Odometry, Yield Estimation, 3D-Mapping, Precision Agriculture.
I. INTRODUCTION
Accurate fruit yield estimation is a critical aspect of agri-
cultural production, as it directly influences harvest planning,
storage logistics, market pricing, and supply chain manage-
ment. Among various agronomic indicators such as blossom
density and fruit size, fruit count remains a key parameter for
reliably estimating yield [1]. Currently, farmers depend on pre-
harvest yield estimations generated by third party software or
agricultural consultants. These estimations are usually derived
from manual counts performed on a small subsample of trees,
which are extrapolated to the entire orchard using regression
models. However, such limited sampling can introduce sig-
nificant errors, as fruit-bearing patterns vary within different
years and are strongly influenced by the growing location [2].
This variability highlights the challenge and importance of
developing robust methods for fruit counting.
The fundamental problem of fruit counting in orchards can
be decomposed into a sequence of subtasks, including the
detection and tracking of individual fruits, followed by the
unique assignment to the corresponding trees.
More recent advancements in computational hardware have
enabled deep learning methods to effectively handle dynamic
environments, making them well-suited for object detection
tasks in orchards. In particular, YOLO-based models combine
high detection accuracy with real-time performance due to
their efficient single-stage detection design [3], [4], [5]. A
preliminary study presented in [6] evaluated the performance
of several modern object detection algorithms, including Faster
R-CNN, YOLOv5, YOLOv8 and RT-DETR, in the context
of apple detection. While the results show that YOLOv8
and RT-DETR achieve comparable performance in mAP50:95,
YOLOv8 achieved lower computational overhead due to its
one-stage architecture.
Counting fruits from a single image can be efficient in some
cases but often leads to errors, particularly when fruits are
partially occluded or not visible due to camera perspective
[6]. Therefore, accurate fruit counting depends on the reliable
tracking of individual fruits across multiple frames [3]. This
can be achieved using multi-object tracking (MOT) algorithms,
which assign consistent identifiers (IDs) to objects over a set
of consecutive images. Moreover, MOT algorithms rely on
well-established methods such as Kalman filter and Hungarian
algorithm and offer a computationally lightweight solution,
making them ideal for real-time applications [6].
Automatic fruit-to-tree assignment is still in its early stages,
primarily due to overlapping tree canopies and the need to
integrate observations from both the front and rear viewpoints
of tree rows. To address these issues, this work introduces a
3D fruit-to-tree assignment framework that integrates object
detection with fruit-level MOT. Unlike prior approaches [7],
[8], which integrated a LiDAR for 3D localization and tree

II. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
The fruit-growing region along the southern banks of the
Elbe River, spanning Hamburg and Lower Saxony, consti-
tutesthe largest contiguous area of fruit cultivation in North-
ernEurope. In this study, the dataset used for fruit counting
was collected in the experimental test fields of the Esteburg, a
regional research and advisory institution located in the Altes
Land near Hamburg.
A. Hardware Setup
To support the development and validation of automated
fruit counting, a multi-sensor carrier platform was developed
in the context of a collaborative research initiative. As illus-
trated in Fig. 1a, the base configuration of the sensor setup is
mounted on a robust steel frame, which can be attached to the
standard three-point hitch of narrow-gauge tractors. Although
a LiDAR sensor is part of the framework, it is currently only
used for validation purposes, as a full integration into the
processing pipeline remains impractical due to high associated
costs. Image acquisition is carried out using two Basler ace 2
Pro industrial cameras (a2A5328-15ucPRO) in a custom stereo
setup, each featuring a resolution of 24.4 MP and supporting
frame rates of up to 15 fps. Given a maximum canopy height
of 3 m and a fixed camera-to-tree distance of approximately
1.5 m, the cameras are equipped with Fujinon 1.2 ′′ lenses
(CF8ZA-1S). Accurate georeferencing of the captured images
is ensured by the simpleRTK3B Heading module with the
mosaic-H chip from Septentrio, connected to two multiband
GNSS antennas. The central computing unit is enclosed in
a protective orange case and includes an NVIDIA Jetson
AGX Orin Developer Kit, delivering the computational power
required for real-time data processing and sensor integration.
However, model training and evaluation were carried out on
a high-performance workstation equipped with an NVIDIA
RTX 6000 Ada GPU to accelerate computation and reduce
processing time. A more detailed description of the sensor
platform is provided in [10].
B. Data Collection
The image data were collected in different orchard sections
of the experimental test fields at ESTEBURG. To facilitate the
training of the detection algorithm, four apple cultivars were

selected: Elstar, Wellant, Rockit, and Pia 41. These cultivars
exhibit inherent variations in fruit size and coloration, while
image acquisition at different times of the day introduced

During data acquisition, the tractor maintained an average

was calibrated in the field prior to data acquisition using a
PuzzleBoard design featuring positional encoding [11]. A total

detection, while 131 consecutive frames of Wellant and Elstar
were labeled with trajectories for the tracking task. For the
final validation of fruit counts, data were collected from 30
Elstar apple trees within the experimental field. All annotations
were performed using the CVAT (Computer Vision Annotation
Tool) data annotation platform.

A. Sensor Localization
In order to estimate the geometry of the scene and the
motion of the camera in an orchard environment, a self-
supervised deep visual steoreo odometry (DVSO) algorithm
[12] is employed to process synchronized stereo image pairs.
The DVSO generates optical flow, dense depth maps and
camera odometry. The optical flow is predicted using a deep
learning model based on a customised PWC-Net [13], which
analyses the displacement of image features between tem-
porally adjacent images. For a well-rectified stereo camera
setup, the relative transformation between the cameras can
be considered constant due to the fixed sensor configuration.
Under this assumption, the depth z of a point can be computed
from the disparity d, the stereo baseline b, and the focal length
f. The disparity is defined as the difference in horizontal image
coordinates between the corresponding points in the left ul and
right ur stereo image and can be inferred from the previously
described flow estimation:

The camera ego-motion, including translation and rotation,
is estimated from the optical flow between consecutive frames
of a single camera and its corresponding depth map. While
classical geometry-dependent methods such as the eight-point
algorithm can be applied, they are sensitive to flow inaccu-
racies. Therefore, an additional neural network architecture
is used to determine camera odometry. For the trainings
pipeline, the system leverages photometric consistency and
3D geometric constraints to learn without requiring annotated
data. This enables rapid collection and training on vast data
quantities, ensuring robustness under the challenging condi-
tions of orchards.
Transforming the detected apples from the 2D image plane
into the 3D reference frame requires knowledge of the position
and orientation of the camera expressed in the global GNSS
coordinate system. Consequently, the ego-motion estimated by
the visual odometry algorithm must be aligned with a global
coordinate system. Since this transformation involves rotations
and translations between local and global frames, this process
exhibits nonlinearity. To address this, an Unscented Kalman
Filter (UKF) [14] is employed to fuse measurements from
the GNSS sensor and the camera’s visual odometry. The filter
infers the global position and orientation of the camera as
hidden states by combining local linear and angular velocity
measurements from visual odometry and GNSS observations.
Additionally, the GNSS observations correct accumulated drift
and serve to anchor the estimates by providing georeferenced
data. As a result, the global position vector pG
cam and rotation
matrix RGG′ of the camera can be determined, where GG′
denotes the relative rotation between two consecutive camera
poses in the georeferenced global frame.
B. Computer Vision
To detect apples in the image sequences, a YOLOv8x
model [15] is employed. The model was initially pretrained
on the COCO dataset and subsequently fine-tuned on the
self-collected dataset described in Section II-B. Apple an-
notations were formatted as (xc, yc, w, h) to match YOLO’s
input requirements. In order to expand the training dataset and
improve model robustness, image augmentations were applied
using the Albumentations library, which enables detailed con-
trol over augmentation types and their respective probabilities.
Additionally, hyperparameter tuning was performed using the
Ray Tune library, applying random search to optimize the
parameter configuration.

ByteTrack implements a two-stage association process using
the Hungarian algorithm. In the first stage, high-confidence
detections are matched with predicted positions using IoU.
In the second stage, unmatched tracks are further associated
with low-confidence detections to compensate for missed asso-
ciations. While ByteTrack focuses on tracking accuracy, OC-
SORT improves robustness under occlusion by incorporating
a motion consistency term into a cost matrix of the Hungarian
algorithm. The object’s velocity is estimated from recent
positional changes and used to evaluate how closely new
detections in the current frame align with its expected motion,
forming the motion similarity cost. Given that optical flow
is estimated by DSVO, the impact of incorporating velocity
as an additional measurement and modeling its corresponding
measurement noise based on certainty values is integrated into
the Kalman filter.

IV. RESULTS
The detection performance is measured using mAP0.5:0.95,
which captures both localization and classification accuracy
across multiple IoU thresholds. The tracking performance
is evaluated using HOTA, MOTA, and IDF1, where HOTA
reflects the joint accuracy of detection and association, MOTA
emphasizes overall object coverage and penalizes identity
switches, and IDF1 measures the consistency of identity
preservation. To ensure an unbiased evaluation, the training
dataset for the object detection algorithm was selected to

In general, a Kalman filter tracker instantiate a separate
Kalman filter instance for every individual object. Each filter
remains active throughout the lifespan of the tracked object
and is terminated once the object can no longer be reliably
associated. During this period, the filter incrementally refines
its state estimates through recursive updates based on in-
coming measurements. However, when the image acquisition
frequency is low, the filter receives fewer updates, weakening
its ability to accurately learn motion dynamics. Consequently,
at an image acquisition rate of 5 fps, the effect of random
initialization becomes more significant, resulting in degraded
tracking performance. In this experiment, the tracker without
optical flow still achieved similar results, primarily because the
initial velocity estimates were pre-set through hyperparameter
optimization using Ray Tune. While effective, this approach
requires manual reconfiguration for each dataset or an ad-
ditional mechanism to infer initial velocities from external
sensors. Failing to do so leads to a substantial decline in
robust tracks, as evidenced in [18]. The performance difference
across datasets suggest that Elstar poses a substantially more
challenging detection scenario than Wellant, as indicated by
the lower DeTA scores. Since OC-SORT with optical flow
integration achieved the best tracking performance, it was
adopted for the following fruit count validation.

For the validation experiment, the Elstar cultivar was ex-
clusively selected, as it was the only variety harvested using
the Pluk-O-Trak platform (Munckhof Fruit Tech Innovators) in
2024, enabling the acquisition of machine-counted fruits. This
choice was made to minimize the potential human bias intro-
duced by hand counting fruits. Based on the collected data,
fruit counts were obtained from both sides of each tree and
subsequently matched using their global GPS coordinates. A
distance threshold was defined based on pre-harvest diameter
of Elstar apples, assumed to range from 70 to 85 mm. Apples
positioned closer to each other than the defined threshold were
considered duplicate detections and therefore one instance for
each duplicate was removed during the matching process. A
scatter plot of the counting result is presented in Fig. 4, where
the ground truth is plotted against the estimated counts per
tree. The red line represents the line of perfect agreement
between the estimates and the actual counts. Using the Pluk-
O-Trak, a total of 535 fruits were machine-counted across the
30 sampled Elstar trees. The counting method proposed in this
study initially estimated 619 apples, which was reduced to 555
after applying the threshold. This corresponds to a total error
of 20 apples, with a root mean square error (RMSE) of 6.24 %
per tree, indicating low average deviation, and a coefficient of
determination (R2) of 0.96, reflecting overall strong agreement
with ground truth counts. Due to the dense canopy structure,
many apples were only visible from one side of the tree
row, resulting in a smaller impact of duplicate detections.
One of the main contributing factors to the overestimation in
total apple count is assumed to be the occurrence of identity
switches, arising from limitations in the tracker’s ability to
consistently associate detections over time as indicated by the
IDF1 score of 0.69 in Table I. Additionally, the inclusion
of green PIA41 apples in the training dataset increased the
likelihood of false positive detections, as their visual similarity
to small leaves can lead to misclassifications.

V. CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK
In this work, a 3D vision-based apple counting frame-
work was developed to enable the association of fruits with
individual trees. A custom dataset was collected using a
multi-sensor carrier platform within an experimental orchard
environment to train and evaluate the object detection and
tracking components.
Specifically, a YOLOv8x model was used and trained on
images of multiple different apple cultivars to ensure robust
detection performance across varying fruit characteristics. The
resulting detections served as input to the tracking pipeline,
where two state-of-the-art algorithms, ByteTrack and OC-
SORT, were evaluated and compared. Given that the data were
collected at a high resolution and a low frame rate of 5 fps, op-
tical flow was incorporated to support motion prediction in the
Kalman filter-based tracker. As a result, OC-SORT with optical
flow outperformed the other methods across all benchmarking
metrics. Moreover, reliable performance without optical flow
is highly dependent on correctly initialized states, particularly
the object’s velocity.
To mitigate 2D image artifacts such as perspective distortion
and to address the issue of double counting fruits observed
from both sides of a tree row, each tracked fruit was projected
into 3D space. Global fruit positions were estimated using
an UKF, incorporating GPS data and camera-based odometry.
The proposed counting framework yielded a RMSE of 6.24 %
per tree and a R2 = 0.96, indicating a strong statistical fit. For
future work, additional data must be collected and annotated
to thoroughly evaluate the generalizability of the framework.
In particular, access to 3D-labeled fruit data from LiDAR
would enable the development and evaluation of tracking
methods operating entirely in 3D space, potentially reducing
or eliminating reliance on 2D trackers.
Ultimately, robust and precise fruit counting serves as a
foundational step for downstream applications in orchard
management. Accurate fruit counts are critical for size estima-
tion, growth monitoring and yield prediction, which informs
logistics and labor planning. Furthermore, per-tree count data
can support precision interventions such as targeted canopy
management and nutrient optimization, while also providing
insights into bearing cycles. As orchard systems move increas-
ingly toward automation and data-driven decision-making,
scalable and accurate fruit counting solutions will become an
essential component of modern horticultural practice.
