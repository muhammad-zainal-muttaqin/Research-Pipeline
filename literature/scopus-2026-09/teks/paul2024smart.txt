Smart solutions for capsicum Harvesting: Unleashing the power of YOLO 
for Detection, Segmentation, growth stage Classification, Counting, and 
real-time mobile identification 
Ayan Paul *, Rajendra Machavaram , Ambuj , Dheeraj Kumar , Harsh Nagar 
Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur, India

Counting and tracking algorithm 
A B S T R A C T  
This research paper explores a comprehensive approach to advancing capsicum harvesting by integrating 
cutting-edge technologies. The study addresses key objectives, including capsicum detection using various YOLO 
algorithms, peduncle detection through YOLO segmentation models in a proposed robotic harvester, laboratory 
testing of cutting target point coordinates using the Real Sense D455 RGB-D camera, growth stage determination, 
and capsicum counting/tracking with a supervision algorithm. The investigation highlights the YOLOv8s model 
as the most successful for capsicum detection, achieving a remarkable mean Average Precision (mAP) of 0.967 at 
a 0.5 Intersection over Union (IOU) threshold. As part of the growth stage determination task, YOLOv8s achieved 
a satisfactory mAP of 0.614 at the same IOU threshold. Additionally, the YOLOv8s-seg model demonstrated 
superior performance in peduncle detection, attaining a box mAP of 0.790 and a mask mAP of 0.771. The 
YOLOv8s-seg model excels in peduncle detection with a box mAP of 0.790 and a mask mAP of 0.771. Laboratory 
experiments using the Real Sense D455 RGB-D camera showcased its capability to localize the target point with a 
maximum error of 8 mm longitudinally, 9 mm vertically, and 12 mm laterally. The developed tracking and 
counting algorithm achieve a notable counting accuracy of 94.1 % during the third harvesting cycle in the 
greenhouse. The Android application developed demonstrated robust performance, achieving high accuracy 
(Mean IoU: 0.92), precise localization (Mean Euclidean Distance: 5 pixels), responsive user interface (Touch 
Response Time: 150 ms), and broad compatibility across Android versions and device types, with effective error 
handling (Success Rate: 95 %). The study not only advances capsicum harvesting techniques but also presents 
practical insights for the integration of advanced technologies, paving the way for efficient robotic harvesting 
systems in agriculture.   
1. Introduction 
Fruits and vegetables hold paramount significance in the context of 
nutrition, catering not only to the health of millions but also playing a 
pivotal role in the global economy. According to the National Horti­
culture Board, India is the world’s second-largest producer of fruits and 
vegetables, after China. It is a major player in the agricultural landscape 
of the world, producing an astounding 99.07 million metric tonnes of 
fruits and 191.77 million metric tonnes of vegetables annually (Na­
tional Horticulture Board, 2019–20). Among these vegetables, 
capsicum, known as Sweet Pepper internationally and Simla Mirch in 
India, holds paramount importance in socio-economic terms. The 
country presently cultivates capsicum across more than 24,000 ha of 
land, yielding a substantial 563,020 metric tonnes annually (APEDA, 
2021–22). 
In recent decades, the cultivation of capsicum in greenhouses has 
surged in popularity due to its ability to ensure high productivity and a 
consistent supply throughout the year, meeting the escalating demand 
for diverse capsicum varieties in the market. Despite notable advance­
ments in agricultural automation, a significant portion of fruits and 
vegetables, including capsicum, continue to be hand-picked each year in 
greenhouses. Harvesting this valuable vegetable presents challenges, 
particularly in manual identification, given the varying stages of 
ripening and the sheer volume of produce. Addressing this challenge, 
integrating artificial intelligence with automation becomes imperative 
for intelligent picking, marking a crucial stride in global agriculture.

Machine vision emerges as a pivotal technology in the realm of smart 
picking, driving precision in agricultural operations. In the context of 
agriculture, particularly in the detection and picking of capsicum, ma­
chine vision faces significant challenges due to the occlusion of these 
vegetables behind leaves and clustered capsicums, varying light condi­
tions, and backgrounds that closely mimic the green hue of mature 
capsicums. These complexities underscore the need for advanced tech­
niques in object classification, object detection, image segmentation, 
key point detection, and object tracking, all of which are under intense 
scientific scrutiny. Additionally, the intricacies extend to the detection 
of capsicum peduncles, which are smaller, varied in shape, and prone to 
interference from complex backgrounds like the main stem. Overcoming 
these hurdles is essential for accurate vegetable detection, localization, 
and yield estimation, highlighting the critical role of machine vision in 
addressing the challenges posed by natural agricultural environments 
and intricate lighting conditions. 
In recent years, deep learning—a machine learning technique based 
on artificial neural networks (ANN)—has revolutionised the field of 
machine vision. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a subset of 
artificial neural networks (ANNs) with convolutional layers, are one of 
the main forces behind machine vision since they can extract features 
straight from the raw values of pixels without the need for hand- 
engineered features. Since its introduction by Joseph Redmon (Red­
mon et al., 2016), YOLO (You Only Look Once) has grown to be one of 
the most well-liked real-time machine vision algorithms. It does this by 
instantly identifying specific objects in videos, live feeds, or images 
using features learned from a deep convolutional neural network, all 
while operating much faster than other networks and maintaining ac­
curacy. YOLOv8, the highest performing YOLO model released very 
recently, setting new standards in real-time detection and segmentation, 
will soon become a hot topic in the research world for solving complex 
issues by providing simple and effective AI solutions. 
Research involving detection and localization in agriculture was 
traditionally based mainly on image processing technology. To enhance 
fruit detection, Bulanon et al. (2009) merged a visual and a thermal 
image of an orange canopy. Using the fuzzy logic fusion approach and 
the Laplacian pyramid transform (LPT), they discovered that, with a 
segmentation success rate of roughly 0.9, the fuzzy logic approach 
outperformed the LPT. A computer vision framework was created by 
Mery et al. (2010) to automatically classify corn tortilla quality based on 
five hedonic sub-classes provided by a sensory panel. In the classifica­
tion of the five hedonic sub-classes, it produced a 95 % performance 
after applying a feature selection technique and cross-validation. Ji et al. 
(2011) used image segmentation to extract colour and shape features 
from colour images of apples. They then introduced a new support 
vector machine-based classification algorithm for apple recognition, 
achieving an average recognition time of 352 ms and an approximate 89 
% recognition success rate. An X-ray machine vision method was created 
by Mery et al. (2011) to automatically identify fish bones in fish fillets. A 
salmon industry-provided sample of fish bones and trouts was used to 
validate the methodology, and the results showed a 99 % detection 
performance. In order to divide chestnuts into five classes, Donis- 
Gonz´alez et al. (2012) suggested a computer vision methodology. A 
sequential forward selection algorithm was then used to identify the 
most pertinent features. They discovered that 36 features worked well 
for creating a quadratic discriminant classifier that had an 89.6 % cross- 
validated overall performance accuracy. By extracting colour and 
geometrical data, Leiva-Valenzuela et al. (2013) suggested a pattern 
recognition method to automatically discriminate stem and calyx ends 
and detect damaged berries. They were able to identify the orientation 
of the blueberry in 96.8 % of the cases by using classifiers. A multi-class 
picture segmentation method was presented by Hung et al. (2013) to 
automate the segmentation of fruit, specifically almonds. They used 
multi-spectral picture data and a feature learning algorithm with a 
conditional random field, and they were able to obtain an 88 % global 
classification accuracy. A vision system that automatically predicted 
yield in vineyards was reported by Nuske et al. (2014). The system used 
lights and cameras installed on a car that was traversing the vineyard. 
Photos were processed by taking advantage of the three main visual 
cues—texture, colour, and shape—to create a powerful classifier that 
can identify berries with up to a 75 % spatial yield variance. By using an 
image analysis technique, Mohammadi et al. (2015) graded persimmon 
fruits into three commercial maturity stages. They discovered that a 
quadratic discriminant analysis classifier might be useful in classifying 
the fruits with a better overall accuracy rate of 90.24 %. According to 
McCool et al. (2016), 69.2 % of field-grown sweet peppers were 
discovered in three sites using a two-stage approach based on local bi­
nary pattern feature that first performs per-pixel segmentation and then 
detects sweet pepper. 
Deep learning technology has been successfully developed and 
implemented in numerous agricultural fields in recent years. In their 
study, Paul et al. (2023) categorically delineated contemporary Con­
volutional Neural Networks (CNNs) employed in object detection into 
two primary classifications. The first category comprises two-stage de­
tectors exemplified by architectures such as Faster R-CNN (Region-based 
Convolutional Neural Networks) and Mask R-CNN. These models 
incorporate a Region Proposal Network in the initial stage to generate 
regions of interest. Subsequently, these region proposals are forwarded 
through the network pipeline for the tasks of object classification and 
bounding-box regression. Despite their capability to deliver superior 
accuracy, these models are characterized by slower processing speeds. 
Zhou et al. (2017) provide a deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) 
based approach for identifying the primary organs of tomatoes. The 
results of the detection experiment demonstrated that the DCNN-based 
tomato organ detector can successfully identify fruits with varying de­
grees of maturity and flowers with varying flower ages. The detection 
average precisions (AP) of the detector for the fruit, flower, and stem are 
81.64 %, 84.48 %, and 53.94 %, respectively. Fu et al. (2018) proposed 
and tested a convolutional neural network (CNN) based kiwifruit image 
recognition system. The results showed that the model’s overall recog­
nition rate was 89.29 %, and its recognition time was 0.27 s. The 
recognition success of occluded fruit, overlapped fruit, adjacent fruit, 
and separated fruit were, respectively, 78.97 %, 83.11 %, 91.01 %, and 
94.78 %. A strategy for fine-tuning a pre-trained convolutional neural 
network to become particularly sensitive to apple blooms was reported 
by Dias et al. (2018). The experimental results showed recall and pre­
cision rates of 90 %. Using the Fruit 360 dataset, Thai et al., (2019) gave 
a succinct overview of convolution neural networks (CNNs) and the 
EfficientNet architecture for fruit recognition. The suggested model is 
95 % more accurate, according to the results. In order to enhance ma­
chine vision performance for fruit detection in a strawberry harvesting 
robot, Yu et al. (2019) introduced a Mask Region Convolutional Neural 
Network (Mask-RCNN). The detection result revealed that the mean 
intersection over union (MIoU) rate for instance segmentation was 
89.85 %, the recall rate was 95.41 %, and the average detection preci­
sion rate was 95.78 %. A model of harvesting robot vision detectors 
based on Mask Region Convolutional Neural Network (Mask R-CNN) 
was proposed by Jia et al. (2020). A random test set including 120 
photos is used to evaluate the approach; the precision rate is 97.31 %, 
and the recall rate is 95.70 %. Fruit and vegetables at supermarket 
checkout counters can be categorised using a progressive coarse-to-fine 
categorization technique, as suggested by Hameed et al. (2020). Ada­
Boost optimisation has been carried out using GoogleNet, MobileNet, 
and a customised CNN, showing potential test accuracy of up to 91.33 %. 
A score-based mask edge improvement of Mask-RCNN was proposed by 
Hameed et al. (2022) to segment images of fruits and vegetables in a 
supermarket setting. Based on the suggested method, a noteworthy 
improvement of 39.9 % in segmentation Average Precision has been 
reported. A more accurate approach to Apple instance segmentation was 
created by Wang et al. (2022) using an enhanced Mask region-based 
convolutional neural network (Mask RCNN). According to the experi­
mental findings, the enhanced Mask RCNN had an average run-time of

The single-stage detectors, like You Only Look Once (YOLO) and 
Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD), approach object detection as a 
straightforward regression problem that takes an input image and learns 
the class probabilities and bounding box coordinates. On the other hand, 
these models are faster but reach lower accuracy. Over the course of 
time, researchers have employed these algorithms in the field of agri­
culture to identify fruits, vegetables, and other agricultural products. 
These algorithms have also been utilised to determine the growth phases 
of these produce to a certain extent. Additionally, there has been a 
restricted application of these algorithms in the domain of tracking and 
counting. An enhanced YOLO-V3 model was shown by Tian et al., 
(2019) to identify apples in orchards with varying lighting, intricate 
backgrounds, overlapping apples, and branches and leaves at different 
growth stages. According to the test results, the model had an F1 score of 
0.809 for ripe apples and an average detection time of 0.304 s per frame. 
The Yolo V3 model’s feature layer was optimised by Liu and Wang 
(2020) to increase detection accuracy and speed after a dataset of to­
mato diseases and pests was built in a real-world setting. Test findings 
revealed that this allowed for a mean average precision of 92.39 % and a 
mean detection time of 20.39 ms. A real-time apple flower recognition 
technique utilising the channel-pruned YOLO v4 deep learning algo­
rithm was proposed by Wu et al. (2020). The test results revealed that 
the apple blossom identification model after pruning had a 96.74 % 
reduction in parameters, a 231.51 MB reduction in model size, a 39.47 % 
drop in inference time, and a 97.31 % mAP—which was just 0.24 % 
lower than the model before pruning. Using the Single-Shot MultiBox 
Detector (SSD) and YOLO architectures, Augusto Magalh˜aes et al. 
(2021) identified green and reddish tomatoes. When compared to SSD 
Inception v2, SSD ResNet 50, SSD ResNet 101, and YOLOv4 Tiny, they 
discovered that SSD MobileNet v2 performed the best, achieving an F1- 
score of 66.15 %, a mAP of 51.46 %, and an inference time of 16.44 ms. 
The YOLOv3-tiny was used by Bazame et al. (2021) to perform coffee 
fruit detection and classification. The results showed an average preci­
sion of 86 %, 85 %, and 80 % for the classes of unripe, ripe, and overripe 
coffee fruits, and a mAP of 84 %, F1-Score of 82 %, precision of 83 %, 
and recall of 82 % for the validation set. In order to detect physiological 
disorders and the ripening stage in ’Abbe Fetetl’ pears. The yield esti­
mating pipeline developed by Osman et al. (2022) counts apples, or­
anges, and pumpkins in a movie by using YOLO object detection and 
tracking. The yield estimating module counts oranges and pumpkins 
with an accuracy of 79 % to 93.9 %, and it displays accuracy between 91 
% and 95 % on actual footage of apple trees with changes. A strawberry 
growth detection algorithm called SDNet (Strawberry Detect Net) was 
created by An et al. (2022). in line with the YOLOX model. According to 
the trial results, SDNet has a monitoring speed of 30.5 ms and a preci­
sion, accuracy, and recall of 94.26 %, 93.15 %, and 90.72 %, respec­
tively. Bonora et al. (2022) tested convolution neural networks with 
“You Only Look Once” (YOLO) architecture using a commercial online 
platform. Preliminary statistics revealed that the model to count the fruit 
inside the boxes reached 64.70 % of true positives with 0.5 intersections 
over the union. In order to facilitate automated harvesting, Egi et al. 
(2022) presented a computer vision and artificial intelligence (AI) based 
drone system for the detection and counting of tomato fruits and 
flowers. The findings of the manual and AI-Drone counting indicate that 
the accuracy rates for red tomatoes, green tomatoes, and flowers are 85 
%, 99 %, and 50 %, respectively. A visual object tracking network 
named YOLO-deepsort, which is based on YOLOv5, was proposed by Ge 
et al. (2022) to recognise and count tomatoes at different stages of 
growth. With mean average precision of 93.1 %, 96.4 %, and 97.9 % for 
flowers, green tomatoes, and red tomatoes, respectively, the algorithm 
produced a competitive outcome. To identify cherry fruits with a 
compact volume, Gai et al., (2023) suggested an enhanced YOLOv4 deep 
learning algorithm and demonstrated that the mAP (average accuracy) 
value produced by employing the improved YOLOv4 model (YOLOv4- 
dense) network was 0.15 greater than that of the state-of-the-art 
YOLOv4. In order to increase the recognition rate and decrease the 
detection time for passion fruit pests, Li et al., (2023) presented a quick 
and lightweight detection algorithm based on YOLOv5. Test results 
revealed that the mean Average Precision was 96.51 % and the mean 
detection time was 7.7 ms, meeting the requirements of accuracy and 
real-time. Mathew and Mahesh (2022) demonstrated that they were able 
to achieve a mAP@0.5 of 90.7 % and an inference time of 20 ms as 
validation findings when they employed YOLOv5 to detect the bacterial 
spot disease in bell pepper plants based on symptoms observed on the 
leaves. An enhanced SE-YOLOv5 network model was suggested by Qi 
et al. (2022) for the identification of tomato viral illnesses. It was 
discovered that the model’s mean average precision (mAP@0.5) was 
94.10 %, which was greater than the YOLOv5 model, SSD model, and 
Faster R-CNN model by 1.23 %, 16.77 %, and 1.78 %, respectively. 
During training and validation, Ranjan et al., (2022) localised the farm 
mangoes with a mean average precision (mAP) of 0.434 and an F1 score 
of 0.57 at a confidence level of 0.876 using the YOLOv5 object recog­
nition and localization model. Wang et al. (2022b) built a convolution 
neural network model called YOLO-CBAM that incorporates an atten­
tion mechanism and YOLO v5 for the purpose of detecting Solanum 
rostratum Dunal seedings. For real-time recognition, the network model 
obtained a Precision of 0.9465 and a Recall of 0.9017. Yadav et al. 
(2022) demonstrated the deployment of a computer vision method 
based on YOLOv5 for recognising VC plants growing in the middle of 
maize fields at three non-identical growth stages (VT, V3, and V6) using 
unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) remote sensing data. They found that 
at the V6 stage of maize, YOLOv5s could detect with a maximum clas­
sification accuracy of 98 % and mAP of 96.3 %. Zhang et al. (2022) 
achieved good performance of the model mAP@0.5 as 0.994, 0.996, and 
0.996 respectively at the three important growth stages by using a novel 
method based on the state-of-the-art method YOLO-V5s to detect the 
survival rate of rape seedlings at multiple growth stages in the plant 
factory. Gui et al. (2023) presented a lightweight tea bud identification 
model based on the Yolov5 model and shown that the modified model’s 
mean average precision rose by 9.66 % when compared to the original 
Yolov5 model. A method based on improved YOLOv5 was proposed by 
Liu et al. (2023) for the perception of soybean phenotype information. 
The experimental results demonstrated that the improved YOLOv5 had 
an FPS of 79, a mAP of 96.13 %, and a reduction in the number of model 
parameters of 39 % and 55.56 %, respectively. developed a multi- 
objective detection model based on a new detection network, YOLOX, 
to identify the bloom, fruit, and fruit colour of crop populations under 
conditions of dense planting, leaf shadowing, fruit overlap, and light 
change interference in greenhouses. The findings demonstrated that the 
YOLOX target detection model was lighter, improved the average 
identification accuracy (mAP) by 10.75 %, and increased detection 
speed by 25.53 frames per second compared to the original YOLOv4 
model.

The existing literature demonstrates a wealth of studies utilizing 
various deep learning models for diverse crops. However, a conspicuous 
gap persists in research focused on computer vision tasks utilizing YOLO 
for the analysis of capsicum crops, particularly the Indian variety. While 
recent investigations have effectively addressed challenges related to 
fruit identification and positioning, the precision of robotic end-effector 
cutting, specifically in accurately severing peduncles using real-time 
algorithms like YOLO, remains inadequately explored. Peduncles, due 
to their smaller and intricate nature, pose challenges in cutting pro­
cesses, susceptible to interference from complex backgrounds such as 
the main stem. Moreover, there is a discernible research gap in the 
comprehensive examination of YOLO-based identification algorithms 
for assessing the harvest readiness of capsicums based on growth stages. 
Additionally, an unexplored area lies in leveraging the YOLO algorithm 
for counting and tracking capsicums, offering potential benefits in 
automation and data-driven decision-making for enhanced crop man­
agement. Furthermore, scant attention has been given to developing an 
Android application harnessing YOLO’s real-time capabilities, which 
could optimize processes, reduce manual labor, and facilitate contin­
uous monitoring in greenhouse environments. 
Therefore, the study aims to first detect capsicum in a greenhouse 
with the application of different YOLO algorithms. Secondly, the latest 
YOLOv8 segmentation model is utilized to detect the peduncle of 
capsicum in the upcoming proposed robotic harvester and perform 
cutting for harvesting purposes. Additionally, a laboratory testing setup 
is employed to calculate the coordinates of the cutting target point using 
the Real Sense D455 RGB-D camera, testing the accuracy of the devel­
oped model. Thirdly, the same algorithm is used for capsicum growth 
stage determination by detecting and classifying capsicum using 
different growth stages. Penultimately, the supervision algorithm is used 
for counting and tracking the capsicum that could be used for the 
purpose of yield detection. Lastly, capsicum identification detection 
applications based on an Android terminal that realizes deployment in 
the mobile terminal using the above-trained models were developed for 
local real-time testing of these models in the greenhouse which could 
also potentially offer technical assistance to real-time capsicum-detect­
ing picking robots, thereby potentially decreasing the hardware ex­
penses associated with high-speed computation.

2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. Image data procurement from Experiment Site 
The experiment was conducted at a greenhouse (Fig. 1) situated at 
the Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur, West Bengal’s Agricul­
tural and Food Engineering Department. Its latitude and longitude were 
22◦18′59″ N and 87◦18′50″ E, respectively, and its elevation was 74.42 
± 2 m. A total of 40 capsicum plants of the indigenous variety capsicum 
Arka Athulya were planted in four rows with row spacing of 120 cm, 
plant spacing of 50 cm, and average plant height of 75 cm for enhancing 
the operational efficiency of capsicum harvesting robot within the 
greenhouse setting. To ensure cost-effectiveness in terms of both time 
and money, the data collection for training the models involved the 
utilization of a smartphone Realme AI Quad 12MP main Camera with 
8MP ultra-wide-angle camera (Fig. 2) and specifications F-stop of f/1.8, 
a focal length of 4 mm, and exposure time of 1/100 s. 450 images at 
different growth stages, each 4000 × 1800 pixels with 72 dpi resolution, 
stored in JPG format, with acquisition details specified in Table 1 of the 
data acquisition protocol. To enrich the diversity of the image dataset, 
the shooting phase encompassed various time periods, encompassing 
the morning, midday, and afternoon from December 2022, to February 
2023, with different shooting angles and heights at different shooting 
distances (30 – 90 cm) incorporating scenarios (Fig. 3) such as capsicum 
hidden by foliage, capsicum obscured by branches, overlapped capsi­
cums and blended occlusion under various weather conditions (sunny 
and cloudy) and lighting intensities (Fig. 4) (dim light from 6:30 to 7:30 
and 17:00 to 18:00, moderate light from 9:30 to 10:30 and 15:30 to 
16:30, and intense light from 11:30 to 14:30).

adopted SPPF module, characterized by the sequential application of

scales, a critical consideration while concurrently upholding a light­

2.4. Expanding a capsicum detection model for its peduncle detection 
The robotic harvester that can be developed utilising this study will 
harvest the capsicum by clipping off the peduncle. The issue with object 
recognition tasks is that they group all images into one bucket and use 
bounding boxes to indicate where the object of interest is in the frame. 
Any computer vision issue that needs something more sensitive or spe­
cific than a bounding box, like peduncle detection in our example, 
cannot be solved with this approach and could only be resolved by 
upgrading the object detection task to segmentation, which entails more 
precisely identifying each object by categorizing each pixel. We seek to 
distinguish between classes of objects (capsicum and background) and 
objects within each class (identity which pixel belongs to a particular 
capsicum veggie and which pixel belongs to its peduncle) using a 
specialized sort of segmentation called instance segmentation. Subse­
quently, the images and corresponding annotations that were generated 
using MakeSense.AI were imported into a Roboflow Project where the 
Polygon Tool (Fig. 11) enables the creation of complex polygons for 
segmenting objects or regions within images. 
The subsequent steps in the process closely followed those carried 
out in the previous capsicum object detection task. In order to tackle the 
demanding task of instance segmentation, the cutting-edge YOLOv8 
segmentation architecture was chosen as the foundation for training. 
Renowned for its remarkable performance in accurately delineating and 
identifying individual instances within an image, YOLOv8 proved to be 
the most fitting solution for this complex computer vision objective. This 
architecture utilizes a combination of convolutional neural networks 
(CNNs), feature pyramid networks (FPNs), and skip connections to 
efficiently process and extract relevant features at multiple scales, 
enabling precise instance segmentation. To harness the computational 
power required for training the model, the NVIDIA Tesla T4 16 GB GPU 
was utilized in conjunction with Google Colaboratory. This powerful 
GPU, known for its high-performance computing capabilities, was in­
tegrated into the training process to significantly accelerate the model’s 
training time and enhance overall efficiency. This collaborative setup 
allowed for the exploitation of both the advanced deep learning capa­
bilities of YOLOv8 instance segmentation architecture and the acceler­
ated computations offered by the NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU. In this specific 
training scenario, leveraging the NVIDIA Tesla T4 16 GB GPU with 
Google Colaboratory. This integration showcases the synergistic effect of 
powerful hardware resources and a collaborative machine-learning 
platform, ultimately leading to accelerated model convergence.

visual features inherent in capsicum growth stage detection, necessi­

Furthermore, the choice was influenced by practical concerns regarding 
training duration, as heavier networks could substantially prolong the 
overall training process, potentially impeding the feasibility of the study 
without commensurate enhancements in model performance. Addi­

further optimize the training process, the formidable computational

In our workflow, capsicum detection begins with the installation of 
Ultralytics. Two essential functions, model loading and single-image

installation process. The detector is integrated with the tracker, facili­
tated by Roboflow’s Supervision library, which is installed with a frozen 
version to prevent issues given its beta status. This library reads frames 
from the source video and processes them, outputting the results to a 
file. A pivotal component of this library, LineCounter, quantifies indi­
vidual detections crossing a virtual line. The entire process is detailed in 
the accompanying flow chart. (Fig. 19).

predicting harvest volumes, and making informed decisions regarding 
crop management practices. 
2.8. Android application development for Real-Time model testing in 
greenhouse 
The identification and harvesting of capsicum, however, pose sub­
stantial challenges due to the labour-intensive and time-consuming na­
ture of these tasks. Moreover, the shortage of skilled labour willing to 
engage in repetitive and physically demanding tasks further exacerbates 
this issue. The detection and monitoring of fruits and vegetables in 
agricultural fields are intricate tasks, primarily owing to the unpre­
dictable and unstructured nature of the natural environment and the 
complexity of lighting conditions. In this context, developing automated 
solutions for capsicum harvesting becomes imperative to enhance field 
efficiency and reduce harvesting losses. Detecting and localizing 
capsicum within agricultural fields is particularly demanding for several 
reasons. Capsicum plants often grow in clusters, making it difficult to 
isolate individual fruits. Furthermore, the leaves of the plants can 
obscure the capsicum, rendering them partially or fully hidden from 
view. Lighting conditions within agricultural settings vary significantly 
throughout the day, introducing further complexity to the detection 
process. Visual similarity between the green capsicum fruit and its 
background is of paramount significance, making it especially chal­
lenging to discern capsicum in its physiologically mature green phase. 
When utilizing robotic harvesters, the necessity to manipulate leaves to 
access occluded capsicum can inadvertently damage plants, empha­
sizing the need for a non-invasive and precise detection approach. 
Addressing the aforementioned challenges necessitates the utilization of 
a well-structured algorithm that can guide the detection and localization 
of capsicum with minimal disruption to the plants and a high degree of 
precision. 
Hence, an app was developed for displaying real time capsicum 
detection using deep learning neural network models. In Fig. 20, we 
have illustrated the detailed procedure for the development of appli­
cation using YOLO models. The weight of capsicum detection models 
was converted from PyTorch format (.pt) to TensorFlow Lite format 
(.tflite). The converted weight was used for the development of a real- 
time capsicum detection application by using the Android Studio plat­
form (APK file size 56.3 MB, respectively). The video-based detection 
app was based on developed YOLOv8, YOLOv7 and YOLOv5 model 
frameworks. The video-based application localizes the capsicum in the 
captured frame with the help of the bounding box and displays the 
confidence score simultaneously. Deployment of models via Android 
app mainly requires conversion of file and interpreter syncing with the 
incoming video feed. This is mainly achieved by video channel frame 
rate processing via model delegate. The application provided bounding 
boxes around the detected capsicum and displayed confidence scores, 
offering real-time feedback to users. The performance of the developed 
Android application was rigorously evaluated under varying conditions. 
The study evaluated the application’s resilience to varying lighting cir­
cumstances by simulating several times of the day (8:00 a.m., 12:00p.m., 
and 4:00p.m.) and analysing their impact on illumination. The Android 
application, which was developed as part of this study, along with the 
outcomes of its testing, is comprehensively presented in the Results and 
Discussion section.

3.1. Training results 
The training duration of YOLO architectures is a critical factor that 
significantly impacts their development and optimization. It is 
commonly measured in terms of epochs, determined based on the 
visualization of loss curves to strike a balance between accuracy and 
efficiency. The training box loss curves provide valuable insights into 
the model’s capability to accurately predict bounding boxes around 
objects. Additionally, the object loss curve and segment loss curves offer 
crucial information about the model’s performance in object classifica­
tion and segmentation, contributing to a comprehensive understanding 
of its overall detection capabilities. 
In the context of activities related to the recognition of capsicum 
objects and the determination of their growth stages, the training pro­
cess exhibited distinct patterns in loss values displayed in Fig. 21. Spe­
cifically, during the initial 80 epochs of network training, there was a 
significant reduction in box loss values, which then stabilized around 
epoch 90. Consequently, a training duration of at least 100 epochs was 
chosen for these tasks. Among the various models evaluated for 
capsicum detection, the YOLOv8s model demonstrated remarkable 
performance. It achieved the lowest box loss of 0.01887 at 97 epochs 
and object loss of 0.00598 at 94 epochs. In comparison, both YOLOv5s 
and YOLOv5l models displayed reasonably close training box loss 
values, with YOLOv5l achieving a box loss of 0.040341 at 94 epochs and 
YOLOv5s recording a box loss of 0.043598 at 92 epochs. However, there 
was a significant level of randomness in object loss values over the 
training duration, with YOLOv5l and YOLOv5s showing object losses of 
0.024711 and 0.022128 at 100 epochs, respectively. This randomness 
underscores the superiority of YOLOv5l over YOLOv5s in these tasks. 
The training loss trend observed in YOLOv7 fell between YOLOv5l and 
YOLOv8, with a minimum box loss of 0.02965 and object loss of 
0.0136635. For the growth stage determination task, the YOLOv8s 
model was chosen due to its compact size, allowing for effective real- 
time application given the inherent complexity of the task. This model 
achieved a minimum box loss of 0.02497 at 92 epochs and object loss of 
0.006507 at 91 epochs. 
In the context of the peduncle detection by segmentation task, the

seg model displayed a more erratic behaviour in segment loss, reaching

3.2. Performance metrics of trained models 
The object detection task-trained models yield the final output, 
which consists of the precise location of capsicum boxes within a specific 
scene. This output provides information about the growth stage of each 
capsicum box, enabling the growth stage determination task. Addi­
tionally, the output includes the confidence score associated with each 
box, indicating the model’s level of certainty in the detection results. 
Similarly, the segmentation task-trained models produce the final 
output, which comprises the location of the bounding box encompassing 
the capsicum vegetable and its corresponding peduncle within a given 
scene. The output includes a segmentation mask that accurately defines 
the boundaries of the capsicum vegetable and its peduncle. Further­
more, the output provides the confidence scores for both the capsicum 
vegetable and its peduncle, reflecting the model’s confidence in the 
accuracy of the segmentation results. The Table 10 presented in this 
context serves as a valuable reference point, meticulously documenting 
the tangible outcomes of most superior YOLOv8-trained models in 
relation to their designated tasks. 
An evaluation was performed to determine the effectiveness and 
sufficiency of the trained models for specific tasks. This evaluation uti­
lised various performance metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and 
mean average precision (mAP), in addition to analysing a confusion 
matrix. The evaluation also considered factors such as model size and 
detection speed. Regarding positive observations, precision refers to the 
ratio of correctly predicted positive observations to the total number of 
predicted positive observations. Recall can be defined as the proportion 
of correctly predicted positive observations in relation to the total 
number of observations belonging to the actual class. Average precision 
is a metric that quantifies the mean of accuracy values over several 
potential intersections over union thresholds (IoU). The term “Mean 
Average Precision (mAP)” refers to the calculation of the average pre­
cision for each class, and then taking the mean of these values. The 
YOLO model size pertains to the file size of its custom-trained weights, 
encompassing the acquired parameters employed for the execution of 
real-time activities in an efficient manner. Detection speed expressed in 
FPS (Frames Per Second) denotes the speed at which the model can 
process video frames, enabling real-time and efficient detection or seg­
mentation of objects in a given scene. These metrics are employed to 
quantitatively measure the models’ accuracy, sensitivity, and overall 
performance in the specified tasks designated in Table 11. 
The model size, maximum precision (at confidence), maximum recall 
(at confidence), F1 score, mAP@0.5, and minimum FPS for all the 
custom-trained models are given in Table 12. The YOLOv8s -based 
trained model outruns all its predecessors with 0.967 mAP@0.5 and F1- 
score 0.93 at 0.146 confidence and have a detection speed of 47.16 FPS 
for the task of capsicum detection. For peduncle segmentation task, 
YOLOv8s-seg model has a box mAP@0.5 of 0.790 and mask mAP@0.5 of 
0.771 with a detection speed of 39.68 FPS which suggest it is performing 
better than YOLOv7s-seg and YOLOv5s-seg model. The F1 score of both 
box and mask of the YOLOv8s-seg is 0.81 and 0.80 respectively which is 
also superior than the other two models tested for the following task. 
Keeping in mind the task complexity, Yolov8s model tested for growth 
stage determination had achieved an mAP@0.5 of 0.614 with a detec­
tion speed of 34.24 FPS.

4. Conclusions 
In conclusion, this research paper has delved deep into the applica­
tion of advanced deep learning techniques for capsicum detection and 
analysis within greenhouse environments. Utilizing iterations of the 
cutting-edge YOLO (You Only Look Once) object detection model, 
including YOLOv5, YOLOv7, and YOLOv8, the study successfully ach­
ieved capsicum detection, peduncle segmentation, and growth stage 
determination. Furthermore, the research extended its scope to pin­
pointing the target cutting point of the peduncle by a RealSense D455 
depth camera in a controlled laboratory setting. The integration of 
YOLOv8 with ByteTrack and Supervision algorithms enhanced the ac­
curacy and efficiency of the capsicum counting and tracking process. 
This amalgamation of state-of-the-art technologies not only showcased 
the robustness of the developed models but also highlighted the po­
tential for real-world applications. To validate the practicality of the 
developed capsicum detection model, the research team successfully 
conducted real-time testing by deploying it in a user-friendly Android 
Application. This seamless transition from theoretical research to prac­
tical implementation demonstrates the feasibility and adaptability of the 
proposed methodology. 
In summary, the findings of this research study can be summarized as 
follows:  
1. Our dataset, consisting of 400 greenhouse images, was meticulously 
curated from various shooting distances and heights, incorporating 
diverse levels of occlusions and lighting intensities. These images 
were subjected to thorough pre-processing and augmentation tech­
niques to enhance dataset diversity and quality.  
2. Google Colaboratory provided the YOLO versions—including 
YOLOv8s—with intensive training, guaranteeing a quick and effec­
tive training procedure. YOLOv8s was shown to be the most suc­
cessful model, with a remarkable mean Average Precision (mAP) of 
0.967 for capsicum detection at a 0.5 Intersection over Union (IOU) 
threshold. Furthermore, at the same IOU threshold, YOLOv8s-seg, 
the top performer in peduncle detection using segmentation, 
showed impressive results with a box mAP of 0.790 and a mask mAP 
of 0.771. YOLOv8s, which performs better on a variety of tasks, was 
used for the development stage determination task. It achieved a 
mAP of 0.614 for the given IOU threshold.  
3. Custom weight files generated for these superior models, namely 
YOLOv8s for capsicum detection, YOLOv8s-seg for maturity deter­
mination, and YOLOv8s for growth stage determination, were 
remarkably compact, with sizes of 21.5 MB, 22.8 MB, and 23.2 MB, 
respectively. These compact weight files facilitate efficient hardware 
implementation. Furthermore, these models exhibited rapid detec­
tion speeds, specifically 46.14 FPS, 39.68 FPS, and 34.24 FPS, 
respectively, when tested on video frames. YOLOv8 models offer 
excellent potential for real-time capsicum detection, peduncle seg­
mentation, and growth stage determination in greenhouse condi­
tions due to their high accuracy, quick detection speed, and small file 
sizes.

target point, the results indicated a maximum error of 8 mm, 9 mm,

mation in greenhouse, limitations include the dataset’s potential gaps in 
representing actual open field variability and challenges from diverse 
weather conditions. The YOLO models’ robustness in capsicum detec­
tion may be affected by unaccounted variations in color, shape, and size. 
Counting accuracy, impressive in the third harvesting cycle, may fluc­
tuate across different environmental conditions and crop varieties. The 
Android application’s robust performance may face challenges in real 
open field settings, emphasizing the need for addressing these limita­
tions and validating methodologies in diverse scenarios for enhanced 
practical utility.

enabling early disease detection and optimized resource management.

models, can empower farmers to make timely, informed decisions,

models into autonomous agricultural robots offers the potential for tasks 
like selective harvesting and precision spraying, increasing efficiency

contribute to the evolution of smart agriculture, making farming more 
efficient, sustainable, and technologically advanced.
