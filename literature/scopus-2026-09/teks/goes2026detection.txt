Abstract — Banana detection and counting play an 
important role in yield estimation, harvest planning, 
and plantation management. Manual counting of 
banana fingers within dense bunches is labour-
intensive, time consuming, and often prone to error, 
particularly under field conditions with occlusion and 
varying 
illumination. 
This 
paper 
presents 
a 
comparative evaluation of three recent object detection 
frameworks i.e. RF-DETR (Small), YOLOv11-Fast, 
and YOLOv26-Fast, for individual banana finger 
detection and counting. This study is conducted on a 
uniformly annotated dataset comprising 938 banana 
images, split into 80% training, 10% validation, and 
10% testing sets. To ensure a fair comparison, all 
models were trained using identical preprocessing 
steps, augmentation strategies, and training protocols. 
Experimental results on the test set demonstrate that 
YOLOv11-Fast achieved the highest overall detection 
performance with mAP@50 of 98.4%, precision of 
97.7%, recall of 93.1% and F1 of 95.4%. RF-DETR 
(Small) obtained mAP@50 of 97.6%, precision of 
97.7%, recall of 92.7% and F1 of 95.2%, while 
YOLOv26-Fast achieved mAP@50 of 97.4%, precision 
of 95.7%, recall of 92.7% and F1 of 94.2% using the 
COCO checkpoint at 512×512 input resolution. 
Although the YOLO-based models attained superior 
mAP and precision, RF-DETR showed comparatively 
stable performance in images containing dense and 
occluded banana bunches. The findings provide 
insights into 
the 
strengths 
and 
trade-offs 
of 
transformer-based and one-stage detectors, offering 
guidance for selecting suitable models for real-world 
agricultural deployment. 
Keywords — banana detection, object detection, RF-
DETR, YOLOv11, YOLOv26, SAM3, computer vision. 
 
I. INTRODUCTION 
   The global banana industry is increasingly turning 
to computer vision to boost efficiency and accuracy 
in tasks such as yield estimation, sorting, and harvest 
planning. Object detection plays a key role in 
automating fruit counting and quality assessment. 
One-stage convolutional detectors like the YOLO 
family are popular in agriculture due to their high 
speed and accuracy. Transformer-based detectors 
(e.g., DETR) offer an alternative approach by 
framing detection as a direct set prediction problem 
without hand-designed components. Recent “real-
time DETR” models such as RT-DETR have shown 
that transformers can match or exceed YOLO’s 
performance on standard benchmarks [1]. Prior 
work in fruit detection has demonstrated that deep 
learning models can achieve very high accuracy on 
orchard datasets. Recent studies have compared 
YOLOv8 and Faster R-CNN for multi-fruit 
identification, where Faster R-CNN achieved higher 
mAP while YOLOv8 demonstrated higher precision 
for banana detection [2].  
   The main contributions of this study are: (i) the 
creation of a banana finger dataset of 938 images 
captured under diverse conditions, including varying 
illumination, 
occlusion, 
and 
background 
complexity; (ii) a controlled comparison of RF-
DETR, YOLOv11, and YOLOv26 under identical 
pre-processing, augmentation, and training setup; 
and (iii) a counting evaluation using Mean Absolute 
Error (MAE) and Counting Accuracy (CA). 
   The remainder of this paper is organized as 
follows: Section II reviews related work in 
agricultural fruit detection and modern object 
detection architectures. Section III describes our 
dataset preparation, annotation process, and the 
three model architectures evaluated. Section IV

II. RELATED WORK 
   Recent agricultural object detection studies have 
shifted from generic fruit recognition to model 
comparison under dense occlusion, edge constraints, 
and counting evaluation. Accordingly, the related 
work below is organized around agricultural fruit 
detection, the evolution of one-stage YOLO 
detectors, transformer-based detectors, evaluation 
standards used, and research gaps. 
A. Deep Learning in Agricultural Fruit Detection 
   Deep learning has become the dominant approach 
for fruit detection in orchards because it can learn 
visual 
features 
under 
occlusion, 
varying 
illumination, and background clutter. However, 
recent studies show that performance depends 
heavily on dataset design and scene complexity. 
Pretty et al. introduced a multi-stage banana 
detection and harvesting decision support system, 
reporting 93% AP50 for YOLOv12n on detection 
and 83.25% accuracy for DenseNet121 on 
harvesting classification task [3]. Wang et al. 
proposed YOLO-BRFB for banana bunch detection 
and localization on edge devices and reported 0.95 
precision, 0.94 mAP, and 8.6 ms inference on Nvidia 
Orin NX, although performance degraded under 
extreme backlighting [4]. 
   Similarly, Rangkuti et al. compared multiple 
banana detectors across a 7,504-image dataset and 
found that YOLOv4-Tiny achieved the best result of 
mAP@50 of 92% [5]. Addressing the absence of 
large-scale banana datasets, Guru and Saritha 
released a dataset of 31,678 images and 547 videos 
across three bunch varieties and three maturity 
grades, using YOLOv8 for segmentation during 
preparation [6]. Yu et al. improved YOLOv5 for 
citrus fruit detection and reported 95.83% precision 
and 79.86% mAP, yet heavy occlusion and very 
small visible regions still remained difficult [7]. 
Valdez et al. obtained 90% mAP with YOLOv11n 
for fruit detection, but severe occlusion still reduced 
reliability [8]. These studies show that modern 
detectors are accurate, but their robustness under 
dense fruit clusters remains an open problem. 
B. Evolution of One-Stage YOLO Detectors 
   The YOLO family has evolved from a single-stage 
detector optimized mainly for speed into a broad set 
of architectures that balance accuracy, latency and 
deployment flexibility. YOLOv4 introduced major 
training and architectural improvements such as 
Mosaic augmentation and modified PAN-based 
feature aggregation, reaching 43.5% AP on COCO 
[9]. The comprehensive review by Terven et al. 
traces the transition from anchor-based detectors to 
anchor-free designs and YOLO-NAS, showing that 
later YOLO variants emphasized better feature 
aggregation [10]. 
   Recent work has focused not only on accuracy but 
also on the export and post-processing pipeline. 
YOLOv26 removes Distribution Focal Loss and 
supports native NMS-free inference: YOLO26n 
achieved 40.9 mAP (50:95) with 1.7ms latency on 
NVIDIA T4 [11], while the more detailed analysis of 
YOLO26x showed that the model can compete with 
advanced 
transformer-based 
detectors 
while 
retaining CNN efficiency [12]. This is important as 
post-processing overhead strongly affects real-time 
field deployment. But the literature shows that 
crowded fruit scenes can expose weaknesses in 
duplicate suppression and counting consistency. 
These developments establish the YOLO models as 
a strong baseline for real-time agricultural object 
detection systems. 
 C. Transformer-Based Object Detection 
   Transformer-based 
detectors 
introduced 
a 
different method of object detection by treating it as 
set 
prediction 
rather 
than 
anchor-based 
classification. DETR removed manual anchor 
design and NMS, achieving 42.0 AP on COCO with 
ResNet-50 backbone [1]. Its main limitation was 
slow convergence and weak small object detection, 
which is relevant for banana fingers that occupy only 
a small portion of the image. Deformable DETR 
addressed this issue by using sparse multi-scale 
sampling, reaching 43.8 AP with approximately 10x 
less training time than the original DETR [13]. 
   RT-DETR further improved practicality by 
combining transformer-style global reasoning with 
real-time performance; it achieved 53.1 AP and 108 
FPS on COCO [14]. This makes it one of the 
strongest examples that transformer-based detectors 
can be both accurate and fast. Segment Anything is 
not a detector, but it's highly relevant in agricultural 
pipelines because its segmentation capability can 
reduce annotation effort and support instance mask 
generation for dense fruit datasets [15]. Even so, 
transformer detectors must still be evaluated 
carefully to ensure reliable instance separation and 
counting.

   Standardized evaluation metrics and datasets 
remain central to object detection research. For 
banana counting, metrics such as mAP@50, 
precision, recall, and f1-score must be considered. 
Similarly, MAE and Counting error is also essential, 
since merged or missed detections directly affect

   Dataset 
characteristics 
further 
influence 
performance. Studies using multi-stage and large-
scale banana datasets show that annotation quality 
and scene diversity directly impact generalization 
[6], [3]. So, evaluation should consider not only 
accuracy but also robustness to occlusion and 
counting consistency. 
E. Research Gaps 
   Despite recent progress, several challenges 
remain. Dense occlusion and overlapping fruits 
continue to degrade performance for both CNN-
based and transformer-based detectors. Many 
studies report only detection metrics without 
evaluating 
counting 
accuracy. 
In 
addition, 
comparisons between transformer models and recent 
NMS-free YOLO variants under identical conditions 
is limited. Furthermore, recently introduced models 
such as YOLOv26 remain underexplored in 
agricultural applications like fruit detection and 
counting. These limitations motivate a unified 
evaluation of modern one-stage and transformer-
based detectors for accurate banana detection and 
counting.

B. Annotation with SAM & Dataset Splitting 
   We used SAM3 for annotation on the Roboflow 
platform. SAM's segmentation feature helped us 
efficiently create high-quality mask annotations 
[15]. The interactive annotation process with SAM3 
greatly cut down on the manual labeling work 
compared to traditional pixel-level annotation. Each 
banana finger was annotated with both a bounding 
box and an instance segmentation mask, even when 
there was significant overlap. Each annotated image 
was manually reviewed and corrected to address 
segmentation errors, missed instances and other 
inaccuracies, this was done to ensure that the 
annotation quality is high. 
   The detection annotations are in COCO/YOLO 
format for compatibility. This dataset design follows 
practices from Baglat et al [3] who introduces a 
multi-stage banana dataset, and ensures coverage of 
real-world variability, as shown in Fig. 1.

C. Data Preprocessing and Augmentation  
   All images were preprocessed to normalize 
orientation and scale. We applied identical 
augmentation pipelines for all models to ensure a 
fair comparison: random horizontal flips, small 
rotations, and shear were used to increase data 
diversity. For the YOLO models, we also included 
mosaic augmentation (a standard technique in 
YOLOv4 and later) and copy-paste of banana 
instances to simulate dense clusters [9]. For 
RF-DETR, which is a transformer-based model, we 
applied 
multi-scale 
cropping 
and 
brightness 
adjustments. These augmentations help the models 
learn robustness to occlusion and variability.

D. Model Architectures  
   RF-DETR (Small): We use a real-time DETR 
variant (“RF-DETR”) with a small backbone (ResNet-
50). This model adopts the end-to-end DETR 
framework, meaning it uses attention-based encoder–
decoder layers to predict a set of object queries. We 
follow the authors’ recommended configuration for 
“Small” (attention heads, transformer layers) while 
removing the need for NMS. RF-DETR was fine-
tuned on our dataset for 50 epochs, using a focal loss 
for classification and L1+GIoU loss for box 
regression. 
   YOLOv11 (Fast): YOLOv11 is a one-stage CNN-
based detector belonging to the Ultralytics YOLO 
family [10]. The model uses a typical CSP-based 
backbone and feature pyramid. We used the “Fast” 
checkpoint and input resolution of 640x640. The 
model was trained with standard YOLO losses (BCE 
for objectness, CIoU/GIoU for bounding box 
regression) and the same augmentation as above. No 
NMS was removed, we used the default NMS post-
processing. 
   YOLOv26 (Fast): YOLOv26 is the newest YOLO 
generation at the time of writing, offering improved 
training strategies and an optional NMS-free model 
[12]. We used the YOLOv26 (Fast) checkpoint 
designed for 640×640 input (COCO label scheme). 
Like YOLOv11, it was trained with its standard recipe 
(comparable augmentations and losses). YOLOv26’s 
experimental features (ProgLoss, STAL) aim to boost 
small-object performance but we treat it as a black-box 
model here [11].

C. Qualitative Analysis 
   In dense banana bunches, RF-DETR consistently 
demonstrated strong instance separation. The model 
was able to detect overlapping bananas and partially 
occluded fruits with high confidence scores, typically 
ranging from 90–98% for clearly visible instances and 
70–90% for partially hidden bananas. The transformer 
encoder’s global self-attention mechanism enabled 
effective understanding of spatial relationships 
between adjacent fruits [14]. 
   YOLOv11 (Fast), while highly accurate in 
uncluttered scenes, showed systematic limitations in 
dense bunch configurations. The model occasionally 
merged overlapping bananas into single detections, 
particularly when occlusion exceeded approximately 
40%. Smaller bananas occupying minimal image area

V. DISCUSSION 
   RF-DETR achieved the best performance in this 
study, which can be attributed to its transformer-based 
design and self-attention mechanism for modelling 
global spatial relationships among overlapping banana 
fingers [1]. This is reflected in its superior counting, 
results with the lowest MAE and highest CA 
indicating stronger instance separation in dense 
bunches. 
   The hybrid encoder model in RF-DETR also 
improves multi-scale feature interaction, which is 
important for banana fingers of different sizes [13]. In 
addition, bipartite matching eliminates NMS and 
reduces suppression of valid detections in crowded 
scenes. In contrast, YOLOv11 had the weakest 
counting performance (MAE: 1.5212, CA: 27.65%), 
consistent with NMS-related missed or merged 
detections in dense clusters. YOLOv26 improved over 
YOLOv11 (MAE: 0.4255, CA: 73.40%), but still 
lagged behind RF-DETR in exact-count prediction. 
   These results suggest that the transformer-based 
detectors are more suitable for dense banana counting 
and yield estimation, whereas one-stage YOLO 
models remain useful when detection efficiency is the 
primary requirement. However, because latency was 
not benchmarked on dedicated hardware, deployment-
speed comparisons should be treated as future work. 
  
VI. CONCLUSION 
   In this study, we conducted a comparative evaluation 
of three object detection models, namely RF-
DETR(Small), YOLOv11(Fast), and YOLOv26(Fast), 
for detecting and counting individual banana fingers. 
The optimal model really depends on the application 
requirements. YOLOv11 (Fast) achieved the highest 
benchmark performance, with a precision of 97.7% 
and mAP@50 of 98.4%, demonstrating strong 
accuracy under standard conditions. However, its 
performance was less reliable in densely packed 
banana bunches. RF-DETR (Small) achieved a 
comparable mAP@50 of 97.6% and demonstrated 
superior performance in dense and occluded scenes 
due to its transformer-based architecture and end-to-
end bipartite matching approach, which eliminates the 
need for NMS. YOLOv26 (Fast) achieved an 
mAP@50 of 97.4% and provided a balanced trade-off 
between accuracy and robustness, also outperforming 
YOLOv11 
in 
high-density 
scenarios. 
Overall, 
YOLOv11 is better suited for fast and accurate 
detection in standard conditions, whereas RF-DETR is 
better suited for dense object scenarios requiring 
precise counting. YOLOv26 offers a balanced trade-
off between accuracy and robustness. It is important to 
note that the evaluation in this study is based on a 
dataset of 938 images. While the results demonstrate 
strong performance under these conditions, the 
generalization of the models to larger and more 
diverse datasets has not been extensively evaluated, 
which represents a limitation of the current study. For 
future work, adding better occlusion handling, depth 
sensor integration for 3D yield estimation, and model 
compression for edge deployment are all directions 
worth exploring. Expanding the dataset with more 
varieties and tougher conditions would also help with 
generalizing the results. And since YOLOv26 is a 
newer model, more research will be needed to 
understand whether its suitable for agricultural 
deployment and fruit detection.
