Abstract—Accurate classification of oil palm fruit ripeness
plays a critical role in optimizing harvest schedules and max-
imizing oil yield in precision agriculture. In this study, we
present an approach leveraging YOLOv8, a recent advancement
in real-time object detection, to detect and classify oil palm fruit
bunches under real-world plantation conditions. A large-scale
dataset comprising over 25,000 annotated images was constructed
and augmented to reflect diverse environmental variability such
as lighting changes, background clutter, and fruit occlusion.
YOLOv8s was deployed as the core model for recognizing palm
fruit ripeness stages. The results highlight YOLOv8’s robustness
in identifying fruit instances under complex field conditions, an
essential factor in minimizing missed harvest opportunities. This
study demonstrates that YOLOv8 offers a feasible and effective
solution for real-time palm fruit ripeness classification, laying
the groundwork for future integration into automated harvesting
systems, drone-based monitoring, and smart agriculture applica-
tions.
Index Terms—Oil palm fruit classification, YOLOv8, object
detection, deep learning, data augmentation, transfer learning,

I. INTRODUCTION
The classification of oil palm fruit ripeness is a vital step in
ensuring the quality and efficiency of palm oil production. The
ripeness level of palm fruit significantly affects oil extraction
rates, and any error in classifying or harvesting at incorrect
ripeness stages can lead to reduced oil yield and economic
losses. Traditionally, workers in plantations rely on visual
inspection and experience to determine the ripeness of fruit
bunches. However, this manual process is inherently subjec-
tive, prone to fatigue, inconsistency, and errors, especially
when carried out across large-scale plantations.
To address these limitations, automation in agricultural
product classification has gained considerable interest, par-
ticularly with the rise of computer vision and deep learning
technologies. Automated fruit classification not only reduces
dependency on manual labor but also enhances throughput and
accuracy in agricultural workflows. In the context of the palm
oil industry, the ability to distinguish between various ripeness
levels—such as unripe, underripe, ripe, and overripe—can
directly influence harvesting strategies, processing schedules,
and overall oil extraction efficiency.
Several deep learning-based models have demonstrated re-
markable capabilities in object detection tasks. Among them,
the YOLO (You Only Look Once) family of models stands
out due to its real-time detection performance and high pre-
cision. YOLO models reformulate object detection as a single
regression problem, significantly reducing computational time
while maintaining high accuracy. YOLOv8, the latest iteration,
introduces an anchor-free detection mechanism and leverages
a CSPDarknet backbone for enhanced feature extraction and
faster convergence, making it well-suited for real-world agri-
cultural applications.
In this study, we explore the potential of YOLOv8 mod-
els for the automatic classification of oil palm fruit bunch
ripeness in natural plantation environments. We focus on
practical deployment scenarios such as handheld mobile de-
vices, drone-based monitoring, or vehicle-mounted camera
systems operating under field conditions. Our dataset covers
a wide range of environmental variability including lighting
changes, background complexity, fruit occlusion, and partial
visibility—factors commonly encountered in outdoor planta-
tion settings.
The goal of this research is to demonstrate the feasibility
of deploying advanced object detection models for real-time
fruit maturity classification in oil palm plantations, thereby
contributing to the development of smart agriculture technolo-
gies and sustainable farming practices.
II. RELATED WORK
Previous research by Robi et al. [1] implemented YOLOv3,
YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv4, and YOLOv4-tiny models to classify
oil palm fruit bunches based on ripeness. Their study used a
dataset of 350 internet-collected images augmented to increase
training diversity. The classification task considered four ma-
turity levels: unripe, underripe, ripe, and overripe.
YOLOv4 achieved the best overall performance in their
experiments, with a mean average precision (mAP) of 77.20%,
precision of 0.70, recall of 0.71, and an F1-score of 0.70.
YOLOv3 achieved 55.70% mAP, with lower overall detection
and classification accuracy. Among the evaluated classes,
YOLOv4 achieved 81.90% precision for the unripe class and

III. METHODOLOGY
A. Overview of YOLOv8s and YOLOv8m
In this study, we evaluate both YOLOv8s and YOLOv8m
models to balance accuracy and computational efficiency.
YOLOv8s (small) is optimized for lightweight deployment,
featuring fewer parameters and faster inference suitable for
edge devices such as mobile phones and drones. YOLOv8m
(medium) offers a deeper architecture and higher representa-
tional capacity, resulting in improved precision and general-
ization at the cost of increased model size and inference time.
Comparing these two variants allows us to assess trade-offs
between performance and deployment feasibility in real-world
agricultural scenarios.

B. Training Strategy and Augmentation Techniques
To improve model robustness under real-world plantation
conditions, an advanced training strategy was employed. A
cosine learning rate scheduler with warmup epochs was used,
along with optimization using stochastic gradient descent
(SGD). A variety of modern data augmentation techniques
were applied to simulate the natural variability found in farm
environments, including Mosaic and MixUp augmentations,
RandAugment policy, HSV color space transformations, ran-
dom erasing, geometric distortions, and horizontal flipping.
These augmentations were designed to address challenges
such as varying sunlight, shadows, occlusions by leaves, and
overlapping fruits. Mixed precision (AMP) training was also
utilized to enhance training efficiency without compromising
accuracy.

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Dataset
The dataset used in this study is based on the publicly
available dataset introduced by Suharjito et al. [2], which was
collected from several palm oil mills in South Kalimantan,
Indonesia. It consists of multimodel data in the form of
videos and images representing six categories of oil palm fruit

maturity: unripe, under-ripe, ripe, overripe, empty bunches,
and abnormal fruit.
The data set was acquired by recording piles of fresh fruit
bunches (FFB) using a smartphone with a 1280×720 resolution
under practical plantation and post-harvest conditions, captur-
ing the variability of lighting in the real world.”. A total of 101
video files were collected (45 single-category and 56 multi-
category), from which sequential frames were extracted at a
rate of one frame per second using VLC Media Player. These
frames were resized to 416×416 pixels and annotated with
bounding boxes using DarkLabel and Roboflow to generate
YOLO-compatible labels.
The original dataset contained 7,171 annotated images with
14,559 labeled objects. To improve model robustness and
generalization, we significantly expanded the dataset through
preprocessing and data augmentation techniques. After en-
hancement, the final dataset used in our study consists of:
• Training Images: 18,968
• Validation Images: 3,205
• Testing Images: 2,787
This enhanced dataset provides better class balance and
diversity under varying environmental and lighting conditions,
which is crucial for training high-performance object detection
models suitable for real-world plantation applications.

B. Hardware and Training Environment
All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped
with an NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU (8GB VRAM), Intel Core
i7-10700 CPU, and 32GB RAM. Training utilized mixed
precision (AMP) to accelerate computation, and model op-
timization was performed using the SGD optimizer with a
cosine learning rate schedule.
C. Validation Results
After training, the model was validated using the best check-
point. The evaluation metrics for the object detection task
included Precision (P), Recall (R), Mean Average Precision
at 50% IoU (mAP50), and Mean Average Precision across
multiple IoUs (mAP50-95).

E. Discussion
The validation results demonstrate that the YOLOv8s and
YOLOv8m models achieve high precision and recall in all
six classes, with overall mAP@0.5 exceeding 0.995. Among
individual categories, the empty bunches and underripe classes
achieved the highest detection scores, while the abnormal fruit
showed slightly lower performance, likely due to greater intr-
aclass visual variability. These results validate the robustness
and generalizability of YOLOv8 architectures under diverse
plantation conditions.

V. CONCLUSION
This paper presents an automated oil palm fruit ripeness
classification system using YOLO-based deep learning mod-
els. The results demonstrate high classification accuracy, mak-
ing this approach viable for smart agriculture applications.
The proposed system is designed to operate under real-world
plantation conditions, addressing challenges such as variable
lighting, occlusion, and background complexity. Future work
will explore real-time deployment on mobile devices, in-
tegration with drone-based monitoring systems, and further
optimization for edge computing platforms, with the aim of
supporting sustainable and efficient harvesting practices in the
palm oil industry.
Future work will focus on several key areas to extend
the capabilities of the proposed system. First, we aim to
expand the dataset by incorporating images from multiple
plantation environments across different geographic regions,
capturing greater diversity in lighting, weather, and fruit
appearance. Second, we plan to explore the use of multi-
spectral or hyperspectral imaging techniques to enhance the
accuracy of ripeness classification, particularly in challenging
cases where color similarities cause misclassification. Since
the cloud computing platform is not suitable for real-time
applications, and many applications consider the cloud and
edge service to enhance the performance of the system, e.g.
the manufacturing [6], the remotesensing [7], and large-scale
computing [8] applications. Therefore, we intend to implement
real-time deployment of trained models on mobile platforms
such as drones, handheld devices, and embedded systems,
followed by extensive field trials to validate performance in
dynamic real-world agricultural scenarios.
