A B S T R A C T  
Accurately detecting and counting winter jujubes during the initial ripening stages are crucial for estimating 
yields and devising preemptive harvesting strategies. However, it can be challenging to accurately detect and 
count winter jujubes in orchards due to factors such as complex weather conditions and the potential for mutual 
obscuring between leaves and jujubes. In this study, we propose a lightweight small object detection YOLOv5n 
(SOD-YOLOv5n) model based on the YOLOv5n model for detecting and counting winter jujubes. The im­
provements to the model include using SPD-Conv to replace strided convolution and pooling layers to better 
detect small targets and low-resolution images. Then the upsampling algorithm of YOLOv5n is optimized using 
the content-aware reassembly of features (CARAFE) module, which enables adaptive and optimized kernel 
recombination at different locations, resulting in improved performance. Finally, a lightweight convolution 
technique, GSConv, is used in the neck to reduce the model size and maintain high accuracy. The experimental 
results show that the SOD-YOLOv5n model has higher counting accuracy than the YOLOv5n model, with im­
provements of 2.40 %, 1.80 %, and 3.00 % in precision, recall, and mAP, respectively, and a reduction of 9.11 % 
and 5.30 % in RMSE and MAPE, respectively. The size of the SOD-YOLOv5n model is 3.64 MB, which is reduced 
by 16.51 % using float16 quantization. The quantized model is used to build an app named JujubeDetector. We 
propose a method for counting winter jujubes based on this app, and we obtain a root mean square error (RMSE) 
of 1.46, coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.97, and detection time of 30 ms-90 ms from experiments in or­
chards. Our approach can effectively meet the requirements for real-time detecting and counting winter jujubes 
and provide a reference for detecting and counting other small object fruits.   
1. Introduction 
The winter jujube is a type of jujube that is popular in China for its 
crisp taste and nutritious properties (Niu et al., 2021). In recent years, 
accurate yield estimation for winter jujubes has become increasingly 
important in China due to the growing area dedicated to winter jujube 
cultivation. Yield estimation is used for a variety of purposes, including 
planning agronomic treatments, labor resources, and market strategy 
(Koirala et al., 2019). The conventional approach to estimating jujube 
tree yield primarily involves manual counting, which is a labor-intensive 
and time-consuming process, making it susceptible to errors (Xiang 
et al., 2023). Consequently, an automated method is needed to count 
winter jujubes accurately. 
In recent years, fruit detection has received widespread attention as 
an automatic intelligent detection technology in orchard management 
(Ren and Yang, 2016). In real orchards, variable lighting conditions, 
obscured fruits and leaves, and overlapping fruits are the main factors 
that affect detection system accuracy. Traditional machine vision 
methods often struggle with robustness and real-time performance in 
different natural environments, making it difficult to meet real-time fruit 
detection requirements. Deep learning is a recent development in image 
processing and data analysis (Pan and Yang, 2010). Deep learning has a 
hierarchical structure and strong learning capabilities (Sarker, 2021) 
and is widely used in medical image processing (You et al., 2022; Zhao 
et al., 2021a, 2021b), intelligent driving (Zhang et al., 2018), and fruit 
picking (Zheng et al., 2023). Convolutional neural networks (CNNS), 
which are the most commonly used type of deep learning network 
(Alzubaidi et al., 2021), have shown promise in fruit recognition tasks

(Hou et al., 2016). CNN-based methods can be divided into two cate­
gories. One category relies on region proposal methods, such as the R- 
CNN (S. Ren et al., 2015) and Fast R-CNN (Girshick, 2015) models, 
which involve a two-stage detection process of first generating region 
proposals and then performing classification. Region proposal methods 
have high detection accuracy and strong generalization capability (He 
et al., 2017) but may not be suitable for real-time detection (Sa et al., 
2016). The other category involves using a single CNN to predict the 
target location and classification in an end-to-end, one-stage detection 
model, such as the YOLO series (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020; Ge et al., 
2021; Redmon et al., 2016; Redmon and Farhadi, 2017, 2018). YOLO 
models are widely used due to their real-time performance and high 
accuracy. In recent studies, researchers have successfully applied 
improved versions of the YOLO models for automatic fruit recognition in 
natural environments. Fu et al. (Fu et al., 2021) improved YOLOv3-tiny 
for automatically detecting kiwifruit, achieving an average precision of 
90.05 %. The detection speed was also higher than that of Faster R-CNN 
using ZFNet and VGG16 as the backbone. Tian et al. (Tian et al., 2019) 
proposed an improved method for detecting apples using YOLOv3 and 
the DenseNet method to process the lower-resolution feature layer in the 
YOLO-V3 network, resulting in enhanced feature propagation and 
improved performance. The average processing time for this improved 
model was 30.40 ms, enabling real-time apple detection. These exam­
ples illustrate the high detection accuracy and rapid detection speed of 
the YOLO series models, which are well-suited for real-time fruit 
inspection. 
Researchers have conducted extensive studies for real-time detection 
and counting tasks using the YOLO model. Qiao et al. (Qiao et al., 2022) 
proposed an enhanced approach for detecting red jujubes by utilizing an 
improved version of YOLOv5s and ShuffleNet V2 as the backbone. The 
results demonstrated a remarkable reduction of 93.75 % in model pa­
rameters. This method was integrated into a ROS (robot operating sys­
tem) to calculate jujube yield, enabling swift and precise red jujube 
detection and facilitating visual jujube yield estimation. Mirhaji et al. 
(Mirhaji et al., 2021) employed the YOLO detection model to detect and 
count oranges native to Iran. They evaluated the model on 100 sample 
trees to create an orchard yield variation map after detecting and 
counting oranges on images of all trees in the orchard. The results 
demonstrated that the YOLO model can effectively provide a rapid 
method for detecting and estimating citrus fruit yield, with an error of +
9.19 % in the final fruit tree yield variation map. Additionally, Liu et al. 
(Liu et al., 2023) proposed a detection and counting method for small 
unopened cotton bolls based on MRF-YOLO. The test results exhibited a 
mean squared error (MSE) of 1.06 and a coefficient of determination 
(R2) of 0.92, based on detecting and counting 100 images. While these 
methods lay the groundwork for fruit detection, counting, and yield 
estimation, it is worth noting that they are primarily designed for 
desktop computers, making their application in orchards for fruit 
detection and counting challenging. 
Mobile phones, being portable and widely used by fruit farmers, offer 
great potential for real-time fruit detection and counting in orchards. 
However, their performance is inferior to that of desktop computers, 
posing challenges for deploying YOLO models on mobile phones for real- 
time detection. Real-time detection and counting in winter jujube or­
chards also face this situation. To address this, Zheng et al. (Zheng et al., 
2022) proposed an improved method based on the YOLOX-Nano 
network for detecting winter jujubes. They optimized the training 
using DIoU loss to obtain a more robust model. The results show that the

proposed enhancing YOLOv5s utilizing ghost-shuffle convolution and 
VoV-GSCSP techniques to reduce computational and network

This study aims to address several key challenges in winter jujube 
detection, including the low accuracy of YOLOv5 series models in 
complex natural environments, the lightweight deployment issue on 
Android devices, and the realization of real-time detection and counting 
of winter jujubes postdeployment. The main contributions of this study 
are as follows: 
(1) The SOD-YOLOv5n model is developed based on the YOLOv5n 
model. The model’s backbone is reconstructed to enhance the detection 
accuracy for small objects and low-resolution images. The model’s neck 
is reconstructed to reduce the complexity of the model structure and 
improve accuracy. 
(2) A model quantization method is found to reduce the file size 
weight of the SOD-YOLOv5n model, thus making the model more 
lightweight. 
(3) A method is developed to deploy the SOD-YOLOv5n model on 
Android devices to enable real-time detection and winter jujube 
counting. Experiments are conducted in winter jujube orchards to 
evaluate the model’s performance in practical applications. 
2. Materials and methods 
This section provides an overview of the winter jujube dataset 
acquisition and production process. Then, an improved SOD-YOLOv5n 
winter jujube detection algorithm is proposed. Subsequently, the SOD- 
YOLOv5n model is quantified and deployed on Android. Finally, the 
evaluation metrics, along with counting performance test experiments, 
are presented. Fig. 1 illustrates the primary process utilized for the SOD- 
YOLOv5n model proposed for detecting small jujube targets in orchards, 
enabling real-time winter jujube detection and counting on an Android 
device. 
2.1. Image data acquisition 
This study focuses on winter jujube trees in a complex natural 
environment. Images are collected at the Jujube Experimental Demon­
stration Station of Northwest A&F University in Dali County, Weinan 
City, Shaanxi Province. The jujube trees at the demonstration station 
were planted 2 m apart in rows and 4 m apart in columns, with an 
average canopy diameter of 2 m. An Intel RealSense D435i depth camera 
was utilized for image acquisition. During the image acquisition process, 
the camera lens was positioned approximately 50–100 cm away from 
the jujube fruit. This distance was chosen to simulate the real situation 
when using a mobile phone to detect winter jujubes and to ensure that 
the image background was similar to the actual orchard situation. We 
collected the winter jujube dataset at three times during the day (8–10 a. 
m., 12–2p.m., and 4–6p.m.) under different weather conditions, The 
dataset included images with different lighting conditions and images of

2.2. Data preprocessing and augmentation 
Various data augmentation techniques were utilized, including 
cropping, panning, adjusting brightness, adding noise, rotating, mir­
roring, and erasing to improve the recognition accuracy and robustness 
of the model. These techniques were implemented using the OpenCV 
library in Python. When applying image augmentation techniques, we 
used a certain amount of randomness. These data augmentation tech­
niques were applied to individually process the original images or in 
combination with a certain probability, which generated more diverse 
winter jujube images. Fig. 3 showcases data augmentation examples 
applied to the winter jujube dataset. To ensure that the generated images 
remained relevant to the original images and to avoid generating images 
that were too similar, we filtered some images that were too similar after 
the augmentation process. As a result of this data expansion process, a 
total of 6510 images were obtained and used as the dataset for the 
model. The dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test 
sets, with proportions of 80 %, 10 %, and 10 %, respectively. 
The Labelimg V1.8.6 tool was employed to annotate winter jujube 
images. The annotation process involved marking rectangular boxes 
around all visible winter jujubes in the dataset, which encompassed 
complete winter jujubes and those partly obscured by leaves or over­
lapped with other winter jujubes. 
2.3. SOD-YOLOv5n structure 
2.3.1. Baseline model YOLOv5n 
Achieving real-time detection and deployment for assessing the yield 
of winter jujubes on an Android device can be challenging due to the 
complexity of the natural environment. A lightweight model from the 
YOLOv5 series must be selected to improve detection speed and enable 
real-time detection. Compared to the commonly used YOLOv5s model, 
the YOLOv5n model has a smaller size of 3.87 MB (27.41 % of 
YOLOv5s), fewer parameters with 1,765,270 (25.14 % of YOLOv5s), 
and lower floating-point operations (FLOPs) at 4.50 G (27.44 % of 
YOLOv5s), satisfying the necessity for lightweight models. Therefore, 
the YOLOv5n model is chosen as the baseline model, upon which the 
SOD-YOLOv5n model is proposed. 
2.3.2. SOD-YOLOv5n model 
SOD-YOLOv5n is a lightweight model for detecting winter jujubes in 
complex natural environments. The general structure of the SOD- 
YOLOv5n model is shown in Fig. 4. The model includes the following 
improvement points: (1) The four conv modules in the backbone are 
replaced by the combination of space_to_depth (SPD) and nonstrided 
convolution(conv) modules. (2) The upsampling module in the neck has 
been replaced by the CARAFE module. (3) The four conv modules in the 
neck are replaced with GSConv modules. The SOD-YOLOv5n model 
significantly improves small object detection while being lightweight. 
2.3.3. CNN building block SPD-Conv 
Convolutional neural networks are a key technology in object 
detection tasks and have achieved tremendous success in various com­
puter vision tasks, such as image classification and object detection. 
However, their performance declines rapidly in more challenging tasks 
involving lower image resolutions or smaller objects. This is primarily 
due to a common but flawed design in existing CNN architectures, which 
rely on strided convolutions and/or pooling layers. These design choices 
result in fine-grained information loss and inefficiently learning feature 
representations. 
The special winter jujube detection task in orchards is particularly 
difficult due to the complex orchard environment and the small size of 
jujube fruits. To solve this problem, we utilize a new CNN module called 
SPD-Conv (Sunkara and Luo, 2022) in its backbone layers. The SPD- 
Conv module consists of space-to-depth(SPD) and nonstrided convolu­
tion(conv) layers and is used to replace all the strided convolution layers 
and pooling layers in the YOLOv5n model structure. The application of 
SPD-Conv helps to improve low-resolution images and small object 
detection accuracy. The structure of the SPD-Conv module is illustrated 
in Fig. 5.

reorganization module (CaRM). The overall structure of CARAFE is 
illustrated in Fig. 6. The KPM module is responsible for predicting the 
upsampling kernels, while the CaRM module performs the actual 
upsampling process. Fig. 6(a) showcases the KPM architecture, which 
comprises three submodules: the channel compressor, the content 
encoder, and the kernel normalizer. The channel compressor reduces the 
channel dimensionality of the input feature map, and the content 
encoder takes the compressed feature map as input, encoding its content 
and generating a reconstructed kernel. Subsequently, the kernel 
normalizer applies a softmax function to each reconstructed kernel. 
Fig. 6(b) illustrates the structure of the CaRM module. At each position 
in the output feature map, a mapping to the corresponding location in 
the input feature map is performed. This mapping facilitates the 
extraction of a kup × kup region centered around that point. The extracted 
region is then elementwise multiplied with the predicted upsampling 
kernel at that specific location, resulting in the desired output value. 
CARAFE has several notable features: 
(1). It has a large field of view, allowing it to gather contextual in­
formation from a wider area than methods such as bilinear 
interpolation; 
(2). It is content-aware, meaning that it uses instance-specific 
adaptive kernels rather than a fixed kernel for all samples (similar to 
deconvolution); 
(3). It is lightweight and efficient, with low computational overhead 
which makes it easy to incorporate into modern network structures. 
Compared with traditional upsampling methods, CARAFE can use 
adaptively optimized recombination kernels at different locations and 
requires a small number of parameters and computational effort, which 
helps to improve the performance of the upsampling operator. 
2.3.5. GSConv module 
Accelerated predictive computation is a key aspect of convolutional 
neural networks. One technique used to achieve this goal is channel 
sparse convolution (DSC), which severs the hidden connections between 
channels while transmitting image spatial information. However, 
expanding the channels while compressing the spatial dimensions 
(height and width) leads to semantic information loss. In contrast, 
channel-dense convolution (SC) can preserve the information between 
channels to the maximum extent. This study applies a new hybrid 
convolution called ghost-shuffle convolution (GSConv) (Li et al., 2022), 
which combines SC and DSC through shuffling, as shown in Fig. 7. This 
method involves convolving the input feature map with C1 channels to 
generate a C1/2 feature vector. Another C1/2 feature vector is obtained 
by depthwise convolution (DW conv). Then, the two feature vectors are 
concatenated. The SC-generated information is then passed through the 
channel shuffle to each DSC section. The output feature map has C2 
channels. This GSConv enhancement leads to an improvement in the 
nonlinear representation capability. The method achieves an output 
similar to standard convolution with a 50 % reduction in computational 
cost. The time complexity of the convolution computation, measured in 
FLOPs, defines the time complexity of SC, DSC and GSConv and can be 
expressed as shown in Equations (5) to (7).

In the backbone, spatial information is transferred to the channels 
through downsampling, however, this process can lead to feature in­
formation loss due to the compression of the feature map’s height and 
width and the expansion of the channel size. In the neck, the feature map 
is downsampled by the backbone, so it has a minimal width and height 
and a maximal channel size. Therefore, the neck has less duplicate in­
formation and does not need to be further compressed. GSConv is a 
dense convolutional calculation that maintains as many connections 
between channels as possible, which helps to reduce feature information 
loss and lowers time complexity. In this study, conv in the neck was 
replaced with GSConv. This change resulted in a model with sufficient 
accuracy and inference speed, while also reducing the number of pa­
rameters, making the model more lightweight and suitable for deploy­
ment on Android devices. 
2.4. Model Quantification and Android Deployment 
In this study, we design a real-time winter jujube detection and 
counting system for Android. To reduce the model size, we use quanti­
zation on the SOD-YOLOv5n model. Quantization involves converting 
higher-precision to lower-precision data types (Park et al., 2018), which 
significantly reduces the size of the model while causing only a slight 
loss in accuracy. This is a model compression method that results in a 
smaller model size and faster computation speed (Jacob et al., 2018). 
TensorFlow-Lite provides the Float16 model quantization technique 
(Gholami et al., 2021), which can reduce the model size by 50 % while 
only slightly compromising accuracy. We implement float16 quantiza­
tion to convert the data types of model constants in the SOD-YOLOv5n 
model, including weights and activation functions, from 32-bit to 16- 
bit floats. 
After quantizing the SOD-YOLOv5n model, we will import the 
resulting SOD-YOLOv5n.tflite model into Android Studio, a tool for 
developing and debugging Android applications. The development 
environment for this study is Android Studio 2021.3.1 on Windows 11 
with Android SDK 29. Using Android Studio, we export an APK file, 
which can be downloaded and installed on an Android device to create a 
winter jujube detection and counting app. This app will be lightweight 
and requires minimal performance from the device and can run on most

3. Results 
3.1. Ablation study 
This study created a winter jujube detection model and evaluated the 
performance of various structural improvements. The results of the 
model evaluation metric comparison for different improvement 
methods are presented in Table 3, and the P-R curves for each model are 
shown in Fig. 10. The purpose of this comparison was to determine the 
most effective approach for winter jujube detection. 
Using the SPD-Conv module in the backbone and neck can improve 
the detection accuracy of a model, as shown by the experimental results. 
Compared to the YOLOv5n model, the model’s precision increased by 
1.20 %, recall improved by 0.20 %, and mAP improved by 1.40 %. 
However, these improvements came at the cost of an increase in model 
parameters (384,000), model size (0.73 MB), FLOPs (4.00 G) and 
inference time (0.50 ms). This increase in complexity is due to the SPD- 
Conv module replacing the strided convolution layer and each pooling 
layer, thereby enhancing the detection performance for small targets 
and low-resolution images. When the upsampling module was improved 
using the CARAFE module instead in the neck, the module was shown to 
improve the detection accuracy of the model without increasing the 
model size. In comparison to the YOLOv5n model, the model’s precision 
and recall improved by 0.20 % and 2.30 %, respectively, and the mAP 
improved by 1.60 %. CARAFE was particularly effective in improving 
small object detection. The GSConv module was used to replace the conv 
module in the neck. Compared to the YOLOv5n model, the improved 
model showed improvements in model precision, recall, mAP, model 
parameters, and model size by 1.50 %, 0.70 %, 1.20 %, 5.86 %, and 5.25 
%, respectively, although the inference time increased by 5.26 %. When 
the model is improved using both the SPD-Conv module and the 
CARAFE module, the FLOPs reach 8.60 G and the parameters reach 
2,185,982, which is the highest model complexity among all the 
improved models, while bringing some accuracy improvement. When 
the SPD-Conv module and the GSConv module are used simultaneously 
to improve the model, precision improves by 2.30 %, recall and mAP 
both improve somewhat, and the number of parameters and FLOPs also 
increase significantly by 280,912 and 3.50 G, respectively. When the 
CARAFE module and the GSConv module are used simultaneously to 
improve the model, both the model parameters and FLOPs are somewhat 
decreased due to the lightweight design of GSConv while improving 
model accuracy. When the SPD-Conv module, CARAFE upsampling 
operator, and GSConv module were used in both the backbone and neck, 
the improved model’s precision, recall, and mAP improved by 2.40 %, 
1.80 %, and 3.00 % compared to the YOLOv5n model. The improvement 
in model detection performance was the highest among all improved 
models. Additionally, the model’s parameters increased by 317,624, the 
model size increased by 0.62 MB, the inference time increased by 2.60 
ms, and the FLOPs increased by 3.60 G. These improvements still 
allowed for real-time object detection. 
The P-R curve is a graph where the horizontal coordinate represents 
the recall and the vertical coordinate represents precision. By taking 
different confidence levels, we can plot points with different recall and 
precision on the P-R curve. The larger the area under the P-R curve, the 
better the overall performance of the model. The figure shows that our 
model has the largest area under the P-R curve and the highest mAP 
value compared to other improved YOLOv5n models.

the app was 19.31 MB. 
The effect of winter jujube detection using this device to detect and 
count winter jujubes in the natural environment is shown in Fig. 13 
below.

jujube and the leaves, leading to lower precision of winter jujube

4. Discussion 
Table 4 shows that the SOD-YOLOv5n model outperforms the 
YOLOv5n model in terms of accuracy, with increases in precision, recall, 
and mAP of 2.40 %, 1.80 %, and 3.00 %, respectively. Additionally, the 
SOD-YOLOv5n model has a 9.11 % lower RMSE and a 5.30 % lower 
MAPE compared to the YOLOv5n model when evaluated using model 
regression indices. When comparing the SOD-YOLOv5n model to the 
other lightweight models in the YOLO series (YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv4- 
tiny, and YOLOv7-tiny), the SOD-YOLOv5n model had the highest pre­
cision, with increases of 4.18 %, 5.32 %, and 1.21 %, respectively. It also 
had the highest recall among these models, with increases of 4.19 %, 
3.16 %, and 1.11 %. The mAP was also higher for the improved model, at 
increases of 4.61 %, 6.44 %, and 3.78 % compared to the other models. 
In terms of model size, since the YOLOv5n model with the smallest 
Depth_multiple and the smallest Width_multiple in the YOLOv5 series 
was chosen as the object of improvement, resulting in a model with 
76.02 %, 65.69 %, and 65.41 % fewer parameters and model sizes that 
were 75.00 %, 81.50 %, and 64.52 % smaller than the other three 
lightweight models, respectively. This makes the SOD-YOLOv5n model 
suitable for deployment on Android systems. In terms of model regres­
sion evaluation indices, the SOD-YOLOv5n model had the lowest RMSE 
and MAPE at 3.89 and 20.33 %, respectively, indicating the highest 
accuracy for winter jujube counting. In contrast, the SSD and Faster R- 
CNN models, which are not lightweight, had 13 and 66 times more 
parameters than the SOD-YOLOv5n model, respectively, making them 
too large and slow for real-time detection. They also had lower detection 
accuracy and lower model regression evaluation indices compared to 
the SOD-YOLOv5n model. 
Fig. 12 and Table 5 present the results of evaluating the performance 
of various models for detecting and counting winter jujubes. The models 
tested include YOLOv5n, YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv4-tiny, YOLOv7-tiny, 
SSD, and Faster R-CNN. The results show the effectiveness of each 
model in detecting and counting winter jujubes. Fig. 12 Image 1 
demonstrates the detection of partial winter jujubes that are obscured by 
leaves. Of the 12 winter jujubes present in the image, all models detect a 
smaller number of winter jujubes, with the SOD-YOLOv5n model having 
the lowest number of missed detections for 1 winter jujube. The Faster 
R-CNN model also detects leaves as winter jujubes, because Faster R- 
CNN has not established a feature pyramid architecture, which cannot 
fully extract image information, leading to detection errors. In images 
with a small number of winter jujubes, some of which are obscured by 
leaves (e.g. Fig. 12 Image 2), the SOD-YOLOv5n model is the only one 
that accurately detects all of the winter jujubes (7 in total). In images 
with many winter jujubes that are obscured by leaves and/or other 
winter jujubes (e.g. Fig. 12 Image 3), all models struggle with detection, 
with the SOD-YOLOv5n model performing the best at missing 2 out of 25 
winter jujubes. Similarly, in images with low ambient light and more 
winter jujubes obscured by leaves (e.g. Fig. 12 Image 4), all models fail 
to accurately detect all of the winter jujubes (16 in total). However, the 
SOD-YOLOv5n model performs the best, missing only 1 winter jujube. In 
terms of detection accuracy, the SOD-YOLOv5n model achieves 93.33 % 
accuracy for detecting winter jujubes in the above images, which is the 
highest among all models. 
Accurate small-target winter jujube detection plays a crucial role in 
counting winter jujubes in orchards. However, poor lighting conditions 
in the orchard can lead to reduced contrast between the winter jujubes 
and the background, resulting in blurred shapes for the small-target 
winter jujubes and a decrease in the model’s detection performance. 
To enhance the recognition capability of winter jujubes under low light 
conditions, we plan to expand the dataset by collecting more images of 
winter jujubes captured under various lighting conditions. Additionally, 
we intend to include images of winter jujubes at different growth stages 
to detect and count winter jujubes at different developmental stages. 
This will be the focus of future work.

which was quantized and deployed on Android devices for real-time

improved the model’s capability to detect small objects and handle low-

for subsequent winter jujube yield prediction. The method can also 
provide a reference for detecting and counting other small target fruits.
