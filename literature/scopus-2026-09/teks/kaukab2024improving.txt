A B S T R A C T  
In automated fruit detection, RGB-Depth (RGB-D) images aid the detection model with additional depth infor­
mation to enhance detection accuracy. However, outdoor depth images are usually of low quality, which limits 
the quality of depth data. In this study, an approach/technique for real-time apple fruit detection in a high- 
density orchard environment by using multi-modal data is presented. Non-targeted background removal using 
the depth fusion (NBR-DF) method was developed to reduce the high noise condition of depth images. The noise 
occurred due to the uncontrolled lighting condition and holes with incomplete depth information in the depth 
images. NBR-DF technique follows three primary steps: pre-processing of depth images (point cloud generation), 
target object extraction, and background removal. The NBR-DF method serves as a pipeline to pre-process multi- 
modal data to enhance features of depth images by filling holes to eliminate noise generated by depth holes. 
Further, the NBR-DF implemented with the YOLOv5 enhances the detection accuracy in dense orchard condi­
tions by using multi-modal information as input. An attention-based depth fusion module that adaptively fuses 
the multi-modal features was developed. The integration of the depth-attention matrix involved pooling oper­
ations and sigmoid normalization, both of which are efficient methods for summarizing and normalizing depth 
information. The fusion module improves the identification of multiscale objects and strengthens the network's 
resistance to noise. The network then detects the fruit position using multiscale information from the RGB-D 
images in highly complex orchard environments. The detection results were compared and validated with 
other methods using different input modals and fusion strategies. The results showed that the detection accuracy 
using the NBR-DF approach achieved an average precision rate of 0.964 in real time. The performance com­
parison with other state-of-the-art methods and the model generalization study also establish that the present 
advanced depth-fusion attention mechanism and effective preprocessing steps in NBR-DF-YOLOv5 significantly 
surpass those in performance. In conclusion, the developed NBR-DF technique showed the potential to improve 
real-time apple fruit detection using multi-modal information.   
1. Introduction 
Automation in agricultural operations, such as autonomous picking 
and yield forecasting, relies heavily on accurate fruit/object recognition. 
With the advancements in computer vision and machine learning tech­
niques, automated fruit detection systems have gained significant 
attention in recent years. Various object detection algorithms have been 
employed to achieve detection accuracy ranging from 85% to 96% 
(Bechar and Vigneault, 2016; Capinha et al., 2021; Gongal et al., 2015; 
Mzoughi and Yahiaoui, 2023; Zujevs et al., 2015). Deep learning algo­
rithms such as Faster Region-based Convolutional Neural Network 
(Faster R-CNN), You Only Look Once (YOLO), and Single-Shot Detector 
(SSD) have been reported to achieve successful detection of apples, to­
matoes, and strawberries (Hu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Tian et al., 
2019; Yu et al., 2020).

Some studies have also utilized over-the-row platforms with artificial 
light tunnels and structures to minimize the impact of non-targeted 
objects in real-time orchard systems (Gongal et al., 2018). However, 
most of these studies have focused on utilizing RGB images, which pose 
challenges for accurate fruit identification in orchard environments, 
such as varying natural lighting, fruit occlusions, complex backgrounds, 
and fruits in clusters. Despite the progress in fruit recognition based on 
Red-Green-Blue (RGB) images, accurate fruit identification in non- 
structural orchards remains challenging. The probable causes for 
lower accuracy in the identification are due to dense fruit distribution, 
blockage by leaves and branches, overlapping fruits, and others. 
To overcome these challenges, multi-modal data (including depth 
images, remote sensing images, and infrared-based images) showed the 
potential to resolve the limitations of each sensor by offering consistent 
and complementary information. Several studies have explored the use 
of depth information (Nguyen et al., 2016; Qixin et al., 2022) and pro­
posed enhanced methodologies such as 3D descriptors (Tao and Zhou, 
2017), background exclusion using depth information (Lin et al., 2019; 
Fu et al., 2020b; Yu et al., 2021). Fu et al. (2020a) reviewed the various 
RGB-D sensor systems and image processing techniques for fruit 
recognition. 
Some researchers have combined colour-based and depth-based fruit 
identification to improve detection (Tu et al., 2018; Tu et al., 2020). 
Conversely, others have utilized multi-modal data, including colour, 
depth, and range-corrected intensity signals, to enhance the perfor­
mance of Faster R-CNN for Apple recognition (Gene-Mola et al., 2019a). 
Qixin et al. (2022) proposed Noise-Tolerant Feature Fusion Technique 
(NT-FFN) and implemented data fusion at the feature layer and obtained 
a 93.1% average precision rate. NT-FFN technique extracted features of 
depth images at feature layer and attention based fusion mechanism 
followed. Such fusion method claims to be noise-tolerant, but there is a 
lack of clarity on how much noise the method can tolerate. The depth 
images are not perfect due to limitations in depth sensors and back­
ground scenarios (Azmi and Hagit, 2022). Depth images contain pixels 
without depth values, which are also known as holes. For a better 
insight, an example of RGB-D images is shown in Fig. 1(a) indoor con­
dition and Fig. 1(b) outdoor condition. In Fig. 1(a), some depth values 
are missing around the boundary of the objects due to the imaging 
principle of depth sensor and image background. However, it is easy to 
distinguish between objects and background in indoor depth images 
compared to outdoor depth images, as shown in Fig. 1(b). This is due to 
the lighting conditions and complex dense background of the orchard. 
The lighting conditions are uncontrollable in outdoor environments, 
which makes it difficult to distinguish between fruits and leaves in 
outdoor depth images. In general, a fusion of multi-modal data with 
noise and missing depth information results in the over-fitting of input 
data into the network. 
Therefore, this study presents a non-targeted background removal 
using depth fusion (NBR-DF) approach to improve real-time Apple fruit 
detection. The NBR-DF method integrates the YOLOv5 object detection 
model. This paper focuses on the following aspects.  
(i) Pre-processing depth data to remove noise: Noise is defined 
comprehensively in the context of feature fusion, considering 
factors like data acquisition, sensor noise, image processing, and 
feature extraction. 
(ii) Impact of fusing additional information: The effect of incorpo­
rating depth data into the YOLOv5 model through NBR-DF was 
analysed.  
(iii) Comparative analysis with state-of-the-art methods: Compared 
the performance of the proposed NBR-DF-YOLOv5 approach with 
following different methods.  
a. YOLOv5 using only RGB images (baseline detector-1).  
b. YOLOv5 using only Depth images (baseline detector-2).  
c. YOLOv5 with RGB-D data fusion without NBR-DF (baseline 
detector-3).  
d. NT-FFN, a recently proposed multi-modal method.  
(iv) Model generalization analysis: Tested on other fruits (Orange and 
Peach) having different orchard architecture and grown in 
different seasons than apple.

2. Materials and methods 
The study focused on real-time fruit detection in high-density apple 
orchards. Detection of apples in high-density orchards is mainly difficult 
due to their complex structure and the presence of fruits in clusters 
(Fig. 2). High-density orchards offer several ecological benefits as 
compared to traditional (lower-density) orchards. Some of the key 
ecological benefits include enhanced biodiversity, soil health, reduced 
chemical use, carbon sequestration, water conservation, improved 
erosion control, and more. 
RGB images with the depth data were acquired using RealSense 
D455 (RealSense™ Depth Camera D455, Intel Corporation USA). RGB 
and depth image datasets were obtained from the apple fields are shown 
in Fig. 3. The RealSense D455 camera can capture both RGB and depth 
images, as well as point data, which can be aligned to remove back­
ground interference. The camera uses stereo-depth technology to mea­
sure depth and specifications are presented in Table 1. This camera was 
selected as an acquisition device for its stability, cost-effectiveness, and 
existing research framework. Stereo depth cameras consist of two or 
more camera lenses placed at known distances from each other. By 
comparing the images captured by these cameras, depth information is 
calculated based on the parallax effect (how objects appear to shift 
relative to each other when viewed from different angles). Depth in­
formation was used to remove non-targeted trees and apples from the 
RGB image frame by applying a threshold of one meter. 
2.1. Image acquisition and dataset creation 
The images of apples (Red Velox variety; RV_APPLE) were collected 
during the harvest season from an apple orchard in Northern India. To 
create a real-time complex environment for fruit detection, multiple 
RGB-D images were captured using a stereo-depth camera in different 
natural lighting conditions. 
The images were taken from apple orchards having trees trained in 
layers and fruits primarily found on branches at each layer. The camera 
was aligned parallel to the tree in a vertical direction such that such that 
a distance of one meter was maintained from the ground and 0.5 m 
between the camera-targeted fruit-tree system (Fig. 4). The study 
collected 1000 (RGB and depth) images of resolution 1280 × 720 and 
point cloud data with depth information from randomly selected apple 
trees. The images were acquired to capture maximum variability and 
complexities of an orchard such as occlusion conditions, lighting influ­
ence, different times in a day (morning, noon and evening) and shading 
condition. 
2.2. Non-targeted background removal using depth fusion (NBR-DF; 
proposed method) 
To enhance the accuracy of apple fruit detection in complex orchard 
environment, the present approach focuses on the effective use of multi- 
modal data, as inputs to the detection model. The method NBR-DF is 
developed to pre-process depth images, to enhance depth features by 
filling holes (missing depth information) to eliminate noise. These depth 
holes are generated by the complex lighting condition in outdoor envi­
ronment, object characteristics and limitations of the image sensors. 
Therefore, a pipeline (NBR-DF) is developed for filtration, segmentation, 
and object extraction from the multi-modal data. These pre-processed 
depth images mapped together with their respective RGB image 
frame. Further these pre-processed RGB-D images used to eliminate the 
unnecessary background information generated by dense foliage in an 
image frame. The developed NBR-DF approach is integrated with 
YOLOv5 model as NBR-DF-YOLOv5 to precisely detect the targeted 
fruits by eliminating noise and irrelevant objects in an image frame, thus 
enhancing the performance of the subsequent detection model. 
2.2.1. Pre-processing depth images 
The depth data captured by the depth sensor is not directly usable as 
it contains holes with missing depth information. Thus, the depth data 
needs to be converted into a more usable format, such as a point cloud 
representation. The point cloud is a set of 3D points with (x, y, z) co­
ordinates and RGB colour values. The apple depth images were con­
verted into raw point cloud forms to eliminate noise and outliers from 
depth frames (Fig. 5). 
A pre-processing pipeline is designed by applying segmentation and 
object extraction processes to obtain a pre-processed depth frame. The 
segmentation process is performed for separating a point cloud into 
meaningful segments or clusters based on the characteristics of the 
points. Object extraction is the third step of the process for separating 
the object of interest from the rest of the scene in the RGB-D image using 
the segmented point cloud data. In object extraction, the first step is to 
identify the points that belong to the object and create a binary mask 
that indicates which pixels or voxels in the RGB-D image belong to the 
object. The resulting images from this process are presented in Fig. 6. 
2.2.2. Non-targeted background removal 
The background removal process pipeline is used to remove non- 
targeted background trees and apples from the RGB-D image frame by 
using a mask created from the point cloud data. This process enhanced 
the attention mechanism focus on the targeted object and eliminate any 
background noise. Depth threshold of one meter was applied to remove 
non-targeted background. The process flow of NBR-DF technique with 
removal of background is shown in Fig. 7.

2.3. Manual annotation 
Rectangular annotations were used to manually label the ground 
truth of the targeted apple in the RGB images using the LabelImg tool 
(Tzutalin, 2015). These annotations were then mapped to the depth 
images (Fig. 8). A total of 39,894 fruits were labelled in the full set of 
RGB-D images including 1000 images. The selection of images in the 
training set was random based on independent and uniform sampling. 
All images were selected in a manner to ensure the reliability of the 
results. 
2.4. YOLOv5 model 
The YOLOv5 model is used as a baseline detector in this study 
because of its lightweight, real-time performance, and mix of precision. 
YOLOv5 has currently reached version 7.1 and achieved a mean average 
accuracy (mAP 0.5–0.95) of 50.7% on the COCO dataset. The input, 
backbone, neck, and head components contribute to the basic structural 
elements of the YOLOv5 model with a depth attention mechanism 
(Fig. 9). 
The input part of YOLOv5 uses self-adaptive anchor box computa­
tion, mosaic data augmentation, and image scaling. Input images were 
scaled down to a fixed size (512 × 512) with normalization using self- 
adaptive image scaling. A high-performance classifier is used by the 
backbone benchmark network in YOLOv5 to extract features. The focus 
structure was used as the backbone of architecture to resize the input 
image by using slice operation and fuse it in the channel. This results in 
lowering the number of FLOPs and parameters. 6 × 6 sized convolu­

feature variety and creating useful feature outputs. The head serves as 
both a classifier and a regressor, predicting of class and position of 
targets. Three feature layers are created in the head by applying 1 × 1

backbone and neck. The size of the feature layer matrix is k × k (Bp (5 +

2.4.1. Depth attention fusion mechanism 
The attention mechanism was used to provide different weights to 
things depending on how significant they are in a certain visual region. 
To implement this mechanism, many techniques like SE, CBAM, Effi­
cient Channel Attention (ECA), Channel Attention (CA), Non-Local, and 
Global Context Networks (GCNet) are used. The attention mechanism in 
the YOLOv5 network is often included in the C3 module or the last layer 
of the backbone. The process of managing the input feature 'n' based on 
its associated weighting, w(n), is represented by Eq. (1) (Jie et al., 2021). 
Attention = f (w(n) , n)
(1)  
where, n represents the input tensor with shape [batch_size, height, 
width, channels]. It contains the input data or feature map used in a 
neural network, w(n) is a weight tensor derived from n, typically ob­
tained through some form of learned parameters or coefficients, and f (w 
(n), n) represents the process of treating the input feature n. 
The network architecture of the depth-attention YOLOv5 is shown in 
Fig. 10. Unlike other attention mechanisms that are usually introduced 
earlier in the network and trained along with the other layers, the depth- 
attention mechanism was placed after the final processing layers of the 
detection model. It remains unchanged and does not adapt during the 
training process. This means that the depth-attention weights are not 
updated during training and remain fixed throughout the learning 
process. Alternatively, the depth-attention mechanism can be incorpo­
rated directly into a pre-trained model. The depth-attention mechanism 
was implemented using a depth-attention matrix, which was obtained 
by processing the depth map using a combination of average pooling and 
max pooling operations, followed by sigmoid normalization. The 
resulting matrix is then multiplied by a set of attention coefficients (Dα 
and Dβ) and added to a bias term (Dβ) to obtain the final depth-attention 
matrix. 
The depth-attention matrix is used to modify the outputs of the ob­
ject detection model using the operation f (w(n), n), which is repre­
sented by Eq. (2) (Jie et al., 2021). 
f (w(n) , n) = n[ :; :; x, y, 4 :] + w(n)[x, y, 1]
(2) 
The notation “[:; :; x, y, 4:]” means that we are selecting all elements 
along the batch size and height dimensions. For each location (x, y), we 
are selecting elements starting from the 4th channel onward. The 
resulting shape will be [batch size, height, width, channels - 4]. w(n) [x, 
y, 1] is an indexing operation that selects a specific element from w(n) at 
the (x, y) location and channel index 1. The resulting shape will be a 
scalar value. 
To create the depth-attention matrix, the depth map was first pro­
cessed to create a JET pseudo-colour image. The image transforms into a 
two-dimensional (2D) matrix known as a depth map (Dmap), which 
contains values ranging from 0 to 255. This matrix represents the depth 
information of the image and is utilized in Eq. (3) for further processing 
or analysis. The dimensions of the pooled Dmap matrix in this equation 
are the same as those of n, input tensor. The pooled depth map matrix

After the depth map has undergone pooling operations and sigmoid 
normalization, the resulting matrix represents the attention weights for 
each element in the depth map. To refine these attention weights, the

This means that each element in the matrix is multiplied by its corre­

for individual adjustments of attention values for different regions of the 
depth map. Additionally, a bias term (Dβ) is added to the attention- 
weighted matrix. The bias term serves as an additional offset or 
adjustment to the attention values. By adding the bias term, the atten­
tion values can be shifted or biased towards certain values or ranges.

3.3. Performance on the test data 
To check the performance of inputting multi-modal data into the 
network and evaluating the statistical performance of proposed method, 
we compared different methods compared using various input modal­
ities and depth-fusion techniques, as shown in Table 2. In proposed 
method, NBR-DF method employed with YOLOv5 (baseline detector) as 
outlined in Section 2.4 and a depth-fusion method detailed in Section 
2.4.1. Table 2 shows the different approaches to compare with the 
proposed NBR-DF-YOLOv5. 
The proposed NBR-DF-YOLOv5 approach is compared with other 
methods using different input strategy, such as YOLOv5 using RGB im­
ages (baseline detector-1); YOLOv5 using depth images (baseline 
detector-2); YOLOv5 using both RGB and depth images (baseline 
detector-3); and a similar Noise-Tolerant Feature Fusion Technique (NT- 
FFN) method (Qixin et al., 2022) using both RGB and depth images as 
input data. 
In the case of single-modal object detection, only RGB or depth im­
ages are used as inputs for the baseline detector. For the depth fusion 
approach, RGB and depth images are merged along the channel 
dimension before input into the detection model. The detection results 
of baseline detector-2 using low-quality depth images achieved only 
0.36 of AP0.5 as noted in Table 3. These proves to be challenging in 
extracting distinct features for the detection of apple fruits in dense and 
complex orchard environments. On the contrary, AP0.5 of baseline 
detector-1 reaches up to 0.91 using only RGB images as input. The dif­
ference between the results of baseline detector-1 and 2 shows the 
dependable detection outcomes using colour images that easily differ­
entiate between background and fruit. Only two-dimensional RGB im­
ages can provide a more accurate detection score with a greater number 
of detected fruits. The results of baseline detector-3, which achieved an 
AP0.5 of 0.92, support the previous findings that multi-modal data is not 
effective in improving the detection accuracy of the model. The probable 
cause of such results is that the depth images have more noise and holes 
generated from a sensor. This behaviour increases the complexity of 
learning while training the detection model. The NBR-DF-YOLOv5 
approach demonstrated significant advantages, unlike previous results 
by achieving an AP0.5 rate of 0.96. 
Pre-processing of depth images enhanced the model accuracy by 
eliminating noise and holes from images to extract more features by 
combining both RGB and depth images. This thorough pre-processing 
ensures that the depth data fed into the YOLOv5 model is more accu­
rate and reliable. The addition of depth-attention mechanism in YOLOv5 
offered a novel approach to integrating depth information into object 
detection. It enhanced the focus on relevant features and simplified the 
training process for detailed control over how depth information in­
fluences the detection outcomes. A similar NT-FFN approach that used a 
feature fusion technique by utilizing fusion of extracted features from 
RGB-D input in the network's feature layer, achieved an AP0.5 acore of 
0.93. The primary reason for this is the presence of many holes in the 
depth images, which, when fused without filtering, introduces noise into 
the network and impairs its learning ability. Due to the enormous 
number of incorrect detection outcomes based on depth images, 
numerous fruits are not detected during intersection decisions. NT-FFN 
is designed for noise immunity, but its approach might not be as fine- 
tuned for the specific types of noise encountered in orchard settings, 
such as those caused by varying light conditions and foliage 
interference. 
In terms of processing speed, both single-modal detectors (baseline 
detector-1 & 2), which share the same network structure, are expected to 
operate at similar speeds. However, the low quality of depth images 
impacts the model speed. Depth images, being of lower quality, result in 
limited feature learning by the network. This leads to the generation of a 
greater number of bounding boxes during the inference time. Conse­
quently, more time is needed for Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) to 
eliminate excess bounding boxes. As a result, the speed of baseline 
detector-2 is significantly lower (15 FPS) as compared to baseline 
detector-1 (47 FPS; Table 3). Baseline detector-3, using depth-fusion, 
which aims to enhance the detection accuracy and speed after the 
operation of independent detection models (baseline detector-1 & 2), 
achieved only 15 FPS speed. The inference speed of the NT-FFN tech­
nique, using feature-fusion, achieved 39 FPS, slower than baseline 
detector-1. Although the NBR-DF-YOLOv5 approach achieved a slightly 
higher inference speed than baseline detector-1 (using only RGB im­
ages), still maintains the speed of 58 FPS on NVIDIA RTX 2050.

3.4. Performance analysis of detection modules on obscured fruits 
This section compared the detection results of different detection 
approaches on images with highly occluded apples. In high-density or­
chards, apple fruits are grown in clusters which make complex dense 
structures with background inference for detection models to detect 
obscured apples. These apples were occluded by branches, by neigh­
bouring apples, and by leaves. These obscured fruits can lead to false 
positives, where the model mistakes non-fruit objects for actual fruits. 
Some visual detection results are shown in Fig. 12, and statistical per­
formance analysis in Table 5. 
High-dense conditions generate more holes in depth images in out­
door environments. Without pre-processing step, depth images have 
more noise as compared to the processed depth images mapped with 
RGB images. This can be observed from the results of baseline detector-3 
and NT-FFN, both used RGB-D multi-modal input. Their performance 
decreased with a generation of more irrelevant information in input 
data. Baseline detector-1 achieved higher performance than baseline 
detector-3 and NT-FFN, potentially due to its better handling of fruit 
colour and background variations. NT-FFN is designed for noise im­
munity, but its approach might not be as fine-tuned for the specific types 
of noise encountered in orchard environment, such as those caused by 
varying light conditions and foliage interference. The proposed NBR-DF- 
YOLOv5 used pre-processed RGB-D as input data, which reduced the 
noise generated by the holes in-depth information. Removal of the 
background step eliminated the extra unnecessary information from 
input data to extract only relevant features. Background removal also 
decreased the complexity of the dense structure of the orchard in an 
image by removing the background leaves, trees, and light. By imple­
menting a depth-attention matrix, the NBR-DF-YOLOv5 method en­
hances its ability to concentrate on the key features in an image. The 
depth-attention mechanism in the NBR-DF-YOLOv5 network adjusted 
the attention values across the different regions of depth map using 
element-wise multiplication with attention coefficients (Dα). The inte­
gration of a bias term (Dβ) into the attention-weighted matrix offered a 
more sophisticated level of control in how depth information is factored 
into the detection process. 
Although the proposed NBR-DF-YOLOv5 techniques offer many ad­
vantages they still lack in the detection of fruit in images having colour 
grading issues generated by depth sensors and more depth holes. Pre- 
processing of depth images sometimes requires more computing time. 
The real-time performance of the proposed NBR-DF-Yolov5 depends on 
the computational and inference speed of the detection model. The 
computational complexity of the detection model depends upon the 
processing of input data. In multi-modal input data, more noise and 
holes in depth images affect the processing due to background light. 
Therefore, a depth enhancement algorithm is required with less 
computational time to fill depth holes for completing missing 
information. 
3.5. Comparison with state-of-the-art models 
To thoroughly evaluate the proposed NBR-DF-YOLOv5 model, we 
compared it with several state-of-the-art object detection models, 
including Shufflenet V2-YOLOX, Improved YOLOv5s, CA-YOLOv5, Des- 
YOLOv4, M2Det, Swin Transformer, YOLOv7, and EfficientDet. The 
statistical performance evaluation results of all these approaches were 
compared and presented in Table 6. These models were chosen for their 
relevance and high performance in the domain of real-time object 
detection. To ensure a fair comparison, these models were tested on the 
same apple dataset. 
The NBR-DF-YOLOv5 model achieves the highest Average Precision 
(AP0.5) of 0.964, outperforming all other models in this metric. The 
precision of 0.924 and recall of 0.941 further demonstrate the effec­
tiveness of model in reducing false positives and negatives, respectively. 
The integration of an attention-based depth fusion module allows the 
model to adaptively fuse multi-modal features. Which improves the 
identification of multi-scale objects and strengthens the network resis­
tance to noise, that is particularly beneficial in complex orchard envi­
ronments with varying lighting conditions and dense foliage. This 
mechanism contributes to the highest AP0.5 of 0.964, indicating the 
exceptional precision in detecting apple fruits. 
Compared to other state-of-the-art models, such as YOLOv8, which 
also shows high performance with an AP0.5 of 0.950, the NBR-DF- 
YOLOv5 stands out because NBR-DF technique includes comprehen­
sive preprocessing of depth images to eliminate noise and fill in missing 
depth information. As a result, processed images provide much cleaner 
and accurate input data for the YOLOv5 (detection) model. By con­
verting raw depth data into point clouds and applying segmentation and 
object extraction, the model reduces false positives and improves recall, 
as evidenced by its precision of 0.924 and recall of 0.941. While models 
like Swin Transformer and YOLOv7 offer competitive precision and 
recall, they do not surpass NBR-DF-YOLOv5 across all evaluated metrics. 
By contrast, models like M2Det and CA-YOLOv5, despite their high 
performance, have higher memory footprints, making them less suitable 
for resource-constrained environments. The NBR-DF-YOLOv5 approach 
surpasses the performance comparison and displayed the novelty by 
utilizing advanced depth-fusion attention mechanism and effective 
preprocessing steps, which significantly contribute to its leading 
performance.

4. Conclusions 
A non-targeted background removal using the depth fusion (NBR- 
DF) technique is proposed and used with YOLOv5 detection model, to 
study the effect of multi-modal data as input and for development of 
detection model for machine vision system for high-density apple or­
chards. A stereo-depth sensor (RealSense d455) was used for image 
capturing in the high-density complex architecture of orchards. In high- 
density orchards, apples are in clusters, which results in maximum oc­
clusion conditions. The use of multi-modal data can improve the accu­
racy of detection, and the addition of a depth-fusion layer improves 
network learning. The proposed NBR-DF-YOLOv5 achieved the AP0.5 of 
0.964, a precision of 0.924, a recall of 0.941, and an F1-score of 0.934 
with a detection speed of 58 FPS. The detection speed is optimized on 
GPU RTX 2060 to meet real-time detection requirements. Multi-modal 
data is pre-processed before input into the model. The pre-processing 
step is used to enhance the depth features of the image by filling holes 
to eliminate noise generated by holes (incomplete depth information). 
Results compared with different input strategies using only RGB images, 
only depth images, both RGB and depth images, with and without pre- 
processing of RGB-D images. Comparison results highlighted the 
advantage of NBR-DF-YOLOv5 that used pre-processing of RGB-D input 
data to eliminate unnecessary background information from the image 
and fusion of multi-modal information in the depth-fusion layer. The 
proposed NBR-DF method enhances the identification of multi-scale 
objects and strengthens the network's resistance to noise by effectively 
eliminating unnecessary background information from the input 
images. 
Overall, the results of this study presented the advantages of pre- 
processing of RGB-D images and the fusion of multi-modal informa­
tion in a network for real-time fruit detection. This developed technique 
can be used in devising automatic fruit harvesters and evaluating their 
real-time on-field performance. In the future, the proposed NBR-DF will 
be used as a preprocessing pipeline for RGB-D input data with other 
detection models to analyze the advantages of fusing RGB-D data. 
Additionally, alternative depth data fusion techniques can be explored.
