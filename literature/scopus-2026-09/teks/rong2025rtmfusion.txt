A B S T R A C T
Accurate and rapid acquisition of semantically annotated point clouds from tomato cultivation regions is crucial
for autonomous tomato harvesting robots. This entails the robot’s ability to perceive tomato point clouds from
various locations and identify the stems and pedicels connecting the fruits. However, amidst background
interference from similar stem and pedicel features, the heterogeneous growth postures of tomato clusters and
the slender and short nature of pedicels pose challenges in semantic point cloud segmentation and matching of
different tomato fruits, pedicels, and stems. Leveraging the salient feature representation of depth images in fine
contours and distance dimensions, this paper introduces RTMFusion, a lightweight dual-stream backbone
instance segmentation model that integrates color images and depth images. A Depth Enhanced Feature Fusion
module is designed for multi-modal feature fusion. Fruit-bearing organ matching is facilitated by a method based
on distance constraints and conditional search. Key characteristics are extracted from semantic point clouds, and
an optimal condition search is conducted on candidates meeting constraint conditions. On the test dataset, our
model improved the mask mAP by 4.6 % compared to the baseline, achieving 72.1 % with a speed of 17.1 ms.
Our matching method achieved 99.2 % pedicel matching accuracy and 97.3 % stem matching accuracy on 150
greenhouse samples. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of accurately identifying and matching tomato
organs in real-world scenarios, addressing the challenge from complex greenhouse environments. This integrated
visual system enhances the success rate of visual identification and the precision of cutting position localization
for tomato harvesting robots.
1. Introduction
Tomatoes are a globally cultivated crop. As yields increase, the de-
mand for manual labor in managing and harvesting crops poses signif-
icant challenges. This, coupled with rising costs of horticultural
maintenance and harvest operations, has spurred the development of
automated systems. These systems are designed to monitor and harvest
tomatoes with heightened efficiency [1,2]. Recent advancements
include the deployment and field testing of robotic harvesters, prom-
ising solutions to these challenges. These robots mechanize harvesting,
boost productivity, and reduce reliance on manual labor in tomato
production [3], [4], [5].
Automated harvesting of crops with pedicels, such as tomato clus-
ters, is a fundamental yet demanding problem, requiring both accurate
localization of shearing points as well as grasping pose reasoning based
solely on sensor input [6]. In the case of a cluster of tomatoes, it is
composed of soft fruits and pedicels. In the process of harvesting, the
end-effector is tasked with delicately gripping and cutting off the pedi-
cels to separate them from the stem without any contact with the fruits
[7]. However, the heterogeneous growth posture of tomato clusters and
the random distribution of obstacles pose challenges in accurately
capturing vital pedicel information. Also, due to similar shape, color,
and texture features, other tomato clusters and stems in the background
can also disrupt the robot’s perception system,.
The recent developments in convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
have had a significant positive impact on agricultural robotics. The
utilization of CNNs in this domain has revolutionized the capabilities of
agricultural robots, enabling them to perform various tasks more effi-
ciently and accurately [8,9]. One of the most direct applications of CNNs
in agricultural robotics is to recognize the fruit from the color image.

apples growing at different stages in orchards. Afonso et al. [11] re-
ported results on the detection of tomatoes using Mask R-CNN, with
classification between ripe and unripe tomatoes. Wang et al. [12] opti-
mized the YOLOv5 model for litchi fruit detection and ripeness assess-
ment in orchards. They employed the lightweight ShuffleNetv2 as the
new backbone network and incorporated the CBAM attention mecha-
nism module to refine informative features. In addition to architectural
improvements and optimizations, the integration of multimodal fusion
algorithms that incorporate multispectral and depth images as supple-
mentary inputs can significantly enhance the detection performance of
the model. Gong et al. [13] presented an improved Mask R-CNN, which
modifies the dimension of the input data by fusing near-infrared, depth,
and color images, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to segment
tomatoes under occlusion and strong lighting conditions. To reduce the
impact of environmental noise in orchards on fruit detection, Sun et al.
[14] proposed a noise-tolerant feature fusion network to integrate out-
door RGB-D information. This model makes full use of the comple-
mentary multi-modal features and learns object features from multi-
scale features from color images and fusion modules. In the test exper-
iments, this multi-modal model could achieve the mAP50 of 95.4 % with
a speed of 39 FPS. Rong et al. [15] designed an improved YOLOv5 fusing
RGB images and depth images to reduce background tomato misiden-
tification in the greenhouse. Test results indicated that the mAP
improved by 3.2 % compared to native YOLOv5. The aforementioned
study integrates color images and depth images into a four-channel
input to compensate for the lack of distance features in color images.
Although directly integrating at the input stage can improve model
performance, it has been shown that depth features are not fully utilized
during the feature extraction phase.
For tasks involving yield estimation and maturity grading, it may
suffice for the robot to gather information solely about the fruits.
However, in the case of fruit harvesting tasks, the visual system neces-
sitates not only the identification of the fruit but also the subsequent
segmentation of its fruit-bearing pedicels and stems. Consequently,
scholars have sought to advance the segmentation and modeling of
pivotal plant organs, aiming to equip robots with accurate harvesting
information [16,17]. Zhong et al. [18] introduced a method for calcu-
lating litchi picking points. The approach utilized the YOLACT model to
segment litchi fruits and fruit-bearing branches. Then, the angle of the
main fruit-bearing branch was determined through skeleton extraction
and least squares fitting. Li et al. [4] proposed a multitask-aware
network, which performed region detection on fruit bunch, and region
segmentation on the fruit-bearing pedicel and stem. The aforementioned
two methods are used to segment plant organs in color images and then
determine a few critical points, including the picking point, based on
pixel coordinates. However, these methods heavily rely on the accuracy
of 2D coordinates. Deviations in the spatial points mapped by these pixel
coordinates can significantly impact the overall accuracy. In the use of
consumer-grade RGB-D cameras, such errors are commonly observed,
especially when it comes to capturing fine details like small fruit-bearing
pedicels. Even ToF cameras face difficulties in ensuring the accuracy of
every single point, as certain areas may contain gaps or voids, and
random errors can occur in object edge points.
By utilizing a cluster of point clouds for plant organ feature analysis,
it is possible to mitigate or eliminate the adverse effects caused by a
small number of erroneous point clouds. Lin et al. [19] employed
instance segmentation and geometric analysis techniques for the
reconstruction of guava fruits and branches. Initially, the Mask R-CNN
model was utilized to detect fruits and branches. The detected objects
were then transformed into 3D point clouds. Fruit reconstruction
involved fitting fruit spheres using the RANSAC algorithm, while branch
reconstruction involved fitting cylindrical segments based on principal
component analysis. The fruit reconstruction achieved an F1 score of
0.833, while the branch reconstruction achieved an F1 score of 0.415. Ni
et al. [20] developed an effective method based on 3D photogrammetry
and 2D instance segmentation to monitor blueberry clusters. The pro-
posed method allows for the reconstruction of each blueberry fruit and
enables differentiation of their ripeness levels. Across all varieties, the
average counting accuracy achieved a rate of 97.3 %. In these research

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Image acquisition and dataset creation
The image dataset utilized in this study was predominantly acquired
through close-range observational capture by harvesting robots (refer to
Fig. 2), complemented by additional handheld camera collections. Data
acquisition spanned from December 2022 to June 2023 and was con-
ducted at two primary locations: the Nijiawan Agricultural Site in Suz-
hou and the Cuihu Agricultural Site in Beijing, China. The Intel
RealSense L515 camera and the Microsoft Azure Kinect DK camera
served as the principal imaging equipment, capturing high-definition
images with a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels. To ensure consistency
and optimal image clarity, the distance between the cameras and the
cherry tomatoes was meticulously controlled to remain within the range
of 0.32 to 0.4 m. The data collection period was from 9:00 AM to 11:00
AM and from 3:00 PM to 5:00 PM, considering image data under varying
lighting conditions.
As demonstrated in Fig. 3, the acquired images contain a large
number of non-target regions. To address this issue, we first utilized an
object detection algorithm to identify and locate clusters of tomatoes.
Subsequently, the identified regions were scaled and meticulously
cropped, pertaining to the color images, depth images, and point clouds.
The cropping method used is described in detail in Algorithm 1. The
tomato growth postures in the acquired dataset are categorized into four
types based on the position relationship between the fruit and the fruit-
bearing stem: forward-facing, backward-facing, left-facing, and right-
facing, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The processed images were manually
annotated using the Labelme annotation tool (https://github.com/la
belmeai/labelme). During the annotation process, annotators precisely
placed points along the edges of the targets to define closed polygons,
categorizing the targets into three classes: fruits, pedicels, and stems (see
Fig. 5).

2.2. RTMFusion
2.2.1. Dual-stream architecture for multi-modal fusion
Compared to traditional convolutional neural network architectures,
the RGB-D fusion model enhances the learning capacity by leveraging
the rich, complementary characteristics of information from two sour-
ces: color images and depth images. This integration has the potential to
yield robustness and high performance in the model’s output. The cur-
rent multimodal feature fusion architectures are primarily categorized
into two types: single-stream and dual-stream structures. As shown in
Fig. 6(a), the single-stream structure involves concatenating RGB and
depth images to form a new four-channel or six-channel input image.
The dual-stream structure consists of two separate convolutional neural
network branches: one is dedicated to extracting features from RGB
images, while the other focuses on depth information extraction, with
the two sets of features being fused at the end of the encoder, as given in
Fig. 6(b). Compared to the single-stream structure, the dual-stream
structure initially applies convolution to the depth images, which sup-
presses noise and prevents the loss of valuable information, thus better
harnessing the depth features [21–23]. The research presented here
utilizes an improved dual-stream branch structure that combines the
advantages of both single-stream and dual-stream approaches, inte-
grating depth information both early and late in the model to achieve
stronger complementary characteristics of the features (see Fig. 6(c)).
In this study, RTMDet (Real-Time Model Detection) [24] was chosen
as the benchmark for improvements due to its performance as a light-
weight instance segmentation model on the COCO dataset, achieving
state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance. RTMDet is a new family from
YOLO series of models, capable of performing tasks such as object
detection and instance segmentation, and has achieved state-of-the-art
(SOTA) results on the COCO dataset. The macro architecture of
RTMDet is decomposed into the backbone, neck, and head. The back-
bone network employed is CSPNeXt, a structure that integrates the Cross
Stage Partial Network (CSP) [25] and the ResNeXt Network [26]. It

2.2.2. Depth enhanced feature fusion module
A simple linear fusion approach may fail to fully capitalize on the
complementary nature of the two types of information at different
levels, which could lead to information loss or the dominance of one
type of information over the other. To address this issue, we have
designed the Depth Enhanced Feature Fusion (DEFF) module to inte-
grate the information from a dual-stream structure. As illustrated in
Fig. 8, a DEFF module primarily consists of two sub-modules: the ACC
and the MCC. The ACC sub-module includes an adaptive average pool-
ing layer, a 3 × 3 convolutional module, and a 1 × 1 convolutional
module, focusing on capturing background information or more gener-
alized features. In contrast, the MCC sub-module, comprising an adap-
tive max pooling module, a 3 × 3 convolutional module, and a 1 × 1
convolutional module, is tailored to capture texture and edge features.
For the multimodal features extracted at a particular scale, the
output feature maps from RGB-D stream F1 and the output feature maps
from Depth stream F2 are processed through the ACC and MCC for
nonlinear transformation and then element-wise added. Subsequently, a
Sigmoid function is applied for nonlinear activation, mapping the values
to the interval between 0 and 1. Finally, the fused result is combined
with the output feature maps from RGB-D stream through a residual
connection, thereby leveraging the complementary information of RGB
and Depth to mine useful features from the RGB-D input. The mathe-
matical representation of this process is formalized as Equation (1).

2.3. Fruit-bearing organ matching method based on distance constraints
and conditional search
Even after cropping images to eliminate the majority of irrelevant
regions, it is inevitable that multiple clusters of adjacent tomato plant
organs still appear within the confined field of view, as given in Fig. 9.
The instance segmentation model partitions each tomato component
individually, without establishing semantic connections between the
various tomato organs. Existing methods typically determine the fruit-
bearing pedicels and stems on two-dimensional images based on pixel
coordinates or directly utilize models to segment fruits containing
pedicels. Subsequently, the pedicel pixels are used to fit curve to
ascertain the shearing points, and finally, they map a pixel coordinate to
a three-dimensional spatial point (Li et al., 2023, [5]). For slender ob-
jects like tomato pedicels, the corresponding point cloud generated from
RGB-D data often contains substantial noise, and the positioning may
fail due to the noise affecting the mapped individual points. To address
this issue, we propose a point cloud matching method for tomato plant
organs that first acquires the peduncle point cloud and then uses the
point cloud clusters to determine the precise location for cutting the
peduncle. This method encompasses four distinct steps: (1) We generate
point cloud clusters corresponding to the fruit, pedicels, and stems from
2D pixel coordinates of masks produced by RTMFusion; (2) Multiple
point cloud filters are adopted to eliminate point cloud noise; (3) For
each tomato fruit, we apply distance constraints to remove portions of
the pedicels and stems that do not conform to the typical growth pat-
terns of tomatoes; (4) Within the pool of qualified targets, predefined
distance conditions are utilized to identify the fruit-bearing pedicel and
stem.
2.3.1. Point cloud sampling and filtering method
In the aligned RGB images and point clouds, semantic point clouds
are obtained by directly indexing the mask coordinates based on the
different category masks output by RTMFusion. As illustrated in Fig. 10,
outlier points invariably appear near the edges of objects, a consequence
of both the model’s edge segmentation precision and the inherent per-
formance limitations of RGB-D cameras. Therefore, it is necessary to
apply filtering processes to the classified point cloud clusters.
In this study, we initially perform voxel down sampling on the
classified point cloud clusters to reduce the computational load, setting
the voxel sizes for fruits, pedicels, and stems at 0.003 m, 0.001 m, and
0.003 m, respectively. Then, a pass-through filter is employed to remove
outlier points along the depth direction, with a threshold set at 0.5 m

2.3.2. Distance constraint method for fruit-bearing organ matching
As illustrated in Fig. 9, multiple clusters of tomatoes are observed to
grow adjacently within the current field of view. Before the correct fruit-
bearing pedicel and stem can be identified, constraints grounded in
spatial distance are established using manually extracted key charac-
teristics, serving to exclude any pedicels or stems which clearly do not
fulfill the specified conditions. To mitigate computational complexity
while preserving critical information of the tomato plant’s components,
geometric feature extraction is performed on the point clouds of the
fruit, stem, and pedicel, as shown in Fig. 11. Owing to the relatively
short length of the stems within the field of view and their minimal
deformation, they can be effectively approximated as straight segments.
Consequently, a straight line is fitted using the least squares method,
denoted as L1. The point cloud of the fruit is encapsulated within the
minimal bounding box by employing the Oriented Bounding Box (OBB)
algorithm, which allows for the representation of the fruit’s point cloud
through the eight vertices of a spatial rectangle. The growth direction of
the fruit, designated as L2, the center of the fruit, Pf, and the rectangular
plane Ap1p2p3p4 that is connected to the pedicel, as well as the center of
this plane, P0, are all computed based on the vertices provided by the
OBB. Lastly, the centroid of the pedicel’s point cloud is calculated,
represented as Pp.

2.3.3. Fruit-bearing organ search method based on optimal conditions
Some invalid solutions that could erroneously match tomato fruits
within the visual field are eliminated upon solving the constraint-
satisfaction problem based on distance conditions for the pedicel and
stem point clouds. However, the dense planting typical in greenhouse
environments can result in the occurrence of multiple pedicels or stems
within confined spaces (see Fig. 9 and Fig. 12), all adhering to the dis-
tance constraints. To tackle this issue, an optimal search methodology
that utilizes the distances between key characteristics has been
introduced.
Given the biological traits associated with tomato growth, wherein
the fruit is affixed to the fruit-bearing stem via a pedicel, it stands to
reason that the point cloud cluster representing the fruit would be in
closest proximity to that of the fruit-bearing pedicel, with the latter’s
point cloud manifesting at the apex of the fruit. Furthermore, it is
inevitable that there exists a juncture within the main stem’s point cloud
where it converges with the peduncle’s point cloud. Building upon this
biological characteristic, we conduct a search for the unique pedicel and
stem that belong to the tomato fruit.
In the initial step, we target the selection of the pedicel matching the
fruit, leveraging the direct physical linkage between them. The pedicel is
consistently connected at the upper extremity of the fruit. Therefore, we
pinpoint the center point P0 on the top plane of the OBB (see Fig. 11),
hypothesizing it as the pedicel’s theoretical junction point. Subse-

samples. The dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test sets
at a ratio of 0.7:0.15:0.15, respectively.

3.1.2. Ablation studies
In this section, we aim to ascertain the effectiveness of integrating
RGB images and Depth images to improve the segmentation accuracy of
tomato organs. Moreover, we explore the outcomes of multi-modal
feature fusion using different architectural frameworks. To this end,
we have examined the effects of three unique fusion configurations and
have also undertaken an evaluation of our proposed DEFF (Depth
Enhanced Feature Fusion) multimodal feature fusion module. To
streamline model improvements and guarantee a fair comparison, we
have developed RTMFusion on the MMDetection platform and have

benchmarked it against RTMDet.
In the training phase, the image input size was set to 640 × 640. The
data augmentation techniques employed included random scaling (with
a scale range from 0.6 to 1.4), random cropping, random enhancements
in the HSV color space, and random flipping with a probability of 0.5.
The batch size for model training was held constant at 16, with vali-
dation occurring every two epochs. The base learning rate was initial-
ized at 0.004, starting with a linear ramp-up as part of the warm-up
strategy, then proceeding with training based on this rate, and ulti-
mately implementing a cosine annealing approach to modulate the
learning rate, as illustrated in Fig. 13(a). For the updating of model
parameters, the AdamW optimizer was selected, coupled with a weight
decay coefficient of 0.05. Each model underwent an extensive training
process lasting for 1,000 epochs. After a thousand iterations, the loss
functions, along with the segmentation mAP and bounding box mAP for
RTMDet and RTMFusion across different scales, were observed to
approach convergence. An initial analysis of their performance on the
validation set indicates that RTMFusion outperforms RTMDet in terms
of performance.
In this study, we have introduced multiple modifications to the
comprehensive framework of the benchmark model, RTMDet. Initially,
to capture the performance outcomes of diverse multi-modal feature

distinct image types, transitioning the input from a 3-channel to a 4-

computational load. However, the mAP for segmentation has improved
by a mere 0.3 %. This strategy is suboptimal for segmentation tasks as it
fails to leverage the effective extraction and utilization of pertinent data
from depth image. In comparison, the dual-stream architecture yields a
more substantial performance enhancement, with the mAP for instance
segmentation and object detection increasing by 2 %, 2.7 % and 1.7 %,

3.1.3. Comparative studies
To further demonstrate the superiority of our proposed model, we
conducted comparative analyses between RTMFusion and state-of-the-
art algorithms, including Mask R-CNN (ResNet50 + FPN) [28],
SOLOv2 (ResNet50 + FPN) [29], YOLACT (ResNet50 + FPN) [30], and
YOLOv5-Seg-S (CSPDarkNet + PAFPN). Each model underwent training
for 1000 epochs under identical hardware configurations, with all other
training parameters set to default values. The frameworks utilized for
these experiments were MMDetection and MMYOLO.
Table 4 presents the comparative performance results of different
models. RTMFusion-T and RTMFusion-S exhibit a decrease in instance
mask segmentation performance, with mAP lower by 4.2 % and 2.2 %,
respectively, when compared to the classical Mask R-CNN model. This
performance disparity is attributed to the reduced complexity of our
models. RTMFusion is a light-weight instance segmentation model, with
the T and S versions possessing only 35.4 % and 63.8 % of the parameter
count of Mask R-CNN, while their FLOPs are a mere 10.1 % and 18.7 %
of that of Mask R-CNN, respectively. In comparison with other real-time
instance segmentation models such as SOLOv2, YOLACT, and YOLOv5-
Seg-S, RTMFusion demonstrates competitive performance. Specifically,
RTMDet falls short by only 3.6 % and 3 % on the Mask AP metric
compared to SOLOv2, while maintaining an edge over YOLACT and
YOLOv5-Seg-S, which do not exhibit any superiority in Mask mAP.
Taking into account both the model size and performance, RTMFusion,
which integrates RGB and depth information, outperforms others in
both bounding box and instance mask tasks. Among these lightweight
models, RTMFusion-S emerges as the leader in performance, and it does
so with fewer parameters and lower computational complexity (FLOPs)

4. Discussion
This work introduces the extraction of object contours and distance
features using depth images to complement the distance perception
limitations of RGB features. By designing a multi-modal feature
extraction network for feature fusion and channel parameter reduction,
these enhancements can improve the segmentation capabilities of to-
mato organs. Compared to RTMDet, RTMFusion demonstrates an in-
crease of 3.6 % (RTMFusion-T) and 4.6 % (RTMFusion-S) in mAP for the
instance segmentation task, showcasing the effectiveness of RGB and
depth fusion. However, there are instances in this study where pedicels
are not recognized, leading to unmatched pedicels. The findings pre-
sented in this study are preliminary, and the proposed methods for
multi-modal feature extraction and fusion may not be optimal. It is
essential to focus more on the feature extraction capabilities of multi-
modal fusion networks and further explore methods for integrating
point clouds and images to enhance segmentation accuracy, especially
when multiple stems are in close proximity. Additionally, our dataset is
relatively small, comprising only 714 images, whereas large datasets
like COCO contain hundreds of thousands of samples. Therefore,
expanding the dataset while considering variations in lighting condi-
tions and different camera angles is necessary. However, annotating
segmentation masks is a labor-intensive process, and even with a suffi-
cient number of images, annotation remains challenging. In the future,
semi-supervised methods could be employed to improve model perfor-
mance or advanced algorithms such as SAM could be utilized for auto-
matic object differentiation and image pre-labeling.
The segmentation of plant organs, such as tomatoes, represents a
critical task poised to seamlessly integrate with the future vision systems
of harvesting robots. This integration has the potential to revolutionize
the field by providing a framework for the precise identification of fruit-
bearing pedicels, thereby paving the way for further exploration into 3D

5. Conclusion
This paper introduces a method for three-dimensional semantic point
cloud acquisition and matching of tomato organs. Due to the suscepti-
bility of tomatoes in the current ridges to interference from similar
features in the background, a lightweight dual-stream backbone
instance segmentation model, named RTMFusion, is proposed by inte-
grating RGB images and depth images. A Depth Enhanced Feature
Fusion (DEFF) module is designed to fully exploit the complementary
nature of the two different modal features and reduce the number of
channels after fusion. On the test dataset, compared to RTMDet,
RTMFusion achieves a 3.6 % (RTMFusion-T) and 4.6 % (RTMFusion-S)
increase in mask mAP. To obtain three-dimensional semantic point
clouds of fruit-bearing pedicels and stems, a fruit-bearing organ
matching method based on distance constraints and conditional search
is proposed. This method involves filtering point cloud clusters, applying
distance constraints to the filtered clusters, and performing conditional
optimal search. In on-site testing experiments, the proposed visual
pipeline was tested in 150 different scenarios, achieving a pedicel
matching accuracy of 99.2 % and a stem matching accuracy of 97.3 %.
These experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the developed
method in accurately identifying and matching tomato plants in various
real-world scenarios. In autonomous tomato harvesting tasks, this
method equips the robots with advanced three-dimensional visual
perception capabilities, enabling them to deliver real-time and precise
information on pedicel shearing.
Building upon existing work, our future endeavors will concentrate
on enhancing the efficiency of data annotation by integrating methods
such as Segment Anything Model (SAM). Furthermore, we intend to
explore multi-angle visual inputs to advance Simultaneous Localization
and Mapping (SLAM) techniques tailored specifically for tomato organs,
further refining the robots’ environmental understanding and naviga-
tional capabilities.
