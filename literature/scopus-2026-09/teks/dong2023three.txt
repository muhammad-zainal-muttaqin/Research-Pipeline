A B S T R A C T  
Estimating three-dimensional (3D) fruit-level phenotypic traits of apple trees can potentially improve apple 
orchard breeders’ management strategy. However, the phenotypic traits of apple fruits (including the quantity, 
3D distribution, and volume of apples) constitute important parameters that influence yield but are difficult to 
quantify manually. Therefore, it is necessary to effectively and efficiently quantify apple phenotyping to monitor 
apple yield and support a better management system. This study developed a novel method for extracting in­
dividual 3D apple traits and 3D mapping for three apple training systems. The 3D point cloud of apples was 
reconstructed from multi-view images collected via a multi-camera system-based unmanned-aerial vehicle. In­
dividual apples in the 3D point cloud were extracted via a 3D instance segmentation algorithm that included 
generalized sparse convolutional neural networks, a weighted discriminative loss function, and a varying 
density-based 3D clustering method. The developed apple trait extraction algorithm can help compute the po­
sition and volume of an individual apple. The R2 values with the average weighted-mean-absolute-percentage 
error (WMAPE) of apple counting and apple volume estimation were 0.84–0.99 (VMAPE = 3.41–12.75%) and 
0.84–0.90 (WMAPE = 4.74–7.03%), respectively. The 3D spatial and volumetric distribution of apples were 
obtained and analyzed. This study developed an effective method combining 3D photography and 3D instance 
segmentation that can accurately estimate individual apple phenotypic traits from different types of apple 
training systems in orchards and can also be utilized for the analysis of other fruit traits.   
1. Introduction 
Digital agriculture refers to the use of technologies such as big data, 
artificial intelligence, and the Internet of Things (IoT) in agricultural 
production, value chains, and food systems to make more informed 
decisions and optimize agricultural productivity (Klerkx et al., 2019). 
Digital agriculture is increasingly being utilized in fruit production 
practices. Information regarding the quantity, volume, and spatial dis­
tribution of fruits in trees is of significant interest for fruit management. 
With this information, harvest and production projections can be made, 
allowing for improved planning of harvesting, storage, and marketing 
operations (Nuske et al., 2014; Bargoti and Underwood, 2016). Further, 
it is also able to understand and relate the spatial distribution of fruits 
and yield to other factors and variables that influence the management 
of orchards, such as fertilization, irrigation, pruning strategies and the 
size and structure of trees (Gen´e-Mola., 2020b). In addition, information 
on the georeferenced distribution of fruits can be target points for robot 
harvesting, as harvesting robots would have the spatial coordinates of 
each fruit and could mainly focus on the grasping operation itself, 
improving the harvesting efficiency and speed. Traditional manual 
methods used for quantifying fruit yield and determining fruit distri­
butions are laborious. Especially, in large-scale orchards, innovative 
methods are needed to improve the measurement accuracy of fruit traits 
such as quantity, size, and spatial distribution of fruits. 
The characterization of the three-dimensional (3D) spatial distribu­
tion of fruits and yield is a highly active research field at both tree and

orchard scales. Previously, many studies focused on two-dimensional 
(2D) image-based technologies for fruit yield and size estimation 
(Stein et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2017; H¨ani et al., 2020). However, these 
previous studies have certain shortcomings. First, as common features 
between image flows cannot be extracted, it is difficult to determine the 
same object in the real world; this leads to the double-counting problem 
in fruit detection (Feng et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2020). Second, due to the 
unstructured environment, fruits may be occluded by leaves and 
branches so the volume of the fruit cannot be estimated accurately. 
Accordingly, 3D sensing technologies provide opportunities to over­
come the limitations of 2D image-based methods. In addition to 
providing important depth information for addressing the occlusion 
problem and acquiring structure information, 3D data can be used to 
reconstruct the real orchard scene and avoid fruit double counting 
(Rosell-Polo et al., 2015; Wang et al.,2017; Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020b). 
Commonly applied 3D sensors include 3D light detection and 
ranging (LiDAR) and depth cameras, which can build the 3D point 
clouds of fruits and plants directly by determining the sensor-target 
distance, with time-of-flight and structured-light being the most com­
mon measuring principles. LiDAR devices are usually operated in 
autonomous driving and mapping, which is expensive and not user- 
friendly. Lack of color information and sparsity of 3D data make it 
difficult to detect fruit and estimate the phenotypic traits (Gen´e-Mola 
et al., 2020a). Depth cameras are commonly low-cost plug-and-play 
sensors, but the performance of depth cameras is severely affected by the 
illumination intensity, especially in a real field environment. With the 
rapid development of image-based 3D computer vision and photo­
grammetry techniques, sets of unordered and unstructured 2D high- 
resolution images can be used to extract common characteristics to 
infer camera pose and reconstruct the 3D scene (Rosell-Polo et al., 
2015), which helps overcome the shortcomings of the above 2D imaging 
methods. Generally, image acquisition using unmanned ground vehicles 
(UGVs) not only needs high-accuracy navigation and obstacle avoidance 
algorithms, but it also has a great soil disturbance and compaction effect 
for orchard fields. In recent years, compared to UGV systems, unmanned 
aerial vehicle (UAV)-based camera systems have been utilized to effi­
ciently collect image data. In addition, UAV-based oblique photography 
techniques with multi-camera systems are becoming increasingly 
mature, which can improve the data collection efficiency and enhance 
the details from different perspectives (Rupnik et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 
2018). Previous studies demonstrated that multi-view image-based 
methods can be utilized to reconstruct the dense 3D point cloud of large- 
scale orchards. 
The accurate extraction of individual fruits from the reconstructed 
3D point cloud of a tree is challenging, especially because of the sig­
nificant occlusion caused by leaves and branches, and fruits that are 
close to each other generate multi-instance fruit clusters. Additionally, 
segmentation is challenging using traditional 3D point cloud processing 
algorithms because of high redundancy, uneven density, and lack of 
explicit structure of point cloud data (Nguyen and Le, 2013). Due to 
environmental issues, such as illumination and fruit overlapping issues, 
multi-fruit clusters cannot be explicitly divided into individual fruits 
using color-based segmentation methods, such as the color-based region 
generation algorithm (Sun et al., 2020). Meanwhile, some traditional 3D 
point cloud processing algorithms are sensitive to the density of points, 
which results in the segmented 3D point cloud of fruit being too sparse, 
making them incapable of evaluating the volume of the fruit (Gen´e-Mola 
et al., 2020a). 3D point cloud segmentation requires an understanding of 
both the 3D global geometric structure and the fine-grained details of 
each point (Guo et al., 2021). Deep-learning-based 3D point cloud seg­
mentation, such as instance segmentation, can outperform traditional 
algorithms and has attracted increased attention in scene understanding 
(Liu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). 3D instance segmentation can 
precisely separate a single instance from the entire 3D point cloud, 
generally including point- and voxel-based networks. The point clouds 
are unordered and scattered in 3D space, so the point-based instance 
segmentation should sample the points according to blocks and train the 
points in each block (Wang et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019). This 
approach is computationally intensive and only focuses on the local 
features so as to ignore the global features of the overall 3D point cloud. 
Unlike point-based methods, voxel-based approaches convert the 
irregular point clouds to regular representations in 3D voxelization 
(Lahoud et al., 2019). This can reduce the computation complexity and 
geometric details can be controlled by the voxel size. Conversely, the 
convolution operation in deep-learning-based instance segmentation is 
executed on a rectangular grid or a dense representation, including the 
empty space represented as 0, for feature extraction (Choy et al., 2019). 
However, as most of the space in the 3D point cloud is empty, it leads to 
wastage of memory and computational resources. More importantly, the 
sparsity of feature maps in neural networks is substantially reduced with 
a deeper convolution operation, causing feature distortion problems, 
especially for small objects. In the case of fruit segmentation, the size 
scale of the fruit differs greatly relative to that of the entire tree. In 
addition, certain fruits are in contact with each other or occluded; 
accordingly, the 3D reconstruction of fruit results in an incomplete 3D 
point cloud of fruit with varied and complex shapes. Therefore, the 
instance segmentation of individual fruits from the 3D point cloud of the 
whole continues to pose challenges. 
Fruits in 2D images may not provide sufficient information for fruit 
trait extraction due to the occlusion and ambiguity caused by perspec­
tive projection (Chaudhury et al., 2019). However, in addition to rep­
resenting the 3D position in the coordinate system, the segmented 
individual fruit from the 3D point cloud can visually represent the shape 
of the fruit, which facilitates the fruit volume extraction. The most 
common approaches for fruit volume evaluation are the largest segment 
method, sphere-fitting algorithms, and template-matching method 
(Gen´e-Mola et al., 2021). The largest segment method assumes the fruit 
diameter is equal to the Euclidean distance between the two most distant 
points, which has low accuracy due to occlusion. The performance of 
sphere fitting based on the least-squares method depends on the quality 
of the 3D point cloud and is sensitive to noise. For irregular fruits, this 
simple ball fitting cannot restore the original fruit shape. The template- 
matching method involves building a model of an apple and scaling it up 
and down to create a large number of templates and then using the 
iterative closest point algorithm to match them one by one with the 
actual segmented apple point cloud to find the template that is closest to 
the actual apple size to calculate the volume. However, there are also 
some differences in shapes between the same fruits, and the template 
model in the template-matching method cannot replace all the same 
fruits. To date, existing fruit volume estimation methods cannot pre­
serve and utilize the original point cloud directly, which inevitably leads 
to certain errors in the volume evaluation. 
To overcome the aforementioned limitations, this study aimed to 
develop a data collection and processing method for extracting indi­
vidual fruit and estimating fruit traits from 3D point clouds recon­
structed from multi-view images using the structure-from-motion (SFM) 
algorithm under large-scale apple orchard conditions. Compared to 
previous studies, the proposed method is expected to extract the fruit 
from different types of fruit trees in large-scale orchards more efficiently 
and accurately and enable the quantitative assessment of more fruit- 
related traits (e.g., quantity, volume, 3D positions) simultaneously. 
The specific objectives of this study were to (1) collect image data by 
designing a UAV-based multi-camera system in an orchard and perform 
the 3D reconstruction of multiple orchards; (2) develop a 3D instance 
segmentation algorithm with a special 3D convolutional neural network, 
weighted discriminative loss function, and varying density-based 3D 
clustering algorithm to extract individual fruits; (3) develop a fruit trait 
extraction algorithm to restore an incomplete fruit and obtain fruit traits 
such as quantity, volume, and 3D distribution; (4) verify the perfor­
mance of the proposed algorithm by comparing it with the ground truth; 
and (5) spatially map the extracted fruit traits.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. 3D data acquisition 
The experiments site was an apple orchard with three different types 
of training systems located at the Apple Use Research Institute in Geo­
chang, South Korea (latitude: 35◦43′9.07″N; longitude: 127◦54′6.72″E). 
The experiments were conducted on three different apple training sys­
tems: the multi-axis apple training system (MAAS) for three-year-old 
Fuji/M9 trees on a single tree row, the vertical-axis apple training sys­
tem (VAAS) for eight-year-old Fuji/M9 trees on eight tree rows, and the 
vertical-wall apple training system (VWAS) for eight-year-old Fuji/M9 
trees on four tree rows, as shown in Fig. 1. Such apple training systems 
are used for growing apple and are crucial to the success of automation 
in orchard production (He and Schupp, 2018). The VAAS field has been 
designed for the traditional narrow pyramidal type of apple tree with a 
dominant central leader to maximize light penetration within the tree 
canopy. The MAAS and VWAS fields have 2D planar tree prototypes 
based on physiological considerations and can achieve lower costs and 
better vigor control for high-density plantings. Every row in each system 
is approximately 30 m long, and the interval between two neighboring 
rows is 4 m. The distance between adjacent trees within each tree row is 
2 m for all the training systems. 
Three Sony α5100 cameras (Sony Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with a 
24-mega-pixel (6,000 × 4,000 pixels) CMOS APS-C sensor (23.5 × 15.6 
mm) and a 20-mm f/2.8 lens were mounted on a DJI S1000 drone (DJI 
Sciences and Technologies Ltd., Shenzhen, China) to capture images of 
the apple trees (Fig. 2a). The central camera (C2) pointed vertically 
down, while the other two cameras (C1 and C3) pointed symmetrically 
in two directions with a tilt angle of 45◦with respect to the vertically 
downward direction. The drone flight height was set to 10 m so that the 
ground sampled distance (GSD) of the central camera was approxi­
mately 2 mm to reconstruct a high-resolution 3D model. The ground 
control points (GCPs) were evenly distributed in the experimental field 
(Fig. 2b), while the checkpoints (CPs) were distributed around the field 
in the center and edge of each row. The global positioning system values 
were measured using an MPR-2000 device (MBC & SYNEREX, Seoul, 
Republic of Korea) and converted into EPSG: 5186 (Korea 2000/Central 
Belt 2010) coordinates from WGS 84 coordinates via Gaussian projec­
tion calculation. To ensure enough overlap between adjacent images 
and improve the triangulation precision, both forward overlap and side 
overlap were set to 80%. During the drone flight, the travel speed was 
approximately 1.5 ms−1, and the trigger from the DJI drone control 
board was enabled to send signals to the three cameras and ensure that 
the cameras capture images at the same time with a frequency of 1 frame 
per second. The drone flight followed the double-grid type path (Fig. 2c) 
so that the camera configuration could capture more details and 
different perspectives of the apple trees. In this manner, occlusion issues 
were greatly reduced, which subsequently improved the level of 
completeness of the 3D model. The drone scanned 24 lines in the east­
–west direction and 20 lines in the north–south direction; thus, there 
were 75 and 120 images collected from all three cameras for each flight 
line in the two directions, respectively. Image acquisition was conducted 
before apple harvesting on October 12, 2020, in the entire experimental 
field, and the apples were almost mature and red in color. The leaves 
surrounding the apple fruits were partially removed by 5–10% to 
facilitate higher illumination exposure, thereby accelerating the matu­
ration processes and promoting the development of a vibrant red color 
in the apples (Musacchi and Serra, 2018). 
The classic multi-view structure from motion (SFM) algorithm based 
on bundle adjustment was used to reconstruct the 3D model from the 
multi-view 2D images. Agisoft Metashape Professional ver. 1.8.4 (Geo­
scan. Inc., Moscow, Russia) was used for 3D modeling. The upper limit of 
the feature points and the matching points for each image was set to be 
40,000 and 4,000, respectively, for the image alignment operation. 
Then, the GCPs were marked in the images to transform the original 
coordinate to the target coordinate and used as hyperparameters to 
calibrate the scale of the generated 3D sparse point cloud. For building a 
dense point cloud, a moderate depth filter was used to reduce the like­
lihood of incorrectly eliminating important features categorized as

The trees in the VAAS were exclusively used for training due to the 
greater complexity of the VAAS structure compared to the MAAS and 
VWAS, encompassing the structural characteristics of the MASS and 
VWAS. For training purposes, a total of 12 trees from the VAAS were 
selected, with one tree chosen from each of the eight rows, along with 
four trees exhibiting unique structural characteristics. For testing, all six 
trees in a single row from the MAAS were selected. Within the VAAS, a 
total of 16 trees were chosen, with two trees selected from each of the 
eight rows. As for the VWAS, a total of 14 trees were selected, including 
three trees from each of four rows and two trees showcasing unique 
structural characteristics. In terms of fruit recognition, all the fruits from 
the training and testing trees were used for their respective training and 
testing phases. To establish the ground truth for apple counting and 
volume estimation, the number of apples was manually counted in the 
orchard, and the volume of each apple was measured using the water 
displacement method. 
2.2. Apple trait extraction pipeline 
The workflow for obtaining apple traits is shown in Fig. 5. The 2D 
images captured by the drone-based multi-camera system were used to 
make a 3D model of the entire orchard, including the VAAS, VWAS, and 
MAAS fields. Individual apples could be extracted by the 3D instance- 
segmentation deep-learning algorithm using a part of the 3D trees in 
the VAAS field as the training dataset. After the 3D individual apples 
were extracted, the apple trait extraction pipeline was developed to 
obtain the number, volume, and spatial distribution of apples.

A U-Net-based network architecture was used in this study. The

by SparseConv Transpose with the same hyperparameters.

2.3.2. Weighted discriminative loss function 
The discriminative loss function for deep metric learning replaces the 
pixel-wise softmax, which is normally used in instance segmentation for 
2D images (De Brabandere et al., 2017). This loss function enables the 
network to transform any pixel in the image into n-dimensional vectors 
in the feature space such that pixel vectors belonging to the same 
instance are tightly abutted, while pixel vectors of various instances are 
separated. The discriminative loss consists of variance and distance 
losses. The variance loss is an intra-class tension that pulls the embed­
dings of pixels of the same instances toward the mean of the corre­
sponding instance embeddings. The distance loss is an inter-instance 
thrust that pushes the embedding centers of different instances far from 
each other. The loss function of the 3D instance segmentation in this 
study is inspired by the above discriminative loss function.

different embedded points in the different-size instances were made 
different. When φ = 0, wi = 0, and Eq. (5) is equivalent to that in other 
studies (De Brabandere et al., 2017; Lahoud et al., 2019). In this con­
dition, the pull force is equally operated for every instance and fails to 
cohere the embedded points of the same instance with more points. This 
is suitable for instance segmentation of the 2D image domain. Compared 
with the 2D image, the 3D point cloud has one more scale and actual size 
in the physical world. Therefore, in this study, φ was set to 1, in which 
the variance loss is equally operated to every embedded point, and the 
larger-size instances were performed by a more powerful pull force. 
2.3.3. Clustering in the embedding space 
After applying the weighted loss function in the embedding space, 
the points from the same instance come close to the instance center at μi 
and are distributed within a 3D sphere of radius δv. The distance be­
tween the two centers of the different instances is equal to or larger than 
2δd. Intuitively, the resulting phenomenon in the embedding space 
displays indefinite-quantity clusters with varying densities based on the 
size of the instance. In short, one cluster in the embedding space rep­

resents one object in the physical world. Thus, a clustering algorithm 
should be used to segment an individual cluster. Owing to the under­
mined number of clusters in the embedding space, clustering algorithms 
such as k-means are not suitable. Thus, the 3D Hierarchical Density- 
Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN) (Cam­
pello et al., 2013) clustering algorithm was selected in this study for 
processing the varying density clusters efficiently and robustly, and 
there were no restrictions on the input for the fixed number of clusters. 
Several parameters of the 3D HDBSCAN clustering algorithm were 
applied to assign the embedded points of the same instance to the same 
cluster. The primary parameter that affects the resulting clustering is the 
minimum number of embedded points in each cluster, which determines 
the smallest size grouping. As this parameter increases, the number of 
clusters decreases. The minimum number of embedded points in each 
cluster was set to 10 in this study because we were interested in small- 
sized apples in the entire apple tree to avoid miss detection. Another 
parameter that affects the resulting clustering is the distance threshold 
for merging the clusters whose distances are below this threshold. This 
parameter was set to 3 cm because of the occlusion caused by leaves, 
even though the average size of the apples is approximately 9 cm. There 
were three types of clusters for the output of the clustering algorithm: 
individual apple clusters, a background cluster, and a noisy cluster. The 
noisy cluster is sparse and can be removed using a density filter. The 
background cluster includes the 3D point cloud of apple trees and the 
ground so that there is a significant difference in size between individual 
apple clusters. Finally, the individual apple clusters can be extracted 
using a size filter, and the points in each apple cluster are mapped to the 
3D points in the original 3D point cloud.

can be adjusted to an appropriate value. For segmentation of instances

segmentation with different scale instances in target point cloud, φ

4. Discussions 
Due to the unstructured nature of orchard environments, apples are 
often partially occluded from a single view, making multi-view imaging 
crucial for improving apple detectability. By leveraging 2D computer 
vision technology, single-frame-based apple recognition has achieved an 
impressive accuracy of approximately 94% (Chu et al., 2023) and 96% 
(Li et al., 2022). However, this approach is not suitable for apple 
counting and yield estimation across the entire orchard, as it fails to 
establish connections between apples in different frames, leading to 
multiple counting. To tackle this challenge, researchers have developed 
tracking algorithms to identify and track the same apple across 
consecutive frames (Villacr´es et al., 2023). Unfortunately, this approach 
resulted in a counting error of 20.1%. Additionally, the adoption of a 
multi-view imaging method, where 2D detected apples are projected 
onto a 3D point cloud, has been employed to mitigate the issue of 
double-counting. However, a certain degree of multi-detection still 
persists. Gongal et al. (2016) and Gen´e-Mola et al. (2020b) reported 
multi-detection rates of 21.1% and 10.6%, respectively. In our study, we 
utilized 3D reconstruction technology to digitize the orchard, enabling 
us to assign unique and comprehensive structural features to each apple. 
This approach facilitated more accurate counting while effectively 
eliminating the multi-detection problem. 
Previous studies in 3D point cloud processing have primarily focused 
on reconstructing apple trees using LiDAR and applying machine 
learning methods for apple recognition. However, these approaches 
have limitations, including the absence of color information and low 
point cloud resolution, resulting in relatively low recognition perfor­
mance of approximately 70.5% (Tsoulias et al., 2020) and 82.4% (Gen´e- 
Mola et al., 2019). Moreover, only a small portion of the raw apple

In our study, we developed a novel 3D instance segmentation algorithm 
that leveraged both color and geometry features. In comparison to 
existing 3D instance segmentation algorithms (De Brabandere et al., 
2017; Lahoud et al., 2019), our proposed algorithm incorporated a 
weighted coefficient in the loss function, resulting in significant im­
provements in object recognition at the target scale. We achieved an 
average F1-score of 0.906, which is much higher than the reproduction 
results (F1-score of 0.497) of Lahoud et al. (2019) using our same 
dataset. However, our algorithm has limitations, particularly in 
handling occlusion. Despite achieving an impressive apple recognition 
rate using oblique photogrammetry technology, recognition perfor­
mance was negatively affected by severe occlusion in the VAAS field. To 
address this, future research can explore the addition of more views, 
such as collecting data through the combination of drones and ground 
vehicles, to minimize tree leaf shading and obtain more accurate and 
robust 3D models. 
In addition to apple counting, this study also evaluated the estima­
tion of apple volume. To improve the accuracy of volume estimation, the 
original apple point cloud was incorporated into a traditional sphere- 
fitting algorithm, resulting in a generated apple model that closely 
resembled the original apple shape. This integration led to a significant 
improvement in accuracy compared to the original sphere fitting algo­
rithm. However, it should be noted that in cases where the recognized 
apple point cloud cluster is too small, the accuracy of the proposed 
method may approach that of the traditional sphere fitting algorithm 
due to leaf and branch shading. In the future, the utilization of big data 
and generative adversarial network techniques (Huang et al., 2020) 
could be considered to generate the obscured parts of the apple, 
enabling the creation of a complete apple model and achieving higher 
accuracy in volume estimation.

encompassed essential phenotypic traits, such as fruit quantity, volume, 
and 3D geographic position. This yield map not only provided direct 
visualization but also proved highly valuable for analyzing the distri­
bution of fruit yield. Furthermore, the map could be generated at various 
fruit growth stages, enabling growers to make informed decisions to 
enhance fruit yield and quality. Moreover, the map could serve as a 
valuable tool for identifying fruit growth anomalies resulting from pests, 
nutrient deficiencies, and drought, which can be recognized more 
effectively through the integration of RGB, multispectral, and thermal 
cameras. 
The experiments were conducted under standardized orchard con­
ditions, where trees were planted on a relatively flat surface with 
consistent and adequate spacing to facilitate the use of a drone for 
capturing individual trees. However, in cases where trees are positioned 
on sloping areas with irregular spacing, specific adjustments to the 
experimental parameters such as camera oblique angle, flight height, 
and flight pathway are necessary based on the unique orchard condi­
tions. The distribution of light within trees can vary depending on the 
position of sun and weather conditions. Therefore, it is important to 
evaluate the performance of the 3D reconstruction and tree trait analysis 
systems at different times of the day and under various weather condi­
tions, including sunny, cloudy, and windy conditions. 
When developing the model for apple counting and volume estima­
tion, we exclusively utilized the VAAS for training due to its widespread 
adoption in real apple orchards and its incorporation of the 2D structural 
characteristics found in the MAAS and VAWS. Due to the recent devel­
opment of the MAAS and VAWS tree systems, the number of available 
trees in the experimental orchard was limited. Although training the 
model solely on the VAAS may introduce some prediction bias, it was 
not deemed critical. Nevertheless, for future improvements, it is 
important to incorporate diverse training systems and ensure the use of

5. Conclusions 
A high-quality 3D model of a large-scale apple orchard was recon­
structed using a drone and a multi-camera platform. Highly accurate 
apple instance segmentation was achieved using the proposed 3D deep 
learning algorithm. The algorithm included the generalized sparse 
convolutional neural network, weighted loss function, and 3D 
HDBSCAN clustering, and proved to be robust for individual apple 
extraction from different types of apple training systems. Moreover, the 
apple trait extraction algorithm could recover the apple shape and 
automatically compute apple traits such as apple number, position, and 
volume. Therefore, 2D and 3D spatial distribution mapping of the apples 
was obtained. Such maps not only intuitively show the distribution and 
spatial variability of apple traits but also help analyze statistics of apple 
traits in each region, which will be helpful for precise yield analysis and 
robot farming operations.
