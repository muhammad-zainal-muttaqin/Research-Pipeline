A B S T R A C T
Existing tasks such as fruit growth monitoring, harvesting, and quality sorting still suffer from low precision and 
insufficient automation. 3D reconstruction technology can accurately capture the external characteristics of 
fruits and shows great potential for enhancing automated fruit detection and processing. This paper, guided by 
two core application needs in the fruit industry: real-time online sensing and offline high-precision analysis, 
provides a detailed overview of research progress in 3D reconstruction technology for fruits. It first introduces 
the principles, workflows, and advantages and limitations of classical 3D reconstruction methods. Then, it fo­
cuses on the basic framework of learning-based 3D reconstruction approaches, their improvement directions, and 
their applications in fruits and other agricultural products. In addition, the challenges encountered in fruit 3D 
reconstruction, such as occlusion and complex lighting conditions, are summarized, along with potential solu­
tions. Finally, future research directions are discussed. This review serves as a valuable reference for promoting 
the integration of computer vision and agricultural intelligence and advancing the fruit industry chain’s digital 
and intelligent transformation.
1. Introduction
As the global population grows and consumption levels rise, con­
sumers’ demand for fruit quality has steadily increased (Harker et al., 
2003). Fruit growth monitoring (An et al., 2022), automated harvesting 
(Li et al., 2011), quality assessment (Pathmanaban et al., 2019), sorting, 
and packaging (Dewi et al., 2020) are critical to enhancing fruit quality 
and have become integral components of the agricultural production 
process. Information on the external characteristics of fruits, such as 
shape and color, serves as a key indicator in these processes (Zhang 
et al., 2014a) and is essential for ensuring the quality of fruit processing 
and the efficiency of subsequent procedures. However, traditional 
manual inspection methods are not only time-consuming and labor- 
intensive (Zhang et al., 2014a) but also prone to subjectivity and 
inconsistency. In this context, the introduction of machine vision tech­
nology provides an efficient, objective, and accurate solution for 
monitoring the external characteristics of fruits.
In recent years, driven by advancements in artificial intelligence, 
machine vision techniques based on 2D images have been extensively 
studied and implemented in various tasks, including fruit classification 
(Hossain et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2014b), identification and 
localization (Zhang et al., 2023a), ripeness assessment (Kaur et al., 
2018), defect and disease detection (Xu et al., 2024a), size estimation 
(Ponce et al., 2018), and yield prediction (Palacios et al., 2023). How­
ever, due to the lack of depth information, 2D images cannot fully 
describe the 3D morphology of fruits, resulting in limited accuracy for 
fruit localization, external geometric feature estimation, and surface 
defect area calculation (Ma et al., 2021).
Three-dimensional reconstruction techniques offer promising solu­
tions to address this issue. By capturing the 3D morphological infor­
mation of fruits, 3D reconstruction can provide more accurate and 
detailed data for evaluating the external characteristics of fruits, thereby 
significantly enhancing detection accuracy (Hong et al., 2024; Wang & 
Chen, 2020). However, when applying 3D reconstruction technology to 
the fruit industry, it is essential to recognize that different application 
scenarios impose markedly different requirements on performance 
metrics such as speed, accuracy, and cost. Broadly, these applications 
can be classified into two categories: (1) Real-time, online perception: 
Represented by robotic automation tasks such as automated harvesting 
(Font et al., 2014; Stavridis et al., 2024), this category prioritizes high 
real-time performance and strong robustness, enabling rapid target 
localization in dynamic, unstructured orchard environments. Absolute

sufficient information about target position, orientation, and approxi­

measurement of fine features such as volume, surface area, and curva­

applications fall between these two extremes, such as online fruit 
grading, which requires both high reconstruction accuracy and strong 
real-time performance.
Classical 3D reconstruction methods provide the foundation to meet 
these two demands. Among them, active methods (such as structured 
light(Yu et al., 2024) and Time-of-Flight) are primarily employed for 
real-time online perception tasks due to their rapid depth acquisition 
capabilities. Many commercial RGB-D cameras, such as the Realscene 
and Kinect series, are based on active methods and are widely used in 
automated harvesting tasks. In passive methods, stereo vision technol­
ogy offers fast processing speeds and can also be applied to real-time 
online perception. Multi-view Stereo (MVS), on the other hand, re­
quires precise matching of multiple images, forming the technical basis 
for offline high-precision analysis. These methods typically output point 
clouds, which often contain noise and occlusions, necessitating a series 
of post-processing steps, such as registration, segmentation, denoising, 
and completion, to provide a solid foundation for subsequent high- 
precision surface reconstruction.
In recent years, the rise of deep learning has brought revolutionary 
changes to 3D reconstruction (Wang et al., 2024a), and it also shows the 
development trend of serving the above two application directions. On 
one hand, methods based on single-view images have emerged as a 
promising direction for real-time online perception due to their fast 
inference speeds. These methods take a single image as input and can 
generate depth maps, voxels, point clouds, or mesh models in an end-to- 
end manner. However, due to viewpoint limitations, they typically offer 
limited accuracy. On the other hand, methods based on multi-view 
images, such as depth map-based approaches, Neural Radiance Fields 
(NeRF), and 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS), have significantly improved 
reconstruction fidelity, making them powerful tools for offline high- 
precision analysis. But they often come with high computational costs. 
Enhancing model efficiency, reconstruction accuracy, and generaliza­
tion remains a key focus of current research.
Despite the significant potential of 3D reconstruction in the fruit 
domain, its practical application is still hampered by considerable 
challenges, notably occlusion and complex illumination. To address 
these issues, many studies have proposed optimization strategies to 
improve the performance of 3D reconstruction.
Although existing reviews have systematically introduced the prog­
ress of traditional 3D reconstruction techniques in fruit and other agri­
cultural applications, a structured overview of emerging deep learning- 
driven reconstruction paradigms remains lacking. Moreover, few studies 
systematically analyze the suitability of various technical approaches 
from the application requirements, or summarized the specific chal­
lenges and corresponding solutions for fruit reconstruction scenarios. 
Therefore, guided by the two core application demands in the fruit in­
dustry (real-time online perception and offline high-precision analysis), 
this paper aims to provide a comprehensive and structured review of the 
principles, advantages, limitations, and technical challenges of 3D 
reconstruction technologies, along with their corresponding solutions. 
Section II introduces classical 3D reconstruction methods. Section III 
presents learning-based 3D reconstruction approaches. Section IV dis­
cusses the challenges encountered in fruit 3D reco‘nstruction and the 
corresponding solutions. By comprehensively summarizing recent 
technological advances, this review aims to foster the integration of 3D 
reconstruction technologies with agricultural intelligence and support 
the transition of modern agriculture from an experience-driven to a 
data-driven paradigm.
2. Classical 3D reconstruction methods
Classical 3D reconstruction methods comprise three main stages: 3D 
information acquisition, post-processing, and surface reconstruction. 
Initially, 3D information is acquired, typically represented as a point 
cloud. Subsequently, the data undergoes post-processing to remove 
noise, repair missing regions, and enhance the quality of the point cloud. 
This is followed by surface reconstruction to generate a high-precision 
3D model of the fruit.
2.1. 3D information acquisition
2.1.1. Active methods
Active methods refer to techniques that actively emit light signals (e. 
g., laser or structured light) and compute depth information by receiving 
the reflected or scattered signals from object surfaces. These methods 
are robust to ambient lighting conditions. Among them, laser triangu­
lation, known for its high precision, is primarily used for offline high- 
accuracy analysis. Time-of-Flight (ToF) cameras, characterized by 
their high frame rates, are preferred for real-time online perception. 
Structured light offers greater versatility and, depending on its imple­
mentation (e.g., coded patterns or speckle), is widely applied across both 
paradigms.
(1) Laser triangulation
Laser triangulation is a non-contact ranging technique based on the 
triangulation principle (Ehlert et al., 2008). By accurately controlling 
the position and angle between the laser light source and the optical 
sensor, the depth information of an object is estimated using triangular 
geometric relationships, enabling the reconstruction of the object’s 
three-dimensional shape (Zhao et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 1, based 
on the relative positioning of the light source and the sensor, laser 
triangulation can be classified into two types: the Direct Type (Ehlert 
et al., 2008) and the Oblique Type (Ding et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). In 
the Direct Type, the laser beam is projected vertically onto the object’s 
surface to be measured, where it is scattered and subsequently captured 
by the sensor (Schlarp et al., 2020). This method is suitable for surfaces 
with good scattering. The Oblique Type projects the laser beam at an 
inclined angle onto the surface, allowing effective measurement even on 
smooth object surfaces (Ding et al., 2023).
Laser triangulation typically operates in single-point or single-line 
scanning mode. To obtain a complete 3D model of an object, the scan­
ning head must be moved by a robotic arm(Alontseva et al., 2020; Arko 
& Jezerˇsek, 2022) or the object placed on a conveyor belt(Leite et al., 
2024), performing point-by-point or line-by-line scanning and stitching. 
This process is time-consuming and cannot meet real-time requirements, 
but its accuracy can often reach the micrometer level. Therefore, it is 
classified as an offline high-precision analysis technique. Its primary 
application is not large-scale deployment, but rather the acquisition of 
“Ground Truth” 3D models in research settings for use as benchmark 
references(Magistri et al., 2025). Li et al. (2023) developed a binocular 
camera-based line laser scanning system for estimating the number and 
volume of Carya cathayensis Sarg, operating in the Direct Type mode to 
accommodate the rough and uneven surfaces of the nuts, achieving a 
volumetric error of only 0.6 %. Uyar & Erdogdu (2009) employed a laser 
scanner to obtain 3D models of pears, strawberries, bananas, apples, and 
eggs to simulate food processing operations. Laser triangulation 
methods offer high accuracy and resolution and are relatively insensitive 
to ambient lighting conditions. However, they are typically limited in 
measurement range and require precise system calibration.
(2) Time of flight.
Time-of-flight (ToF) is a ranging technique widely used in devices 
such as ToF cameras (Yu et al., 2020), LiDAR (Li et al., 2022a), and laser 
rangefinders (Sedek et al., 2024). Its principle involves emitting a 
modulated light signal (typically near-infrared (Park et al., 2011)) from

a transmitter, which is then reflected off the surface of an object and 
received by a sensor. By measuring the time delay between emission and 
reception and combining it with the speed of light in the given medium, 
the distance from the camera to the object surface can be determined, 
thereby enabling reconstruction of the object’s three-dimensional shape 
(Lange & Seitz, 2001). As shown in Fig. 2, the technique comprises two 
primary methods: pulsed modulation and continuous wave modulation 
(Foix et al., 2011; Horaud et al., 2016). Pulsed modulation enables the 
direct measurement of the time difference between light pulses. Because 
of its high irradiated power relative to ambient light, it operates reliably 
outdoors and under harsh conditions. This method supports long- 
distance measurements, ranging from a few meters to several kilome­
ters (Horaud et al., 2016). In contrast, continuous wave modulation 
estimates the time difference indirectly by measuring the phase shift 
between transmitted and received signals. This approach is typically 
used for short-distance measurements in indoor settings (Horaud et al., 
2016).
Since all pixels in the sensor can simultaneously measure depth in 
their respective directions, ToF-based systems offer strong real-time 
performance, easily exceeding 30 fps(Cai et al., 2024). As a result, 
ToF-based RGB-D cameras (e.g., Kinect v2 (Gong et al., 2022; Liu et al., 
2022; Parr et al., 2022), Kinect Azure (Neupane et al., 2021; Parr et al., 
2022), Blaze 101 (Neupane et al., 2021)) and LiDAR systems (e.g., 
RealSense L515 (Burusa et al., 2024; Menon et al., 2023; Xu et al., 
2024b)) have become the preferred technologies for real-time online 
sensing tasks(Shamshiri et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024a). Researchers 
have employed these devices to reconstruct the 3D structure of fruits 
such as apples effectively (Yamamoto et al., 2018), pears (Wang & Chen, 
2020), and grapes (Liu et al., 2022; Parr et al., 2022). Neupane et al. 
(2021) compared the performance of eight depth-sensing cameras under 
indoor, outdoor diffuse sunlight, and outdoor bright light conditions for 
mango fruit localization and sizing. Their results indicated that ToF 
cameras (Blaze 101, Azure Kinect) performed best and maintained 
reliable accuracy across all tested environments. Despite their superior 
ranging capabilities, ToF cameras typically suffer from systematic errors 
(Dashpute et al., 2019) and low pixel resolution (Yu et al., 2020). 
Moreover, in multi-device setups, signal interference can occur 
(Sarbolandi et al., 2015). This issue can be mitigated by assigning 
different modulation frequencies to each camera (Horaud et al., 2016).
(3) Structured light.
The structured light method is a 3D reconstruction technique that 
calculates depth information by projecting a specific pattern onto an 
object’s surface and analyzing the pattern’s deformation (Geng, 2011). 
Structured light methods can be categorized into coded and speckle- 
based approaches (Feng et al., 2021; Yang & Gu, 2024). In coded 
structured light, system calibration is critical to establish the mapping 
between the world coordinate system and the image plane coordinates 
of the camera or projector (Zhao et al., 2024a), ensuring reconstruction 
accuracy. Common configurations include “single projector + single 
camera” and “single projector + dual cameras” (Fig. 3). This method 
involves projecting a sequence of temporally encoded patterns onto the 
scene, with the camera synchronously capturing the corresponding 
image sequence (Lu et al., 2024). The process typically takes several 
hundred milliseconds to a few seconds, limiting real-time performance. 
However, because each pixel carries a unique temporal code, matching 
is highly accurate, enabling high-precision reconstructions suitable for 
offline analysis. In contrast, speckle structured light does not rely on 
encoded information. Instead, it projects pseudo-random dot patterns to 
introduce rich textures to low-texture surfaces, improving matching 
performance. This method is typically paired with infrared stereo

used in consumer-grade depth cameras (e.g., Kinect v1) (Pezzuolo et al.,

In fruit reconstruction, Lu & Lu (2018) developed a monocular

issue of fruit peduncle calyx being easily confused with surface defects 
during quality grading, thereby improving sorting accuracy. When 
tested on a reference block, the system achieved a relative error of less

Initially, the surface of the apples was reconstructed to extract height 
features, which were subsequently modeled using LS-SVM and PLS 
models. The results indicated that the LS-SVM model was more accurate, 
with correlation coefficients of 0.90 and 0.86 for volume and weight 
prediction, respectively, and RMSEs of 10.12 and 9.96. Other scholars 
have used devices based on the structured light principle for effective 3D 
reconstruction of fruits such as apples (Yamamoto et al., 2018), grapes

challenge of feature matching on weakly textured surfaces, which 
traditional stereo matching methods struggle with, by actively projec­
ting distinctive patterns (Salvi et al., 2010; Zhang, 2018). However, it is 
sensitive to ambient light, and depth cameras based on this principle (e. 
g., Kinect V1) may not perform effectively under intense outdoor 
lighting conditions (Parr et al., 2022).
2.1.2. Passive methods
Passive methods refer to 3D reconstruction techniques that do not 
emit light signals but instead rely on natural or ambient lighting con­
ditions. Common approaches include shape from silhouette, stereo 
vision, and multi-view vision. Shape from silhouette requires specific 
viewpoints and sufficient view coverage, making it suitable for online 
fruit grading. Stereo vision offers strong real-time performance and 
flexibility, making it well-suited for real-time online sensing tasks. 
Multi-view vision, while more time-consuming, delivers higher accuracy 
and is a classical method for offline high-precision analysis.
(1) Shape from silhouette.
Shape from silhouette is a method used to recover the 3D shape of an 
object by utilizing silhouette information from different viewpoints 
(Díaz-M´as et al., 2012). The fundamental principle involves extracting 
silhouette information through multi-view image segmentation and 
back-projecting these silhouettes into 3D space. The contours and their 
corresponding optical centers form visual cones, and the intersection of 
all visual cones constitutes the approximate 3D shape of the object, i.e., 
the Visual Hull (Haro, 2012). Compared to multiview stereo matching, 
this method is less accurate, incapable of reconstructing surface details, 
and can only provide an approximate 3D shape (Haro, 2012). However, 
its accuracy improves with increasing views (Cheung et al., 2003; Díaz- 
M´as et al., 2010; Jadhav et al., 2019; Laurentini, 1994). Additionally, 
this method cannot reconstruct depressed areas (Feldmann & Tabb, 
2021; Imou et al., 2006), resulting in poor general applicability, and is 
primarily used for online fruit grading tasks. Jadhav et al. (2019)
employed a shape-from-silhouette method based on voxel sets for the 3D 
reconstruction of fruits such as apples, mangoes, oranges, pomegranates, 
and strawberries, to estimate fruit volume and classify the fruits ac­
cording to their maturity. The accuracy of volume estimation exceeded 
98 %. Feldmann & Tabb (2021) utilized the Shape from Inconsistent 
Silhouette (SfIS) method (Tabb, 2013) to reconstruct 3D models of 
grapes, pears, potatoes, and peppers. This method can reconstruct ob­
jects even with segmentation and camera calibration errors.
(2) Stereo vision
Stereo Vision is a 3D reconstruction technique that emulates the 
mechanism of human binocular vision (Jin et al., 2010). As illustrated in 
Fig. 4, the core concept involves obtaining the 3D shape of an object by 
matching corresponding points in images from different viewpoints to 
compute parallax, and then applying triangulation, using the camera’s 
focal length and baseline distance to calculate depth information (Wang 
et al., 2015). It typically involves the following key steps: camera cali­
bration, image acquisition, image correction, feature detection and 
matching, parallax calculation, and depth computation. Traditional 
stereo matching algorithms can be categorized into local matching

(Xiang & Wang, 2023), based on different matching strategies. The local 
matching method is computationally simple and offers high real-time

matching method provides high accuracy, but comes with high 
computational demands and relatively poor real-time performance 
(Wang et al., 2015). The semi-global matching method combines the

while maintaining high accuracy. It is suitable for scenarios requiring a 
balance between accuracy and real-time performance. SGBM is a widely 
used semi-global matching method (Hirschmuller, 2008). Binocular 
stereo vision is extensively used in various fields, such as depth cameras 
(e.g., Zed series, certain RealSense series), autonomous driving (Jin

In the fruit industry, stereo vision is primarily used for real-time 
online sensing tasks(Jing et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025). Li et al. (2022b)
and Gao et al. (2024) respectively employed traditional methods and 
deep learning-based stereo matching techniques for the 3D reconstruc­
tion of oranges, which are used for fruit localization and quality sorting. 
However, stereo matching faces several challenges, such as recon­
structing weakly textured surfaces and transparent objects (Tian et al., 
2022). Moreover, issues like surface overexposure, lighting in­
consistencies due to viewpoint differences, and occlusion (Hu & Mor­
dohai, 2012) can all lead to difficulties in stereo matching.
(3) Multi-view vision
Multi-View Vision is a 3D reconstruction technique that generates a 
point cloud by capturing 2D images of an object from multiple view­
points and matching them (Gao et al., 2022). By leveraging information 
from a large number of different viewpoints and applying global opti­
mization, this method can produce highly dense, complete, and detail- 
rich 3D point clouds. It is a classic approach for acquiring fine geo­
metric information and forms the technical foundation for offline high- 
precision analysis. To achieve effective feature detection and matching, 
overlapping regions between views are essential (Kucak, 2020). Image 
acquisition can typically be performed in one of three ways: fixing the 
object while moving the camera (M´endez-Barroso et al., 2018), fixing 
the camera while rotating the object (Ma et al., 2021; Masoudi et al., 
2024), or using multiple synchronized cameras. In general, increasing 
the number of viewpoints leads to greater coverage and reconstruction 
accuracy of the model. Multi-view vision techniques are primarily 
divided into two stages: Structure from Motion (SfM) and Multi-View 
Stereo (MVS) (Meinen & Robinson, 2020), corresponding to sparse 
and dense reconstruction processes, respectively. Due to the scale un­
certainty in reconstruction results, introducing reference objects of 
known size into the scene is often necessary for scale calibration (Wu 
et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020). Multi-View Vision is cost-effective and can 
generate high-precision 3D models with fine details, making it widely 
used in fields such as mapping (M´endez-Barroso et al., 2018), heritage 
conservation (Kucak, 2020), and agriculture (Dong et al., 2023; Ni et al., 
2021).
In the context of fruit reconstruction, the ’SfM + MVS’ method has 
been successfully applied to the phenotyping and quality inspection of 
strawberries (He et al., 2017; Kochi et al., 2018), apples (Dong et al., 
2023; Ma et al., 2021), tomatoes (Masoudi et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024), 
and blueberries (Ni et al., 2021). For example, Ma et al. (2021) designed 
a dual-camera, roller-based apple sorting device that initially used the 
Accelerated-KAZE algorithm for feature detection and matching to 
reconstruct a sparse point cloud of the apple. This was followed by dense 
reconstruction using the Patch-based Multi-View Stereo (PMVS) 
method. The point clouds obtained from the two cameras were then 
registered using the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm. The esti­
mation errors for apple volume, height, fruit shape index, maximum 
diameter, and minimum diameter were 8.73 cm3, 1.43 mm, 1.28 %, 
0.90 mm, and 1.23 mm, respectively. Multi-View Stereo offers high 
accuracy and broad applicability; however, because it requires pro­
cessing a large volume of image data, it incurs high computational costs 
and long processing times (Zhou et al., 2021). Consequently, it is un­
suitable for dynamic objects or scenarios requiring real-time 
performance.
2.2. 3D information processing and fruit model reconstruction
2.2.1. Point cloud preprocessing
Point cloud processing is a pivotal step in 3D reconstruction. Com­
mon point cloud processing methods include registration, segmentation, 
filtering, completion, and downsampling (Fig. 5).
(1) Registration
Point cloud registration aims to calculate the optimal transformation 
matrix to register point clouds from different coordinate systems into a 
standard coordinate system, thereby generating a more complete point 
cloud model (Yang et al., 2020). Depending on the registration sequence 
and initial conditions, registration can be classified into coarse and fine 
registration (Bueno, 2017). Coarse registration can be achieved by 
classical algorithms (e.g., 4PCS (Aiger et al., 2008), RANSAC (Ma et al., 
2021), and SAC-IA (Guo et al., 2020) or manually, using predefined 
marker points or surface texture features (Xie et al., 2021). In the case of 
multi-view point clouds acquired by multiple fixed cameras, coarse 
registration can also be performed by deriving the transformation matrix 
via camera calibration (Hong et al., 2024). Fine registration further re­
fines the registration results, with commonly used methods including 
ICP (Besl & McKay, 1992) and Normal Distributions Transform (NDT) 
(Magnusson et al., 2007), among which ICP is the most widely used in 
fruit 3D reconstruction (Huang et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2023; Magistri 
et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2024).
Since traditional registration methods are sensitive to outliers and 
computationally expensive, numerous studies have increasingly adopted 
neural networks for point cloud registration, such as PPFNet (Deng et al., 
2018) and PREDATOR (Huang et al., 2021). Huang et al. (2021) pro­
posed PREDATOR, specifically designed for low-overlap scenarios, 
which encodes the point cloud’s geometric features and contextual in­
formation through an overlap attention module. It also introduces an 
overlap score to direct the model’s focus toward points relevant to 
registration, improving over 15 % in the registration success rate of low- 
overlap point clouds. The operational pipeline of these methods typi­
cally consists of four components: feature extraction, feature matching, 
outlier removal, and motion parameter estimation.
(2) Segmentation
Point cloud segmentation aims to isolate the target object from the 
background. Current methodologies can be broadly classified into four 
categories: RGB information-based, feature-based, model-fitting-based, 
and neural network-based methods. RGB information-based methods 
segment RGB images to extract regions of interest (ROIs), which are then 
used as masks on the point cloud (Hong et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024a; 
Menon et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024b). These methods are straightforward 
and yield high accuracy, making them widely used in fruit 3D recon­
struction. Feature-based methods perform segmentation based on dif­
ferences in features among points in the point cloud, such as point-to- 
point distance, curvature, and normal vector orientation. Common ap­
proaches include region-growing algorithms (Liu et al., 2022; Mack 
et al., 2017), Euclidean clustering (Guo et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023), and 
DBSCAN (Masoudi et al., 2024; Tsoulias et al., 2020). For example, 
Tsoulias et al. (2020) initially used DBSCAN to identify apple clusters 
and further refined the segmentation using features such as curvature 
and linearity. Liu et al. (2022) segmented grape point clouds using 
filtering and region-growing algorithms. Model-fitting-based methods 
employ predefined geometric models (e.g., planar, spherical, or cylin­
drical shapes) to fit the point cloud and extract regions that conform to 
the model. Standard techniques include RANSAC and the Hough 
Transform, with RANSAC being the most widely adopted in fruit 3D

(3) Filtering.
Outliers are points that deviate significantly from the prevailing data 
distribution, typically representing anomalous data generated during 
the acquisition process. Their elimination can be achieved by imple­
menting methodologies such as statistical outlier removal, radius outlier 
removal, or DBSCAN. Noise refers to stochastic errors or signal in­
terferences, typically manifested as minor deviations from the ground 
truth, and can be mitigated using techniques such as moving least 
squares, median filtering, and average filtering. Studies have demon­
strated that the moving least squares method can effectively denoise 
point clouds of apples (Yamamoto et al., 2018) and pears (Wang & Chen, 
2020). In recent years, several neural network-based approaches for 
point cloud denoising have been proposed, such as GPDNet (Pistilli 
et al., 2020) and RePCD-Net (Chen et al., 2022a).
(4) Completion
Point clouds are frequently incomplete owing to limitations of 
acquisition devices, reflections, and occlusion caused by viewing angles 
(Fei et al., 2022; Zhuang et al., 2024). Point cloud completion seeks to 
reconstruct missing regions and transform sparse, incomplete point 
clouds into complete 3D shapes. The primary approaches include geo­
metric model-based and neural network-based methods. Geometric 
model-based methods typically rely on the symmetry of the point cloud 
or specific geometric shape assumptions, and completion is performed 
through model fitting or utilizing symmetry features. Commonly 
employed geometric models include the sphere and superellipsoid, 
among others. In contrast, neural network-based methods reconstruct 
missing point cloud regions by learning both global and local object 
features. They are particularly effective in handling complex, irregular 
shapes and large missing regions (Zhuang et al., 2024). Notable network 
architectures include TopNet (Tchapmi et al., 2019), GRNet (Xie et al., 
2020b), and PoinTr (Yu et al., 2021a), among others.
(5) Downsampling.
Direct processing of dense point clouds typically involves significant 
computational costs and memory demands (Lyu et al., 2024), and the 
number of points does not always correlate positively with data quality 
(Wang et al., 2024c). Therefore, downsampling the point cloud is 
necessary to reduce its size while preserving as much geometric infor­
mation as possible, thus enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of sub­
sequent 
processing.

2.2.2. Surface reconstruction
Surface reconstruction aims to generate continuous 3D models from 
point cloud data for high-quality visualization of objects. You et al. 
(2020) classify surface reconstruction methods into approximation, 
interpolation, soft computing, and learning-based techniques. Surface 
reconstruction can be categorized into two types based on the form of 
representation: display-based and implicit representation (Lim & Haron, 
2014). In fruit 3D reconstruction, commonly methods include Poisson 
Surface Reconstruction (He et al., 2017; Wang & Chen, 2020; Ye et al., 
2024), Marching Cubes (Magistri et al., 2022), Delaunay triangulation 
(Ma et al., 2021), Alpha Shape Algorithm (Xu et al., 2024b), and Greedy 
Projection Triangulation (Xie et al., 2021). Poisson reconstruction is 
based on the implicit function, and Marching Cubes can convert implicit 
representations into explicit meshes. Poisson Surface reconstruction is 
the most widely used due to its robustness to noise, generating smooth 
and closed surfaces, and its strong performance in fruit surface recon­
struction tasks (Liu et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2021). Inherent defects in 
point cloud data, such as noise, outliers, misalignments, missing data, 
and inhomogeneous densities, present significant challenges for high- 
quality surface reconstruction (Huang et al., 2024b).

3.1. Single-view image based
3D reconstruction based on single-view images typically employs 
end-to-end networks to extract image features from extensive training 
datasets and subsequently reconstruct 3D models of scenes or objects. 
Owing to their fast inference speed, such methods are regarded as a 
highly promising direction for enabling real-time online perception. 
Early approaches primarily employed depth maps as outputs. However, 
these representations were less intuitive and inadequate for fully 
describing the shape of objects. As a result, subsequent studies have 
increasingly adopted richer 3D representations such as voxels, point 
clouds, and meshes.
3.1.1. Methods based on depth maps
Depth maps record the perpendicular distance from the 3D scene 
point corresponding to each pixel to the camera imaging plane, serving 
as a critical bridge between 2D visual perception and 3D geometric 
reconstruction. Monocular depth estimation aims to generate depth 
maps by inferring per-pixel depth values from a single RGB image. 
Current approaches are generally categorized into three types: super­
vised, self-supervised, and semi-supervised. Supervised learning 
methods rely on datasets with real depth annotations for training and 
optimize the model by minimizing the discrepancy between the pre­
dicted and ground-truth depth maps (as illustrated in Fig. 6). To address 
the issue of scale ambiguity in monocular depth estimation, Zeng et al. 
(2024) proposed WorDepth, which incorporates textual priors into 
image features to improve estimation accuracy. Although supervised 
methods have achieved promising results, acquiring accurate and 
comprehensive ground-truth depth data for network training remains 
challenging in real-world applications. In contrast, self-supervised 
methods require only image sequences during network training 
without the need for real depth annotations, making them increasingly 
popular in recent research. Classical self-supervised approaches include 
the image-pair training method proposed by Garg et al. (2016) and the 
continuous video frame training strategy introduced by Zhou et al. 
(2017). However, self-supervised monocular depth estimation (SSMDE) 
tends to perform poorly in complex scenarios such as reflective surfaces. 
To tackle this limitation, Shi et al. (2023) introduced the 3D Distillation 
framework, which generates accurate pseudo-labels for reflective sur­
faces by aggregating depth predictions across multiple frames, sub­
stantially enhancing model performance in reflective and complex 
environments. Despite reducing the dependency on ground-truth data, 
self-supervised methods generally lag behind supervised approaches in 
accuracy due to the absence of strong supervisory signals. To bridge this 
gap, semi-supervised methods have been proposed to combine the 
strengths of both paradigms by leveraging a small set of labeled data 
alongside a large volume of unlabeled data. For instance, Guizilini et al. 
(2020) proposed a semi-supervised training framework that integrates 
self-supervised photometric loss with reprojection distance loss, opti­
mizing the model based on a pre-trained self-supervised backbone. This 
approach significantly improves performance in scenarios with sparse 
depth annotations.
Methods based on depth maps have been widely used in agriculture, 
but since depth maps are not intuitive enough, it is usually necessary to 
convert depth maps into point clouds using camera intrinsic parameters 
and then perform subsequent processing and optimization. For instance, 
P´erez-Patricio et al. (2024) proposed a convolutional neural network 
trained via supervised learning for estimating the depth of strawberry 
seedlings, and then reconstructed 3D point clouds using a pinhole 
camera model to monitor plant stress. Similarly, Zhao et al. (2022)
employed the LeReS model to generate depth maps and reconstruct 3D 
point clouds based on RGB images of plants, enabling the estimation of 
phenotypic parameters.
In the fruit domain, depth map–based methods are widely used for 
volume estimation and fruit localization. For example, Graikos et al. 
(2020) employed Monodepth2, a self-supervised monocular depth esti­
mation network proposed by Godard et al. (2019), for food volume 
estimation. The network was pre-trained and fine-tuned with the EPIC- 
KITCHENS dataset and a custom video dataset of food products to 
enhance its performance in food scenes. However, this method un­
derestimates the volume of spherical fruits (e.g., apples and oranges) 
due to viewpoint limitations, as it can only calculate a portion of the 
total volume. To address this, Nair & Fernandez (2024) proposed 
capturing full-surface images of apples using a rotating platform and a 
Microsoft LifeCam camera. They employed the Global-Local Path 
Network (GLPN) for depth estimation, then converted the depth maps 
into point clouds and performed registration to obtain a complete apple 
point cloud for dynamic monitoring of the apple bruise area. However, 
this method is time-consuming, with an average depth estimation time 
of 52.46 s per image. To reduce estimation time, real-time monocular 
depth estimation methods have been proposed. Divyanth et al. (2023)
used the Pix2Pix model from Conditional GANs (cGAN) to generate 
depth maps from monocular RGB images, replacing traditional RGB-D 
sensors and providing 3D spatial information for orchard robots. The 
results showed an RMSE of 1.83 cm, MRE of 3.5 %, Structural Similarity 
Index Measure (SSIM) of 0.56, and Peak Signal to Noise Ratio (PSNR) of 
17.2 dB, with a processing time of only 0.494 s per image. Additionally, 
Balaji & Madhavaram (2024) compared the performance of the Dep­
thAnything model and depth sensors in grape picking localization, 
finding that depth maps directly obtained from sensors were more ac­
curate. Other studies have used monocular depth estimation to assist

3.1.2. Methods based on voxel
Depth maps cannot fully describe the 3D shape of an object and are 
often referred to as 2.5D representations. To describe the 3D information 
more accurately, some studies have proposed using voxel models as the 
output of networks. Voxel models are simple, intuitive, and easily 
extendable from 2D convolution to 3D, making them one of the earliest 
3D representations used in deep learning-based 3D reconstruction.
Voxel-based deep learning methods typically employ an encoder- 
decoder architecture, as shown in Fig. 7. A typical method is 3D-R2N2 
(Choy et al., 2016), where the network consists of CNN, 3D-LSTM, and 
3D-DCNN components. The CNN encodes images into low-dimensional 
features, 3D-LSTM updates the hidden state after new view inputs, 
and 3D-DCNN decodes the hidden state into a voxel model. This network 
can reconstruct 3D voxel models of objects from single-view or multi- 
view images. Other classic voxel-based methods include Pix2Vox (Xie 
et al., 2019) and Pix2Vox++ (Xie et al., 2020a). However, these 
methods have drawbacks. As the resolution of the voxel model increases, 
the number of voxels grows cubically, leading to a significant increase in 
computational complexity and storage requirements. To address this 
issue, some methods(Tatarchenko et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018a) have 
proposed voxel space representations based on octrees. An octree is an 
efficient data structure that recursively divides 3D space into eight 
subspaces, each of which can be further subdivided as needed. 
Compared to conventional voxel grids, octrees can flexibly adapt to 
resolution requirements in different regions, significantly reducing 
storage requirements for empty areas.
In the field of fruit, to address the issue of scarcity of real-world 
datasets and poor generalization ability of models trained on synthetic 
datasets, Hartley et al. (2021) proposed an end-to-end framework called 
GANana, aimed at generating voxel models of bananas from a single 
image and estimating their volume. Specifically, GANana introduces an 
Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) method, utilizing CycleGAN to 
map the synthetic image domain to the real image domain. It also in­
corporates a volume consistency loss to ensure the geometric structure 
remains consistent during domain adaptation. Subsequently, the Volu­
metric Regression Network (VRN) is used to generate 3D voxel grids 
from 2D images. This method relies on a small amount of unlabeled real 
data and a large quantity of labeled synthetic data for training, 
demonstrating strong generalization and the potential for scalability to 
3D reconstruction tasks for other fruits.
3.1.3. Methods based on point cloud
Point clouds are discrete sets of points representing the surface of 3D 
objects. Compared to voxel grids, they offer higher storage efficiency. 
Point cloud-based deep learning methods typically adopt an encoder- 
decoder architecture. Due to the unordered nature of point clouds, 
traditional decoder architectures are not directly applicable. Thus, 
Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP), consisting of fully connected layers, are 
often used for decoding. Fan et al. (2017) were among the first to 
introduce point cloud models into learning-based single-view 3D 
reconstruction tasks, proposing a network architecture called PSGN, 
which consists of an encoder and a decoder. The decoder includes two 
branches: a fully connected branch and a deconvolutional branch. The 
model uses Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) as 
loss functions. Given a single RGB or RGB-D image as input, this model 
can generate multiple possible point cloud models. To address the 
blurriness caused by occlusion, Mandikal et al. (2019) proposed a point 
cloud generation model, 3D-LMNet, based on Latent Embedding 
Matching. This model utilizes a point cloud autoencoder to learn prior 
knowledge and construct a latent representation space. The image 
encoder then maps the input image to this latent space, and a probabi­
listic training scheme is introduced to resolve the inherent ambiguity in 
shape prediction.
Point cloud-based methods have been widely applied in agriculture, 
primarily for 3D reconstruction of crop leaves, root systems, or entire 
plants. For example, Wang et al. (2024d) combined an image encoder 
with a Transformer-based point cloud decoder to generate leaf point 
clouds from 2D images, which serve as intermediate representations for 
subsequent mesh reconstruction. Lu et al. (2021) proposed a self- 
supervised neural network to generate point clouds of plant root sys­
tems from 2D images, introducing a fusion strategy in the network that 
significantly improves the accuracy of the generated point cloud by 
mutually constraining the point clouds produced by two different 
methods. Yang et al. (2021) designed a two-stage method for wheat 
plant point cloud generation, where a coarse point cloud is first gener­
ated using the PSGN model, then refined using PointNet++ and a point 
cloud fusion module to enhance point density and geometric precision. 
In the fruit domain, Ma et al. (2024) proposed an end-to-end multimodal 
model for point cloud-based reconstruction to estimate the volume and 
energy content of food items such as bananas from monocular images. 
They introduced two schemes: the first uses depth maps as intermediate 
representations, employing the ZoeDepth model for depth estimation 
and subsequent conversion to point clouds; the second uses mesh rep­
resentations generated by the TripoSR model, from which point clouds 
are sampled. Experimental results showed that the depth-based method 
yielded smaller errors. Although the sparsity of point clouds reduces 
computational cost, it also limits their ability to represent continuous 
surfaces and fine texture details. As a result, point clouds are often used 
as intermediate representations for downstream tasks. Point cloud-based 
methods are commonly used for objects less affected by occlusion, such 
as leaves and roots, or fruits with high symmetry, such as bananas,

3.1.4. Methods based on mesh
Meshes are a 3D geometric representation composed of vertices, 
edges, and faces. They offer advantages such as compactness, strong 
expressive power, and high computational efficiency. Mesh-based 
methods can generally be categorized into two types: multi-stage mesh 
reconstruction and template mesh deformation.
Multi-stage methods typically rely on intermediate representations, 
such as depth maps and surface normal (Yao et al., 2020), point clouds 
(Groueix et al., 2018), or implicit functions (Popov et al., 2020), to 
reconstruct mesh models. For example, Mescheder et al. (2019) pro­
posed Occupancy Networks, which implicitly represent 3D shapes by 
learning a continuous occupancy function, from which mesh models are 
extracted using the Marching Cubes algorithm. Template mesh defor­
mation methods begin with a coarse mesh and iteratively refine it based 
on image features to obtain a high-fidelity 3D representation. For 
instance, Wang et al. (2018b) proposed an end-to-end deep learning 
framework, Pixel2Mesh, which generates mesh models from single-view 
images. This model uses a progressive deformation strategy, employing 
a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) to deform an initial ellipsoid 
mesh into a high-resolution target mesh over three stages. However, 
template mesh deformation methods are generally constrained to 
reconstruct meshes with a fixed number of vertices, which limits their 
ability to capture complex and fine-grained geometric details.
In the agricultural domain, mesh-based methods have been widely 
applied to crop leaf reconstruction. For example, Li et al. (2022c) pro­
posed a single-view leaf reconstruction method based on Pixel2Mesh, 
integrating an improved ResNet18 architecture to enhance feature 
extraction, and incorporating differentiable rendering techniques to 
optimize mesh contours. Experimental results demonstrated that the 
method outperformed Pixel2Mesh across evaluation metrics such as F- 
Score, CD, and EMD, achieving values of 76.192, 0.808, and 3.567, 
respectively. Due to viewpoint limitations, mesh-based approaches are 
predominantly used for thin, planar structures such as leaves, where 
they yield satisfactory results. However, their application to agricultural 
objects with more complex geometries, such as fruits, remains limited 
and requires further investigation and refinement.
3.2. Multi-view image based
Due to viewpoint limitations, single-view image-based methods 
often struggle to reconstruct occluded regions accurately, making it 
difficult to estimate the complete 3D shape of an object. To improve 
reconstruction precision, researchers have proposed multi-view image- 
based approaches. Early approaches were dominated by depth 
map–based methods. In recent years, the emergence of neural rendering 
techniques such as NeRF and 3DGS has elevated the fidelity of 3D 
reconstruction to unprecedented levels. With unmatched detail and 
appearance reproduction capabilities, these methods have become rev­
olutionary tools in offline high-precision analysis, opening new possi­
bilities for precise phenotyping and the broader adoption of digital 
orchards. Fig. 8 compares the workflows of these three approaches, each 
of which will be described in detail below.

Depth map-based methods represent one of the earliest deep learning 
approaches for multi-view 3D reconstruction. Their core idea is first to

feature extraction stage, GeoMVSNet(Zhang et al., 2023b) enhances

improve depth estimation in low-texture and reflective regions, as well 
as under view-dependent effects. The method also introduces a novel 
photometric consistency loss that focuses the model’s attention on ac­

In the agricultural domain, depth map-based multi-view recon­
struction methods are primarily employed for crop phenotyping and 
precision agriculture management. For example, Chen et al. (2022b)
estimated camera parameters using Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and 
combined them with MVSNet to reconstruct crops such as Arabidopsis 
thaliana and wheat for phenotypic analysis. Liu et al. (2023) proposed 
RepC-MVSNet, an unsupervised network for wheat reconstruction, 
based on RC-MVSNet with an integrated RepVGG module replacing the 
original feature extractor to enhance the network’s ability to capture 
complex crop features. To address the challenge of low reconstruction 
accuracy for small objects such as wheat grains, Tian et al. (2024)
introduced a Transformer-based super-resolution network to enhance 
grain image resolution before feeding them into the Vis-MVSNet for 3D 
reconstruction. The method achieved a 38.96 % improvement in 
reconstruction accuracy (SSIM), effectively mitigating performance 
degradation caused by low resolution and weak textures. For fruits, 
existing research has focused more on large-scale orchard scene recon­
struction, laying the groundwork for precision agriculture. For instance, 
Ling et al. (2024) proposed an improved MVSNet model for multi-view 
jujube tree reconstruction, introducing a composite U-Net and an 
enhanced DRE-Net with self-attention. Compared to the baseline, their 
approach improved accuracy, completeness, and overall score by 20.4 
%, 12.8 %, and 16.8 %, respectively. Zeng et al. (2025) introduced MT- 
MVSNet, an end-to-end multi-view stereo network for efficient and ac­
curate 3D fruit tree reconstruction. The network employed a modified 
Mobile Transformer Block (MTB) to enhance both local and global fea­
tures, integrated an Edge Attention for Features (EAF) module to 
improve depth estimation near object boundaries, and adopted a Multi- 
stage Bi-directional Progression Search (MBPS) strategy to maintain 
accuracy while reducing computational cost. Although multi-view 3D 
reconstruction methods based on depth maps offer high reconstruction 
accuracy, their application in agriculture, particularly in the fruit 
domain, remains limited. This is partly due to the complexity and dy­
namic nature of agricultural environments, which present significant 
challenges for 3D reconstruction, and partly because the lack of high- 
precision agricultural datasets hinders the widespread adoption of 
these techniques.
3.2.2. Methods based on NeRF
NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2022), an implicit 3D scene representation

then integrated using volumetric rendering to compute the final color of 
each pixel in the synthesized image. The principle of volumetric 
rendering is to integrate color and density along the camera ray, 
resulting in the appearance of a pixel from a particular viewpoint. NeRF 
employs hierarchical sampling to approximate the rendering integral

The original NeRF method is computationally expensive, typically

rendering speed has become a central focus in this field. For example, 
the Instant-NGP (Müller et al., 2022) model introduces a general-

varying resolutions, with each level linked to a fixed-size hash table that 
stores trainable feature vectors. By replacing the original large MLP with 
a smaller MLP combined with the multiresolution hash encoding, 
Instant-NGP reduces NeRF’s training time from several hours to seconds 
or minutes while maintaining comparable accuracy. It also achieves 
real-time rendering speeds of up to 60 FPS at 1080p resolution. To 
enhance reconstruction quality, the Zip-NeRF(Barron et al., 2023) 
model integrates the anti-aliasing techniques of mip-NeRF 360 with the 
grid-based acceleration of Instant-NGP. It employs a multisampling and 
downweighting mechanism to mitigate spatial aliasing and introduces 
an anti-aliasing loss function to address Z-aliasing along ray directions. 
Results show that Zip-NeRF reduces error by 19 % and increases training 
speed by a factor of 24 compared to mip-NeRF 360. Furthermore, 
NeRF’s geometric representation lacks constraints on surface structure, 
making it challenging to extract high-quality 3D surfaces. To address 
this, methods such as VolSDF(Yariv et al., 2021) and NeuS(Wang et al., 
2021) integrate NeRF with Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) to enhance 
geometric representation. The zero-level set of an SDF corresponds to 
the object’s surface boundary, enabling high-precision geometric 
reconstruction. Subsequent approaches, including S-VolSDF (Wu et al., 
2023) and NeuS2 (Wang et al., 2023), have built upon these models as 
baselines for further refinements. Leveraging volumetric rendering, 
NeRF-based methods can naturally simulate lighting, shadows, and 
color variations, offering significantly superior results compared to 
traditional mesh-based 3D reconstruction, all while maintaining high 
storage efficiency without explicitly storing 3D structures.

3.2.3. Methods based on 3DGS
Following NeRF, 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) was introduced in 2023 
for novel view synthesis and 3D scene reconstruction. Unlike NeRF’s 
implicit representation, 3DGS adopts an explicit approach by recon­
structing a scene as a discrete set of 3D Gaussian points, each modeled as 
an independent 3D Gaussian distribution, mathematically expressed as:

(3) 
where x denotes any point in 3D space, and μ represents the mean, while 
Σ denotes the covariance matrix of the Gaussian, respectively. Each 
Gaussian also carries color (c) and opacity (α) attributes. The rendering 
pipeline operates as follows: First, 3D Gaussian kernels are projected 
onto a 2D plane to generate a series of 2D Gaussian distributions. These 
are then superimposed and blended to produce a synthetic image. When 
multiple 2D Gaussians contribute to the same pixel, they are composited 
in depth order, from nearest to farthest, updating pixel transparency and 
color accordingly to properly handle occlusion and transparency. 
Similar to NeRF, 3DGS is trained by minimizing the difference between 
the synthetic and real images. However, unlike NeRF, which relies on 
neural networks, 3DGS directly updates the parameters of each 3D 
Gaussian point to reconstruct the entire radiance field. 3DGS is typically 
initialized using the SfM method to generate a sparse 3D Gaussian point 
cloud. However, the initial number of points is often insufficient to 
represent the complete 3D scene. To address this, 3DGS introduces an 
adaptive density control strategy (as illustrated in Fig. 12), which clones 
under-reconstructed Gaussians, splits over-reconstructed ones, and 
prunes points with opacity below a predefined threshold. This approach 
balances rendering quality and computational efficiency while man­
aging memory consumption.
While the 3DGS method enables photorealistic scene rendering, the 
optimized Gaussian functions lack an ordered structure and do not 
correspond well to actual surfaces, limiting its effectiveness in surface 
reconstruction (Gu´edon & Lepetit, 2024; Wang et al., 2024e). To address 
this limitation, several studies have proposed enhancements and opti­
mizations aimed at improving reconstruction quality. For example, 
NeuSG (Chen et al., 2023) combines 3DGS with Neural Surface (NeuS) in 
a joint optimization framework, introducing scale regularization and 
normal regularization techniques to ensure that the point cloud gener­
ated by 3DGS is closely aligned with the object surfaces, thereby 
enabling the reconstruction of complete surfaces with fine-grained de­
tails. In addition, to tackle issues such as multi-view inconsistency and 
poor modeling of thin structures, 2DGS (Huang et al., 2024c) introduces 
two-dimensional Gaussian primitives along with efficient differentiable 
rendering and regularization strategies. This approach enables 
geometrically accurate radiance field reconstruction while maintaining 
the advantages of real-time rendering and high-fidelity novel view 
synthesis (NVS).
In the field of agriculture, 3DGS-based methods have been applied to 
crop phenotyping. For instance, Jiang et al. (2024) employed a 
YOLOv8x + 3DGS approach to construct 3DGS models of cotton bolls 
and main stems, enabling the estimation of boll count and stem length, 
with mean absolute percentage errors (MAPE) of 11.43 % and 10.45 %, 
respectively (Fig. 13a). Similarly, Ojo et al. (2024) utilized a YOLOv8 +
3DGS method for the reconstruction of crops such as canola, wheat, and 
peas, facilitating organ-level segmentation and phenotypic analysis 
(Fig. 13b).
However, studies have shown that for crops with dense foliage, the 
internal structures are often unclear, and rendering results appear 
blurred. This issue is also prevalent in orchard environments. To address 
model aliasing caused by occlusion in peach orchards, Chen et al. (2025)
proposed improvements to the 3DGS model by introducing a dynamic 
opacity reset strategy. This strategy reduces erroneous pruning opera­
tions during adaptive density control and enhances the model’s capacity 
to capture features across different spatial scales. Additionally, they 
proposed a sampling and anti-aliasing module that limits the represen­
tation frequency of Gaussian primitives and incorporates supersampling 
techniques to improve reconstruction quality. Results demonstrated

high-fidelity reconstruction of fruit surface micro-features while sup­
porting real-time rendering, showing broad application prospects in the 
field of fruit 3D reconstruction. However, this approach remains chal­
lenged by multiple factors in orchard environments, including the 
impact of varying lighting conditions on reconstruction quality, infor­
mation loss caused by occlusion from leaves and branches, and the 
relatively high storage costs associated with its explicit representation. 
Compared with NeRF-based methods, 3DGS holds significant advan­
tages in training efficiency. Nevertheless, it still requires training from 
scratch when processing novel scenes, which falls short of achieving true 
real-time reconstruction and represents a key direction for future 
optimization.
Earlier, Fig. 8 compared the operational workflows of MVSNet, 
NeRF, and 3DGS. A key commonality among these three mainstream 
approaches is their strong reliance on pre-estimated camera parameters 
as input. However, they differ markedly in supervision strategies and 
geometric priors. In terms of supervision, MVSNet typically requires 
supervised training on large-scale datasets containing ground-truth 
depth maps. In contrast, NeRF and 3DGS follow a scene-specific self- 
optimization paradigm, requiring no ground-truth depth. They rely 
solely on input RGB images as supervisory signals and optimize a neural 
representation independently for each scene. Regarding geometric 
priors, 3DGS generally uses sparse point clouds from SfM to initialize the 
Gaussian representation. NeRF, by contrast, requires no geometric priors 
and learns directly from random initialization. The original MVSNet 
model also does not use sparse point clouds as input, though some 
subsequent variants incorporate them for initialization or auxiliary 
supervision.
3.3. Comprehensive analysis: Application-driven selection of technical 
approaches
Framing 3D reconstruction technologies as “classical methods” 
versus “learning-based methods” helps researchers build a clear 
knowledge structure and understand the field’s evolution from a his­
torical perspective. This division highlights the paradigm shift from 
reliance on explicit geometric priors and physical models to dependence 
on data-driven approaches and feature learning. Such a classification is 
both necessary and valuable. However, in practical applications, the 
central question faced by decision-makers is not whether to adopt 
classical or learning-based methods, but rather how to solve a specific 
task within given time constraints and cost limitations. Therefore, 
application-driven requirements should be the fundamental criterion for 
technology selection.
In the fruit industry, this necessitates a return to the two application 
paradigms defined earlier: real-time online perception and offline high- 
precision analysis. Within these distinct scenarios, the boundary be­
tween “classical” and “learning-based” methods becomes less important, 
giving way instead to a trade-off between speed and accuracy. In real- 
time online perception tasks, the primary objectives are speed and 
robustness. Classical methods such as ToF, speckle-based structured 
light, and stereo vision offer rapid depth estimation through well- 
defined physical models. In parallel, learning-based single-view recon­
struction achieves millisecond-level response times through powerful 
neural inference. Together, these approaches form a “fast perception” 
toolbox, sacrificing some geometric accuracy in exchange for minimal 
latency. Overall, while learning-based methods currently exhibit weaker 
generalization compared to traditional techniques, they possess greater 
long-term potential. In contrast, the offline high-precision analysis 
paradigm emphasizes the pursuit of geometric and visual detail. Active 
methods such as laser triangulation and coded structured light provide 
deterministic physical guarantees for high-accuracy 3D modeling 
through direct geometric measurement and precise system calibration.

4. Technical challenges and solutions
The need for 3D reconstruction of fruits spans the entire production 
chain, both pre-harvest and post-harvest. Unlike industrial applications, 
fruits, as agricultural objects, exhibit diverse shapes and complex sur­
faces. Additionally, most fruits grow in outdoor orchards, which are 
heavily influenced by natural factors such as sunlight, wind, and 
shading, all of which can lead to decreased reconstruction accuracy. As a 
result, 3D reconstruction of fruits presents several challenges, including 
occlusion, complex lighting conditions, the accuracy-efficiency trade- 
off, and more.
4.1. Occlusion
Occlusion is one of the primary challenges in fruit 3D reconstruction, 
especially in complex environments such as greenhouses or orchards 
(Bortolotti et al., 2024, 2022; Li et al., 2022d; Yu et al., 2021b). In these 
settings, fruits are often partially obscured by leaves, branches (Dong 
et al., 2023; Jim´enez et al., 2000; Li et al., 2022d), and other fruits (Dong 
et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2021), making tasks such as crop monitoring 
(Marangoz et al., 2022; Ohashi et al., 2020), phenotypic analysis (Dong 
et al., 2023; Magistri et al., 2022; Sch¨oler & Steinhage, 2015), fruit 
localization (Gong et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022b; Yu et al., 2021b), and 
automated harvesting (Gong et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022b; Magistri et al., 
2022) significantly more difficult. Occlusion can be classified into three 
types: occlusion by the fruit itself, occlusion between fruits, and occlu­
sion by leaves and branches.
To address occlusion in greenhouse or orchard environments, 
various solutions have been proposed to improve the completeness of 
fruit 3D reconstruction. One approach involves viewpoint planning 
(Burusa et al., 2024; Menon et al., 2023), where accumulated 
observational data is used to plan the next optimal viewpoint to maxi­
mize information gain. By sequentially selecting the best viewpoints and 
capturing images multiple times, previously unobserved areas can be 
effectively covered, leading to more complete fruit models. Addition­
ally, shape completion is a commonly employed method to tackle oc­
clusion. Some studies have analyzed the visible fruit surface point clouds 
and used completion techniques to predict and fill in missing parts, thus 
generating a more complete fruit model. For example, certain studies 
use deep learning methods to complete occluded depth maps (Gong 
et al., 2022) or generate complete fruit models from single-frame RGB-D 
images (Magistri et al., 2022). In addition, to accommodate the unique 
shapes of certain fruits, researchers have employed spherical models (Li 
et al., 2022b; Ni et al., 2021) or superellipsoid models (Lehnert et al., 
2016) to fit incomplete point clouds and generate relatively complete 3D 
models. Some studies leverage the inherent symmetry of strawberries to 
complete their point clouds (Ge et al., 2020). Other research combines 
viewpoint planning with shape completion (Marangoz et al., 2022), 
taking advantage of both techniques: first acquiring as much surface 
information as possible through optimal viewpoint selection, and then 
applying completion algorithms to the missing regions. In indoor envi­
ronments, occlusion primarily includes self-occlusion of the fruit (Li 
et al., 2023), mutual occlusion caused by fruit stacking (Ali et al., 2024), 
and occlusion of the fruit’s bottom by fruit holders (Hong et al., 2024; Yu 
et al., 2024). Owing to the more controlled conditions indoors, these 
occlusions can often be mitigated by increasing the number of view­
points (Ali et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Wang & Chen, 2020). For cases 
where the fruit base is occluded by holders, some studies have adopted 
shape completion techniques (Yu et al., 2024) to ensure model integrity. 
Table 3 summarizes the corresponding solutions for different scenarios.
Looking ahead, improvements in planting strategies from a horti­
cultural perspective may enhance fruit visibility. By adjusting planting 
density, pruning strategies, and canopy structures, fruit occlusion can be 
reduced, thereby improving visibility and facilitating more accurate 3D 
reconstruction. Moreover, current completion techniques are mostly 
designed for fruits with regular shapes. For irregularly shaped fruits, 
these algorithms still exhibit limitations in adaptability and accuracy. 
Therefore, further improvements are needed to enhance the completion 
performance for fruits with irregular geometries.
4.2. Complex lighting conditions
Lighting conditions significantly impact fruit image acquisition and 
3D reconstruction. In indoor environments, lighting can be manually 
controlled, resulting in minimal interference. However, outdoor orchard 
scenes present greater challenges due to factors such as direct sunlight 
causing bright spots, low-light conditions at night, fluctuating illumi­
nation from weather changes, and complex shadow patterns created by 
sunlight filtering through leaves. All of these factors significantly hinder 
accurate 3D reconstruction of fruits (Bortolotti et al., 2022; Fu et al., 
2020; Yu et al., 2021b).

conditions in the orchard. Other studies have proposed building shading 
structures to eliminate the influence of ambient light. For example, 
Nguyen et al. (2016) constructed a shading setup using black plastic film 
placed above the fruit trees to block direct sunlight from reaching the 
fruit surfaces, which indirectly improved the reconstruction accuracy of 
depth cameras. Additional strategies include the use of optical filters (Fu 
et al., 2020) or artificial lighting at night to maintain a consistent illu­
mination environment (Mirbod et al., 2023; Ohashi et al., 2020). 
However, most existing solutions for addressing complex lighting con­
ditions primarily focus on hardware-level selection and optimization. 
Future research may consider algorithmic innovations to develop more 
generalizable and efficient solutions.
4.3. The accuracy-efficiency trade-off
In addition to the “real-time sensing” and “offline analysis” para­
digms discussed earlier, there exists a demanding middle ground, 
exemplified by online fruit grading. These tasks require accurate 3D 
modeling of fruit within millisecond-level time windows on high-speed 
production lines for size measurement and defect detection, posing a 
sharp trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. Many high-precision 
reconstruction techniques, due to their inherent time cost, are difficult 
to apply directly to online grading scenarios. For example, 3D scanners 
can provide high-precision estimations of shape and volume, but the 
reconstruction process is time-consuming and computationally expen­
sive (Anders et al., 2019). Similarly, SfM and MVS methods require the 
acquisition and processing of dozens of fruit images, which also leads to 
significant time costs (Kochi et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2021). NeRF and 
3DGS approaches require retraining for each individual fruit, limiting 
their feasibility for real-time deployment on production lines. The 
combination of RGB-D cameras with rotary tables can also achieve full- 
surface reconstruction, but the image acquisition and point cloud 
registration processes are relatively time-intensive, typically taking 
several seconds per fruit (Hong et al., 2024).
Therefore, overcoming the dilemma of the “mutual exclusivity of 
high precision and high efficiency”, specifically, how to ensure sufficient 
measurement accuracy while meeting real-time requirements, has 
become a central research focus in the field (Jadhav et al., 2019; Lu & 
Lu, 2017). Some studies attempt to simplify the reconstruction process 
in exchange for speed. Imou et al. (2006) proposed a volume intersection 
method based on nine viewpoints, which completes 3D reconstruction 
within one second, achieving an RMSE between 0.5 and 2 mm. Others 
employ a multi-sensor parallel acquisition strategy, Hong et al. (2024)
replaced the traditional rotary stage with five RGB-D cameras to capture 
point clouds from multiple viewpoints simultaneously. By using a cali­
bration plate to compute extrinsic matrices between the cameras, rather 
than relying on the time-consuming ICP method for point cloud regis­
tration, they achieved a total processing time of just 40 ms. Experi­
mental results showed RMSEs of 2.08 mm for length estimation, 3.77 
cm3 for volume, and 10.73 kg/m3 for density estimation of oriental 
melons. In summary, the prevailing strategy to address this challenge 
involves maximizing parallelism through multi-sensor systems and

4.4. Other challenges
In addition to the issues mentioned above, 3D fruit reconstruction 
faces several other challenges. For example, learning-based methods 
typically rely on high-quality datasets for training. However, high- 
quality and diverse 3D fruit datasets are extremely scarce, limiting the 
application of deep learning techniques in the fruit domain. Future work 
should focus on building open and collaborative 3D fruit databases. 
Moreover, studies have shown that cameras based on the TOF principle 
often encounter increased errors and generate spiky shape distortions 
when measuring certain fruits, such as mangoes (Neupane et al., 2021), 
grapes (Parr et al., 2022), and oriental melons (Hong et al., 2024). These 
errors have been found to be closely related to fruit ripeness (Neupane 
et al., 2021). It has been suggested that this phenomenon may be due to 
internal light scattering within the fruit (Parr et al., 2022). Diffusely 
scattered light increases the effective path length, thereby leading to 
overestimated depth values (Hong et al., 2024). Future research should 
further investigate the relationship between these errors and distortions 
and the intrinsic characteristics of different fruits. In addition to devel­
oping effective correction methods, this property could be exploited for 
more valuable applications, such as non-invasive assessment of fruit 
ripeness. Furthermore, current 3D fruit reconstruction solutions often 
suffer from high equipment costs (Abeyrathna et al., 2024; Imou et al., 
2006; Kochi et al., 2018; Yamamoto et al., 2018) or computational 
complexity (Anders et al., 2019). Therefore, future efforts should pri­
oritize the development of lightweight 3D reconstruction systems based 
on consumer-grade hardware to promote wider applicability and cost- 
effectiveness.
5. Conclusions and Outlooks
This paper provides a systematic review of research advances in 3D 
reconstruction technologies for the fruit industry. Guided by its two core 
application needs, real-time online sensing and offline high-precision 
analysis, it offers a structured overview of existing methods. Within 
this framework, we examine how classical methods (active and passive) 
and deep learning–based methods (single-view and multi-view) serve 
these two paradigms. For classical methods, we present the main 
workflow in detail, including 3D data acquisition, post-processing, and 
surface reconstruction. In addition, we also summarized cutting-edge 
solutions addressing common challenges like occlusion and complex 
lighting. We specifically analyzed “Category III” challenges, exemplified 
by online fruit grading, which demand both high real-time performance 
and precision.
Despite the significant progress of 3D reconstruction technologies in 
the fruit domain, future development paths and challenges vary across 
application paradigms. For real-time online perception, achieving 
robust and autonomous 3D reconstruction in complex orchard envi­
ronments remains a major challenge. This calls for further integration of 
multi-sensor fusion and autonomous navigation technologies. Another 
critical direction is the extreme lightweighting of algorithms to enable 
real-time inference on low-cost, low-power embedded devices. For off­
line high-precision analysis applications, the focus lies in continually 
pushing the limits of model fidelity and reconstruction efficiency. Future 
research should explore the transition from static 3D to dynamic 4D 
reconstruction, modeling the entire growth cycle of fruit from flowering 
to maturity, thereby constructing time-resolved 3D models. Addition­
ally, integrating 3D visual data with other modalities such as hyper­
spectral imaging, thermal imaging, and even CT scanning offers the 
potential for comprehensive, non-destructive evaluation of both 
external and internal fruit quality. Even in offline settings, efficiency and 
scalability remain critical constraints. Reducing the high training cost of 
cutting-edge methods like NeRF to enable efficient large-scale orchard 
modeling is still an urgent challenge that must be addressed.
Looking toward broader technological frontiers, the rapid develop­
ment of Foundation Models has provided a new paradigm for fruit 3D 
reconstruction. By pre-training on vast amounts of multimodal data, 
Foundation Models exhibit powerful feature learning and generalization 
abilities, as well as high transferability. This enables them to maintain 
high reconstruction accuracy even in situations with limited data or 
significant environmental variation, effectively addressing challenges 
such as complex agricultural environments, lighting changes, and multi- 
sensor data fusion. In the future, driven by the combined advancements 
in algorithm optimization, technology integration, and hardware 
improvement, this field is expected to achieve more precise, efficient, 
and intelligent applications in fruit cultivation, growth monitoring, and 
post-harvest processing, thus providing strong support for the modern­
ization of agricultural technology.
