ABSTRACT
The automation of agriculture increasingly relies on Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs);
however, the detection and analysis of fruits such as Hass avocados remain challenging due to their irregular
shape, dark skin, and complex background. This work proposes a computer vision system for avocado
detection and analysis that integrates a YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once version 8) neural network to perform
maturity classification, fruit counting, distance estimation, height calculation, and 2D mapping within a fixed
reference frame defined by the UAV. The system was evaluated in both simulated and real environments.
In the simulated environment, the entire processing pipeline was validated, achieving reliable detection
and stable spatial analysis performance. In real-world tests, only the image acquisition stage was assessed
using an onboard vision platform, demonstrating the feasibility of in-flight image capture for subsequent
processing. Preliminary results from the training of multiple YOLOv8 variants showed consistent learning
behavior, with the YOLOv8s model achieving the best balance between accuracy and efficiency. It reached
a mean Average Precision at IoU = 0.5 (mAP@0.5) of 84.01% and a mean Average Precision at IoU range =
0.5:0.95 (mAP@0.5:0.95) of 76.94%, along with a precision of 79.66% and a recall of 85.37%. These values
confirm the model’s robust detection capability and validate the reliability of the proposed system in both
simulated and real environments, highlighting its potential for future real-time UAV-based fruit detection in
precision agriculture.

I. INTRODUCTION
Precision agriculture has emerged as a key pillar in ensur-
ing sustainable and efficient production, supported by the
growing use of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) for crop
monitoring and management [1], [2], [3]. However, the
automatic detection and analysis of fruits such as Hass
avocado (Persea americana) remains a critical challenge due
to their irregular shape, dark skin, and heterogeneous orchard
backgrounds [4], [5]. These complex conditions reduce the
effectiveness of conventional computer vision algorithms
and demand more robust solutions, particularly consid-
ering the increasing global economic importance of this
crop.
Several studies have demonstrated that techniques based
on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as the YOLO

family of models, achieve remarkable results in real-time fruit
detection, including apples [6] and mangoes [7]. Likewise,
non-destructive methods such as microwave scanning or deep
learning approaches have been explored to estimate fruit
maturity and quality [8], [9], [10]. This approach is aligned
with recent research that demonstrates the potential of YOLO
models for agricultural tasks in complex conditions [11], [12],
[13], as well as studies that have validated the use of UAVs
equipped with computer vision to improve crop management

Despite these advances, accurate and real-time detection
under field conditions remains constrained by factors such
as light variability, fruit occlusion, lack of three-dimensional
information, and limited integration with autonomous aerial
platforms. These aspects hinder the practical adoption of
existing systems in real agricultural environments.

In response to this need, the present work proposes a
computer vision system for the detection, localization, and
analysis of Hass avocados using UAVs, with an approach
that combines RGB images and depth data to estimate
the three-dimensional position of each fruit. Unlike other
UAV-based agricultural systems, our proposal integrates
two complementary phases (a simulated stage and a field
stage), enabling the validation of the perception pipeline
under controlled conditions as well as the assessment of its
practical feasibility in cultivation environments. Furthermore,
the system goes beyond simple detection, incorporating
maturity-state classification, fruit counting, distance and
height estimation, and the generation of three-dimensional
maps from sequential scans, thereby establishing itself as a
more comprehensive tool for precision agriculture.
To evaluate its performance, the system was tested in
two complementary stages. The first was conducted in a
simulated environment replicating an artificial plantation,
where the full perception pipeline was implemented and
validated. This phase allowed testing different network
configurations, assessing the integration of virtual sensors,
and verifying spatial analysis under controlled conditions,
thereby establishing a solid foundation before moving to real-
world trials.
The second stage was carried out in a real cultivation
environment using a UAV equipped with an RGB-D camera
and an onboard computer. In this case, the objective was not
to replicate all the functionalities tested in simulation, but
rather to verify the overall functionality of the system and its
remote control during flight, ensuring the correct acquisition
of information from the RGB-D camera. The recorded
data were stored in a format compatible with the system’s
working environment, facilitating their subsequent analysis
with YOLOv8. Although with some technical limitations,
the results confirmed the practical feasibility of the approach
under real field conditions.
Preliminary results demonstrate a solid performance of the
proposed system, validating the capability of the YOLOv8
model to accurately detect and locate Hass avocados in both
simulated and real environments. This performance confirms
the feasibility of the approach as a support tool for precision
agriculture, representing an initial step toward the integration
of perception systems with autonomous aerial platforms.
Future research will build upon the strengths and limitations
identified to further optimize the model and advance its
deployment in fully autonomous drone systems for intelligent
crop monitoring and management.
II. RELATED WORK
In recent years, the YOLOv8 algorithm has established itself
as a key tool for fruit detection, classification, and tracking,
particularly in agricultural applications requiring precision,
speed, and efficiency. Its optimized architecture enables
deployment across diverse platforms, including controlled
environments, open fields, and embedded systems such as
UAVs and robotic platforms [7], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20].
Recent studies demonstrate that YOLOv8 outperforms
previous versions including YOLOv5 and YOLOv7, achiev-
ing mean Average Precision (mAP) values exceeding 90%
and approaching 99% in certain applications [16], [17], [7],
[18], [19], [20]. For instance, mango ripeness classification
achieved 96.6% mAP with 22.07 ms inference latency [7],
while date fruit classification reached 99.1% precision and
99.4% mAP [16]. In blueberries, the integration of MPCA
and MultiSEAM modules yielded 94.1% mAP [21], and for
grape detection, YOLOv8 surpassed YOLO-NAS in both
precision and mAP@50 metrics [22].
However, our experimental results with Hass avocado
detection reveal lower performance compared to the high-
est values reported in the literature. The best-performing
YOLOv8s model achieves 84.01% mAP@0.5 and 76.94%
mAP@0.5:0.95, with a precision of 79.66% and a recall
of 85.37%. These results highlight the inherent challenges
of Hass avocado detection, particularly due to the color
similarity between the fruit and surrounding foliage, as well
as complex occlusion conditions in natural environments.
Beyond YOLO-based approaches, several studies have
demonstrated effective UAV applications in agriculture using
alternative computer vision and guidance methods that
achieve real-time performance with embedded processing.
Agroview integrates artificial intelligence with cloud com-
puting to process UAV imagery for plant detection, counting,
geolocation, and canopy dimension estimation in citrus
orchards [25]. Similarly, GarcÍa-Murillo et al. employed
digital surface models combined with morphological trans-
forms for individual citrus and avocado tree detection,
enhancing accuracy in farm monitoring tasks [23]. Other
research has focused on UAV navigation, such as the system
developed by Basso and de Freitas, which implements crop
row detection and line follower algorithms for real-time
autonomous guidance [26]. Similarly, Li et al. developed a
rapid detection scheme for longan fruits using UAV imagery,
achieving high accuracy in fruit localization within natural
environments [27]. These approaches, combined with local
research on UAV applications in the Majes-Arequipa region
conducted at the Pedro Paulet Institute of Astronomical
and Aerospace Research (IAAPP) [24], demonstrate that
UAV-based precision agriculture solutions extend beyond
deep learning detection to encompass diverse methodologies
tailored for crop monitoring and autonomous operations.
Nevertheless, critical analysis reveals that these impressive
metrics often mask significant limitations in real orchard
environments. While attention mechanisms such as CBAM,
EMA, and SA modules enhance feature extraction under
partial occlusion conditions [18], [19], [20], they struggle
with heavy foliar occlusion prevalent in avocado canopies
where fruit-to-leaf color similarity presents distinctive chal-
lenges [7], [23], [28], [29]. Illumination variations further
complicate detection, as object recognition systems fre-
quently fail under changing light conditions [30], [31]. The
YOLOv8-MR implementation, despite achieving 121 FPS,
demonstrates only marginal mAP improvement (1.7%) in

measurement technologies [34] reveal that standard deep
learning approaches fail to capture the unique phenotypic
traits of Hass avocados under natural growing conditions.
Comparisons between YOLOv8 variants [17], [22], [35]
reveal speed-accuracy trade-offs but do not address the core
challenge of reliable detection under complex occlusion
patterns typical of avocado trees. While systems like
Agroview [25] provide comprehensive cloud-based analysis,
their post-processing dependency limits real-time decision-
making capabilities essential for harvesting applications.
For advanced applications, YOLOv8 integration with
tracking algorithms [36], [37], [38] and UAV systems [27],
[39], [40], [41] has progressed significantly, including
specialized systems for longan detection using UAVs [27]
that demonstrate the feasibility of these approaches for
tropical fruits. Autonomous planning with sensor fusion [41],
[42] and semantic segmentation approaches [43], [44] show
potential for orchard navigation. However, these systems
exhibit critical gaps in ROS2 integration for real-time
control [45] and lack the nonlinear control strategies required
for stable UAV navigation in orchard environments [46].
Research at IAAPP has significantly contributed to this
domain through the development and evaluation of embedded
control architectures based on ROS 2 and Micro-ROS for
autonomous robotic systems [45], providing foundational
work that informs our approach. Traditional guidance
systems [26] provide reliable row following but cannot
handle the unstructured navigation required for fruit-specific
harvesting operations.
The literature reveals three fundamental gaps specifically
affecting Hass avocado detection: (1) inadequate YOLOv8
optimization for the fruit’s unique visual characteristics
under natural conditions [23], [34], as evidenced by our
maximum 75.31% mAP performance; (2) insufficient multi-
sensor fusion for navigation in dense canopies [39], [41];
and (3) absence of integrated ROS2 frameworks connecting
perception with manipulation [45], [47]. Our work addresses
these limitations by combining the strengths of deep learning
approaches with the robustness of traditional computer
vision, creating a domain-specific optimization strategy for
Hass avocado that explicitly handles occlusion scenarios and
lighting variations, integrated with a unified ROS2-Gazebo
framework for end-to-end system validation. Specifically,
our approach targets improvement beyond the 75.31% mAP
baseline while maintaining real-time performance constraints
essential for UAV deployment, building upon foundational
research conducted at IAAPP in aerospace applications and
embedded control systems [24], [45].

This section presents the theoretical framework and the
development of the complete system for the detection and
analysis of Hass avocados, which combines computer vision
with depth image processing and spatial transformations.

tary scenarios: a simulated environment and a real flight
environment. Figure 1 shows the hardware configuration of

2) DEPTH, HEIGHT, AND COORDINATE TRANSFORMATION
BY DETECTION
The three-dimensional detection of avocados is carried out
using aligned RGB images and depth maps, generated
both in simulation and through real sensors. This approach
is commonly used in vision-assisted harvesting systems,
as shown in [48], where depth maps are also integrated to
accurately estimate the position of the detected objects.
From the center of the detected object in the RGB image,
defined by the coordinates (u, v), the corresponding depth Z
is extracted from the depth map. For greater robustness, the
median of the values within the delimited region is used.
In this context, Z represents the direct distance
between the observed point and the camera plane,
in meters.
With the intrinsic parameters provided by the sensor itself
(focal lengths (fx, fy) and optical center (cx, cy)), the pinhole
camera model [51], [52] is applied, allowing the conversion
of 2D coordinates into a relative 3D position in the camera
frame:

To train the detection model, a dataset was constructed
with high-quality images obtained from online sources,
selected for their good lighting and contrast, and focused
on identifying Hass avocados. These images were classified
according to their stage of ripeness, using pigmentation as an
indicator of the optimal harvest time (see Figure 5).

C. SYSTEM IMPLEMENTATION
1) GENERAL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
Figure 6 shows the overall flow of the system, divided into

The PLATFORM block corresponds to data acquisition,
carried out in two complementary contexts: a simulated

subsequently processed on a ground station using YOLOv8

the project’s modules, from data acquisition and intelligent
processing to the visualization and spatial analysis of the
detected fruits. In this context, ROS 2 not only facilitates
the connection between components such as the perception
camera, the inference system, and the visualization tools,
but also enables a distributed architecture in which each
specialized task of the fruit perception system operates as an
independent node.
The interaction between ROS 2 and PX4_Autopilot is
established through the MicroXRCEAgent, which uses
the Micro XRCE-DDS protocol for communication. In the
ground station, this agent is executed with the command:
MicroXRCEAgent udp4 -p 8888
Creating a critical bridge between ROS 2 and the PX4
firmware (in px4_autopilot), which enables real-time
data exchange. This link translates uORB messages from

spatial reconstruction of the environment during flight. The
combined use of this data flow with the transformation

the global frame, without the need for additional mapping
processes, and provides a useful spatial context for tasks
such as analysis, navigation, and validation of the explored

2.5±1 m).

IV. RESULTS
A. TRAINING RESULTS
The training sessions were conducted using a configuration
of ‘‘150 epochs’’, an image size of ‘‘848 pixels’’, and a batch
size between 11 and 20. This configuration was selected to
ensure stable and efficient performance with the available
hardware, including the training workstation and the camera
system, which provides smooth acquisition at ‘‘60 fps’’ with
a maximum resolution of ‘‘848 × 480’’.
All trained models were designed as ‘‘multi-class detec-
tors’’, meaning that a single network can simultaneously
recognize multiple ripeness categories of Hass avoca-
dos (HASS_DURO, HASS_FIRME, HASS_MADURO and
HASS_PASADO) within the same trained weight file. This
multi-class approach eliminates the need to train a separate
model for each fruit category and enables a more versatile
and efficient detection pipeline.
To contextualize the results, three versions of YOLO
were evaluated: YOLOv5, YOLOv7, and YOLOv8, using
the same training dataset and a separate validation dataset.
The performance metrics for all models are summarized
in Table 4, and Figure 8 illustrates the comparison of
performance across these three versions. This comparison
allows validating the model selection used at the time the
experiments were conducted, reflecting the balance between
precision, recall, and mean average precision (mAP@0.5 and
mAP@0.5:0.95) achieved by each version.
In the case of YOLOv7, validation was performed using
a GPU-compatible environment based on CUDA 11.3 and
specific PyTorch versions, due to incompatibilities in more
recent setups. Under this configuration, YOLOv7 was
evaluated consistently alongside YOLOv5 and YOLOv8.
Different versions of the YOLOv8 model were evaluated,
ranging from the lightest (‘‘n’’) to the most advanced (‘‘x’’).
Each version presents a distinct trade-off between accuracy
and inference speed: the ‘‘n’’ model is the fastest but least
accurate, while the ‘‘x’’ model provides the highest precision
at the cost of slower processing. This trend is consistent
with the performance data reported in the official Ultralytics
documentation [54] and is reflected in the results of this study.
Figures 9 and 10 summarize the performance of the
trained models, showing the evolution of detection metrics
and training losses. All models exhibit a positive and
consistent learning trend, with a progressive decrease in
the losses (train/box_loss, train/cls_loss, and
train/dfl_loss) and a steady improvement in the
main evaluation metrics. Precision, recall, and mean average
precision (mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95) reached values
above 80% and 75%, respectively (see Table 5), indicating
a robust detection capability. To complement this global
assessment, Figure 11 provides a granular evaluation through
comparative confusion and normalization matrices. This
class-wise analysis confirms that the high mAP values are
consistently supported by a strong diagonal intensity across
all maturity stages, particularly as the model complexity
increases from the nano to the extra-large architecture.
According to the results in Table 5 and Figure 12, the
‘‘YOLOv8s’’ model achieved the best overall performance,
with a mAP@0.5 of 84.01% and a mAP@0.5:0.95 of 76.94%,
while also providing the highest recall (85.37%) and a
strong precision (79.66%). Although the larger models such
as YOLOv8l and YOLOv8x achieved competitive results,
their increased model size and computational requirements
make them less suitable for real-time aerial deployment,
where onboard hardware resources, power consumption, and
communication bandwidth are limited.
For these reasons, the ‘‘YOLOv8s’’ model was selected,
as it offers the best balance between detection accuracy,
computational efficiency, and inference speed. In addition,
the trained model file (best.pt) has a compact size of
22.6 MB, which facilitates its implementation on UAV
platforms in future work.

1) EXPERIMENTATION AND RESULTS ANALYSIS
Controlled experiments were conducted in an environment
designed to simulate the conditions of a Hass avocado
orchard. Static 10 s recordings were acquired at distances
ranging from 0.40 m to 1.50 m, with the camera fixed at a
height of 1.37 m measured from the ground to the center of
an artificial mesh. This mesh, measuring 4 m in width by 3 m
in height, served as a support surface for positioning the test
fruits. Data collection began at 15:00 h on a day with partial
cloud cover, which conveniently reduced direct sunlight
and improved illumination uniformity. The evaluation set
consisted of 12 fruits (8 firm–hard and 4 firm–ripe). Each
recording was captured at a resolution of 848 × 480 pixels
and 60 fps, generating approximately 1 GB of data per 10 s
segment, reflecting the high information density required for
subsequent analysis.
For post-processing, the previously selected YOLOv8s
multiclass model was employed as the optimal architecture
for balancing accuracy and speed. The processing node
integrated detection, classification, counting, and distance
estimation using stereo camera data, recording in each frame
the individual metrics of every unique identification (ID)
assigned to each fruit. As an example of the data extraction
procedure, Figure 17 presents a representative frame obtained
from one of the recordings, showing the individual metrics
generated by the system. Subsequently, all frames from each
recording were processed to compute the global averages
summarized in Figure 18.
During the analysis, the presence of outliers that distorted
the averages was identified. To clean the data and reflect the
true behavior of the system, two complementary filters were
implemented. The first consisted of a Gaussian filter applied
to the height variable, with a standard deviation of σ = 1.5,
retaining only values within ±1.5 σ of the mean. Under a
normal distribution, this range encompasses approximately
86% of the data, allowing the removal of height measure-
ments that deviated from the central tendency. The second
filter was based on the persistence of the identifications (IDs)
generated by the tracking algorithm, discarding those that
did not reach at least 5% of the appearances of the most
frequent ID. This criterion eliminated transient detections

V. CONCLUSION
This study successfully developed and validated an integrated
system for the automatic detection of Hass avocados
using UAVs, combining computer vision (YOLOv8), depth
sensing, and a distributed architecture based on ROS 2. The
main findings and contributions are summarized as follows:
1) Optimal YOLOv8 Model Selection: The YOLOv8s
model demonstrated the best overall performance,
achieving a mAP@0.5 of 84.01%, a mAP@0.5:0.95 of
76.94%, and the highest F1-score of 82.42%. Further-
more, its compact size of 22.6 MB provides an optimal
balance between detection accuracy and computational
efficiency, making it the most suitable model for the
proposed system.

ments: The system was thoroughly validated in both
simulated and real environments. The simulation phase
enabled full testing of autonomous navigation, percep-
tion, and 3D mapping, while real-world experiments
confirmed the functionality of the acquisition system
and the model’s effectiveness under actual flight condi-
tions.

VI. LIMITATIONS
Although the developed system demonstrated promising
results, several limitations were identified, primarily related
to hardware constraints, environmental conditions, and the
current scope of implementation.
1) Onboard Computational Platform Constraints: The sys-
tem was conceived as a scalable and cost-effective
solution for future UAV deployment. For this reason, the
Raspberry Pi 5 was selected as the onboard computer
due to its balance between computational capability,
energy efficiency, reduced weight, and compatibility
with the Intel RealSense D455 camera. However, real-
time execution of the YOLOv8 model onboard was not
implemented at this stage. The primary focus was to
ensure stable acquisition, recording, and evaluation of
RGB and depth data, as well as to assess live transmis-
sion feasibility under realistic operating conditions.
2) Wireless Communication Limitations: Experimental
tests involving real-time transmission of RealSense
camera topics over Wi-Fi networks at both 2.4 GHz
and 5 GHz revealed significant limitations, including
data loss, communication instability, and increased
computational load on the onboard computer, which
resulted in transmission interruptions. In contrast,
Ethernet-based transmission demonstrated stable per-
formance without data loss or system failures. These
findings indicate that current limitations are primarily
associated with wireless bandwidth constraints, commu-
nication stability, and the computational limitations of
embedded hardware platforms.
3) Sensor and Lighting Condition Limitations: The Intel
RealSense D455 enabled the integration of a compact
stereo vision system, significantly reducing weight
compared to conventional dual-camera stereo setups,
which is critical for UAV flight stability and safety.
However, experimental observations showed that the
sensor performs more reliably in indoor environments
than under direct outdoor sunlight. In high solar illu-
mination conditions, the camera requires an adaptation
period and may exhibit overexposure and glare effects,
particularly in wide or panoramic scenes. Additionally,
detection reliability was observed to improve at closer
object distances. These limitations primarily affect the
data acquisition stage. To mitigate these effects, image
capture during periods of moderate illumination, such
as cloudy conditions or late afternoon hours, is rec-
ommended. Future work will explore the integration
of optical filters to improve outdoor image acquisition
performance.
4) Limited Validation of Fully Autonomous UAV Opera-
tion: Although the system demonstrated correct func-
tionality in simulation and real-world flight tests with
the onboard computer installed, fully autonomous
UAV operation was not implemented in this stage.
Flight experiments confirmed the feasibility of onboard
system integration without compromising UAV sta-
bility or safety. Additionally, reliable communication
between the ground station and the onboard com-
puter was validated using SSH access, which allows
flexible control from devices such as laptops or
smartphones. Data acquisition was managed using the
pyrealsense2 library, enabling direct integration
between the RealSense camera and Python. However,
flight operations were conducted manually due to terrain
irregularities and the primary focus of this study on
perception system validation.
5) Pending Integration of LiDAR-Based Autonomous
Navigation: LiDAR-based mapping and environment
reconstruction were implemented and validated within
the Gazebo simulation environment as an extension of
the system. The simulation results confirmed its correct
operation and demonstrated its potential for autonomous
navigation applications. However, its integration into
the real UAV platform has not yet been completed.
Considering the importance of LiDAR for robust
autonomous navigation and obstacle avoidance, its full
implementation in real-world flight conditions will be
addressed in future work.
These limitations indicate that the present work represents
an initial stage toward a fully autonomous UAV perception
system. Future developments will focus on enabling real-
time onboard inference, improving wireless communication
