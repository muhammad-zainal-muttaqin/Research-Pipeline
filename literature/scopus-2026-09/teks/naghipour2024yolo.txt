Abstract— The palm oil industry is a huge contributor 
to Malaysia’s economic growth. Adopting new technologies 
such as AI to optimize the different processes involved in 
the palm oil industry and minimizing the problems such 
as 
high 
time consumption 
and 
labor 
intensiveness 
associated with the traditional ways of handling fruits in 
the industry will further assist the economic growth of 
Malaysia and will help reduce negative environmental 
effects. This research offers insights on the related literature 
and employs a YOLOv10-S model with mAP (mean average 
precision) of 0.95 for detecting ripe FFB (fresh fruit bunch) 
from unripe FFB. After the completion of the training the 
model was exported and used in an interface. 
Keywords—Agriculture, Artificial intelligence, 
Computer vision, Ripeness, YOLO 
I. INTRODUCTION
Agriculture has been a cornerstone of human civilization 
for 
approximately 
12,000 
years 
[1], 
and 
its 
importance continues to grow. The agricultural industry is a 
massive and rapidly expanding sector that provides 
essential goods for human consumption. 
In Malaysia palm oil, which is extracted from oil 
palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB), is a significant contributor 
to the country's economy, accounting for 2.7% of the gross 
domestic product (GDP) [2]. The production of vegetable 
oils has seen a significant increase over the past two 
decades, largely driven by the growth of oil palm 
production. According to the Food and 
Agriculture 
Organization of the United Nations, the production of 
vegetable oils has doubled in time period of 2000-2019 
[3].  
Fig. 1 shows the difference between ripe and unripe 
FFB. The ripe FFB tend to be red in color, while the unripe 
FFB are black or green in color. In most cases, the 
difference between the ripeness stages will not be so 
obvious and more careful consideration is demanded to 
determine ripe FFB from unripe FFB, hence the need for 
a new approach to accurately differentiate between ripe 
and unripe FFB. 
To further optimize the benefits of oil palm for 
Malaysia's 
economy 
and 
environment, 
artificial 
intelligence 
(AI) techniques can be applied to various 
aspects of the production process, such as detecting oil 
palm trees, estimating yields, and detecting diseases.  
The goal of the agricultural industry is to produce 
and harvest healthy fruits, which requires accurate 
detection and counting of crops, classification of ripeness 
levels, quality assessment, 
yield 
estimation, 
disease 
detection, 
and 
more. Traditionally, these tasks are 
performed manually by staff or farmers, but this approach 
has several limitations, including:  
•
Time-consuming processes [4]
•
Human errors and subjectivity [5]
•
Labor intensiveness [5]
•
High labor costs [6]
These issues lead to producing unnecessary and avoidable 
waste and destruction of FFB trees that in turn leads to 
negative impacts on the environment. Modern technologies 
like AI can be leveraged to tackle these issues. AI enables 
computers and machines to mimic human problem-solving 
and decision-making capabilities. Machine learning is a 
branch of AI that focuses on using data and algorithms to 
imitate human learning, gradually improving accuracy. Deep 
learning is a sub-field of machine learning that involves neural 
networks with more than three layers [7]. 
This paper aims to analyze the related literature to 
understand the different methods regularly used in the 
literature, and aims to employ YOLOv10, a state-of-the-art AI 
model, to perform ripeness detection of oil palm FFB.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
The analysis of various agricultural products, including oil 
palm fresh fruit bunches, tomatoes, mangoes, bananas, and 
berries, has been explored in the literature.  
Researchers have employed machine learning and deep 
learning approaches to detect and classify these products. For 
instance, Xu et al. [8] proposed a machine learning method 
using Random Forest (RF) algorithm based on improved grid 
search optimization (IGSO-RF) to identify oil palm 
plantations. Another study used a deep learning approach, 
specifically a Faster-RCNN model, for the same task [9].  
In the context of oil palm ripeness detection, Ashari et al. 
[10]
developed a deep learning model based on a
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Meanwhile, other
researchers have utilized various machine learning models,
such as Naive Bayes, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Support

Fruit quality detection has also been achieved using deep

AI methods in agriculture can be broadly categorized into 
two groups: conventional methods and deep learning methods. 
Conventional methods are older computational/statistical 
models such as Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest 
Neighbor (KNN), and others. Deep learning is recent 
approach. It is a subset of machine learning. It encompasses 
models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), 
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), and others.

III. METHODOLOGY 
A. Dataset 
A private dataset has been used for the testings. The 
dataset includes 500 images of FFB in different maturity 
stages. 321 images are provided by Multimedia University 
and the rest are collected from the internet.  
On the next step, the raw images were annotated using the 
“LabelImg” application. In order to detect the ripeness of FFB, 
each of the images of the dataset must be annotated. Data 
annotation involves marking our objects of interest. This 
marking is done by drawing bounding boxes that contain the 
objects. In our case, the FFB on the trees are our focus, so we 
will draw the bounding boxes around them. Data annotation is 
a necessary step that labels and quantifies objects in the 
images and provides essential information for the training of 
the AI models. Fig. 3 shows the image annotation process on 
a sample image. 
 
Fig. 3.  Image annotation. 
B. Coding Environment 
The coding was done in Google Colaboratory. By utilizing 
the GPUs that Google Colaboratory provides, codes can be 
executed much faster.  
C. YOLOv10 
YOLOv10 is the latest release of the renowned YOLO AI 
model series. This model has an improved architecture that 
results in better performance that makes it a state-of-the-art 
model for object detection. It comes in six different sizes nano 
(n), small (s), medium (m), big (b), large (l), and extra-large 
(x). The six different sizes of the model range from 2.3 million 
to 29.5 million parameters, resulting in improved speed and 
accuracy in comparison to the previous releases of YOLO. 
Taking into consideration the computational needs to run 
these models, the testings in in this paper only involves the n, 
s, and m versions of YOLOv10. Table I presents some of the 
important details, specifically the accuracy in terms of mAP 
(mean average precision) and the training speed (latency) of 
these three models attained from experiments with the COCO 
dataset.

As seen in Fig. 4, the architecture of YOLO is comprised 
of three main building blocks:  
1. The backbone which is responsible for extracting 
features from the dataset images. The extracted 
features help identify the visual characteristics of the 
FFB. YOLOv10 includes an enhanced Cross Stage 
Partial Network (CSPNet) for feature extraction. 
2. The neck which combines various features from the 
backbone and infers meaningful insights from the 
dataset. At this stage the YOLO model will be  
familiarized with all the features and visual 
characteristic of the FFB. Path Aggregation Network 
(PAN) layers are utilized for combining the extracted 
features. 
3. The head in which classification of the data occurs 
and bounding boxes are drawn around the detected 
objects. A lightweight classification head is used to 
assign label to objects

YOLOv10 has a few major improvements from the 
previous YOLO models: 
• 
Improvements to the head of the architecture by 
introducing consistent dual assignments for NMS-
free training which improves performance and 
lowers 
inference 
latency. 
Non-maximum 
suppression (NMS) is a post-processing technique 
used in object detection to eliminate duplicate 
detections and select the most relevant detected 
objects.  
• 
Implementing a holistic efficiency-accuracy driven 
model design that optimizes YOLO components for 
better efficiency and accuracy. “Spatial-channel 
decoupled down sampling” is one of the examples of 
this improved design. This sampling method is 
helpful in minimizing information loss and 
computational costs during the feature extraction 
stage. 
• 
YOLOv10 also utilizes large-kernel convolutions 
and partial self-attention modules for improving its 
feature extraction process [16]. Fig. 4 offers an 
overview of the architecture of  YOLOv10.

50 epochs, with batch size of 32.

D. Interface 
After the training of the YOLOv10-S model on the dataset 
is completed, the model will be exported and used in an 
interface for easier and more user friendly accessibility. 
The interface was created using Python’s Streamlit library. 
Streamlit is an open source framework that helps create web 
applications. The app can read and display the uploaded 
images, perform the FFB ripeness detection task by the help 
of the exported YOLOv10-S model, and showcase the 
detection results.  
IV. RESULTS 
The results of the experiments are shown in Table II and 
Fig. 5. Table II presents the results in terms of mean average 
precision (mAP). mAP is the average of the AP values for all 
classes in the dataset. It offers a comprehensive assessment of 
the model's performance across all classes. All the models 
were trained for the same duration of 50 epochs (iterations).   
The YOLOv10-S model had the best performance out of 
the three. It will be saved and exported to be used in the 
interface as seen in Fig. 6. Fig. 5 goes into detail regarding the 
performance of YOLOv10-S. Different evaluation metrics are 
displayed. The top row includes: 
1. Train/box_om: measures the loss for bounding box 
regression during the object matching phase. 
2. Train/cls_om: measures the classification loss during 
object matching. 
3. Train/dfl_om: distribution focal loss during object 
matching. 
4. Train/box_oo: measures the bounding box loss for 
object outputs.  
5. Train/cls_oo: measures the classification loss for 
object outputs. 
The bottom row includes:  
6. Metrics/recall(B): measures how many true positive 
objects were detected. 
7. Metrics/mAP50(B): measures the mean average 
precision (mAP) at an intersection over union (IoU) 
threshold of 0.5.  
8. Metrics/mAP50-95(B): measures the mean average 
precision across different IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 
0.95.  
9. Train/dfl_oo: distribution focal loss for object 
outputs.  
10. Metrics/precision(B): measures how many of the 
detected objects are correctly detected. 
 
In summary, the loss functions (metrics number 1, 2, 3, 4, 
5 and 9) achieved scores of 0.91, 0.47, 1.04, 0.95, 0.43 and 
1.05 respectively. The precision metrics (metrics number 6, 7, 
8 and 10) got scores of 0.87, 0.95, 0.59 and 0.96 respectively.

V. CONCLUSION 
Ripeness detection of FFB using AI is an underexplored 
field that presents opportunities for aiding the palm oil 
industry and market of Malaysia.  
In this project a web application, which can accurately 
detect the ripeness level of FFB, has been created. The 
YOLOv10-S model, which is utilized in the web 
application, achieved R (recall), P (precision) and mAP 
(mean average precision) of 0.87, 0.96 and 0.95 
respectively. An interface has been developed using 
Python’s Streamlit library to improve the accessibility and 
usability of this project.
