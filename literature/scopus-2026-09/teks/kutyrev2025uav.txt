UAV-based sustainable orchard management: 
deep learning for apple detection and yield 
estimation 
Alexey Kutyrev*, Dmitry Khort, Igor Smirnov, and Valeria Zubina 
Federal Scientific Agroengineering Center VIM, 109428 Moscow, Russia 
Abstract. This article presents a method for automated apple counting 
using high-resolution images obtained from unmanned aerial vehicles 
(UAVs). The YOLO11 architecture, specifically models from YOLO11n to 
YOLO11x, was employed for fruit detection. Key steps included creating 
orthophotos, segmenting data into tiles, training a convolutional neural 
network (CNN) with transfer learning and data augmentation, and merging 
results. Images were captured using a DJI Mavic 3 Multispectral drone with 
a 20 MP RGB camera. Data augmentation including flipping, hue 
adjustment, blurring, and Tile 8×8 transformation increased the dataset from 
11 to 2,000 images with 51,797 objects (34,383 apples and 17,414 fallen 
apples). The YOLO11x model achieved the highest performance metrics: 
mAP@50 = 0.816, mAP@50-95 = 0.547, Precision = 0.852, and Recall = 
0.766, demonstrating its effectiveness in complex, high-density orchards. 
The YOLO11n model, with lower computational demands, is suitable for 
resource-limited environments. The method maintains geospatial alignment 
and visualizes fruit distribution across the orchard. An experimentally 
determined correction coefficient will account for fruits hidden from the 
camera, enhancing the accuracy of yield estimation. A Tkinter interface 
displays detection results and summary data for each orchard section. Future 
work includes integrating multispectral data and 3D modeling to enhance 
precision. These findings highlight the potential of deep learning to automate 
orchard monitoring and yield assessment. 
1 Introduction 
Traditional methods for inspecting orchards to assess yields rely on visual surveys 
conducted by agronomists. The main drawback of this approach is its subjectivity: human 
factors lead to significant errors in yield forecasting [1]. This reduces the accuracy and 
reproducibility of results and makes the process labor-intensive and inefficient on a 
production scale. Various technical solutions have been proposed to address these limitations. 
Ground sensors and stationary RGB cameras enable continuous monitoring of specific 
orchard areas but are limited in coverage and flexibility for data collection [2]. Hyperspectral 
and thermal imaging facilitate early detection of plant stress and diseases but require complex 
equipment and specialized data processing techniques [3,4]. Laser scanning (LiDAR)

provides highly accurate information about tree structures and biomass, but its use is 
constrained by high costs and complex data interpretation [5].  
In recent years, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have become widely used in agriculture 
due to their ability to collect data quickly and accurately, advancing precision farming 
technologies. UAVs automate the monitoring of plant conditions, disease diagnostics, and 
yield estimation [6]. However, most existing approaches to remote plant monitoring using 
UAVs are optimized for field crops or forests, making them less suitable for intensive 
orchards with high planting density. 
Monitoring fruit orchards, particularly apple orchards, imposes specific requirements on 
data collection and processing technologies. UAVs are especially valuable for apple 
orchards, where high accuracy is needed to assess the condition of individual trees and fruits 
for yield prediction [7]. Intensive orchards with high planting density and regular planting 
structures pose challenges for fruit detection due to light interactions with canopies, 
overlapping fruits and leaves, and complex plant textures. Additionally, lighting variations 
and shadows can significantly affect image quality, complicating object recognition [8]. 
Effective use of UAV data requires modern image processing and analysis methods. 
Various computer vision and machine learning algorithms, including segmentation and 
classification, have been applied for plant identification and condition assessment [9]. Deep 
learning methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), excel in these tasks by 
automatically extracting features and adapting to varying imaging conditions [10]. YOLO 
(You Only Look Once) architectures stand out for their high speed and accuracy, which are 
critical for processing large datasets collected by UAVs [11-14]. Other models, such as Faster 
R-CNN, SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) [14], and RetinaNet [13] have also 
demonstrated high accuracy for agricultural object detection using UAVs. Transfer learning 
methods [18] further enhance detection performance by pre-training models on large datasets. 
CNN-based models and their architectures are widely used to detect and count fruits in 
orchards, including apples, citrus, and vineyards. These models enable accurate detection of 
small objects in high-resolution images, compensating for detection errors caused by scene 
complexity and spatial variations [11]. To improve model accuracy under varying lighting 
conditions and object orientations, preprocessing techniques such as perspective correction 
and distortion removal from UAV altitude changes are employed [8]. These approaches 
significantly improve detection in dense canopies where fruits are partially obscured by 
leaves [8]. Thus, developing adapted methods for high-resolution orchard images obtained 
from UAVs is a relevant and timely task. 
This study aims to develop a method for automated apple counting in high-resolution 
UAV images using the YOLO11 architecture to improve accuracy and efficiency in industrial 
orchard analysis. 
2 Materials and methods 
A comprehensive method has been developed for automated apple counting on high-
resolution images obtained using UAVs. The method consists of several stages, each aimed 
at recognizing apple fruits in the conditions of an industrial orchard. 
At the first stage (data preprocessing), aerial photographs obtained from UAVs were used 
to create high-resolution orthophotomaps using Agisoft Metashape software. The DJI Mavic 
3 Multispectral (Mavic 3M) was used for the survey; only the RGB camera with a CMOS 
sensor (4/3", 20 MP), a focal length of 24 mm, an aperture of f/2.8, a field of view of 84°, 
and a maximum resolution of 5280×3956 pixels was utilized. The images were captured at 
an altitude of 30–60 meters under natural lighting conditions (clear weather, daytime), 
enabling detailed aerial photographs with sufficient resolution for detecting small objects, 
suitable for subsequent photogrammetric processing and analysis.

The Agisoft Metashape software enabled photogrammetric processing of the images, 
including image alignment, dense point cloud generation, and orthomosaic creation. During 
the image alignment process, algorithms for matching key points and camera optimization 
were used to ensure accurate image alignment. As a result, an orthophotomap of the orchard 
was generated, providing a detailed representation of the study area (an industrial orchard 
with ripened apple fruits) (fig.1).

At the second stage, to optimize subsequent processing and reduce computational load, 
the high-resolution orthophotomap (GeoTIFF .tif format) was divided into fragments of fixed 
size. This process was carried out using a Python script with the PIL (Python Imaging 
Library) library. The division of the image into tiles is described by formula 1:

image division. 
The parameters of the division included tile sizes of 200×200 pixels to maintain a balance 
between image detail and the volume of data for processing. To ensure correct positioning 
during subsequent stitching, each tile was assigned a unique name containing information 
about its position along the X and Y axes on the original orthophotomap image, which 
facilitates further processing and subsequent frame stitching. 
At the third stage, a trained convolutional neural network model based on the YOLO11 
architecture was applied to each of the obtained image fragments for apple fruit detection. 
The model was implemented using the PyTorch framework and trained with the application 
of transfer learning methods to improve training efficiency. Image annotation was performed 
using a bounding box tool. Two classes were designated as detection targets: apples on the 
tree, labeled as the «apple» class, and fallen apples, labeled as the «fallen_apple» class. 
Annotation was performed manually using the Roboflow service (Bounding Boxes tool), 
which ensured high-quality data required for training the model (fig. 2).

To expand the dataset, eliminate class imbalance, and increase the model's robustness to 
changes in lighting and object orientation, image augmentation was performed using the 
following methods: Tile 8x8 grid transformation, horizontal and vertical flipping (flip 
horizontal and flip vertical), hue adjustment with an intensity of up to 15%, and image 
blurring (Blur) with a coefficient of 1%. These methods allowed for diversifying the original 
data, increasing its volume, and improving the model's robustness to object variations in real-
world conditions (fig.3). 
 
Fig. 3. Methods of Artificial Data Augmentation. 
As a result of applying augmentation methods, the original dataset, consisting of 11 high-
resolution images of 5280×3956 pixels, was expanded to 2000 images, significantly 
increasing the volume and diversity of the training data and improving the model's 
generalization ability. The total number of labeled objects for training amounted to 51,797, 
of which 34,383 belong to the «apple» class (apples on the tree) and 17,414 to the 
«fallen_apple» class (fallen apples). For training the model, pre-trained YOLO11 models 
were used, including YOLO11n (39.5% mAPval, 2.6M parameters, 6.5 GFLOPs), YOLO11s 
(47.0% mAPval, 9.4M, 21.5 GFLOPs), YOLO11m (51.5% mAPval, 20.1M, 68 GFLOPs), 
YOLO11l (53.4% mAPval, 25.3M, 86.9 GFLOPs), and YOLO11x (54.7% mAPval, 56.9M, 
194.9 GFLOPs).  
For optimizing the hyperparameters of the YOLO11 model, a batch size of 12, a learning 
rate of 0.1, and 1500 epochs were used. To prevent model overfitting, early stopping was 
applied when no improvement in metrics was observed on the validation dataset. To eliminate

At the fourth stage, after detecting apples on individual tiles, the images were merged 
back into a single orthophotomap with the identified objects (apple fruits) highlighted by 
bounding boxes. This process was performed using a Python script with the PIL and OS 
libraries for image processing and file management. During stitching, the tile coordinates 
extracted from their filenames were used, ensuring accurate restoration of the geometry of 
the original image. Overlaps between tiles were taken into account to correctly align the 
detected objects. To prevent double counting errors, intersection elimination algorithms were 
applied. During stitching, the data on the number of detected fruits obtained in the previous 
stage were summed. The total number of apples Ntotal was calculated as the sum of apples

3 Results and discussion 
As a result of training the YOLO11n-x models, an evaluation of the Precision, Recall, and 
mean Average Precision (mAP) metrics was conducted at various IoU thresholds to analyze 
the accuracy and completeness of object classification. The mAP metric was calculated at 
standard IoU thresholds, including mAP@50 and mAP@50-95, providing a comprehensive 
assessment of the model's performance. On the mAP50-Iteration graph, which illustrates 
changes in average precision at a fixed IoU threshold of 50, it is observed that the YOLO11l 
and YOLO11x models achieve the highest values (around 0.8). This indicates their high 
ability to accurately detect objects in the images. The YOLO11n and YOLO11s models, 
which have simplified architectures, show lower average precision values (fig.4).

partially occluded or poorly visible objects. Models of lower complexity (YOLO11n,

loss reduction and less accurate results, but their use is justified in conditions with limited

4 Conclusions 
The developed method provides automated apple counting in industrial orchards with high 
planting density and challenging shooting conditions, using data obtained from UAVs. The 
application of the YOLO11 model at the stage of processing individual tiles allowed for 
reducing computational load while maintaining high detection accuracy due to detailed 
analysis of each image fragment. Post-processing, including the merging of detections, 
ensures the integrity of geospatial information, enabling the visualization of fruit distribution 
across the entire orchard area. This approach reduces labor costs, enhances the scalability of 
monitoring, and improves analysis accuracy, making it an effective tool for yield assessment 
and informed decision-making. 
Future research directions include the integration of multispectral data to enhance 
detection and achieve more accurate classification. Additionally, the introduction of a 
correction coefficient is proposed, determined by comparing the number of fruits recognized 
in the images with the actual number of fruits on the tree. The correction coefficient will be 
calculated experimentally based on field observations, including the quantitative correlation 
of photographic data with the results of manual fruit counting on a representative sample of 
trees. This will allow for accounting for fruits hidden from the camera and improving the 
accuracy of the final yield estimation. To consider all factors, including hidden fruits, the

of vertical shooting, it is proposed to supplement the data with images taken at an angle 
relative to the tree canopy. This approach will allow capturing additional areas of the trees, 
including partially hidden fruits, and using this data to build 3D models of the trees with the 
application of dense point cloud algorithms available in Agisoft Metashape. This will provide 
a more comprehensive representation of the canopy structure, improve object detection, and 
enhance the accuracy of analysis in dense plantings.
