Abstract 
To better address the accuracy bottleneck of RGB-only Camellia oleifera C.Abel fruit detec-
tion in complex orchard environments, this paper proposes CIAFNet—a dual-stream 
RGB-D fusion detection network—and evaluates its potential as a visual front end for rel-
ative 3D localization using sensor-measured depth and camera back projection under con-
trolled conditions. With RGB images and depth maps as parallel dual-branch inputs, the 
network integrates the C3k2_PartialNetBlock for efficient intra-modal feature extraction 
with reduced computational redundancy, devises the cross-modal interaction and differ-
ence-aware adaptive fusion (CIDAF) module for adaptive cross-modal feature fusion, and 
adopts an SC-EUCB-augmented BiFPN in the neck to optimize multiscale feature aggre-
gation and detail restoration during upsampling. Pseudo-depth maps generated from nat-
ural orchard RGB images via Depth Anything V2 were paired with RGB counterparts to 
build an RGB–pseudo-depth dataset. Synchronized RGB-D data collected by an Intel Re-
alSense D435i under controlled conditions were used to quantify pseudo-to-sensor depth 
discrepancies and evaluate input adaptability. On the natural orchard test set, CIAFNet 
achieved 93.33% mAP@0.5 with only 10.49 GFLOPs and 3.80 M parameters. Second-stage 
fine-tuning improved CIAFNet’s adaptation to D435i-measured depth under controlled 
conditions. In the subsequent relative displacement consistency experiment, the mean ab-
solute consistency errors along the X, Y, and Z axes were 3.10, 3.15, and 3.27 mm, respec-
tively, and the mean 3D Euclidean consistency error was 5.60 mm. These results demon-
strate that CIAFNet improves Camellia oleifera fruit detection using natural orchard RGB–
pseudo-depth data and has potential as a visual front end for relative 3D localization un-
der controlled conditions. 
Keywords: Camellia oleifera fruit; CIAFNet; RGB-D object detection; cross-modal fusion; 
depth domain adaptation; 3D localization

processed into tea oil of considerable nutritional and commercial value [1]. As plantation 
areas expand and seasonal labor costs rise, manual harvesting increasingly fails to meet

stability. Selective harvesting robots offer a route towards mechanized and intelligent har-
vesting of high-value crops, for which target recognition, spatial localization, path plan-
ning and end-effector operation are tightly coupled. In natural orchards, variation in illu-
mination, foliage occlusion, target overlap and restricted working spaces jointly compro-
mise visual perception. Previous studies show that detection must be coupled with relia-

whereas occlusion, overlapping targets, and localization errors can reduce grasping suc-
cess [3]. A harvesting-oriented vision system must therefore identify fruit accurately and 
provide stable target regions for subsequent spatial perception. 
Deep learning-based object detection is widely used for agricultural target recogni-
tion, fruit counting, phenotyping and robotic harvesting [4]. Advances in feature repre-
sentation, multiscale modeling, and real-time inference have established a strong algorith-
mic basis for target recognition in complex agricultural scenes [5]. Real-time detectors in 
the YOLO family are particularly suitable for agricultural deployment because they bal-
ance speed and accuracy [6], and lightweight architectures and attention mechanisms can 
further improve detection under occlusion, small target, and cluttered background condi-
tions [7]. Nevertheless, small target size, mutual occlusion, and background clutter remain 
major sources of false positives and missed detections in RGB-only fruit detection in nat-
ural orchards [8]. These limitations are especially pronounced for Camellia oleifera fruit. 
Severe foliage occlusion, marked scale variation, and complex background structures 
make it difficult for RGB appearance features alone to distinguish these fruits from foliage, 
background regions, and adjacent fruit [9,10]. Multiscale feature fusion can improve the 
representation of objects at different scales [11], and related agricultural detectors have 
employed feature pyramids and lightweight feature-processing modules to preserve 
small target details [12]. However, these methods primarily reorganize appearance fea-
tures and may remain limited when RGB information alone cannot distinguish fruit from 
foliage, background regions, or adjacent targets [13]. 
RGB-D vision provides a complementary perception pathway for object detection 
and spatial localization. RGB images primarily encode color, texture, and semantic ap-
pearance, whereas depth maps can provide relative structural information, front–back or-
dering, and target boundary cues. Synchronized RGB-D data can also support spatial es-
timation for robotic operation [14]. The complementarity between RGB and depth may 
help reduce appearance ambiguity caused by partial occlusion, adjacent fruit overlap, and 
similarity between fruit and foliage. Noise-tolerant RGB-D fusion has consequently been 
investigated for outdoor fruit detection, with particular attention to the influence of unre-
liable depth measurements [15]. RGB-D feature fusion has also been applied to the detec-
tion of overlapping tomato clusters in complex agricultural scenes [16]. In addition, mul-
timodal image information has been explored for recognizing Camellia oleifera fruits in 
natural environments [17]. These studies suggest that structural information can comple-
ment RGB appearance in agricultural perception, although the resulting performance de-
pends on how heterogeneous features are represented and fused. 
From the perspective of multimodal learning, simply introducing an additional mo-
dality does not necessarily yield a more discriminative representation. RGB and depth 
encode different types of information, and their reliability may vary across spatial loca-
tions. Early RGB-D detection research demonstrated the importance of learning modality-
specific and shared feature representations rather than directly treating the two modalities 
as homogeneous inputs [18]. More recent RGB-D representation-learning studies have 
further emphasized the need to coordinate modality-specific feature extraction with cross-
modal interaction [19]. In agricultural scenes, task-specific fusion architectures have been 
investigated to coordinate complementary appearance and structural information [20]. At 
a broader methodological level, token-level cross-modal exchange provides a mechanism

for selectively replacing less informative features with information from another modality 
[21]. Cross-modal feature rectification and fusion have also been introduced to reduce the 
influence of uncertain auxiliary modality responses before multimodal aggregation [22]. 
Existing RGB-D fusion methods generally employ feature concatenation, element-
wise addition, attention-based weighting, or explicit cross-modal interaction [23]. Concat-
enation stacks RGB and depth features along the channel dimension and relies on subse-
quent convolutions to learn their joint representation, whereas element-wise addition di-
rectly superimposes spatially aligned responses. Attention-based methods reweight chan-
nels, spatial locations, or modalities, while interaction-based methods exchange or rectify 
information between modality-specific branches before aggregation. The limitations of 
these approaches arise from the spatially nonuniform reliability of RGB and depth infor-
mation. Direct concatenation and addition cannot distinguish useful geometric cues from 
background interference [24]. Attention-based weighting improves fusion selectivity [25]; 
however, when the weights are estimated mainly from individual modalities or coarse 
global statistics, local agreement and conflict between spatially aligned RGB and depth 
features may remain insufficiently represented [26]. Overall, some existing RGB-D fusion 
strategies remain limited in explicitly modeling local agreement and discrepancy between 
RGB and depth features and using these relationships to guide fusion decisions. Conse-
quently, they may have difficulty preserving complementary structural information while 
effectively suppressing unreliable depth responses. 
Monocular depth estimation provides another means of introducing structural infor-
mation when synchronized sensor depth is unavailable. Relative and metric depth esti-
mation methods can infer spatial relationships from individual RGB images [27]. Depth 
Anything and Depth Anything V2 further improve the generalization of monocular depth 
estimation through large-scale training [28,29]. Recent surveys have summarized the de-
velopment and remaining limitations of monocular depth estimation [30]. Other studies 
have investigated universal metric-depth estimation and monocular geometric founda-
tion models [31,32], while diffusion-based depth prediction provides an additional esti-
mation framework [33]. Nevertheless, DA-V2 pseudo-depth should not be regarded as 
sensor-measured metric depth. In this study, it is used as a relative structural prior for 
natural orchard RGB images that were acquired without synchronized depth measure-
ments. Moreover, pseudo-depth and D435i-measured depth differ in representation, edge 
continuity, missing value patterns, and noise characteristics. Sensor-measured depth can 
itself contain holes and incomplete boundary information, as also discussed in depth com-
pletion research [34]. Camellia oleifera fruit perception therefore presents two related chal-
lenges: effective fusion of RGB and pseudo-depth during detection, and reduction of the 
input mismatch between pseudo-depth-based training and deployment with measured 
depth. 
To address these challenges, this study developed CIAFNet, an RGB-D dual-stream 
detector integrating the C3k2_PartialNetBlock for lightweight intra-modal feature extrac-
tion, CIDAF for cross-modal interaction and adaptive fusion, and an SC-EUCB-enhanced 
BiFPN for multiscale feature aggregation. 
The specific objectives of this study were as follows: (1) to investigate whether DA-
V2 pseudo-depth can provide complementary structural information for Camellia oleifera 
fruit detection using natural orchard RGB images; (2) to assess the detection performance 
of CIAFNet using RGB–pseudo-depth data and its adaptation from DA-V2 pseudo-depth 
to D435i-measured depth under controlled conditions; and (3) to evaluate the potential of 
CIAFNet detections as a visual front end for relative 3D localization under controlled con-
ditions. The overall study design and experimental workflow are summarized in Figure

2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. Dataset Acquisition and Construction 
Two complementary datasets were constructed. The natural orchard RGB–pseudo-
depth dataset comprised RGB images acquired with an iPhone 13 (Apple Inc., Cupertino,

CA, USA) in real orchard environments and corresponding pseudo-depth maps gener-
ated by Depth Anything V2. It was used for CIAFNet training, model comparison, abla-
tion studies, and pseudo-depth-assisted detection evaluation. The measured RGB-D da-
taset was acquired with an Intel RealSense D435i (Intel Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, 
USA) under controlled conditions and was used for second-stage fine-tuning and con-
trolled domain evaluation of adaptation from DA-V2 pseudo-depth to sensor-measured 
depth. 
Natural orchard data were collected during the 2024 Camellia oleifera fruit harvesting 
season at a plantation in Xian’an District, Xianning, Hubei Province, China. Images were 
acquired with an iPhone 13 in unstructured orchard environments without artificial con-
trol of illumination, background, or fruit pose. The dataset encompassed front lighting, 
backlighting, foliage occlusion, fruit overlap, and variable imaging distances. In total, 1758 
RGB images containing 12,496 target instances were obtained. All instances were anno-
tated as Camellia oleifera fruit. Bounding boxes were manually annotated using T-Rex La-
bel (https://trexlabel.com; accessed on 9 April 2026) and stored in YOLO format. The da-
taset was divided into mutually exclusive training, validation, and test subsets at approx-
imately 70%, 15%, and 15%, comprising 1231, 264, and 263 images, respectively. 
The natural orchard dataset was originally collected as an RGB image dataset using 
an iPhone 13, without the synchronous deployment of a D435i or any other depth sensor 
during field acquisition. Consequently, no sensor-measured depth data were spatially and 
temporally aligned with the existing RGB frames, and such data cannot be retrospectively 
obtained for the same acquisition scenes. To enable these natural orchard RGB images to 
be used for dual-stream training, a pseudo-depth map was generated offline for each RGB 
image using the official Depth Anything V2 implementation with the ViT-L encoder 
(https://github.com/DepthAnything/Depth-Anything-V2; accessed on 15 April 2026). The 
target inference size was set to 1024 pixels, with the original aspect ratio preserved and 
the resized dimensions adjusted to multiples of 14. The predicted relative depth map was 
restored to the original RGB image resolution using bilinear interpolation. For each depth 
map, values outside the 1st–99th percentile range were clipped, followed by min–max 
normalization to map the values to [0, 1]. The normalized depth maps were stored and 
loaded as 8-bit single-channel grayscale images, and each pseudo-depth map was paired 
one-to-one with its corresponding RGB image. The resulting maps encode relative depth 
relationships and spatial structure rather than physical depth on an absolute metric scale. 
Accordingly, pseudo-depth is treated as a DA-V2-derived relative structural prior that 
helps distinguish structural differences among Camellia oleifera fruit, foliage, background 
regions, and adjacent fruit; it is not interpreted as a direct measurement of absolute dis-
tance. Because each pseudo-depth map is deterministically inferred from its paired RGB 
image, RGB and pseudo-depth should be understood as two representations of the same 
visual observation rather than two statistically independent sensing modalities. There-
fore, while the depth branch extracts scene-related relative structural information, its 
learned representation may also be influenced by characteristics of the DA-V2 outputs, 
including their relative value distributions, spatial smoothness, and boundary continuity 
patterns. 
An additional controlled scene RGB-D dataset was acquired with the D435i. The ex-
perimental targets consisted of artificial Camellia oleifera fruit models and cut Camellia oleif-
era branches rather than live plants in a natural orchard. These materials were arranged 
to simulate fruit targets and foliage structures under controlled conditions. The dataset 
contained 352 synchronized RGB-D image pairs, which were divided into approximately 
60%, 20%, and 20% for training, validation, and test subsets of 211, 70, and 71 pairs, re-
spectively. After this split, offline augmentation was applied only to the training subset 
and comprised random cropping, horizontal flipping, and random brightness

2.2. CIAFNet Architecture 
2.2.1. Overall Architecture 
CIAFNet is an RGB-D dual-stream network for Camellia oleifera fruit detection in nat-
ural orchards. The network comprises an RGB branch, a depth branch, a CIDAF cross-
modal interaction and fusion module, an SC-EUCB-enhanced BiFPN neck, and a detection 
head. The RGB branch captures appearance cues, including color, texture, and edges, 
whereas the depth branch captures relative spatial structure among targets, foliage, back-
ground regions, and adjacent fruit. The two modalities are not simply stacked; instead, 
they are selectively fused by CIDAF according to their local complementarity, thereby 
imposing structural constraints on target representations under occlusion, fruit overlap, 
and background interference. The overall architecture is shown in Figure 3.

During dual-branch feature extraction, C3k2_PartialNetBlock is inserted at the high-
level stages to reduce redundant channel computation and strengthen intra-modal repre-
sentations. Each branch produces features at three scales: P3, P4, and P5. P3 and P4 are 
passed through CIDAF to couple mid- and low-level detail with local spatial structure. P5 
features are concatenated along the channel dimension and then processed by C3k2 and 
C2PSA to preserve high-level semantic representations. This hierarchical design treats 
mid- and low-level features that require fine geometric constraints differently from high-
level features that require stable semantic abstraction. The dual-backbone RGB-D feature 
extraction architecture is shown in Figure 4. 
CIDAF provides the principal mechanism for RGB-D interaction in CIAFNet. Chan-
nel alignment, discrepancy awareness, consistency modeling, and adaptive gating are 
used to fuse complementary information selectively and to allow depth structure to con-
tribute directly to target representation during detection. The fused multiscale features 
are then supplied to a BiFPN-based neck augmented with SC-EUCB. BiFPN performs bi-
directional weighted cross-scale fusion, whereas SC-EUCB improves preservation of local 
detail and transfer of spatial information during upsampling, thereby strengthening rep-
resentations of Camellia oleifera fruit boundaries, small targets, and occluded regions. The 
detection head finally outputs class labels, confidence scores, and bounding boxes.

2.2.2. RGB-D Dual-Backbone Feature Extraction 
Natural orchard images of Camellia oleifera fruit frequently contain foliage occlusion, 
overlapping fruit, weakly textured targets, and complex background interference. Alt-
hough dual RGB-D backbones introduce complementary structural information, they also 
increase computational cost. To balance representational capacity and efficiency, 
C3k2_PartialNetBlock is inserted into the high-level feature extraction stages of both the 
RGB and depth branches. The module follows the partial channel processing strategy of 
PartialNetBlockV1 in the Partial Channel Network [35] and is integrated with the C3k2 
structure to reduce redundant spatial computation while retaining compact intra-modal 
representations before CIDAF fusion. 
PartialNetBlockV1 exploits redundancy among feature channels by partitioning the 
input along the channel dimension and processing the resulting branches with different 
operations. For an input with Cin channels and a partial channel ratio rp, approximately Cin 
× rp channels are assigned to the spatial convolution branch, whereas the remaining Cin × 
(1 − rp) channels retain channel context and undergo response modulation. The spatial 
branch applies a 3 × 3 convolution to extract local information, including fruit edges, tex-
ture, occlusion boundaries, and depth contours. The complementary branch retains a 
larger proportion of the channel context and uses global statistics-based response modu-
lation to strengthen inter-channel representation. Unlike lightweight schemes that con-
volve only a subset of channels and leave the remainder unchanged, PartialNetBlockV1 
preserves informative statistics in unconvolved channels. This reduces redundant dense 
convolution computation while maintaining a balance between computational efficiency 
and feature representation. 
The partial channel ratio rp controls the proportion of channels assigned to spatial 
convolution and therefore affects the balance between local feature extraction and com-
putational efficiency. To examine its influence on C3k2_PartialNetBlock, rp values of 1/8, 
1/4, and 1/2 were evaluated under the same training configuration. Based on the

comparative results presented in Section 3.3, rp = 1/4 was retained as the selected accuracy–

In C3k2_PartialNetBlock, partial channel processing is embedded within the C3k2 
module. Input features first pass through the standard C3k2 transformation path to model 
local structure and cross-channel relationships. A subset of channels then enters the spa-
tial convolution branch to strengthen local detail, whereas the complementary branch re-
tains and modulates channel context. The branch features are subsequently concatenated 
and reorganized using a 1 × 1 convolution to promote information exchange. This design 
preserves the feature extraction capacity of C3k2 while reducing redundancy through se-
lective spatial computation. The module is illustrated in Figure 5.

In CIAFNet, C3k2_PartialNetBlock is applied to the high-level feature extraction 
stages of both the RGB and depth backbones. In the RGB branch, it improves discrimina-
tion based on color, texture, and edge cues. In the depth branch, it emphasizes structural 
differences among fruit, foliage, background regions, and adjacent fruit. The resulting 
compact multiscale features provide more discriminative inputs for CIDAF to determine 
which depth information should be retained. 
2.2.3. CIDAF Cross-Modal Interaction and Adaptive Fusion 
CIDAF is designed to promote effective interaction and complementary information 
exchange between RGB and depth features. In natural orchards, RGB features may be af-
fected by similarly colored foliage, shadows, and backlighting, whereas depth features 
may vary in their local reliability and discriminative value, particularly around target 
boundaries, occluded regions, and complex backgrounds. Conventional concatenation 
and element-wise addition directly aggregate the two feature streams without explicitly 
assessing their local relationships. Attention-based fusion can improve selectivity; how-
ever, some methods based primarily on independently estimated, global, or modality-
level weights may not explicitly distinguish local cross-modal agreement from conflict. In 
contrast, CIDAF first aligns the RGB and depth features and explicitly constructs discrep-
ancy and consistency representations from the paired features. These representations 
jointly generate spatially adaptive gates for the RGB and depth streams, after which a 
complementary residual branch restores information that may be weakened during gated 
fusion. Therefore, the principal design distinction of CIDAF lies in using explicitly

on direct aggregation or modality reweighting.

2.2.4. Multiscale Feature-Fusion Neck 
Natural orchard images can contain distant small targets, nearby overlapping fruit, 
and foliage-occluded targets within the same frame, producing substantial variation in 
target scale and local boundary quality. The neck must therefore fuse cross-scale semantic 
and localization information while preserving fruit boundaries, occlusion gaps, and local 
structure during upsampling. BiFPN is adopted for multiscale fusion, and its upsampling 
pathway is augmented with SC-EUCB to form an SC-EUCB-enhanced BiFPN neck. BiFPN 
propagates P3, P4, and P5 features through top-down and bottom-up pathways and uses 
learnable weights to regulate the contribution of each scale, thereby linking high-level se-
mantics with low-level localization detail. Weighted fusion is calculated as follows:

feature is first upsampled by a factor of two to restore spatial resolution, after which a 3 ×

low computational cost. Channel shuffling and ShiftChannelMix are then applied. The

For Camellia oleifera fruit detection, SC-EUCB contributes at two levels. First, depth-
wise convolution after upsampling reinforces local structure and helps recover fine-
grained responses along small target boundaries and within occluded regions. Second, 
ShiftChannelMix expands local information exchange through lightweight shift-based 
mixing, thereby preserving boundary differences between densely distributed fruit and 
spatial gaps between fruit and foliage during cross-scale fusion. The combination of SC-
EUCB and BiFPN therefore balances global scale selection with local detail recovery and 
supplies the detection head with more stable and discriminative multiscale features.

2.3.2. Relative Displacement Consistency Test 
A relative displacement consistency test was performed to evaluate the stability and 
spatial consistency of 3D coordinates derived from CIAFNet detections. Because the true 
absolute 3D coordinates of Camellia oleifera fruit relative to the camera are difficult to meas-
ure directly in natural orchards or simulated scenes, a single coordinate output cannot 
establish reliability. Coordinate changes were therefore compared with a known camera 
translation. During each experimental group, five model Camellia oleifera fruit targets ar-
ranged on a simulated branch remained stationary, whereas only the D435i camera mount 
was moved. The camera coordinates of the same target were recorded before and after 
movement. Because coordinates are expressed in the camera coordinate system, the ex-
pected change in target coordinates is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the 
prescribed camera translation. The difference between the observed and expected target 
coordinate changes was defined as the relative displacement consistency error, which 
quantifies the stability and spatial consistency of the visual front end rather than absolute 
positional accuracy. 
The test was performed as follows. The Camellia oleifera fruit model or branch was 
first fixed. The D435i pose was then adjusted until the target was within the field of view 
and could be detected and localized stably in 3D by CIAFNet. The 3D coordinates of each 
target before camera movement were recorded as the initial coordinates. While the target 
remained stationary, the camera mount was translated along a predefined combined di-
rection, with prescribed displacement components of 100 mm along the horizontal, verti-
cal, and depth axes. A second RGB-D image was then acquired, and the coordinates of the 
same target were recorded. The observed coordinate changes along each axis were com-
pared with the corresponding expected target coordinate changes induced by the camera 
translation to obtain the relative displacement consistency error. Ten experimental groups 
were conducted under controlled conditions. In each group, the coordinates of five model 
fruit targets were recorded before and after the prescribed camera translation, yielding 50 
target-level relative displacement measurements in total. Group-wise errors and pooled 
descriptive results across all target observations were calculated. This experiment evalu-
ated the consistency of coordinate changes under prescribed camera translation rather 
than the absolute positioning accuracy of individual targets.

2.4. Experimental Environment and Training Settings 
All models were trained and tested in the same hardware and software environment 
to ensure comparability and reproducibility. An identical base configuration, including 
input resolution, number of training epochs, batch size, optimizer, and initial learning 
rate, was used for model comparisons, ablation experiments, and analyses of RGB-D mo-
dality effectiveness. The experimental environment and training settings are listed in Ta-
ble 1. 
A two-stage training procedure was used to separate the learning of natural orchard 
appearance and DA-V2-derived relative structural priors from adaptation to D435i-meas-
ured depth. Throughout this study, CIAFNet denotes the unchanged network architec-
ture. In the first stage, CIAFNet was trained using natural orchard RGB images and their 
DA-V2 pseudo-depth maps to establish the RGB–pseudo-depth detection representation; 
the resulting model state is referred to as CIAFNet (Stage 1). In the second stage, the best 
CIAFNet (Stage 1) weights were loaded and fine-tuned using RGB-D data acquired with 
the D435i under controlled conditions; the resulting measured depth-adapted model state 
is referred to as CIAFNet (Stage 2). Thus, CIAFNet (Stage 1) and CIAFNet (Stage 2) rep-
resent two sequential training states of the same architecture rather than two different 
network structures. This sequential configuration was adopted according to the different 
data sources and depth input domains and is not claimed to be a globally optimal training 
schedule. In the second stage, the initial learning rate was reduced from 0.01 to 0.001 to 
allow conservative parameter updates while retaining the representations learned during 
the first stage. The maximum number of training epochs was kept at 150, consistent with 
Stage 1, to maintain a comparable training budget across the two stages. Only the RGB 
backbone was frozen during the second stage; the depth branch was not frozen. Freezing 
the RGB backbone was intended to preserve the appearance representations learned from 
the larger natural orchard dataset and to reduce the risk of overfitting to the smaller con-
trolled scene D435i dataset or excessively modifying the previously learned appearance 
representation. The depth branch, CIDAF fusion module, SC-EUCB-enhanced BiFPN 
neck, and detection head remained trainable so that the depth-related representations and 
fusion weights could adapt to the statistical characteristics of D435i-measured depth, in-
cluding sensor noise, missing values, and edge discontinuities. The remaining training 
settings were kept unchanged. Only the RGB images and their synchronized D435i-meas-
ured depth maps were used in the second stage; DA-V2 pseudo-depth and measured 
depth were not introduced simultaneously as additional channels. 
The network-only inference efficiency of CIAFNet was evaluated on the workstation 
listed in Table 1. Before inference, the convolution and batch normalization layers were 
fused. All measurements were conducted using FP16 precision with a batch size of 1. Each 
input consisted of paired RGB and depth images resized to 640 × 640 pixels, forming a six-
channel RGB–depth input. To minimize initialization and GPU warm-up effects, 100 pre-
liminary forward passes were performed before timing. The latency was then measured 
over 1000 forward passes, with CUDA synchronization applied during timing to ensure 
accurate GPU latency measurement.

3.6. Relative Three-Dimensional Localization Consistency Under Controlled Conditions 
Following validation of the detection performance, CIAFNet (Stage 2), obtained by

group, five model Camellia oleifera fruit targets arranged on a simulated branch remained 
stationary, while the D435i camera was translated along the prescribed three-axis direc-
tion. The coordinates of the five targets were recorded before and after camera translation, 
yielding 50 target-level coordinate comparisons. The observed coordinate changes were 
compared with the expected changes induced by the prescribed camera translation. The 
experimental setup and an example of the coordinate output are shown in Figure 15, and 
the relative displacement consistency errors under controlled conditions are reported in 
Table 9.

4. Discussion 
The central finding is that pseudo-depth improves Camellia oleifera fruit detection in 
natural orchards not by replacing RGB information but by providing relative structural 
cues that can be exploited selectively when appearance cues are ambiguous. CIAFNet em-
beds this prior within the detector rather than using it only after detection for coordinate 
calculation. Under the modality-matched RGB–pseudo-depth setting, CIAFNet (Stage 1) 
improved mAP@0.5:0.95 by 2.78 percentage points compared with YOLO-RGBDtea, the 
strongest multimodal baseline in Table 2. Separately, the within-framework comparison 
in Table 6 showed that CIAFNet (Stage 1) improved mAP@0.5:0.95 by 4.73 percentage 

Agriculture 2026, 16, 1884 
29 of 33 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture16171884 
points over the RGB-only configuration. Pseudo-depth performed poorly when used 
alone but improved performance when combined with RGB; the gain is therefore more 
plausibly attributed to complementarity between appearance and structural cues than to 
replacement by either modality. Through discrepancy awareness, consistency modeling, 
and gated allocation, CIDAF enables selective use of depth structure around fruit bound-
aries, occlusion edges, and adjacent targets while suppressing unreliable responses 
caused by noise or holes. The ablation results, feature separability metrics, and visualiza-
tions collectively support this interpretation, although none independently proves every 
internal mechanism. 
The experiments also revealed a substantial input mismatch between training with 
pseudo-depth and deployment with measured depth. DA-V2 pseudo-depth encodes rel-
ative structural relationships and can therefore support training on natural orchard im-
ages acquired without synchronized depth. Because these pseudo-depth maps are deter-
ministically predicted from the corresponding RGB images rather than obtained through 
independent geometric measurements, their relative value distributions, boundary conti-
nuity, and noise characteristics may differ substantially from those of D435i-measured 
depth. The current results also cannot fully exclude the possibility that the depth branch 
partly relies on statistical patterns specific to DA-V2 outputs. Consistent with this inter-
pretation, directly replacing DA-V2 pseudo-depth with D435i-measured depth reduced 
the detection performance of CIAFNet (Stage 1), indicating that the model is sensitive to 
the source and statistical characteristics of the depth input. After second-stage fine-tuning 
with synchronized D435i RGB-D data collected under controlled conditions, the perfor-
mance with measured depth input improved substantially, indicating that CIAFNet 
(Stage 2) can adapt to D435i-measured depth without modification of the principal net-
work architecture. However, the D435i dataset was collected using artificial fruit models 
and cut branches in a controlled scene and therefore does not encompass the full range of 
illumination, foliage occlusion, sensing distances, fruit surface properties, background 
structures, and depth quality conditions encountered in real orchards. For an active infra-
red sensor such as the D435i, the infrared reflectance of artificial fruit surfaces and leaves 
on cut branches may differ from that of living fruit and leaves. These material-dependent 
differences may affect the infrared pattern received by the sensor, thereby influencing 
missing depth patterns, target boundary quality, and depth stability. Accordingly, the sec-
ond-stage results demonstrate adaptation to D435i-measured depth only within the cur-
rent controlled data domain; they do not establish that CIAFNet (Stage 1) learned a rep-
resentation invariant to the depth input source or that the measured depth performance 
will generalize to real orchards. These findings indicate that the source and statistical char-
acteristics of the depth input should be treated as integral components of multimodal 
model design and deployment. 
The localization experiment conducted under controlled conditions further suggests 
that stable detection regions can serve as a visual front end for relative 3D coordinate es-
timation. The controlled experiment comprised 10 groups and yielded 50 target-level rel-
ative displacement measurements. The mean 3D consistency error was 5.60 mm, with 
group-level mean values ranging from 4.94 to 6.20 mm. These results indicate only that 
the coordinate changes produced by the visual front end were relatively consistent under 
the prescribed camera translation within the current controlled scene. Nevertheless, all 
target measurements were obtained using artificial Camellia oleifera fruit models and cut 
branches under controlled conditions. Moreover, the evaluation compared the observed 
coordinate changes with those expected from the prescribed camera translation rather 
than comparing the estimated absolute target coordinates with independently measured 
ground truth positions. Therefore, the experiment evaluates relative displacement

30 of 33 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture16171884 
consistency under controlled conditions and does not establish absolute positioning accu-
racy, localization performance on real fruits, or harvesting reliability in natural orchards. 
The circular-ROI scaling coefficient and its adjustment range, as well as the learning 
rate, training budget, and parameter-freezing configuration used in the second stage, were 
empirically selected according to the requirements of the current task and the character-
istics of the available datasets. Because exhaustive sensitivity analyses and systematic 
comparisons of alternative freezing strategies were not conducted, these settings should 
be regarded as task-specific design choices rather than universally optimal configurations. 
In future work, we will conduct dedicated studies on the 3D localization of Camellia 
oleifera fruit by systematically evaluating the sensitivity of localization performance to the 
circular ROI scaling coefficient and investigating the stability of depth-based 3D coordi-
nate estimation under different levels of target visibility and occlusion. Large-scale syn-
chronized RGB-D data will also be collected from fruit on living Camellia oleifera trees in 
natural orchards, and absolute 3D positioning errors will be evaluated using an independ-
ent high-precision reference system under diverse illumination, occlusion, sensing dis-
tance, and depth quality conditions. End-to-end manipulator positioning and harvesting 
experiments will further be conducted to assess practical harvesting reliability. 
5. Conclusions 
This study proposed CIAFNet, a lightweight RGB-D dual-stream network for Camel-
lia oleifera fruit detection in natural orchards. The principal methodological novelty of 
CIAFNet lies in its discrepancy- and consistency-aware cross-modal fusion mechanism. 
Unlike fixed feature concatenation or element-wise addition, this mechanism explicitly 
models local consistency and discrepancy between RGB and depth features to guide the 
selective fusion of RGB appearance information and DA-V2-derived relative structural 
priors. Specifically, C3k2_PartialNetBlock reduces redundant spatial computation 
through partial channel processing; CIDAF generates content-adaptive fusion weights to 
enhance complementary structural information and attenuate the influence of unreliable 
depth responses; and the SC-EUCB-enhanced BiFPN improves the recovery of fine-
grained features across different scales. Together, these modules form a dual-stream de-
tection framework that balances detection performance and computational complexity. 
On the natural orchard RGB–pseudo-depth test set, CIAFNet (Stage 1) achieved a 
precision of 94.54%, a recall of 84.92%, an mAP@0.5 of 93.33%, and an mAP@0.5:0.95 of 
73.67%, with 10.49 GFLOPs and 3.80 M parameters. The modality comparison and abla-
tion experiments validated the effectiveness of pseudo-depth-assisted detection and the 
proposed network components. The image-level paired bootstrap analysis further 
showed that the performance improvements of CIAFNet over the RGB-only and Baseline 
configurations were statistically significant on the current test set. After second-stage fine-
tuning using sensor-acquired D435i RGB-D data collected under controlled conditions, 
CIAFNet (Stage 2) showed improved adaptation to sensor-measured depth within the 
controlled data domain. In addition, the relative displacement consistency experiment 
conducted under controlled conditions yielded a mean 3D consistency error of 5.60 mm, 
indicating that CIAFNet detections could serve as a visual front end to support relative 
3D coordinate estimation in controlled environments. However, the current conclusions 
apply only to pseudo-depth-assisted detection in natural orchards and to measured depth 
adaptation and relative localization under controlled conditions. Future work will use 
synchronized sensor-acquired RGB-D data collected in real orchards and continuous ro-
botic harvesting experiments to further evaluate the end-to-end processing speed, robust-
ness, absolute localization accuracy, and cross-environment generalization of CIAFNet.
