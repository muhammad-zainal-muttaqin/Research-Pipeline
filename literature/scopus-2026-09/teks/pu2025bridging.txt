A B S T R A C T
Field crop counting is crucial for precision agriculture and global food security. The integration of Unmanned 
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) and deep learning has become the leading technology for this task. However, existing 
reviews often overlook the unique challenges of large-scale field crops, such as vast areas and high planting 
density. This paper provides a comprehensive review of UAV-based deep learning methods for in-field object 
counting. We analyze 52 key studies and introduce a framework to guide method selection. This framework 
connects agronomic targets to detection, segmentation, or regression approaches based on their specific char­
acteristics. Our review also examines the entire data workflow, covering data acquisition, annotation strategies, 
and eight public datasets. We also link data choices directly to the applicability of different models. Finally, we 
identify the key gaps preventing successful real-world deployment and propose a future research roadmap. This 
roadmap focuses on four strategic frontiers: large foundational models, unified open-set counting paradigms, 
dynamic spatiotemporal phenotyping, and autonomous edge-cloud systems. This review aims to support the 
advancement of intelligent and sustainable agriculture by bridging the gap between research and practical 
application.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background
Field crop counting is a fundamental component of precision agri­
culture, essential for optimizing planting density [1,2], managing di­
sasters [3,4], and predicting yields [5]. By precisely quantifying crops at 
various growth stages and types, researchers facilitate informed 
decision-making in farming practices. This is crucial for government 
policy-making [6,7], agricultural insurance [3], and sustainable devel­
opment initiatives [8]. Moreover, accurate data on plant density [8,9] 
and uniform seedling emergence [7] enable the optimization of field 
management, directly affecting food security and economic efficiency.
Traditional crop counting methods, which rely on manual sampling 
and extrapolation, are often labor-intensive and error-prone, failing to 
meet the demands of modern large-scale agriculture [7,10–14]. This 
inefficiency spurred the development of automated technologies. Early 
explorations utilized spectral-based statistical regression with satellite 
remote sensing imagery. These methods correlate vegetation indices, 
such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), with crop 
parameters to estimate density or yield [15–17]. However, their effec­
tiveness is limited by the coarse spatial resolution of satellite data, which 
prevents individual plant identification, and their susceptibility to 
confounding factors such as soil background and weed interference.
With the advent of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), high- 
resolution imagery became accessible, leading to a shift towards

methods use a sequence of pre-defined operations, such as color space 
transformation, threshold segmentation (e.g., Otsu’s method), and 
morphological analysis to isolate and count plant objects [5,18,19]. 
Some research combined these image features with machine learning 
classifiers such as Support Vector Machines [19] or developed special­
ized models based on plant morphology and spatial distribution [9,20]. 
These approaches demonstrated considerable potential in various field 
crops, including wheat [9,21], maize and sunflower [22], and safflower

Despite these advancements, traditional computer vision remains 
limited by its reliance on handcrafted features. These features, designed 
based on prior knowledge of crop characteristics, lack the robustness to 
handle the high variability of real-world field conditions. Their perfor­
mance degrades significantly under fluctuating illumination, complex 
soil backgrounds, heavy weed infestation, and intra-species morpho­
logical diversity [23–25]. Consequently, these methods often require 
tedious, case-by-case parameter tuning and exhibit poor generalization 
capabilities, creating a critical need for a more powerful and adaptive 
technological paradigm.
The combination of UAVs and deep learning has emerged as the 
mainstream solution to overcome these limitations. UAVs provide flex­
ible access to high-resolution imagery, while deep learning models can 
automatically learn robust features from complex data, significantly 
outperforming traditional methods. This review focuses on large-scale 
cultivated field crops, such as corn, rice, wheat, and cotton, which are 
crucial for global food security [26]. Field crop counting presents unique 
challenges, including large population sizes, severe plant occlusion in 
dense canopies, and rapid morphological changes across phenological 
stages. These characteristics differ significantly from those of horticul­
tural crops (e.g., fruit trees and vegetables), which often involve 
different imaging strategies and challenges such as 3D structural oc­
clusion. Horticultural crops are not included to ensure the depth and 
systematicity of this review.
1.2. Contributions
Valuable reviews are available on related but distinct topics. For 
instance, some works focus on horticultural crops, addressing the unique 
challenges of tree fruit detection and yield estimation [24,27]. Other 
reviews tackle broad-scale yield prediction using multi-source remote 
sensing, which estimates aggregate yield rather than enumerating in­
dividual plant organs [28]. Even when focused on UAVs and row crops, 
existing analyses often limit their scope to a single growth phase, pri­
marily the evaluation of early-stage stand count for seedling emergence 
[7]. A recent review, although covering smart agriculture broadly, treats 
counting as a single application and lacks focused analysis of the severe 
occlusion and high-density challenges unique to field crops [23].
This review presents a comprehensive and decision-oriented frame­
work for UAV-based crop counting in field conditions using deep 
learning. It systematically connects each stage of the pipeline, including 
data acquisition, model selection, and deployment in agricultural pro­
duction. Unlike algorithm-focused surveys, this review explicitly ad­
dresses the “lab-to-field gap.” It examines how model performance 
depends on annotation strategies and the level of information granu­
larity. It also analyzes how deep learning methods must adapt to support 
agronomic decision-making, satisfy operational requirements at scale, 
and fit within the spatial, economic, and practical realities of large-scale 
crop management.

This work adopts an agriculture-centered perspective. Instead of 
treating crop counting only as a computer vision challenge, it provides 
insights to help integrate advanced artificial intelligence (AI) technol­
ogies into real-world agricultural production. The main contributions of 
this review are fourfold: 
• A framework for target-driven method selection
This review addresses in situ object counting for field crops. Adopt­
ing an agriculture-first perspective, it focuses on challenges from vast 
cultivation areas and high density. We first analyze diverse agronomic 
targets, ranging from seedlings to reproductive organs. We then intro­
duce a framework driven by their distinct morphological characteristics. 
This framework connects target morphology to specific methodological 
categories, mapping agronomic needs directly to technical choices. It 
helps determine if a point-based or a box-based approach is more suit­
able for a given task. 
• An analysis of deep learning paradigms
The review then examines the underlying technical solutions. We 
categorize methods into three core deep learning paradigms: detection, 
segmentation, and regression. Our analysis is framed by the concept of 
information granularity, interpreting the methodological evolution and 
evaluating the trade-offs of each paradigm in tackling challenges like 
severe occlusion. This evaluation provides a basis for selecting and 
developing appropriate model architectures. 
• The end-to-end data workflow
Effective models rely on high-quality data. This review systemati­
cally examines the entire data workflow to bridge the gap between 
model theory and deployment reality. It covers UAV acquisition, 
annotation strategies, and a curated list of eight public datasets. We 
establish a clear link between data costs and the applicability of different 
learning paradigms. This work addresses the foundational data-model 
disconnect often overlooked in prior reviews. 
• The lab-to-field gap and a future roadmap
This review moves beyond a purely algorithmic survey to diagnose 
why these technologies often fail in practice. We identify four key 
“operation-centric” bottlenecks that create the “lab-to-field” gap. These 
include the semantic gap between technical outputs and agronomic 
decisions and the challenge of scaling to field-level reliability. They also 
encompass the dilemma of integrating domain knowledge and the 
constraints imposed by agricultural economics. Based on this analysis, 
we propose a structured research roadmap. This roadmap focuses on 
four strategic frontiers: large foundation models and generative AI; 
unified open-set counting paradigms; spatiotemporal dynamic pheno­
typing; and integrated autonomous edge-cloud systems.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Chapter 2 in­
troduces the literature retrieval process and analysis. Chapter 3 analyzes 
data collection, data annotation, and public datasets. Chapter 4 provides 
a comprehensive summary of deep learning-based counting algorithms 
for field crops using UAV imagery. Chapter 5 introduces crop-counting- 
related application systems and platforms. Chapter 6 discusses the main 
challenges and in-depth issues related to UAVs and deep learning in field 
crop counting tasks. Finally, Chapter 7 outlines future research 
directions.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Research methods
A systematic literature review was conducted across three primary 
academic databases: Agricola (https://www.nal.usda.gov/agricola) 
[29], Web of Science (https://clarivate.com.cn/solutions/web-of-sci 
ence/) [30], and Scopus (https://www.scopus.com) [31]. All relevant 
articles were identified using search criteria that satisfied the following 
five conditions within the title, abstract, and keywords: (1) Technolog­
ical conditions encompassed “deep learning” OR “computer vision” OR 
“neural network*”, focusing on deep learning-related methodologies. 
(2) Research objectives targeted field crop counting, incorporating 
keywords (“crop*” OR “plant*”) NEAR/5 (“population” OR “count*” OR 
“density” OR “emergence”). This search string requires crop or 
plant-related terms to appear within five words of terms such as popu­
lation, counting, density, or emergence terms. (3) Data collection 
methodology was required to utilize UAV aerial photography technol­
ogy, specified as (“unmanned aerial vehicle” OR “UAV” OR “unmanned 
aircraft system” OR “UAS” OR “drone” OR “aerial imag*” NOT “satel­
lite*”), including UAV-related terms while excluding satellite-based data 
collection. (4) The search timeframe was restricted to publications 
before 2025. (5) Only English-language publications were considered, 
including research articles and review articles.
It should be noted that this review exclusively included English- 
language publications, a methodological constraint that may introduce 
specific biases. Excluding non-English articles risks a geographical bias, 
potentially overlooking innovations and locally adapted methodologies 
from major agricultural regions such as China, Brazil, Russia, and parts 
of Europe. Furthermore, it may induce a crop-specific bias, skewing the 
review towards globally common crops (e.g., corn, wheat) at the 
expense of regionally critical ones documented in other languages. 
Finally, this limitation could create a technical blind spot to novel al­
gorithms or cost-effective solutions developed and reported within non- 
Anglophone research communities. Consequently, while our findings 
accurately reflect the state-of-the-art within the English-language liter­
ature, future work incorporating multilingual sources is necessary for a 
truly global synthesis.
Fig. 1 displays the identification process of the target research arti­
cles. The literature filtering process followed three sequential steps: (1) 
application of identical search criteria across all three databases, (2) 
removal of duplicate articles, and (3) screening to determine whether 
articles aligned with our research objectives. By July 3rd, 2025, 225 
articles were retrieved from Web of Science, 4 articles from Scopus, and 
0 articles from Agricola, yielding 229 articles in total. In the second step, 
226 articles remained after eliminating duplicates. The third step 
involved screening and filtering irrelevant publications. Ultimately, 52 
articles from the three databases satisfied the criteria for field crop 
counting based on UAV and deep learning technology.

3. Datasets for UAV-based crop counting
The high cost of 3D data acquisition often outweighs its benefits in 
large-scale agriculture; thus, acquiring high-quality 2D imagery via 
UAVs is fundamental to applying deep learning for high-throughput 
field crop counting. This section outlines the primary methods for 
UAV data collection and annotation, which collectively form the basis 
for training and evaluating deep learning models. The agricultural 
domain grapples with data scarcity, expensive acquisition, and a lack of 
large-scale standardization, often narrowing the scope of research re­
flected in available datasets. This review compiles eight public datasets 
for UAV-based field crop counting that are accessible online as of July 
2025.
3.1. UAV data acquisition
UAVs have emerged as the dominant data collection platform for 
large-scale, spatially continuous scenarios such as field crops due to 
their unique advantages in providing high spatiotemporal resolution, 
operational flexibility, and an unobstructed aerial perspective. In 
contrast to methods such as smartphone photography [52,53] or ground 
robotics [54], which are better suited for small-scale or highly detailed 
studies, drones can efficiently survey vast farmlands. They overcome the 
accessibility 
and limited field-of-view challenges inherent to 
ground-based equipment, making them the ideal choice for large-scale 
commercial crop counting.
During data acquisition, the following key parameters directly in­
fluence the performance of subsequent deep learning models: 
1. Flight altitude [7,55,56]: This parameter determines the ground 
sampling distance of the image. 
• Lower altitudes yield higher-resolution images, which is critical 
for resolving small targets such as wheat heads or seedlings. 
However, this approach increases the number of required flights 
and the total data volume.
• Higher altitudes allow for rapid coverage of large areas but result 
in lower spatial resolution, which can cause targets to appear 
blurry and difficult to distinguish.
2. Shooting angle: 
• A nadir (vertical overhead) view [10] is the most common 
approach. It effectively minimizes technical deviations caused by 
plant occlusion, making it particularly suitable for density map 
estimation methods.

3. Sensor type: 
• RGB cameras [10,55,57] are the most widely used sensors for field

As field crop counting typically covers large areas with complex 
backgrounds and must remain cost-effective, the most common practice 
is to deploy UAVs equipped with RGB cameras, flown at low altitudes 
and a nadir viewing angle.

annotated data to provide the necessary supervised information. How­
ever, the choice of annotation method is not arbitrary; it represents a 
critical trade-off between the desired level of informational granularity 
and the practical constraints of cost and time. This choice is directly 
driven by the specific challenges of the counting task—such as target

the primary annotation methods used for UAV-based field crop counting 
directly correspond to distinct deep learning paradigms. 
1. Image-level count annotation: This method provides a single count 
for the entire image without localizing individual plants. It is a 
simple annotation approach beneficial for training the global 
regression models aimed at high-level yield prediction or density 
evaluation. However, because the model cannot learn spatial fea­
tures, its generalizability and robustness are often limited, making it 
less commonly applied [12,59].
2. Point annotation: This technique involves marking the center of each 
target object, such as a plant or a wheat head. It is highly efficient 
and the most common method for counting dense, small-target crops 
such as wheat and rice from UAV imagery [45,60–62]. The resulting 
point-level data is ideally suited for density-based or local 
regression models.
3. Bounding box annotation: This method uses rectangular overlays to 
define the location and approximate size of each target. It is well- 
suited for object detection tasks, particularly for crops that are 
relatively sparse and individually large, such as corn plants or cab­
bage, as it provides a count and positional data for each instance [32,
47,56,63,64].
4. Pixel-level annotation: Often called semantic segmentation, this 
approach involves assigning a class label (e.g., “crop” or “back­
ground”) to every pixel in the image. It offers the highest precision 
and is ideal for training segmentation models that can accurately 
measure crop area or morphology; however, it is the most labor- 
intensive and costly annotation method [22,40,48,51,65].

4. Deep learning methods
Deep learning methods for field crop counting have evolved to tackle 
the many challenges encountered in real-world agricultural environ­
ments. These challenges span tasks such as counting sparse, large plants 
as well as estimating small, heavily occluded targets within complex 
backgrounds. No single approach has proven universally optimal. This 
chapter categorizes and analyzes these methods within three dominant 
paradigms: object detection, image segmentation, and object counting 
(regression). Each paradigm is tailored to specific problem settings and 
agronomic requirements.
These methodological advances can be interpreted within the con­
ceptual framework of information granularity, which refers to the level 
of detail a model is intended to predict and is required to achieve for a 
given agricultural task. Object detection provides an intermediate level 
of granularity by localizing objects through bounding boxes. Image 
segmentation offers the highest level, generating detailed pixel-wise 
masks that facilitate phenotyping and morphological analysis. In 
contrast, regression-based counting operates at the coarsest granularity, 
estimating aggregate counts or a continuous density map rather than 
explicit, instance-level bounding boxes. This approach is particularly 
suitable for high-density or severely occluded scenarios.

4.1. Intermediate-grained approaches: object detection methods
4.1.1. Foundational detector architectures
Object detection techniques use deep learning frameworks such as 
Faster Region-Based Convolutional Neural Network (R-CNN) or You 
Only Look Once (YOLO) to locate and recognize individual crop entities 
within images, along with their specific positions, necessitating exten­
sive image datasets and detailed bounding box annotations for model 
training. These methods classify an object as a target crop and regress its 
corresponding bounding box coordinates. Additionally, they apply non- 
maximum suppression to select the optimal predictions from over­
lapping boxes. In crop counting, object-detection-based methods focus 
on identifying and classifying plant bounding boxes and tallying these 
detections to estimate crop numbers. Challenges include occlusion, scale 
variation, and complex crop backgrounds [69].

directly in a single network pass, offering significant speed advantages 
suitable for real-time or near-real-time applications [70]. For example,

backgrounds. Two-stage detectors, such as Faster R-CNN, generate re­

accuracy but are slower, with higher computational costs, making them 
less suited for resource-limited settings. They are widely used in sce­
narios requiring high precision, such as counting tobacco plants [69].
A second key distinction is between anchor-based and anchor-free 
methods (Fig. 6(b)). Anchor-based detectors are found in both two- 
step frameworks (such as Faster R-CNN) and some single-step de­
tectors (such as SSD). They utilize pre-defined bounding boxes to 
generate candidate regions for subsequent processing. For instance, a 
parameter-tuned single-shot detection algorithm was successfully 
implemented to identify and count sorghum heads from aerial imagery, 
and the deep-learning model achieved an out-of-sample mean average 
precision of 0.95 [72]. In contrast, anchor-free methods directly predict 
target center points or key points on feature maps, simplifying the 
procedure and potentially enhancing generalization capabilities. For 
example, the CenterNet model used for cotton seedling counting sur­
passes the performance of the traditional Faster R-CNN model [73]. 
Selecting between anchor-based and anchor-free detectors requires 
balancing speed and precision based on application needs.
4.1.2. Advanced strategies for field crop counting
To address the unique challenges of UAV-based imagery, research 
has focused on adapting and enhancing baseline detectors. These stra­
tegies can be grouped into three main trends: enhancing feature repre­
sentation, improving operational efficiency, and leveraging generative 
techniques for data augmentation.
A key strategy involves strengthening the model’s feature represen­
tation to handle significant scale variations inherent in field crops. This 
is achieved by modifying network architectures to better capture multi- 
scale information. For example, researchers have improved small-object 
detection by integrating a specialized detection layer into YOLOv8 [56] 
or by leveraging an augmented feature pyramid network (AugFPN) to 
better fuse low-level, high-resolution features for detecting early-stage 
seedlings [39]. Furthermore, innovative modules have been designed 
to enhance feature discriminability. By incorporating self-calibrated 
convolution (RSCconv) and an adaptive spatial-channel feature fusion 
module (ASCFF), models can more effectively distinguish young seed­
lings from complex backgrounds [64]. More complex architectures, such 
as dual-branch networks with multi-stage optimization, have also been 
developed to simultaneously perform plant counting and plantation-row 
detection, using cross-branch feedback to improve overall performance 
[61].
Beyond architectural modifications, research also focuses on adapt­
ing models for real-world, high-throughput applications. A notable 
trend is the extension of static image detection to the temporal analysis 
of UAV video streams. Systems can track individual plants across frames 
by integrating detectors such as YOLO with tracking algorithms such as 
the Kalman Filter or Simple Online and Realtime Tracking (SORT). This 
facilitates dynamic, online counting while effectively preventing 
duplicate counts in overlapping image regions [32,63]. Another critical 
direction is model lightweighting, which aims to reduce computational 
demands for deployment on UAVs or edge devices. This is achieved 
through techniques such as model pruning, adopting lightweight back­
bones, and designing efficient convolution modules, all of which 
significantly increase inference speed while maintaining acceptable 
accuracy [45,74].
Recently, generative methods, particularly Generative Adversarial 
Networks (GANs), have emerged as a novel paradigm to address the 
critical challenge of data scarcity. As manually annotating high- 
resolution UAV imagery is prohibitively time-consuming, the lack of 
sufficient high-quality training samples often limits model performance. 
To overcome this, researchers leverage image-to-image translation 
GANs (e.g., pix2pixHD, SPADE) to learn from a small set of labeled real 
images and generate photorealistic synthetic crop images with corre­
sponding bounding box labels. This synthetic data is then used to 
augment the training dataset, enabling the detection model to train on 
more diverse examples. This approach has proven effective for 
enhancing the model’s generalization and detection accuracy in real- 
world scenarios, demonstrating great potential in executing chal­
lenging tasks such as detecting sorghum panicles [75].
4.2. Fine-grained approaches: image segmentation methods
Object detection is effective for localizing plants; however, its 
bounding boxes provide only coarse spatial information, struggling to 
accurately represent irregularly shaped plants or quantify their size. To 
overcome these limitations, segmentation-based methods offer a sig­
nificant leap in precision by performing pixel-wise classification. Spe­
cifically, image segmentation assigns each pixel to a specific category (e. 
g., crop or background) using networks such as U-Net or DeepLab. This

approach can address occlusions and overlaps between crops and 
generate detailed crop-coverage maps for total-count estimation. The 
technique emphasizes high-quality images with clear edges, and may 
involve post-processing to remove small errors. Moving beyond the 
bounding-box paradigm, these methods delineate the exact contour of 
each plant, enabling more precise counting and the extraction of valu­
able morphological traits—a key requirement for advanced phenotyp­

perspective based on how the segmentation output is utilized in the 
counting pipeline: either for direct enumeration or as an enhancement to 
detection frameworks (Fig. 7).
The most straightforward application, which we term direct 
counting via segmentation, involves using a model to create a binary 
mask that separates plants from the background. The number of plants is 
then determined by counting the discrete, connected components in the 
mask. This approach is highly effective in fields with low to moderate 
plant density where individual plants are clearly separable. This is 
exemplified in studies on tobacco emergence, where a U-Net architec­
ture generates plant masks for direct counting [63], and in potato fields, 
where a U-Net-based PlantSegNet was developed for robust segmenta­
tion [48]. Moreover, non-deep learning techniques such as k-means 
clustering have been successfully used to segment and count maize in 
this manner [76].
In more complex scenarios, particularly with moderate occlusion or 
significant weed pressure, the paradigm shifts to segmentation- 
enhanced detection. Here, segmentation is integrated into detection 
frameworks to improve localization accuracy, acting as an auxiliary task 
that forces the model to learn finer-grained features. Instance segmen­
tation models such as Mask R-CNN exemplify this approach, having been 
fine-tuned to count and size potato plants by generating a unique mask 
for each detected instance [77]. Other approaches use it as a two-stage 
process, where Mask R-CNN first isolates maize seedlings from weedy 
backgrounds to enable a more accurate subsequent count [69]. Simi­
larly, a Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) was designed to produce 
pixel-wise classifications of sugar beet and maize fields, simultaneously 
determining stem locations for counting while differentiating from 
weeds [51].
Collectively, segmentation-based methods represent a significant 
advancement over standard detection, providing rich, pixel-level infor­
mation crucial for accurate counting and phenotypic analysis. However, 
their strength is also their weakness. The reliance on precise, pixel-level 
masks leads to two major challenges. The first is the prohibitively high 
annotation cost, which creates a significant bottleneck for training 
robust models. The second, and more fundamental, limitation is the 
failure of these methods in high-density scenarios. As crop density in­
creases, severe occlusion and overlapping make it impossible for seg­
mentation algorithms to distinguish and separate individual plants, 
causing the concept of a discrete, countable instance to break down. This 
fundamental limitation paves the way for the third category of 
approaches: regression-based counting methods.
4.3. Coarse-grained approaches: object counting methods
Despite the rich information provided by detection and segmenta­
tion, their high annotation and computational costs present significant 
barriers to large-scale deployment. Furthermore, segmentation methods 
fundamentally fail in high-density, severely occluded scenarios. 
Regression-based techniques have emerged as a mainstream and prac­
tical alternative to overcome the dual challenge of efficiency and 
robustness. These methods simplify the problem by directly estimating 
the total plant count, avoiding the need for precise boundary delinea­
tion, such as tight bounding boxes or pixel-level masks. This approach is 
particularly effective for tasks focused solely on counting, as it signifi­
cantly reduces both the annotation burden and computational overhead. 
These deep learning methods are typically categorized into three main 
strategies: global count regression, local count regression, and density- 
based regression (Fig. 8). Global count regression directly infers the 
total number of crops from the entire image, whereas local count 
regression divides the image into sub-images and predicts the number of 
crops per segment. Density-based regression constructs a density map to 
estimate the spatial distribution and quantity of crops. These techniques 
can be adapted to different growth stages and provide accurate and 
robust counting results.
4.3.1. Global count regression
Global count regression uses CNNs for holistic feature extraction and 
regression analysis to predict a single, total crop count for an entire 
image. This process simplifies counting by relying on image-level an­
notations rather than identifying individual plants. Research on this 
method for field crops is relatively sparse, but notable examples exist. 
For instance, one study utilized a pre-trained ResNet18 model to directly 
estimate the stand count of cotton seedlings from UAV imagery [43]. A 
similar approach was later applied to estimate corn emergence param­
eters, including plant density, demonstrating the method’s utility for key 
agronomic traits [59]. This approach significantly reduces data anno­
tation and preprocessing costs; however, it provides no spatial infor­
mation about plant locations, and its performance can be sensitive to 
variations in crop distribution, image quality, and environmental 
variables.
4.3.2. Local count regression
Global counting methods have evolved toward local count regression 
to reintroduce spatial awareness. This method estimates crop numbers 
in subregions of an image, typically using a sliding window to assess 
local crop quantity. By learning from local features, it generates a local 
count map where the sum of all values yields the total crop count. A key 
study benchmarked this approach for wheat ear counting, demon­
strating its robustness and high heritability compared to manual and 
detection-based methods [38]. Subsequent work has focused on 
improving the efficiency and applicability of this paradigm, leading to 
an optimized implementation that runs an order of magnitude faster and 
is validated on wheat ears, maize tassels, and sorghum heads [13]. The 
evolution continued with a focus on enhancing model interpretability by 
integrating guided up-sampling and background suppression to produce 
more explainable visualizations alongside accurate counts for maize, 
wheat, and rice [66].
4.3.3. Density-based regression
Most recently, counting methods have shifted from local count 
regression to density-based regression. This approach addresses the 
limitations of fixed receptive fields by learning a mapping relationship 
between image features and a spatial density map, where the map in­
tegral yields the total count. This has become a key strategy for handling 
dense scenes and heavy occlusion, often implemented using point-level 
supervision. This method has proven versatile across various field crops.

4.4. Method selection guidelines and summary
4.4.1. Technology evolution and agricultural requirements
The application of deep learning methods in field crop counting 
follows the development timeline of general computer vision technolo­
gies. However, this trajectory does not fully reflect the optimal choices 
for agricultural needs. Detection and segmentation methods are often 
designed not for pure counting but as auxiliary technologies for other 
agricultural tasks. To the best of our knowledge, the cited works in 
Table 2 represent the earliest applications of each paradigm in their 
respective domains.
Object detection was introduced to agriculture in 2017 [54], just 
three years after its emergence in computer vision. This quick adoption 
was due to mature technology and available pre-trained models. In 
agriculture, detection is especially valuable where precise location and 
size are needed, such as for yield prediction, growth monitoring, auto­
mated harvesting, and targeted pest or disease treatment. Detection 
methods offer spatial and size information that simple counting methods 
lack.
Segmentation methods began to be used in agriculture in 2019 [81], 
four years after the introduction of FCN [80]. Their main applications 
include spatial assessment of seeding quality, crop coverage and land 
use analysis, canopy structure analysis, and precise localization of dis­
ease areas. Seeding quality assessment relies on obtaining the position, 
size, and spacing of each seedling. Crop coverage analysis requires ac­
curate discrimination between crop and background. Canopy structure 
analysis needs complete contour information for structural modeling. 
Disease localization depends on precise boundaries of infected regions. 
Across all cases, the core goal of segmentation is to extract detailed 
spatial and morphological information, with counting as a secondary 
result.
Regression-based methods provide a direct approach to crop count­
ing, comprising three main variants with distinct timelines for their 
agricultural adoption. Global count regression was applied to agricul­
ture in 2017 [83], just two years after its 2015 introduction in computer 
vision [82]. Although simple and computationally efficient, this 
approach offers limited accuracy as it disregards spatial distribution 
information. Local count regression emerged simultaneously in both 
computer vision [84] and agriculture [12] in 2017. This method is 
particularly advantageous for handling significant morphological 
changes during plant growth by predicting counts within local regions. 
In contrast, density-based regression, despite being established in com­
puter vision in 2010 [85], was not utilized for crop counting until 2019 
[38]. This nine-year delay is attributed to the technical challenge of 
training on point coordinates, which necessitates generating heatmaps 
as intermediate representations. Before 2017, techniques for creating 
Gaussian kernel-based heatmaps with the smooth gradients required for 
effective network training were not yet well-developed.

The distinct characteristics of the three primary deep learning par­
adigms for field crop counting are summarized in Table 3 to provide a

For pure crop counting tasks, object counting methods, particularly 
density-based and local count regression, are strongly recommended. 
These approaches have proven to be the most cost-effective and 
advanced choices, achieving an optimal balance between annotation 
cost, computational load, and counting accuracy. In contrast, detection 
and segmentation methods, despite their prevalence in research litera­
ture, are often suboptimal for pure counting. Their strengths lie in 
comprehensive tasks requiring precise location, size, or shape informa­
tion, but this comes at the cost of significantly higher annotation effort 
and computational complexity. The challenges of ambiguous bound­
aries, severe occlusion, and inconsistent annotations in field 
environments often lead to “dirty gradients” during training, which can 
outweigh their potential benefits.
The choice between density-based and local count regression hinges 
on the specific characteristics of the target and imaging conditions. 
Density-based regression is generally the preferred method for high- 
altitude, nadir (top-down) imagery, which often features significant 
variations in object scale. By transforming point annotations into 
probability density maps using Gaussian kernels, this approach provides 
smooth gradients for stable model training and excels at capturing 
precise spatial distribution patterns. To further enhance its robustness to 
scale variance, current research is focused on developing multi-scale 
fusion techniques and adaptive kernels. Conversely, local count regres­
sion demonstrates superior performance for non-rigid, elongated objects 
such as wheat spikes or maize tassels, where large intra-class shape 
variations are common. Instead of regressing a density map, it predicts a 
count value for local image patches. This simplifies the learning target 
and proves more tolerant to morphological changes during plant 
growth. However, this method has limitations. It discards precise spatial 
location, and its performance can degrade under conditions of extreme 
sparsity or density. Its reliance on a predefined grid size can also lead to 
over-counting large objects or under-counting dense clusters. Despite 
these drawbacks, it remains a highly effective choice for applications 
where counting morphologically diverse objects is the primary 
objective.
Ultimately, we emphasize that annotation quality remains more 
critical than model complexity. A simple, well-chosen counting model 
trained on high-quality data will consistently outperform a complex one 
trained on noisy annotations. Refining these specialized models is 
crucial for current applications; however, the long-term trajectory of the 
field points towards a paradigm shift. The potential of large foundation 
models to create a unified, open-set counting framework will be 
explored in detail in our “Future Research Directions” section.

5. Application systems and platforms
Deep learning-based crop counting is transitioning from theoretical 
research to practical application, giving rise to various systems and 
platforms. This chapter analyzes their implementation models and 
technical features, categorized into academic open-source platforms and 
commercial integrated platforms, to illustrate the current technology-to- 
application ecosystem.
The core value of academic and open-source platforms lies in 
fostering algorithmic innovation and technological sharing, providing 
the research community with reproducible baselines and validation 
environments. AgroCounters [6], an open-source repository, offers 
various deep learning-based methods, such as regression, detection, and 
density estimation, to automate the counting of agricultural items, 
including fruits, vegetables, and livestock. It uses transfer learning to 
enhance model performance in environments with scarce labeled data 
and demonstrates its efficacy using models such as YOLOv5, 
Faster-RCNN, and RetinaNet. AgroCounters advances automated agri­
cultural counting by sharing algorithms and encouraging computer 
vision applications to reduce labor and improve crop management and 
yield. AICropCAM [86] leverages edge computing and deep learning for 
comprehensive real-time crop monitoring using four bespoke neural 
networks. Sensor nodes featuring RGB cameras and long-distance 
communication ensure timely data relay and analysis. AICropCAM’s 
real-time capabilities streamline decision-making processes and can be 
deployed on mobile units to widen monitoring reach and strengthen 
field management.
In contrast, commercial platforms focus on delivering user-friendly, 
end-to-end solutions to address practical agricultural challenges. They 
provide seamless “data-to-decision” workflows by integrating UAV data 
acquisition, cloud-based processing, and results visualization. For 
example, Solvi [87] utilizes drone-captured imagery and PlantAI™ for 
quick assessment of crop quantity and size, streamlining the process into 
four steps: upload, area delineation, recognition, and data reception. 
This enhances efficiency in precision agriculture management. Pix4D­
fields [88] offers a specialized software suite for agricultural drone 
imagery analysis. It provides dedicated modules for automated or 
semi-automated plant stand counts from orthomosaic images, making it 
particularly suitable for emergence assessment and gap detection in 
crops such as corn, soybean, and cotton during early growth stages.
A comparative analysis reveals a clear distinction in the current UAV- 
based crop counting ecosystem: academic platforms drive technical 
progress through transparency and innovation, while commercial plat­
forms validate market viability through usability and integrated solu­
tions. Despite their different strategies, the development trajectory for 
the ecosystem is converging toward integrated edge-cloud management 
systems. In this architecture, edge devices handle simple data feedback 
and real-time monitoring under network constraints, while cloud sys­
tems run high-precision models and manage large-scale data processing. 
Future advancements will likely focus on automating the integration of 
UAV data into the cloud, enabling autonomous drone monitoring, and 
facilitating automated training and inference cycles. Despite this clear 
technological trajectory, both academic and commercial platforms face 
common fundamental challenges that hinder widespread, reliable 
deployment. These challenges will be addressed in the following 
chapter.
6. Key challenges and discussions
Existing reviews have summarized the fundamental technical chal­
lenges in large-scale crop counting using UAV imagery and deep 
learning. These challenges primarily include: (1) severe occlusion and 
overlap among plants in complex field environments, which impact 
detection accuracy [7]; (2) the need for models to generalize across 
different growth stages due to changes in crop morphology [7]; (3) the 
significant influence of imaging conditions, such as lighting, flight alti­
tude, and sensor type, on model performance [7]; (4) common problems 
like scarce annotated data, high computational costs, and poor model 
interpretability [24,27].
These previously identified challenges still persist. Although this 
body of work is crucial, its emphasis has largely been on algorithmic 
performance, which has inadvertently created a gap between technical 
advancements and the practical requirements of agricultural operations. 
Consequently, many models that excel in laboratory settings fail to 
deliver their expected value within real-world production cycles.
Building on this work, our paper broadens the perspective beyond 
pure algorithmics to examine operation-centric challenges that hinder 
the transition of technology from “usable” to “practical” and from “pa­
pers” to “fields.” These challenges, grounded in the realities of farming, 
ultimately determine whether a technology can be deployed reliably and 
at scale.
6.1. Semantic conversion from technical output to decisions
Semantic gap from metrics to management decisions: Research 
efforts in UAV-based deep learning counting systems have achieved high 
accuracy in measuring crop attributes, such as plant density or seedling 
loss rates. However, these metrics alone rarely translate into actionable 
agronomic directives. Farm management requires contextual judge­
ments that integrate multiple data sources—soil diagnostics, weather, 
nutrient status, and economic constraints—rather than isolated visual 
metrics. For example, decisions about replanting or adjusting inputs 
depend on a complex balance between crop density, nitrogen manage­
ment, and weed pressure [1,2]. Existing systems fail to provide semantic

models that connect raw perception outputs with operational decision 
frameworks. The lack of such conversion mechanisms is a core barrier to 
achieving impact in field-scale crop management.
Mismatch between decision windows and model temporal 
decay: Crops are dynamic systems that change rapidly during growth. A 
static model trained early in the season quickly loses accuracy due to 
temporal decay, a challenge especially prominent in agricultural AI. 
Meanwhile, farm decisions have strict time windows. Seedling assess­
ments must occur within 7 to 10 days to allow for replanting. Early spike 
assessment is essential for yield prediction and subsequent management 
optimization. Fitting data acquisition, processing, and decision-making 
into these narrow windows demands extreme automation and effi­
ciency, making time sensitivity a unique challenge for agricultural AI.
6.2. Scaling from lab accuracy to field-level reliability
Inherent conflict between data fidelity and operational effi­
ciency: High-resolution imagery, essential for accuracy, requires low 
and slow flights. This method sacrifices operational efficiency and cost- 
effectiveness in large fields. Conversely, efficient, high-speed flights 
reduce critical image resolution. Most studies avoid this fundamental 
conflict by using small, idealized datasets. This inherent trade-off be­
tween data fidelity and operational efficiency is a specific constraint of 
agricultural applications not typically observed in general computer 
vision.
Lack of spatial mapping and precision agriculture interfaces: 
Agricultural value requires spatially explicit data to create prescription 
maps used in variable-rate applications. Current models often provide 
only aggregate counts, lacking the spatial mapping capability to 
generate fertilizer or seeding maps compatible with farm machinery. 
This transition from “point counting” to “area management” is a key 
bottleneck in precision agriculture. A deeper challenge is modeling the 
inference bias between 2D visual data and 3D physical reality. This in­
volves calibrating the “visual count” from algorithms to a more accurate 
“agronomic count.” Current studies often validate on small patches, 
failing to systematically verify reliability for large-scale applications.
6.3. Integrating agricultural knowledge and cross-domain adaptation
Difficulty of encoding domain knowledge for agronomic 
context: Effective crop counting requires encoding prior agricultural 
knowledge such as planting patterns, variety characteristics, and man­
agement practices. Models must incorporate this unstructured agro­
nomic knowledge as an inductive bias, rather than treating crops as 
generic objects. The challenge lies in formalizing and modeling this 
domain knowledge to improve learning efficiency and generalization.
The “long-tail problem” of cross-region and variety adaptation: 
Models trained in one region or on a specific crop variety often fail in 
new environments. The high heterogeneity of agriculture, with its 
different crops, locations, and cultivation methods, demands that 
models resist catastrophic forgetting when adapting. This challenge of 
cross-domain adaptation is more complex than in general AI problems. It 
requires developing models with strong self-adaptation capabilities that 
can generalize to new scenarios with very low retraining costs.
6.4. Technical design under economic constraints
Cost-benefit trade-offs within agricultural profit margins: Low 
profit margins in broad-acre crop production heighten cost sensitivity. 
This distinguishes agricultural applications from industrial vision, 
where high-value manufacturing supports substantial investment. In 
deep learning-based field crop counting, the cost-benefit trade-off is 
particularly pronounced. Reliable, actionable counts often require high- 
resolution imagery from low-altitude, slow UAV flights, professional 
pilots, and GPU servers for model inference. While this approach ach­
ieves high accuracy, its costs hinder wide deployment in large-scale 
cropping systems. Alternatively, consumer-grade drones and local pro­
cessing reduce expenses but typically yield counts that lack sufficient 
reliability for essential decisions such as replanting or targeted thinning. 
The choice between costly, accurate solutions and affordable, unreliable 
ones remains the central economic barrier to large-scale adoption.
Challenges in system integration with agricultural workflows: 
Even with accurate perception modules, the practical value for pro­
ducers is limited unless these outputs are efficiently integrated into farm 
management platforms and machinery control systems. The absence of 
unified standards and open interfaces inhibits seamless interoperability 
across devices and software. Count maps and density data remain 
disconnected from prescription workflows required for variable-rate 
operations, such as targeted replanting or thinning. This lack of inte­
gration confines research to demonstration-scale solutions and prevents 
scaling to routine agricultural practice. Overcoming this systems 
bottleneck is essential for transitioning advanced phenotyping from 
academic prototypes to industry-wide adoption in broad-acre cropping.
6.5. Discussions: an integrated framework for bridging the lab-to-field gap
The success of UAV-based crop counting will depend on bridging 
four key gaps: translating counts into agricultural decisions, scaling 
image accuracy to robust field performance, integrating computer vision 
with agronomic knowledge, and ensuring economic viability for real- 
world deployment. Rather than focusing solely on algorithms, our 
analysis highlights system-level bottlenecks resulting from agriculture’s 
dynamic environments, broad scale, and strict cost constraints. 
Addressing these challenges calls for collaboration across agronomy, 
technology, and economics.
Our proposed framework systematically addresses these challenges. 
It is structured as a three-tier system, bringing together UAV-based 
sensing, cloud-based decision-making, and mobile interfaces for end 
users. Challenge 6.1 can be addressed by using cloud-based decision 
engines that combine perception data with farm management inputs to 
enable actionable prescription maps within critical time frames. A 
centralized platform (Challenge 6.2) could support data of varying fi­
delity, balancing flight efficiency and accuracy while generating 
spatially mapped outputs for precision agriculture. By integrating agri­
cultural knowledge and cross-domain adaptation, the framework may 
improve adaptation to diverse and rare field conditions, thereby tackling 
the long-tail problem (Challenge 6.3). A distributed architecture can 
further separate high-cost processing from affordable farmer interfaces, 
supporting economic scalability (Challenge 6.4).

7. Future research directions
Building on the key challenges detailed in Chapter 6, this chapter 
outlines a research roadmap for UAV-based field crop counting. The goal 
is to advance the technology toward robust, practical field deployment. 
This roadmap highlights four interconnected frontiers, each addressing 
specific bottlenecks that currently limit real-world deployment.
7.1. Leveraging large foundational models and generative AI
To address the semantic gap in decision-making (Challenge 6.1), 
overcome data scarcity, and improve model generalization (Challenges 
6.3 & 6.4), future work should harness large vision and multimodal 
foundational models to UAV-based crop counting in broad-acre fields. 
These models enable few-shot or zero-shot learning, reducing the de­
pendency on extensive manual annotation in variable field environ­
ments. Although universal counting tasks have advanced computer 
vision, agricultural scenarios still require models that can handle mixed 
crop varieties, spatial complexity, and different growth stages.
Integrating agricultural domain knowledge into model architectures 
remains essential. Approaches such as visual prompting and encoding 
agronomic priors, including crop morphology and developmental pat­
terns, can facilitate adaptation to real field conditions (Challenge 6.3). 
Generative AI offers new opportunities to create high-quality synthetic 
training data that cover diverse plant densities, canopy structures, oc­
clusion levels, and environmental backgrounds. This enables models to 
learn from rare or complex cases that are difficult to capture with UAVs 
(Challenge 6.4).
However, generated synthetic images may differ from true field 
conditions, especially in plant appearance and background realism. 
Such discrepancies can affect model performance during real-world 
deployment. Therefore, effective domain adaptation and rigorous vali­
dation using authentic field data are necessary to ensure robust and 
reliable counting in practical, large-scale agricultural settings.
For Challenge 6.1, an important research direction is to leverage the 
reasoning capabilities of large multimodal models to build context- 
aware decision engines. By fusing visual data with other agronomic 
information, these models can help translate technical outputs into 
actionable farm management decisions.
7.2. Towards a unified paradigm for open-set counting
Addressing architectural rigidity and cross-domain adaptation 
(Challenge 6.3) requires moving beyond single-task architectures to­
ward a unified model paradigm capable of counting multiple crop cat­
egories and organs. This paradigm shift would reframe the task from 
simple detection or regression to more flexible object retrieval or 
matching. Leveraging foundational models, a universal system could use 
visual prompts or a few examples to identify new targets, enabling 
adaptability and scalability without costly retraining. Such an approach 
is crucial for agricultural deployment, where the diversity of crops, va­
rieties, temporal growth stages, and growing conditions demands 
models that can rapidly adapt. Maintaining model performance as crops 
transition through different phenological stages presents a distinct 
challenge for agricultural AI that requires continuous adaptation 
throughout the growing season. The development of cross-category 
generalization capabilities represents a pivotal advancement that 
could significantly reduce deployment costs and accelerate technology 
adoption.
7.3. From static counts to dynamic phenotyping
To move beyond static, single-snapshot analysis and address se­
mantic conversion challenges (Challenge 6.1), future research must 
unlock deeper agronomic insights through spatiotemporal dynamic 
analysis that provides agronomically meaningful insights. The use of 
time-series imagery or video streams can enable the continuous 
modeling of crop growth trajectories, addressing temporal model decay 
while generating richer phenotypic metrics such as emergence rates, 
growth velocity, and developmental patterns. This represents a funda­
mental shift from treating crops as static counting targets to under­
standing them as dynamic biological systems with predictable growth 
behaviors.
By integrating counting results with comprehensive phenotypic pa­
rameters such as plant height, canopy coverage, and biomass estimation, 
this approach establishes the foundational data for bridging technical 
outputs to agronomic understanding. The fusion of multi-temporal 
counting data with growth modeling creates contextual frameworks 
that transform isolated metrics into predictive agricultural intelligence, 
directly addressing the multi-modal nature of farm decision-making 
identified in Challenge 6.1.
7.4. Fully autonomous and integrated edge-cloud systems
To translate the integrated framework from Section 6.5 into practice, 
future research must build a fully autonomous and cost-effective edge- 
cloud collaborative system. This approach is essential for addressing the 
critical bottlenecks of scalability (Challenge 6.2) and economic viability 
(Challenge 6.4). Key research directions include developing autono­
mous multi-UAV fleets equipped with optimized, low-cost RGB cameras. 
This will enable efficient data acquisition while minimizing labor costs. 
Furthermore, a seamless edge-cloud architecture is crucial. Edge 
computing will handle real-time, on-site analysis to meet critical deci­
sion windows, while the cloud provides the robust computational power 
for complex deep learning model training and large-scale data process­
ing. A critical task to close the loop from perception to action is the 
development of open-standard interface protocols. This will ensure 
seamless integration with existing farm management information sys­
tems and agricultural machinery, enabling the automatic generation and 
transmission of compatible prescription maps. Realizing this closed-loop 
system will ultimately transform algorithmic advances into practical, 
on-farm tools.
7.5. Conclusion
The transition from algorithmic development to practical, in-field 
decision-making for UAV-based crop counting demands systematic 
integration of technical, agronomic, and economic considerations. This 
review has identified and synthesized four core research directions that 
address key challenges for large-scale, real-world deployment: 
(1) harnessing foundational models and generative AI for data effi­
ciency, generalization, and context-aware decision making;
(2) developing unified paradigms that enable open-set and cross- 
domain counting;
(3) applying spatiotemporal phenotyping to move beyond static 
analysis, thus delivering agronomically meaningful insights;
(4) building fully autonomous, integrated edge-cloud systems to 
achieve operational scalability and economic viability.
