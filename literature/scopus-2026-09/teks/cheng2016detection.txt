Detection of apple fruit in an orchard for early yield 
prediction as a function of crop load 
H. Cheng1, Y. Sun2, L. Damerow3 and M.M. Blanke4,a 
1College of Science and Technology, Agric. Univ. of Hebei, Baoding, China; 2China Agriculture University, Beijing, 
China; 3Institute or Agricultural Engineering, University of Bonn, Bonn, Germany; 4INRES-Horticultural Science, 
University of Bonn, Bonn, Germany. 
Abstract 
Yield estimation in the commercial production of fruit tree species is becoming 
an increasingly popular research topic. The outcome, however, is subject to both 
variability from tree to tree, and from year to year (alternate bearing). Hence, the 
objective of this contribution was to investigate any potential effects of tree crop load 
on fruit detection for the prediction of tree yields, as used in image analysis. 
Approximately 100 apple (Malus domestica Borkh.) trees in Bonn, Germany, were 
subjected to six crop load modifications, including: (a) mechanical thinning at 
flowering in April; (b) manual thinning after the June drop; and (c) control without 
thinning, each time with hand or machine pruning over two years. Trees were 
photographed three times during fruit ontogeny and these RGB images were 
converted to YIQ color space and a shape analysis to segment the apple fruits from the 
background. YCbCr and RGB color spaces were then combined to segment leaves from 
the tree images. The efficiency of fruit recognition (EFR) improved with fruit 
ontogeny. EFR was lower without either thinning (38%) or manual pruning (41%) 
because the lack of either of these measures resulted in many very small fruits. EFR 
was improved from the unthinned control trees, to those with a single crop load 
regulation from thinning (hand or mechanical: 49%), to those with the combination of 
the two crop load regulation practices (58%). Overall, the results indicate that crop 
load may have a strong influence on fruit recognition in yield prediction work. Apple 
is used here as a model crop, but the results may be transferable to the wide range of 
trees where yield estimation is used. 
Keywords: alternate bearing, fruit abscission, fruit ontogeny, image analysis 
INTRODUCTION 
In many horticultural fruit crops, alternate bearing may induce significant fluctuations 
in year to year yields (Krasniqi et al., 2013). Yield estimation and prediction with an 
emphasis on yield variability from tree to tree and year to year is a crucial step in precision 
orchard management, for example, in citrus in South Africa (Blanke and Bower, 1991), apple 
in Europe and China (Stajnko et al., 2009; Krasniqi et al., 2013), and mango (Mangifera 
indica) in Australia (Payne et al., 2013). Yield is estimated after the last natural (June) drop 
of the respective fruit. 
Crop load management (CLM) for regular fruit bearing can be accomplished either by 
removal of complete branches (winter or hand pruning) or e.g., selective removal of fruit 
(e.g., mechanical thinning; Schupp et al., 2008). In a comparison of un-thinned and hand-
thinned ‘Empire’ apple trees, Goffinet et al. (1995) found that fruit thinning increased fruit 
size and fruit colour. Schupp et al. (2008) proposed mechanical thinning of peach (Prunus 
persica L.) and apple trees to increase fruit size. 
Untiedt and Blanke (2001) reported on fruit thinning agents that had effects on apple

Since these CLM measures may influence fruit quality and control tree growth and 
productivity, they are likely to influence fruit detection by image processing in yield 
prediction. Previous work on yield prediction concentrated on improving the algorithms of 
fruit detection, irrespective of crop load employed (e.g., Zhou et al., 2012; Stajnko et al., 
2009), but to our knowledge, there is no report on the effect of CLM on fruit recognition. The 
objective of the present work was to investigate any potential effects of tree CLM brought 
about by mechanical, hand pruning versus no thinning on fruit detection as used in yield 
estimation or prediction throughout phase III of fruit growth (Jackson, 2003; Naor et al., 
2008). 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Site description and image acquisition 
Approximately 100 thirteen-year-old ‘Pinova’/M.9 apple trees, spaced at 3.5×1.5 m, 
and trained to slender spindles were situated at Campus Klein-Altendorf of the University of 
Bonn, Bonn, Germany. Fruit trees were planted in N-S orientation to optimize light 
utilization. In 2012, six plots (9 trees each) received one of three treatments, which were 
repeated in 2013: a) mechanical thinning (MT) at flowering in the end of April; b) manual 
thinning after the June drop; and c) control without thinning. Pruning (HP) was conducted in 
the previous winter of each year in this experiment. After June drop and manual thinning, 
the apple tree canopy images were captured at a constant distance of 1.5 m height above the 
ground surface and 1.4 m perpendicular to each tree row in natural daylight using a 
commonly available digital camera in ‘auto-focus’ mode without use of the zoom. Green and 
red calibration spheres of 50 mm diameter were used to estimate fruit size and a white 
drapery was placed behind the target tree to distinguish fruits from trees in other rows 
(Figure 1). 
Fifty-four ‘Pinova’ canopy images were captured twice in 2012, i.e., when fruit were 
light-green after June drop on July 12, 2012 and on August 9, 2012 (i.e., about 1.5 months 
before harvest) on the preferred west side of the tree. Seventy-two ‘Pinova’ canopy images 
were captured three times in 2013, i.e., on August 2 (about 2 months before harvest), on 
August 16, and on August 30 (about one month before harvest). 
Fruit recognition and fruit segmentation algorithms 
These captured Red-Green-Blue (RGB) images were converted in YIQ colour space, in 
which the Y component stands for the luminance, I for the chrominance from orange to blue, 
and Q for the chrominance information from purple to green (Zhou et al., 2012). Apple fruits 
with colour from light green to red in an image could be segmented from the background at 
a threshold I value in excess of 0.1. if I≥0.1 → apple fruit; if I<0.1 → background. 
After this first segmentation, the light green abaxial sides of leaves and branches were 
first falsely classified as fruits. In the subsequent red (R), green (G) and blue (B) colour 
analysis for each pixel, green apple leaves were distinguished by the following values of 
green (G) >180, red (R) <180 and blue (B) <90 (Figure 1). 
Since similar circular regions represent apple fruits and slender rectangular regions 
represent branches, aspect ratio (AR) and roundness (RN) were employed to remove the 
branches from the images. For each connected region, if AR>5 or RN<0.3, then it was 
designated as a branch region (and not a fruit). Then, morphology analysis was used to 
remove other noises.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
All captured images were processed using MATLAB and the fruit recognition 
algorithms described above. The efficiency of fruit recognition (EFR) of apples in the tree 
canopy images improved from August 2 to 16 and August 30 (Figure 1). With harvest 
approaching, leaves ceasing to grow (Wibbe and Blanke, 1995; Jackson, 2003), and apples

Statistical analysis of the results for each group of 2012 showed significant differences 
between a) mechanical pruning (MP) and manual pruning (HP) both without thinning 
(control 1 and control 2), b) mechanical thinning (MT) and mechanical pruning (MP) and c) 
mechanical thinning and manual pruning (Figure 2). To our knowledge, this is the first time, 
possibly because mechanical pruning has become a new tool in horticultural fruit crops and 
now opens up the canopy sides ca. 40 cm from the trunk and removes one-year-old 
branches. 
The efficiency of fruit recognition (EFR) in both controls (without thinning) of 38-41% 
(Figure 2) was lower, because the lack of this measure resulted in many small fruits. Both 
averages and medians of fruit recognition for the other four treatments with mechanical 
thinning were improved by increments of ca. 10% per group, respectively. Interestingly, the 
averages and medians of the EFR were only more efficient in trees pruned by hand when 
combined with mechanical thinning (Figure 2); this manual pruning in fruit trees in the 
previous winter principally removes flowering wood and thick branches to renew growth 
and enables light penetration. 
There was a significant difference between thinned trees and un-thinned trees (Table 
1). There was also a significant difference between thinned trees with machine pruning and 
thinned trees with hand pruning. 
The EFR for each crop load management in 2013 is shown in Figure 3. This efficiency 
in fruit recognition increased from the un-thinned control trees, via those with a single crop 
load regulation viz. thinning motion (hand or mechanical) to those with the combination of 
the two crop load regulation techniques (Figure 3). The addition of hand thinning to 
mechanical thinning showed a positive effect on reducing crop load. Duncan’s multiple range 
test resulted in significant differences between the thinned plots and un-thinned plots (Table

During 2013, canopy images of the trees were captured four times: on August 2, 16 
and 30, and on September 28. The fruit area and leaf area were detected for each image and 
the results are shown in Figure 4. The leaf area, which could be detected in the apple tree 
canopy image, decreased and became smaller than the fruit area, which could be recognized, 
and the fruit increased in size in line with fruit ontogeny (Naor et al., 2008). Figure 4 allows 
to interpret the results shown in Figures 1 to 3: over time, the detected leaf area in pixels per 
image decreased, whereas the detected fruit area in pixels per image increased slowly. This 
is in line with tree physiology studies (Wibbe and Blanke, 1995) showing that leaf expansion 
in apple trees of the Northern Hemisphere ceases in late June-early July while fruit continues 
to grow (Naor et al., 2008).

CONCLUSIONS 
This study has shown that a) crop load has a major influence on the efficiency of fruit 
recognition in the yield prediction for fruit trees, b) the combination of two crop load 
management practices, hand winter pruning and mechanical thinning (both at 360 and 420 
rpm) had the strongest effects, and c) the efficiency of fruit recognition improves with time 
during fruit growth approaching harvest. It seems likely that these results on apple are 
transferable to most other tree crop species.
