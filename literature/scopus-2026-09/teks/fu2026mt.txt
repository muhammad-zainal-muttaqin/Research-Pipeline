A B S T R A C T
One of the key challenges in orchard robots is accurately localizing occluded fruits in complex environments, 
especially when the fruit targets are split into multiple isolated regions within images. Traditional single-task 
network models exhibit limited capability in discerning fragmented targets that belong to the same fruit but 
are segmented into multiple spatially isolated regions within images. In addition, fruit localization largely relies 
on high-cost sensors or additional 3-D localization algorithms. To address this issue, we propose a fruit detection 
and centroid localization method based on a Multi-Task Wavelet-Enhanced YOLO (MT-WavYOLO) to enhance 
the success rate of robotic operations on occluded fruit targets. Initially, a lightweight semantic segmentation 
branch was integrated into the YOLOv8 backbone network to precisely segment exposed fruits, while retaining 
the original object detection branch to fully identify occluded fruits. To address the diminished sensitivity of 
conventional models to geometric profiles of heavily occluded fruits, a novel feature fusion module, 
C2f_WTConv, was designed by incorporating wavelet transform convolution, leveraging the multi-frequency 
robustness of wavelet representations to enhance the model’s feature extraction capabilities under complex 
orchard occlusions. Subsequently, a 3D frustum-based point cloud processing method was proposed, combining 
the detection results from MT-WavYOLO with the semantic segmentation masks to accurately localize occluded 
fruits. MT-WavYOLO demonstrated a 2%, 1.5%, and 2.2% improvement in Precision, Recall, and mAP50, 
respectively, on our custom-built dataset compared to the latest YOLOv10s model. Semantic segmentation 
performance, measured by Intersection over Union (IoU) and Accuracy, was improved by 5.2% and 3.8%, 
respectively, over the state-of-the-art Deeplabv3+ network. Compared to the adapted multi-task network 
YOLOP, MT-WavYOLO achieved a 3.4% increase in mAP50 and a 2.7% improvement in IoU. In addition, MT- 
WavYOLO has a compact footprint of 10.2 M parameters and achieves approximately 27 FPS in real-time 
inference, thereby meeting the requirements of robotic harvesting operations. The proposed localization 
method was evaluated through 600 fruit localization tests using six different RGB-D cameras in an orchard 
environment. The average experimental results demonstrated that the centroid localization and radius estimation 
errors were reduced by 42.5%, 73.7%, 16.17%, and 11.25%, respectively, compared to traditional 3D bounding 
box methods and our previous approaches. These results indicate that the MT-WavYOLO combined with the 
frustum-based method significantly enhances the accuracy of apple localization under complex orchard condi­
tions using consumer-grade sensors, providing a strong practical foundation for non-destructive robotic 
harvesting.
1. Introduction
Apples rank among the most extensively cultivated and agricultur­
ally significant fruit crops globally. In recent years, with accelerating 
population aging, advancing urbanization, and rising labor costs, or­
chards have faced seasonal labor shortages and mismatches in labor 
supply. Harvesting is highly time-sensitive, physically demanding, and 
repetitive; traditional manual picking is time-consuming and labor- 
intensive, and prolonged high-intensity work elevates health risks for 
workers. Under the “dual-carbon” agenda and the rise of digital agri­
culture, reliance on manual labor alone can no longer meet modern 
orchards’ requirements for efficient and stable management, making

during peak seasons, reduce dependence on seasonal labor, and improve

harvesting is accurate detection and localization of apples through 
robust perception technologies, which is critical to ensuring picking

Considerable progress has been made in fruit recognition, with 
numerous studies focusing on the detection of fruits. Traditional com­
puter vision methods based on color, texture, and shape features (Liu 
et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2021) have been replaced by deep learning- 
based approaches (Elgazzar et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2023), which 
exhibit higher robustness and adaptability under various complex con­
ditions. In the field of deep learning-based apple fruit recognition, sig­
nificant advancements have been made with object detection models 
such as the YOLO series (Redmon et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024) and 
Faster R-CNN series (Ren et al., 2016), as well as segmentation models 
such as Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017) and YOLACT (Bolya et al., 2019). 
These techniques, initially designed for general object detection (Guo 
et al., 2024a), have been successfully adapted and optimized for the 
specific challenges of fruit recognition, significantly improving 
performance.
YOLO-based detection models are renowned for their speed and real- 
time processing capabilities, making them particularly effective for 
apple detection in dynamic environments (Wu et al., 2024). Kang and 
Chen (2020) developed a deep learning detector for apple detection, 
“LedNet”, which achieved a recall rate of 0.821 and an accuracy of 0.853 
with an inference time of 28 ms, enabling real-time and accurate apple 
detection in orchards. Tian et al. (2019) proposed an improved YOLO- 
V3 model using DenseNet, achieving real-time apple detection in or­
chards with a model resolution of 3000×3000 pixels and a detection 
time of only 0.304 s. Liu et al. (2024) designed the PDWFasterNet 
module using a computationally efficient Partial Depthwise Convolution 
(PDWConv), reducing the parameters and floating-point operations 
(FLOPs) of Faster-YOLO-AP to 0.66 M and 2.29 G, while achieving 
84.12% mAP@.50:.95. Segmentation models such as Mask R-CNN and 
YOLACT have shown remarkable performance in accurately delineating 
individual fruit contours, enabling precise localization and segmenta­
tion. Chu et al. (2021) developed a novel Suppressive Mask R-CNN for 
apple detection, adding a suppression branch to the standard Mask R- 
CNN to filter out non-apple features, achieving an F1 score of 0.905 and 
a detection time of 0.25 s per frame. Wang and He (2022) introduced an 
enhanced Mask Scoring R-CNN, incorporating convolutional layers and 
a dual-attention network to improve the accuracy of bounding box 
detection (bbox_mAP) and instance segmentation (mask_mAP), 
achieving bbox_mAP and mask_mAP scores of 0.932 and 0.920, 
respectively, with an average processing time of 0.27 s per image.
Although previous studies have made significant progress in 
detecting unobstructed fruits, the recognition of occluded fruits has not 
been thoroughly investigated. Precise localization of occluded fruits 
remains a technical challenge in orchard environments, where fruits are 
often partially obstructed by tree branches, leaves, and other fruits. This 
issue has attracted considerable attention from researchers. Chu et al. 
(2023) developed a new Occluder-Occludee Relationship Network 
(O2RNet) to detect overlapping apples by embedding relationships be­
tween occluders and occludees, significantly reducing false positives and 
achieving a detection rate of 94%. Rathore et al. (2023) proposed a two- 
stage deep learning-based method, using the state-of-the-art YOLOv7 
model in the first stage to detect apples as a single class, followed by an 
enhanced Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) network in the second 
stage to classify the detected apples into non-occluded, leaf-occluded, 
stem/branch-occluded, and apple-occluded categories, achieving F1 
scores of 94.64%, 90.91%, 86.87%, and 90.25%, respectively. While 
these studies have advanced the detection of occluded fruits in orchards, 
obtaining multi-dimensional information of occluded fruits (estimating 
the complete size and pixel-level segmentation of occluded fruits) using 
a single network still poses significant challenges.
In robot operations, detecting the target fruit solely at the image level 
is insufficient, it is also necessary to extract fruit information from the 
3D point cloud to achieve accurate localization. However, apples are 
often hidden in cluttered branches and leaves, and occlusions caused by 
branches, leaves, and other fruits can result in incomplete point cloud 
data, negatively affecting visual localization accuracy and harvesting 
efficiency. To address the degradation of point cloud quality under 
occluded conditions, some researchers have employed multi-sensor 
fusion methods for fruit localization, including the use of mobile 
ground-based laser scanners (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2019) and LiDAR (Kang 
et al., 2022). Although these methods achieve high localization accuracy 
in orchards, their high costs and complex calibration procedures remain 
bottlenecks for practical applications. More researchers have focused on 
using stereo vision-based localization methods. Gen´e-Mola et al. (2020)
combined 2D instance segmentation and Structure from Motion (SfM) 
for 3D fruit localization, projecting the Mask R-CNN detection results 
onto SfM-based 3D point clouds. Since SfM only generates 3D models for 
objects appearing in at least two different images, false positives 
detected in a single image are automatically discarded. The 3D locali­
zation results reported an F1 score of 0.881. Yoshida et al. (2022) used 
an RGB-D camera to identify fruits in 2D images and then fitted the fruits 
to spheres in 3D space by setting the center and short side of the 
bounding box as the center and diameter of the sphere, respectively, to 
localize apples.
However, due to sensor limitations and occlusions from branches and 
leaves, the acquired point clouds are often fragmented and distorted. 
Therefore, developing accurate localization methods for fruits with 
limited point cloud information is crucial. Lin et al. (2019) proposed an 
algorithm for citrus detection and RGB-D image localization, using 
density clustering and SVM to filter images and detect and localize citrus 
fruits. The average localization error across all directions was about 13 
mm, but the method required 1.25 s to detect a single citrus fruit. Gong 
et al. (2022) addressed the detection of occluded tomatoes in green­
houses by designing an extended Mask-RCNN network to extract target 
tomatoes, followed by a shape and position recovery method to restore 
partially occluded tomatoes. This algorithm can directly extract tomato 
radius and centroid coordinates from the recovered depth image, 
achieving an Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.895 and a centroid 
localization error of 0.62 mm under conditions of less than 25% occlu­
sion and illuminance between 1 and 12 KLux. However, its performance 
is not ideal under severe occlusion and high illuminance conditions. In 
our previous work (Li et al., 2022), we proposed a point cloud processing 
method based on view frustum and DBSCAN clustering for occluded 
apple localization. This method can estimate the 3D centroid co­
ordinates and radius of occluded fruits even when point cloud data 
generated by consumer-grade depth cameras is imperfect. Although 
promising results were achieved in orchards, our experiments revealed 
that the performance of fruit localization also depends on the precision 
of the centerline extraction based on 2D bounding boxes and 2D masks, 
which affects centroid localization along the XOY plane and perfor­
mance along the Z-axis. Additionally, using DBSCAN clustering in­
creases processing time.
In summary, fruit detection under occlusion still faces challenges in 
detecting occluded fruits (such as multiple detections, redundant de­
tections, and the inability to predict the complete size of occluded 
fruits); in addition, it is hindered by the complexities and long pro­
cessing times associated with existing localization methods. These 
problems often lead to missed picks during field operations, forcing 
robots (or human workers) to perform secondary picking, which sub­
stantially increases labor costs and results in significant resource waste. 
To address these challenges, this paper proposes MT-WavYOLO, a multi- 
task network that can simultaneously perform apple detection and 
segmentation, generating precise masks and bounding boxes capable of 
predicting the complete fruit size. Furthermore, we introduce a frustum- 
based point-cloud processing method combined with the multi-task

The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 
(a) Proposes a multi-task learning network (MT-WavYOLO) for 
simultaneous apple detection and segmentation in complex or­
chard environments. This network integrates an object detection 
model with a novel lightweight segmentation branch, enhancing 
the extraction of multi-dimensional information, particularly for 
occluded fruits.
(b) Designs a novel feature fusion module, C2f_WTConv, utilizing 
wavelet transform convolution (WTConv). This module replaces 
standard convolutions to capture contextual information across 
different frequency scales, significantly improving the multi-task 
model’s multi-scale feature fusion capabilities while reducing 
computational complexity.
(c) Introduces a novel localization method for occluded apples by 
combining frustum-based point cloud processing with the MT-

without requiring additional network structures, and its effec­
tiveness was validated using multiple consumer-grade depth 
cameras.
Overall, contributions (a)–(c) are designed to address the core 
challenges identified above, including robust detection and segmenta­
tion of occluded fruits, exploitation of multi-frequency wavelet cues for 
heavily occluded instances, and efficient 3D localization of fruits via 
frustum-based centroid estimation from fragmented point clouds.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Image acquisition
An apple image dataset was collected from standardized orchards in 
Beijing, Yan’an, and Yuncheng, China, using a mobile robotic platform 
(Fig. 1). The platform, equipped with two RGB-D cameras (FOV: 
69◦×42◦, max range: 3 m) positioned at 1.3 m and 2.0 m heights, 
captured images from perspectives relevant to robotic harvesting. 
Operating at 0.5 m/s, it acquired 15 fps video at resolutions of 640×480 
and 1280×720 pixels. Data collection occurred between 08:00 and 
19:00 to encompass diverse lighting (including front/backlight and 
artificial light) and occlusion scenarios (non–, slight-, severe-occlusion), 
as sampled in Fig. 2.
Initially, 3,500 images featuring two apple varieties (’Magic Star’, 
’Wageningen 29′) were gathered from 336 trees. The dataset was par­
titioned into training, validation, and testing sets in an 8:1:1 ratio. To 
enhance the robustness of the model and mitigate overfitting, standard 
data augmentation techniques were applied exclusively to the training 
set. These included salt-and-pepper noise (probability=0.02), horizontal 
flipping (probability=0.5), brightness adjustment factor (0.8–1.2), 
random translation (±10%), and random rotation (±15◦). Following 
manual screening to remove augmented samples unsuitable for training 
(e.g., images with excessive augmentation such as overexposure), the 
training set was expanded to 6,300 images, comprising 2,800 original 
images and 3,500 augmented images. In total, the final dataset consisted 
of 7,000 images. All images were annotated with both bounding boxes 
and segmentation masks, which were used for model development and 
evaluation.

In this study, we propose a novel multi-task network, MT-WavYOLO, 
designed for simultaneous fruit detection and segmentation in orchard

key components: the backbone, detect-neck, segment-neck, detect-head,

network, enabling object detection at the image level and semantic 
segmentation at the pixel level.
To optimize feature integration, distinct neck networks were 
designed specifically for the detection and segmentation heads. 
Together, the backbone and necks form the encoder, while the detection 
and segmentation heads act as decoders. Notably, the decoders avoid 
redundant shared blocks to minimize computational overhead while 
preserving the one-stage structure of the network.
By combining the outputs of object detection and semantic seg­
mentation, MT-WavYOLO achieves accurate detection of occluded 
fruits, providing a robust foundation for precise localization by orchard 
harvesting robots.
2.2.1. Backbone and C2f_WTConv
The backbone network consists of a series of convolutional layers 
designed to extract features from input images. Given the outstanding 
performance of YOLOv8 in detection tasks, the backbone of YOLOv8 was 
adopted as the feature extraction network for MT-WavYOLO.
To better extract features at different levels of abstraction and detail 
from the input data, we propose a novel feature fusion module, 
C2f_WTConv. Traditional convolutional layers achieve larger receptive 
fields by increasing the kernel size, but this approach results in a 
quadratic growth in the number of parameters and leads to performance 
saturation or even degradation once the receptive field exceeds a certain 
size. Furthermore, larger convolutional kernels are prone to over- 
parameterization, reducing the generalization ability of the network. 
In contrast, wavelet transforms decompose input signals into different 
frequency components, enabling operations at multiple frequency scales 
and effectively addressing these challenges (Ma et al., 2024). The pro­
cessing flow of the wavelet transform is illustrated in the Appendix E.
Finder et al. (2025) demonstrated that cascading wavelet decom­
position with small-kernel convolutions achieves exponential growth in 
receptive fields while effectively controlling the number of parameters 
and computational cost. Fig. 6 illustrates a two-level wavelet-transform 
convolution (WTConv). At each level, the low-frequency branch is 
decomposed by DWT into four sub-bands (LL, LH, HL, HH), which are 
concatenated and refined with a small-kernel depthwise convolution; 
the features are then reconstructed top-down via IWT, and a base 
depthwise-convolution branch is added as a residual to produce the final 
output. By combining the advantages of wavelet-domain feature pro­
cessing with the efficient feature aggregation mechanism of the C2f 
module, as shown in Fig. 5, we integrate wavelet convolution into the 
Bottleneck structure of the C2f module. This integration enables multi- 
scale feature extraction and enhancement, significantly improving the 
receptive field and overall performance of convolutional neural 
networks.
2.2.2. Neck
The neck component enhances feature representation by fusing 
feature maps at different scales extracted from the backbone (Guo et al., 
2024b). In the MT-WavYOLO model, two necks were designed: one for 
the fruit detection and another for the fruit semantic segmentation.
For the detection neck, the path aggregation network (PAN) (Liu 
et al., 2018) and feature pyramid network (FPN) (Lin et al., 2017) 
combination method used in the YOLOv8 neck was not adopted. While 
this method effectively integrates shallow and deep features, improving 
the model’s ability to identify subtle details, it filters feature pathways 
through FPN, discarding some typical feature information initially ob­
tained from the backbone. To address this, we implemented a bidirec­
tional feature pyramid network (BiFPN) (Zhu et al., 2018) structure

In semantic segmentation tasks, architectures such as HRNet and 
BiFPN are commonly employed as the neck of the segmentation branch 
to facilitate effective multi-scale contextual feature fusion. However, the 
segmentation of occluded fruits poses greater challenges: the target re­
gions often occupy only a small portion of the image, with blurred 
boundaries and low contrast, thereby placing higher demands on both 
spatial detail preservation and semantic representation. To address 
these challenges, this study adopts a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) as 
the segmentation neck. By employing a top-down hierarchical fusion 
mechanism, FPN effectively integrates high-resolution low-level fea­
tures with semantically rich high-level features, enhancing the model’s 
capability to recognize small and partially occluded targets. This 
structure is particularly well-suited for segmentation tasks involving 
small fruit objects in complex natural orchard environments. Moreover, 
FPN offers a compact architecture with high computational efficiency, 
achieving a favorable balance between segmentation accuracy and real- 
time performance, making it highly suitable for deployment on 
resource-constrained edge devices.

The head component processes feature maps from the neck to make 
predictions for each task, including classification, corresponding 
bounding boxes, and segmentation masks for the objects.

For the detection head, the decoupled head structure from the

reliance on predefined anchor boxes for target localization. The anchor- 
free decoupled detection head adopts two parallel branches for object

extract contextual information and one 1×1 Conv2d modules for 
dimensionality reduction. This decoupled design enables MT-WavYOLO 
to perform efficient object detection without anchor boxes while 
maintaining a relatively small model size and low computational cost.
For the segmentation head, this study designed a lightweight seg­
mentation head to process feature maps from the segmentation neck, as 
illustrated in the segment module in Fig. 5. It consists of only three 
convolutional modules for extracting contextual information and one 
deconvolutional (DeConv) module to restore the resolution to the orig­
inal image size. Thanks to the strong feature extraction capabilities of 
the backbone and FPN, the computational burden of the decoder is 
effectively reduced, resulting in a lightweight overall structure. Finally, 
a pixel-level binary mask matching the size of the original image is 
obtained.
2.2.4. Loss function
MT-WavYOLO trains the entire multi-task network by combining the 
loss of the detection decoder and the loss of the semantic segmentation 
decoder, as shown in the following equation: 
Ltotal = Ldet + Lseg
(1) 
where Ltotal represents the total loss, Ldet is the loss for the object 
detection task, and Lseg is the loss for the segmentation task.
The classification branch and the bounding box branch in the 
detection task use different loss functions. For the classification task, the 
binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss is employed. For the bounding box 
prediction task, a combination of distribution focal loss (DFL) and 
complete IoU (CIoU) loss is adopted, which effectively addresses class 
imbalance issues and enhances the detection performance for small 
objects. The detection loss is defined as follows: 
Ldet = λBCELBCE + λDFLLDFL + λCIoULCIoU
(2) 
where LBCE represents the classification loss BCE loss,LDFL and LCIoU 
represent the bounding box regression loss DFL loss and CIoU loss. λBCE, 
λDFL, λCIoU are corresponding coefficients.
For the segmentation task, a combination of BCE loss and IoU loss is 
used. In orchard scenarios, apples may be occluded by branches, leaves, 
or other fruits, resulting in incomplete target regions. The BCE loss helps 
ensure accurate classification of foreground pixels, while the IoU loss 
optimizes the overlap between the predicted and ground truth regions. 
This enables the model to maintain consistency and completeness when 
predicting targets with occlusions or complex shapes. The segmentation 
loss function is defined as: 
Lseg = λBCELBCE + λIoULIoU
(3) 
where LIoU represents the IoU loss, and λIoU denotes the corresponding 
coefficient.
2.3. 3D fruit localization
3D localization refers to the process by which a robot extracts three- 
dimensional information of a target using vision sensors (Tang et al., 
2020). In automated apple harvesting, 3D localization is essential for the 
robot to obtain the spatial coordinates of the target fruit and estimate its 
contour, thereby guiding the robot’s end-effector to approach the target. 
However, due to the performance limitations of depth sensors, it is 
challenging to acquire complete point clouds of the target fruit in 
complex orchard environments with significant occlusions.
MT-WavYOLO can accurately segment the exposed parts of apples 
and estimate the two-dimensional size of the complete fruit. To address 
the challenges of 3D localization, we propose a frustum method that 
converts the 2D information obtained from the multi-task network into 
3D centroid coordinates and fits a 3D model of the apple. This approach 
facilitates precise harvesting by the robot, even in occluded and complex 
orchard conditions.
2.3.1. Fruit central line and frustum
Accurate fruit centroid determination is critical for robotic harvest­
ing. However, sensor limitations and segmentation inaccuracies often 
result in fragmented or distorted point clouds, meaning the point cloud 
subset corresponding to the 2D bounding box fails to represent the 
complete fruit geometry. Consequently, direct centroid calculation from 
such partial point clouds yields significant localization errors. To 
address this limitation, we propose an enhanced frustum-based locali­
zation method that integrates 2D bounding box information with depth 
data to accurately determine the 3D fruit centroid.
Initially, the 2D bounding box output by the MT-WavYOLO network 
identifies the approximate location and extent of the target fruit in the 
RGB image. Leveraging the quasi-spherical symmetry of apples, the 
center of this bounding box serves as an approximation of the fruit’s 
projected center. Crucially, the MT-WavYOLO detection box estimates 
the full extent even for occluded fruits. Thus, the bounding box center 
provides a reliable estimate of the fruit’s projected center (x, y

2.3.2. Centroid determination and radius estimation
Given the near-spherical morphology of apples, the fruit is approx­
imated as an ideal sphere during localisation. Under this assumption, the 
estimated radius closely matches the major-axis radius and is slightly 
larger than the minor-axis radius. In practical harvesting operations, the 
robotic gripper must open sufficiently to encompass the fruit’s 
maximum dimension; configuring the gripper according to the minor- 
axis radius could result in an incomplete grasp and a failed pick.
The centerline of the frustum is defined by the ray from the origin of 
the camera coordinate system to the center of the far plane of the 
frustum. After obtaining the centerline of the apple, the X-axis and Y- 
axis coordinates of the apple’s centroid in the camera coordinate system 
are already known. To fully acquire the 3D information of the target 
fruit, it is only necessary to determine the Z-axis coordinate of the 
centroid and the radius. The 3D centroid and radius of the fruit can be

3. Results and analysis
To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, this section 
conducted ablation and comparative experiments on the MT-WavYOLO 
model, analyzing the contribution of each component in the multi-task 
learning framework to the final performance and comparing it with 
other state-of-the-art models. Since harvesting robots typically rely on 
stereo vision systems, we employed six different RGB-D cameras—util­
izing either ToF or stereo vision technologies—to validate the frustum- 
based localization method in a real orchard environment. The experi­
mental results were compared with the bounding-box-based method and

3.4.3. Overall analysis of the results
To assess robustness under different distances and multi-sensor 
conditions and to quantify the uncertainty of mean estimates, we 
report 95% confidence intervals (CI) for all continuous measures. For 
the 216 fruits included in the localization experiments, MT-WavYOLO 
achieved a mean segmentation IoU of 93.1% (95% CI: 93.1% 
±0.0133). Location method comparisons were performed using two- 
sided paired t-tests with cameras as paired units (n=6); when the dif­
ferences did not satisfy normality or symmetry assumptions, the Wil­
coxon signed-rank test was applied as a non-parametric alternative (α =
0.05).
As shown in the figures, at both 0.8 m and 1.6 m, our method ach­
ieved significantly lower centroid and radius errors than the 3D 
bounding-box baseline (p<0.001); relative to the previous method, 
centroid errors were significantly reduced at both distances (p<0.001), 
while radius errors were significantly lower at 0.8 m (p≈0.026) and 
showed a decreasing trend at 1.6 m (p≈0.08). At 0.8 m, the pooled SD 
was approximately 9.15 mm with N=300, yielding a 95% CI half-width 
of 1.04 mm; at 1.6 m, the pooled SD was approximately 14.14 mm with 
N=300, yielding a half-width of 1.60 mm. These CI half-widths are 
much smaller than the observed between-method mean differences (7–8 
mm), indicating that N=300 per distance (600 in total) is sufficient to 
support statistically robust and practically meaningful distinctions 
(Figs. 14–18).
The improvements achieved by the proposed method, which in­
tegrates the multi-task network MT-WavYOLO and the frustum 
approach, can be attributed to the network’s ability to predict the full 
size of fruits through its detection boxes. This eliminates the need for 
clustering, simplifying the processing workflow while significantly 
enhancing the accuracy of 3D centroid and radius localization for target 
objects.
4. Discussion
4.1. Accuracy vs efficiency
The proposed apple 3D localization method in this study, utilizing 
the multi-task network MT-WavYOLO and frustum projection, demon­
strated significant performance advantages in experimental evaluations. 
Compared to the baseline method based on bounding boxes (bbx mtd.), 
the proposed method reduced the mean centroid localization error by 
49.5% and 35.5% at distances of 0.8 m and 1.6 m, respectively, and 
markedly decreased the mean radius estimation error by 81.9% and 
65.5%. Furthermore, compared to our previous method employing 
YOLOACT++ and DBSCAN clustering (previous mtd.), the new method 
also achieved improvements in centroid (error reduced by 11.7%– 
20.6%) and radius (error reduced by 9%–11%) localization accuracy, 
and exhibited higher stability (lower standard deviation). These results 
consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in 
enhancing localization accuracy and reliability. In addition, cascading 
the wavelet transform with small-kernel convolutions enables expo­
nential expansion of the receptive field without excessive parameteri­
zation, and can even reduce the parameter count, while capturing both 
global context and local details, thus enhancing Precision and Recall.
The performance gains of the proposed method primarily arise from 
its mechanistic innovations and robust architectural design. First, MT- 
WavYOLO’s multi-task framework couples a shared backbone with task- 
specific necks, enabling cross-task feature interaction that strengthens 
semantic constraints in occluded regions and improves global consis­
tency for fragmented targets. Second, the proposed C2f_WTConv con­
ducts joint frequency–spatial modeling, expanding the effective 
receptive field without a substantial increase in parameters and miti­
gating interpretation errors caused by mask fragmentation. Conse­
quently, the model can directly infer parameters related to the fruit’s 
overall size from the detection bounding box, thereby obviating explicit 
point-cloud clustering (e.g., DBSCAN) and its attendant error propaga­
tion and computational overhead. Furthermore, the proposed method 
demonstrates good robustness when processing low-quality point cloud

MT-WavYOLO model has a larger parameter count compared to the

allows it to handle both detection and segmentation tasks simulta­
neously without significant delays. This is particularly advantageous for 
edge computing platforms where real-time processing is crucial. The 
performance of MT-WavYOLO in this regard is competitive with single- 
task detection models and far exceeds that of single-task semantic seg­
mentation models such as U-Net (4.41 FPS), E-Net (8.30 FPS), and 
DeepLabv3+ (12.4 FPS). Although the parameter count is higher, the 
model’s efficient multi-task learning mechanism allows it to achieve a 
good balance between high performance and computational cost.
4.2. Remaining challenges
Despite these positive results, this study has limitations. Under se­
vere occlusion, the reduction in available point cloud data may lead to 
deviations in the estimated Z-axis coordinate of the fruit centroid, 
thereby affecting overall localization accuracy. This is particularly 
evident when the target fruit is heavily occluded by others, causing the 
segmentation mask within the 2D bounding box to include both the 
occluding and occluded fruits, which interferes with frustum-based 
point cloud processing. While the proposed method aligns with the 
practical harvesting sequence—prioritizing the removal of occluding 
fruits so that occluded ones become observable—future efforts to 
address this limitation could further enhance the precision and robust­
ness of robotic fruit localization. The practical deployment of deep 
learning models on embedded platforms faces several challenges, 
including limited memory capacity, resource contention in multi-task 
processing, high computational overhead from preprocessing tasks, 
power consumption and thermal constraints, as well as model compat­
ibility issues.
4.3. Future work
To address the centroid localization deviations caused by severe 
occlusion, future work will explore a multi-view fusion strategy. Spe­
cifically targeting fruits occluded by others, this approach leverages 
complementary information from different viewpoints to enable cross- 
frustum point cloud reconstruction and joint localization. It is ex­
pected to mitigate errors caused by incomplete point clouds from a 
single view and improve the accuracy of Z-axis estimation under oc­
clusion, thereby enhancing the system’s robustness and applicability in 
complex orchard environments. Additionally, we plan to apply light­
weight strategies such as model pruning and quantization in real-world 
applications to reduce model complexity while maintaining accuracy. 
This will facilitate the efficient deployment of MT-YOLO on low-power 
platforms such as the Jetson Nano, thereby enhancing the adaptability 
and robustness of multi-task vision systems in real-world agricultural 
production. Recent studies have proposed end-to-end frameworks that 
integrate multi-task learning with 3D vision to address more complex 
perception tasks. In contrast to our proposed method, some end-to-end 
frameworks unify 3D detection, semantic segmentation, and other 2D

However, this study adopts a near-spherical prior for radius esti­

future work will extend the multi-task pipeline to a hybrid 2D/3D

The dataset used in this study covers only two apple cultivars from 
three orchards, which, although to some extent reflecting differences in 
fruit characteristics and occlusion patterns across regions, is still insuf­
ficient to represent the full variety of planting conditions in broader 
areas and diverse cultivars. In future work, we plan to collect apple 
datasets from more orchards, encompassing different cultivars, tree ar­
chitectures, and cultivation modes, in order to further validate the 
generalization ability and robustness of the proposed method and to 
provide more comprehensive experimental support for subsequent 
model optimization and cross-regional deployment.
5. Conclusion
This study provides a solution to the critical challenge of recognizing 
and localizing occluded apples in robotic harvesting by proposing an 
integrated system. Our approach combines MT-WavYOLO, a novel 
multi-task deep learning network excelling at simultaneous detection 
and segmentation under occlusion, with a robust, sensor-agnostic 3D 
localization pipeline utilizing frustum projection. Experimental results 
show that the system achieves state-of-the-art performance in 2D visual 
perception, achieving 93.6% precision and 93.1% IoU, while main­
taining only 10.2 M parameters and running at a real-time speed of 27 
FPS, effectively balancing accuracy and efficiency. Crucially, the 3D 
localization component achieved millimeter-level accuracy (e.g., mean 
errors of 14.22 mm for centroid and 1.16 mm for radius) using various 
consumer-grade depth cameras, significantly outperforming both con­
ventional bounding box methods and our prior approach. The core 
contribution of this work is a unified framework that delivers accurate 
and robust perception in cluttered, real-world orchard environments, 
while maintaining superior performance on consumer-grade depth 
sensors, and effectively handling occlusion without reliance on complex 
point cloud processing. Validated through field tests, this research pro­
vides a practical, cost-effective, and feasible vision solution for agri­
cultural robotics and offers valuable insights for other challenging fruit- 
harvesting scenarios.

Appendix D. Joint calibration and synchronization
Rig constraints
All sensors were mounted on a rigid metal frame; all cameras were oriented parallel to the ground; geometry remained fixed during experiments. 
Five RGB-D cameras were vertically spaced by 5 cm; the Livox LiDAR and M4Pro were separated by 10 cm vertically with a fixed 10 cm horizontal 
offset; overall rig height was 50 cm.
A1 Acquisition and pairing
A large rectangular checkerboard served as the common target; paired images and point clouds were collected over multiple distances and poses. 
To handle different sampling rates, each pose was recorded separately and the first frame of each recording was selected to form image–point-cloud 
pairs; pairs were exported as matched image and point-cloud files for joint calibration.
A2 Camera intrinsics and image-plane correction
Paired data were imported into the joint-calibration tool; camera intrinsics were estimated with the checkerboard cell size specified; the check­
erboard region was designated for every image; clustering thresholds and size tolerances were adjusted to complete detection and sample selection.
A3 Camera–LiDAR joint calibration
Joint calibration across poses was performed by minimizing image reprojection error together with point-cloud geometric consistency, yielding the 
rigid transform from camera to LiDAR:
