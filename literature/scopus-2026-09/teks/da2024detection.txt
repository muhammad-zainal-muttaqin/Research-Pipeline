ABSTRACT 
 
Fruit detection is an essential task for automatic crop 
forecasting and mechanization. In this context, LiDAR data 
acquired by terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) systems can be 
explored to perform fruit counting and size estimation, since 
this data presents a high geometric quality, and it is not 
affected by lighting conditions. This paper investigates the 
application 
of 
intensity 
information 
and 
geometric 
descriptors/features for orange fruit detection. In addition, we 
explore statistical graphical analysis, density clustering and 
filtering techniques for individual fruit segmentation. The 
results demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed 
approach, achieving Fscore around 83% for orange fruit 
detection by combining intensity and planarity feature. 
 
Index Terms — Agriculture, 3D mapping, Point Cloud, 
Intensity, Geometric Features.  
 
1. INTRODUCTION 
 
The use of high spatial and spectral resolution remote sensors 
in precision agriculture aims to improve sustainable and 
profitable production models. These kinds of sensors are 
applied in diverse tasks, including fruit counting, maturity 
estimation, disease detection, and extraction of biometric 
variables [1]-[3]. 
For fruit detection, proximal sensors such as RGB 
cameras [4],[5], hyperspectral cameras [3], and terrestrial 
LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) systems [6]-[8] can be 
used. However, image-based detection is susceptible to 
challenges like lighting problems and occlusion caused by 
branches and leaves, especially in crops with high leaf 
density, such as citrus, where up to 80% of the fruit can be 
occluded by shadows [6].  
Different from images, LiDAR data enables fruit 
identification even in unfavorable lighting conditions. This 
sensor also provides three-dimensional information on the 
structure of objects, and radiometric information. This last 
one is obtained through the intensity of the received laser 
beam, which can be used to differentiate objects and the 
structures that make them up [9], [10]. 
In this paper, we evaluated the use of radiometric 
(LiDAR intensity) and geometric features, combined with 
density clustering and filtering techniques for the detection 
and segmentation of individual orange fruit. The proposed 
approach explores fruit geometric characteristics to 
overcome some limitations found in the literature, such as: i) 
the use of radiometry information obtained from images, 
which can be affected by lighting conditions; ii) fruit 
detection inside the tree canopy, not visible from outside; and 
iii) dense and structurally complex foliage. 
This work aims to contribute to accurate fruit detection, 
especially for harvest estimation purposes. In comparison 
with other studies, we evaluated different geometric features 
to distinguish leaves, fruit, and branches. 
 
2. STUDY AREA AND DATA 
 
The data used in the experiments were acquired using the 
FARO Focus Premium terrestrial laser scanner in an area 
dedicated to the commercial cultivation of oranges, located in 
Matão, São Paulo/Brazil. A total of 11 stations were 
strategically positioned to scan both sides of a row containing 
six orange trees in the harvest phase. In the experiments, one 
single tree was selected (highlighted in red in Figure 1). This 
scanner operates at wavelengths λ = 1553.5 nm and was 
configured to capture up to 400,000 points per second (1/5 of 
the maximum possible) with a scan quality of 4x for 
measuring the point data. These settings are recommended by 
the manufacturer for outdoor environments and varying 
weather conditions. The correct definition of acquisition 
parameters is essential for precise geometric scanning and 
minimizing noise in received laser beam intensity 
measurements.

3. METHODOLOGY 
 
In this paper, the proposed approach for orange fruit detection 
and segmentation relies on the combination of radiometric 
and geometric descriptors. Figure 2 illustrates the stages of 
the method adopted. The radiometric descriptor is based on 
the intensity of LiDAR data, whereas the geometric 
descriptors are derived from analytical relationships of the 
eigenvalues (λ1, λ2, λ3 ∈ R3x3). These eigenvalues are derived 
from the covariance matrix, determined for each point in the 
cloud, from the 3D coordinates of the points within a 
spherical neighborhood [11], [12].  
In total, five descriptors are estimated (Eq(s). 1 to 5): 
curvature (Cλ), linearity (Lλ), omnivariance (Oλ), planarity 
(Pλ), and scattering (Sλ). These descriptors can be used to 
characterize the elements of the tree (branches, leaves, and 
fruit).

To assess the separability between fruit (oranges) and 
non-fruit classes (branches and leaves) in the point cloud, a 
statistical graphical analysis is conducted using Gaussian 
curves with a normal distribution. This analysis aims to 
identify the features that allow an adequate separation of the 
fruit candidate points. For this purpose, 15 samples from each 
class were manually selected from the point cloud. The mean 
(µ) and standard deviation (σ) of both intensity and geometric 
features are calculated for these samples. For geometric 
descriptors, the evaluation is performed using three different 
radii to define the most suitable neighborhood (r = 2 cm, 
r = 3 cm, and r = 4 cm).  
In sequence, the fruit candidate points are grouped using 
the DBSCAN (Density Based Spatial Clustering) algorithm 
[13], which requires two parameters: the radius of the 
neighborhood and the minimum number of points. The 
DBSCAN allows to obtain individual clusters and remove 
noisy (regions with low point density). In the experiments, a 
radius of 4 cm (the average radius of the 25 fruits collected in 
the field) and a minimum number of 20 points were defined 
for clustering. 
In the last stage, a sphere is fitted to each fruit cluster to 
estimate the center position and radius (fruit size). This step 
also eliminates points belonging to leaves, branches, and the 
ground. The sphere fitting employs the general least squares 
method (LSM) to calculate the sphere’s parameters (center 
position and radius) from the observations, i.e., X, Y, Z 
coordinates of the points belonging to each cluster. Clusters 
with an estimated sphere radius outside the range of 2 cm to 
10 cm are eliminated at this stage, since they are considered 
unrelated to fruit.

Conducting a visual analysis, a neighborhood defined by 
r = 2 cm enables better separability of fruit compared to other 
radius values considered. This characteristic is related to 
geometric variation on the fruit surface, which become more 
evident as the size of the neighborhood increases. 
After defining the best radius for feature estimation, 
Gaussian curves were plotted to assess the degree of 
separability for all attributes, assuming three main classes: 
oranges, leaves, and tree branches, as shown in Figure 4. 
These graphs indicate that the attributes intensity, 
linearity, and planarity provide better separability between 
the classes considered, compared to curvature, scattering and 
ominivariance. These last features (Cλ, Sλ, Oλ) present 
Gaussian distributions with high overlapping. In addition, the 
graphics suggest that intensity is the feature that best 
differentiates fruit from leaves, with a small area of confusion 
between fruit and branches. This is due to the greater 
backscattering of the laser beam through the surface of 
oranges, resulting in lower recorded intensity compared to 
leaves.

5. RESULTS 
 
The proposed approach was evaluated using reference data 
generated by manually labeling the original point cloud. In 
total, 298 fruit were identified in one single orange tree, 
including those externally visible and located inside the tree 
(partially occluded). We use the object-based evaluation 
technique since the focus is to detect individual fruit.  
In the evaluation process, the following quality metrics 
were calculated [14]-[16]: completeness, correctness and 
Fscore. To estimate these metrics the values of true positives 
(TP), false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) were 
determined analyzing the 3D Euclidean distance between the 
central position of the reference fruit and the detected fruit. A 
threshold (tdist) of 10 cm was considered to establish the 
correspondence between reference and detection result. This 
distance (tdist) was chosen based on the average distance 
between neighboring fruit, as verified in the reference data.

6. DISCUSSION  
 
The quality metrics (Table 1) indicate the potential of the 
proposed approach. In addition, the results are comparable 
with previous studies [7] and [8], which used terrestrial 
LiDAR data to detect apple fruit and achieved Fscore around 
80%.  
Notably, this work emphasizes certain characteristics, 
such as tree with dense foliage and the detection of fruit not 
externally visible, presenting a high degree of challenge in 
the context of orange cultivation.  
Specific details of the obtained results are illustrated in 
Figures 5c and 5d. In the first case (Figure 5c, example S1), 
a group of fruit closely positioned on the same branch was 
not correctly detected. Even though the adopted features 
allowed the correct identification of these fruits, they were 
removed in the radius-based filtering. It occurred because 
they were grouped into a single cluster, resulting in a radius 
larger than the predefined interval for filtering. In the second 
situation, it is possible to observe that even in the case of 
isolated fruits, some noisy points are grouped with orange 
points, impacting the estimation of fruit size (Figure 5d, 
example S2).

7. CONCLUSION 
 
The purpose of this paper was to investigate the use of 
radiometric and geometric features, density clustering, and 
filtering techniques for detecting and segmenting orange fruit 
from terrestrial LiDAR data. The quality metrics indicated 
that although the proposed approach does not identify all the 
oranges, it achieves a high correctness rate (around 93%). As 
a suggestion for future work, we recommend exploring 
improved clustering and filtering approaches to address 
connected fruit clusters and eliminate noisy points that may 
affect the fruit size estimation.
