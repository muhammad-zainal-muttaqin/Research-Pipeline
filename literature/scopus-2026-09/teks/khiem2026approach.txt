Abstract. The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into agriculture
offers substantial advantages, including increased productivity and im-
proved product quality. Tomatoes, as a widely cultivated and consumed
crop, require accurate counting and ripeness classification to streamline
harvesting, estimate yields, and optimize cultivation strategies during
the harvest phase. This research adopts the YOLOv11 algorithm to clas-
sify tomatoes into three ripeness categories: fully ripe, partially ripe,
and green. The dataset comprises 11,267 annotated tomato instances
extracted from 904 images, which were randomly divided into training
(70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets for model training and eval-
uation. The YOLOv11 model achieved mAP50 scores of 0.888, 0.860, and
0.931 for fully ripe, partially ripe, and green categories, respectively. To
enhance detection accuracy, especially for partially occluded or distant
tomatoes, the Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI) algorithm was in-
corporated with YOLOv11. This hybrid approach significantly improved
classification and counting performance, achieving an overall accuracy of
95% on the test set, compared to 84% when YOLOv11 was used inde-
pendently.
Keywords: Tomato ripeness classification · tomato counting · YOLOv11
· SAHI integration.

Introduction
Leveraging technological advancements in agriculture is regarded as a pivotal
strategy for increasing productivity, enhancing the quality of agricultural prod-
ucts, and addressing food security challenges to support sustainable agricultural
development. Advanced techniques can be implemented across various stages of
crop growth, from the beginning of the planting season to the harvesting phase.
During the harvest stage, the identification of ripe fruits, their classification, and
the accurate counting of yields are crucial. These processes aid in evaluating crop
productivity, determining the optimal harvest time, and planning for harvesting,
transportation, and distribution.

Tomatoes, in particular, are among the most widely consumed vegetables
globally due to their high nutritional value. They are a staple in most human
diets, with an annual consumption exceeding 200 million tons [1]. Tomatoes
are an excellent source of vitamins A and C, which support digestive health,
improve vision, and enhance skin health. Additionally, the high lycopene con-
tent in tomatoes has been linked to a reduced risk of cancer and cardiovascular
diseases, making them an essential component of a healthy diet.
However, manual harvesting can negatively impact the quality of the fruit
and lacks precision in determining the optimal harvest time. Assessing tomato
ripeness by visual inspection is prone to errors, particularly for fruits that have
not yet reached the required maturity for harvesting. Additionally, estimating
tomato yields by eye can lead to inaccuracies, resulting in unreliable productivity
assessments.
Modern AI algorithms have proven highly effective in analyzing crop images
for various purposes, such as detecting pests and diseases, assessing fruit color,
and evaluating plant health [2], contributing significantly to the agricultural rev-
olution. Among these, the YOLO (You Only Look Once) algorithm stands out as
a prominent model for object detection, owing to its unique architecture. YOLO
employs a single neural network to identify objects, enabling rapid and efficient
detection in a single pass. This efficiency makes YOLO particularly suitable for
agricultural object recognition tasks [3]. Specifically, it has been effectively uti-
lized for diagnosing plant diseases, allowing farmers to identify issues at early
stages, diagnose leaf diseases, and implement timely crop management strategies
[4]. Detection inference on large images is still a major issue in practical usage.
SAHI (Slicing Aided Hyper Inference) has provided very active support in object
detection, especially for small objects. Detecting small objects is a challenging
task in computer vision, yet significant for many use cases. SAHI is a common
method of improving the detection accuracy of small objects, which involves
running inference over portions of an image and then accumulating the results
[5]. The integration of SAHI into deep learning algorithms enhances both the ac-
curacy and processing speed of these models. Specifically, in agriculture, SAHI
facilitates the early detection of issues such as plant diseases, pests, or water
deficiency by analyzing detailed images of small areas (slices) within a field. By
applying SAHI, the diagnostic and forecasting processes are optimized, enabling
farmers to make timely decisions regarding crop care, minimize losses, and im-
prove productivity. Additionally, SAHI reduces computational loads, offering a
cost-effective solution for the agricultural sector [6].
In this study, SAHI was integrated with YOLOv11 to enhance the detection
of tomatoes by improving image detail, including shape and color, particularly
for small, distant, or partially occluded instances across various ripeness stages.
The dataset comprised 904 images with 11,267 annotated tomato instances, cat-
egorized into three ripeness levels: green, partially ripe, and fully ripe. This
integration significantly enhanced the accuracy in tomato detection, counting,
and ripeness classification. The process of combining YOLOv11 and SAHI is
described in detail, and experiments were conducted on both the test dataset

Related works
Recently, many researchers have applied advanced computer vision technolo-
gies to the agricultural sector to increase crop productivity and economic value,
thereby addressing issues related to food security [7]. In particular, YOLO is a
CNN-based model used for object detection, recognition, and classification in
images, which has been employed in identifying and classifying plant diseases
[8]. The YOLO family [9] consists of a series of object detection models that
have significantly advanced the field of computer vision due to their remarkable
speed and accuracy. The most recent version, YOLOv11, continues to build upon
the foundation laid by the original YOLO model in 2016. These models are de-
signed to detect multiple objects within a single image in a single pass through
the network, making them highly efficient. With each new iteration, including
YOLOv3, YOLOv4, YOLOv5, and YOLOv8, there have been substantial im-
provements in detection accuracy, scalability, and the ability to recognize smaller
objects, further enhancing their performance across various applications.
In the agricultural sector, YOLO has demonstrated significant effectiveness in
detecting plant diseases. One study [10] applied YOLOv8 to diagnose diseases in
maize leaves, achieving an impressive accuracy rate of 99.8%. Another investiga-
tion [11] utilized YOLOv5 to identify diseases in bell pepper plants, successfully
detecting even minor spots of infection. Additionally, YOLOv5, when integrated
with self-attention and convolutional block attention modules, was employed
to predict diseases and insect infestations in tea tree leaves [12]. Furthermore,
YOLOv8 has shown exceptional speed and accuracy in detecting diseases on
sweet orange leaves [13].
SAHI is an advanced technique in deep learning that has been developed
to address challenges related to detecting small objects and complex details [5].
The integration of SAHI with YOLOv8 has been shown to enhance the model’s
accuracy, such as in the task of counting Oil Palm Trees [14]. SAHI has also
proven effective in identifying fundus lesions in diabetic patients when combined
with YOLO [15]. To improve multi-face detection accuracy, SAHI significantly
enhances performance, increasing accuracy from 81.97% to 86.65%, demonstrat-
ing the effectiveness of pre-processing with slicing techniques [16]. In agriculture,
combining YOLO with SAHI not only improves accuracy in detecting small ob-
jects but also optimizes the processing of images with high complexity [17][18].
However, there is limited research on the integration of YOLO and SAHI for
detecting ripe tomatoes and counting fruits in tomato images.

YOLOv11 YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a real-time object detection algo-
rithm known for its speed and accuracy in object identification [8]. YOLOv11,
the most recent version, offers powerful object recognition capabilities with high
accuracy and performance. One of the key advantages of YOLOv11 is its ability
to balance speed and precision, enabling fast processing while maintaining high
efficiency [20]. YOLOv11 supports object detection across various scales due to
its multi-scale prediction mechanism. Additionally, the application of anchor-
free or hybrid anchor techniques simplifies the prediction process and reduces
computational requirements.
In this study, the number of epochs during the training process was set to
20, meaning the model would iterate over the entire dataset 20 times, ensuring
sufficient opportunities for the model to improve accuracy during the learning
process. The batch size was set to 16, indicating that in each training step,
the model processes 16 images simultaneously. This configuration accelerates
the learning process while maintaining stability in weight updates. Additionally,
the number of workers was set to 8, enabling parallel data processing across 8
threads, which further accelerates the data loading process into the model.
The optimization algorithm selected was Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD),
with an initial learning rate of 0.01. This choice ensures the model can learn effec-
tively while minimizing the risk of non-convergence. The momentum parameter

Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI) To enhance accuracy in image
recognition, SAHI is an algorithm designed to improve both the accuracy and
inference speed by dividing high-resolution or large-sized images into smaller
segments and processing each part individually. This approach not only optimizes
computational resources but also enhances the overall performance of object
detection systems [5]. In this study, the SAHI technique was applied as follows:
The size of each slice was set to 300x300 pixels, with a 30% overlap between
the slices in both height and width, ensuring that no objects were missed at
the boundaries between slices. After the YOLOv11 model predicted the objects,
the results were outputted with bounding boxes surrounding the objects, each
marked with a color corresponding to its label (e.g., fully ripened objects were
marked with a red box, partially ripened with a yellow box, and green with a
blue box). Additionally, the code displayed the number of detected objects for
each type and visualized the results by overlaying them onto the original image.

Discussion and Conclusion
The use of the YOLOv11 algorithm for recognizing and classifying tomatoes into
three ripeness stages—fully ripe, partialy ripe, and green—has resulted in high
accuracy. In this study, the SAHI technique was applied to enhance the detec-
tion of small or partially occluded objects. This technique splits large images
into smaller parts, allowing the YOLO algorithm to process each slice individu-
ally, thereby improving the detection of details. The combination of YOLOv11
and SAHI significantly improved overall accuracy, achieving 95%, compared to
84% for detecting tomatoes in the test set using YOLO alone. Specifically, the
detection accuracy for fully ripe tomatoes increased from 84% to 95%, partially
ripe tomatoes from 78% to 98%, and green tomatoes from 89% to 92%.
The integration of YOLO and SAHI achieves superior accuracy compared to
both dataset-based and real-world data. This was demonstrated and validated
through experimental evaluations on the test set (Figure 6) and real-world images
(Figure 9). The model excels in accuracy and recall when classifying the ripeness
stages of tomatoes, particularly in detecting green tomatoes. Detecting green
tomatoes poses a greater challenge due to their color similarity to the leaves

factor for accurately estimating the crop’s yield potential.
Additionally, the SAHI technique contributes significantly to improving ob-
ject detection capabilities. By slicing the original image into smaller parts be-
fore applying the recognition algorithm, SAHI mitigates challenges caused by
occlusions or degraded image quality. It also allows YOLO to focus on detect-
ing objects within each slice without interference from large objects or complex
backgrounds. The findings from this study demonstrate the exceptional perfor-
mance of combining YOLOv11 and SAHI, especially for applications requiring
high accuracy, such as classifying tomato ripeness and assessing the quality of
agricultural products in general.
Counting tomatoes and classifying their ripeness are crucial steps in crop
management and play a key role in accurately predicting crop yield. By deter-
mining the exact number of tomatoes, yield predictions become more reliable,
enabling farmers and managers to gain a comprehensive view of the potential of
the harvest. Furthermore, classifying the ripeness of tomatoes provides additional
benefits, as farmers can estimate the optimal harvest time, optimizing both the
quality and market value of the product. Yield estimation based on counting and
classifying tomatoes also contributes to improving agricultural production pro-
cesses. Accurate information about expected yield helps in adjusting resources
such as fertilizers, labor, and irrigation, thus increasing production efficiency and
environmental sustainability.
With the ability to detect small objects, the combination of YOLO and SAHI
represents a pioneering solution for automatic tomato recognition systems. This
integration promises to further enhance object detection capabilities in complex
environments, improving both accuracy and processing efficiency, even with high-
resolution image data. As artificial intelligence and computer vision continue
to develop, integrating YOLO and SAHI could be expanded to simultaneously
perform multiple tasks, such as counting, ripeness classification, and detecting
abnormalities or pests on tomatoes. In the future, we will continue to improve
and address challenges related to tomato size determination and predicting the
optimal harvest time. This will not only enhance the effectiveness of smart agri-
cultural management but also contribute to building sustainable models that
optimize resources and minimize waste in production.
