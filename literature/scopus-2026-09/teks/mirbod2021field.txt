ABSTRACT. Monitoring of fruit size development has important implications for apple orchard management decision making 
such as scheduling for fruitlet chemical thinning and allocating resources for harvest. The current method for tracking fruit 
size development is by tagging a sample of fruits and using calipers or sizing rings to make measurements, which can be 
labor-intensive and time-consuming. In this study, a stereo vision system was developed which sized fruits on a tree and kept 
track of their growth during the season by matching fruit in images across time. Neural network models including Faster R-
CNN and Mask R-CNN were used for fruit detection and on-tree fruit sizing. Camera pose estimation using feature matching 
of apples was used for tracking individual fruit growth. The best performance on fruit matching for the ‘Golden Delicious’ 
variety during the growing season in an apple orchard was observed in September and October; 74% of all detected fruits 
which were fully visible were matched between the two months. Fruitlets averaging 25 mm in diameter also had a matching 
accuracy of 73% during two imaging trials performed on the same day for the month of June.  
Keywords. Camera Pose Estimation, Feature Matching, Apple Fruit Thinning, Precision Agriculture, Stereo Vision 
1. Introduction 
On-tree measurement of apple fruit size has two benefits for orchard management decision making. First, near harvest 
time, fruit size can be an indicator of fruit quality. Larger and richer colored fruit are preferred by consumers, making it 
more profitable for growers. Also, larger fruit can reduce labor cost due to less time spent by workers picking and storing 
fruit, therefore growers can better plan for harvest resources and materials. A second benefit for growers on knowledge of 
fruit size is during chemical thinning. Early in the season, small fruitlets are chemically thinned to increase harvest fruit size 
and return bloom for the following year. The primary indicator on efficacy of chemical thinners is the measurement of fruit 
growth (Green, D.W., Krupa, J.; 1999). Ward & Marini (1999) on a study with ‘Golden Delicious’ and ‘Delicious’ apples 
determined the first indication of fruit abscission is the stoppage of growth. They concluded that non-destructive daily 
measurements of fruitlet size would be the only practical method of evaluating thinning treatments to determine whether 
additional applications would be needed.

assessment. For chemical thinning, fruit size measurements can be a labor-intensive task. Greene et al. (2013) introduced

growth progression 3 days and 8 days after thinning application to determine abscission versus retainment of fruit. An 
automated system which could keep track of individual fruit growth would help to reduce labor cost, increase the number 
of fruit sampled to help better extrapolate fruit size for a larger orchard, and also help reduce the amount of chemicals used 
for thinning by more accurately assessing fruit density to determine chemical thinner timing and rates. Such an autonomous 
system would need to achieve high accuracy in fruit sizing and be able to keep track of fruitlet locations so they can be 
measured again at a future date. 
A common remote sensing device used for automating fruit detection and fruit sizing is an RGB camera combined with 
a depth sensor or in a stereo pair configuration. While fruit grading systems used in packing houses have claimed fruit size 
accuracy of 0.1 mm (Ellips B.V., Noord-Brabant, NL; GP Graders LLC, Victoria, AU), on-tree fruit sizing has been more 
difficult to achieve high accuracy. Wang et al. (2017) achieved an accuracy of 5 mm on mango fruit claiming depth sensor 
resolution as the main culprit for higher accuracy. Underwood et al. (2018) also achieved 5 mm accuracy on mango sizing. 
Applications in fruit registration have included mapping of orchard rows using camera and lidar (Peng et al. 2016; Roy, 
2015), GPS with machine vision system (Wang et al., 2012), and more sophisticated computer vision algorithms for merging 
3D reconstructed point clouds of trees (Roy et al., 2018). Remote sensing analysis on outdoor deciduous tree fruits have 
focused more on tree level measurements, such as fruit count per tree or fruit size per tree, rather than fruit level 
measurements. With the emergence of precision agriculture aiming to increase productivity and yield while decreasing the 
amount of input into the field, fruit level measurement of size with the ability to keep track of individual fruit growth can be 
applicable in areas of precision spraying and robotic harvesting. 
In this study, a stereo vision system with high resolution cameras was deployed in an orchard to measure fruit size for a 
row of apple trees. The objectives were to (1) register the 3D location of each fruit to a tree, (2) match registered fruit from 
one month to the next during the growing season to monitor fruit growth, and (3) evaluate the results by creating a growth 
curve of each individual fruit that was registered. Neural network models were used to segment fruit from images, stereo 
vision was used for computing the metric diameter of detected fruits, and techniques in computer vision allowed for matching 
of fruit across time. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1 Data Acquisition 
Imaging data were collected at the Russell E. Larson Agricultural Research Center in Pennsylvania Furnace, PA, USA. 
The machine vision system used was a stereo setup consisting of two FLIR Blackfly S cameras (model BFS-U3-88S6C-C) 
and an LED-based flash system for obtaining more uniform lighting in images. The equipment was mounted on a modified 
four-wheeled wagon and pulled through a row of the orchard at approximately 0.5 m/s with a 3 Hz image acquisition frame 
rate. This resulted in image overlaps of 70-80% with the lesser amount of overlap in June and July because the camera 
needed to be closer to the trees to view the smaller fruitlets. Table 1 provides detailed information about each data collection 
trial. For each trial, data from one row consisting of 50 trees were imaged with 20 trees used for training neural network 
models for fruit detection and sizing, and the remaining 30 trees for testing. Only one side of the row was used for registering 
fruit location during the five months of imaging. Ground truth diameter data were collected from a sample of fruit per tree 
after each trial using an electronic band-loop fruit sizing device (UP GmbH Inc., Ibbenbüren, Germany). Harvest data on 
fruit count and fruit diameter per tree were also collected at the end of the season.

2.2 Fruit Detection and Sizing 
The Faster R-CNN model (Ren et al., 2016) was used to detect fruit in images and Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017) to 
segment the boundaries of the fruit. Once fruits were segmented from the image, stereo vision was used to find the depth 
from the camera to each fruit to compute the diameter in metric units. Since the images were high resolution (4096 x 2160 
pixels) they had to be scaled down to fit into GPU memory for training and inference. However, for fruit sizing it was more 
desirable to segment detected fruits on the original image to reduce any errors from downscaling and then upscaling the

image again. Therefore, once Faster R-CNN detected all the fruits in the scaled down image, a square patch of each fruit 
would be cropped from the full resolution image and fed into Mask R-CNN for segmentation. Only fruit that were fully 
visible or with minor occlusions, such that the boundary could be extrapolated, were used for sizing. 
2.3 Fruit Registration and Tracking Pipeline 
To track fruit growth rate, it is important to register each fruit in the image frame to a tree. Jarvinen et al. (2019) had used 
Faster R-CNN for tree trunk detection with the boundary space of a tree being the midpoint from one tree trunk to the 
adjacent tree trunk in an image. Ideally, in an orchard block, all trees would be planted using the same training system and 
tree spacings would be equidistant. In this study, both tall spindle and bi-axis training systems were planted in the same row 
for a ‘Golden Delicious’ apple variety and tree trunks were not always visible due to dense canopies. Also, the camera would 
sometimes be too close to a bi-axis tree, making the two branch leaders appear as two separate trees in an image. These 
factors made reliance on trunk detection for separating trees in images more difficult. Since it was assumed tree spacing 
would normally be the same in a block and known beforehand, the distance between each tree was measured prior to the 
experiments. Stereo visual odometry was then used to traverse the distance between the 30 trees, extracting an image frame 
for each tree at the measured location. The September dataset had the least amount of camera drift when traversing from 
tree #1 to tree #30 (0.43 m error from a total distance of 33.5 m), therefore all processed data and detected fruits were 
referenced to the September dataset. 
Figure 1 outlines the process for matching each frame from the October dataset to the September dataset. Given similar 
starting and end points for image acquisition in two datasets, SIFT descriptors (Low, 2004) were extracted from each image’s 
detected fruits. The OpenCV library’s Brute Force Matcher (OpenCV, 2015) method was then used to find matching fruits 
in both images. If the median distance between detected fruits were less than a threshold value, the two images would be 
considered a match and the next image in the September dataset would be queried, otherwise the next image from the 
October dataset would be used and the matching process would repeat again. The threshold value was based on how far 
away the camera was from the trees. If the camera was closer then displacement of the scene would be greater in each 
consecutive image as the camera moved; therefore, the threshold needed to be lower to better align the frames. A lower 
threshold was used in the June dataset and a higher one for the other four months. 
 
Figure 1: The process for matching each frame in the October dataset to September dataset. Image on the left is a depiction of matching fruit  
using their SIFT descriptors and finding the distance of each fruit between frames. Once the distance falls below a threshold, the two frames are 
considered a match (indicated by checkmark). On the right is a pseudo code of the frame matching process given two image datasets.    
Once all frames between two image datasets were matched, the 30 frame pairs representing 30 trees in both months were 
then processed for fruit matching and sizing. OpenCV’s SolvePNP method with Ransac (OpenCV, 2015) was used to find 
the camera pose between the set of SIFT descriptors. Detected fruit locations from the October image were then projected 
onto the September image using the camera pose. If a projected point fell inside the boundary of fruit, the two fruits were 
considered a match. When not enough points would be matched to find a camera pose, the results from the Brute Force 
Matcher of SIFT descriptors would become the only matches for the pair of images. 
3. Results and Discussion 
Table 2 shows the performance of the fruit matching method on 30 trees for consecutive months. Fruits that were not matched 
were either due to imprecise projection of apple locations from one image to the next, or fruits being detected in one month 
but not in the other. Imprecise projections were often due to a lack of matching SIFT feature points across two frames. 
Numerous reasons contributed to the lack of fruit visibility and hence fruit mismatching. For example, in trial #1 for June,

Even though the fruits were much smaller in the June datasets, the percentage of matched fruit were still high relative to 
the other months. This is because the scenes in the two datasets were very similar and the detected fruits were nearly identical 
in texture which gave accurate matches between the SIFT descriptors. This result shows potential benefits in applications of 
late-stage chemical fruit thinning where a camera system can sample and size fruitlets accurately and rapidly to determine 
whether a final chemical application is needed. The fruit matching method was not tested for fruitlets averaging under 25 
mm in diameter. Also, the two June datasets were collected on the same day, even though for determining chemical thinning 
applications fruit size is recommended to be measured three days and eight days after a spray (Robinson et al., 2013). It was 
assumed that the texture of fruitlets and the canopy fill would not change significantly three days after a spray application 
and with  minimal changes after eight days. No attempt was made to match fruit from June 13 to July 17 due the complete 
change in fruitlet texture and sometimes displacement of the fruits from their original location as they increased in weight 
and hung lower from branches. 
The lowest accuracy in fruit matching was found during the July to August transition. One reason was due to the 
discrepancy in fruit texture as the fruits were still maturing in shape and size during the growing season. The second reason 
was that pruning of the row had taken place, therefore the scene looked very different for the two datasets. The camera pose 
could not be estimated for four of the trees in July due to severe occlusion of fruit or change in scenery, an example of such 
a scenario is shown in Figure 2A. The best performance on fruit matching occurred in the September to October transition 
(Figure 2B). In both these months, the camera was placed further back compared to June and July, therefore allowing a wide 
angle of the scene to be visible and more fruit to be detected in a single image. Also, fruits in those two months were similar 
in both texture and size, although there was some additional coloring of fruits in October. A time-lapse of change in texture 
and size of a single fruit is shown in Figure 2C.

4. Conclusion 
A method for in-field tracking of fruit diameters using a stereo vision system was developed and tested. For each month, 
SIFT feature descriptors of detected fruit were extracted and matched across the previous month, therefore each image 
captured of trees from one month had a corresponding image in the previous month. This method was able to correctly match 
74% of all detected fruits during the mature fruit growth stage in September and October as fruit texture and size were more 
similar. The method also appeared to work well in a June dataset (73% matching accuracy) when imaging trials were done 
in close time proximity, allowing the scenery in images across the different trials to be very similar. The shortcomings of 
this method were that sometimes not enough fruit would be detected which prevented a good camera pose between two trials 
from being estimated. This in turn caused further mismatch of the remaining fruits in the pair of images. Also, the method 
relies on a matching sequence of image frames based on visible fruit. If no fruit was visible or if trees were missing in a row, 
then images from one month could not be matched with the next month. Future work will include finding more robust 
features to match image frames for better fruit alignment or using 3D scene information for point cloud matching. Detecting 
fruits on a tree from multiple camera perspectives will also help to increase matching accuracy, however there are limitations 
to improving fruit detection and matching accuracy due to canopy structure. Narrower canopy architecture designs can 
improve the outcome of automation methods by allowing greater and more uniform visibility of fruit.
