A B S T R A C T
The growth of the global population has increased the demand for fruits and vegetables, while high harvesting
labor costs severely constrain industry development. Currently, relevant personnel primarily utilize 2D object
detection technology to facilitate automated harvesting, aiming to reduce labor costs. However, 2D detection
technology is limited to providing planar information and cannot meet the requirements of scenarios that need 3D
spatial data, whereas 3D object detection technology can effectively address these needs, including point cloud-
based methods and monocular-based methods. Since point cloud-based object detection methods require
expensive equipment, they are not suitable for low-cost agricultural harvesting scenarios. In contrast, monocular
3D object detection methods have the advantage of only requiring a camera and being easy to deploy. However,
there is a lack of specialized monocular 3D object detection datasets and algorithms suited for natural scenes in
the agricultural ﬁeld, which limits the application and development of this technology in agricultural automation.
To address this, we construct a 3D object detection dataset for wax gourds and propose a network called TPDNet,
which aims to capture the 3D information of objects from a single RGB image for fruits and vegetables in ﬁelds.
Speciﬁcally, we construct a depth estimation and enhance module that introduces depth information into the
model with the help of depth auxiliary labels, and improves the representation of depth information by utilizing
weight information across spatial and channel dimensions. Meanwhile, since depth features and image features
are heterogeneous, we design the phenotype aggregation and phenotype intensify module to capture the corre-
spondence between image and depth features, promoting the effective fusion of image and depth information. The
experimental results show that our method signiﬁcantly outperforms others in terms of mAP3D and mAPBEV
metrics, demonstrating the effectiveness and validity of our proposed method. We open our code and dataset at:
http://tpdnet.samlab.cn.
1. Introduction
Fruits and vegetables are essential for human sustenance, and the
growing global population increases their demand. Labor costs for har-
vesting currently account for 30–50 % of total production expenses,
which severely hinders the development of the fruit and vegetable in-
dustry [1]. To address this, researchers focus on efﬁcient automated
harvesting technologies to reduce costs, with effective object detection
playing a critical role [2]. Object detection technology can accurately
identify and locate object crops, enabling automated harvesting ma-
chines to precisely ﬁnd the correct crops for picking [3]. In detail, object
detection methods are classiﬁed into 2D and 3D, with 2D identifying
object positions on a plane [4,5] and 3D capturing spatial coordinates
and object size, as shown in Fig. 1. These technologies are a key research
area in the development of agricultural automation, gradually receiving
more and more attention [6].
Speciﬁcally, Li et al. [7] design a lightweight algorithm for passion
fruit pest detection by optimizing YOLOv5, incorporating a point-line
distance loss function, attention modules, and mixup data augmenta-
tion. Jia et al. [8] enhance apple detection and segmentation in orchards
by improving the Mask R-CNN model through a fusion of ResNet and
DenseNet. Despite these successes, 2D detection techniques remain
limited to planar information and are inadequate for scenarios requiring
3D spatial data, such as automated harvesting and morphology

To achieve a monocular 3D object detection in agricultural ﬁeld, we
need to address the three challenges compared with computer science.
(1) Traditional agricultural object detection methods mainly rely on 2D
frame labeling datasets, and the lack of monocular 3D object detection
datasets restricts the application of monocular 3D object detection
techniques in agriculture. (2) In natural environments, the minimal
morphological differences (such as color) between crop objects and
background elements (e.g., leaves, branches, etc.) limit the effectiveness
of depth information obtained through pixel-based depth feature esti-
mation. (3) Image features and depth features belong to two kinds of
heterogeneous features, and how to capture the complex relationship
between them. To address the these challenges, we try to construct a 3D
object detection dataset speciﬁcally for agriculture and develop a new
monocular 3D object detection algorithm. Our goal is to enhance the
representation of deep features through phenotypic deepening and to
investigate the potential relationships between vision and depth
phenotype.
In detail, we construct a 3D object detection dataset for wax gourd,
and annotate 3D bounding boxes for the objects, which can be used to
train deep learning models. In addition, we propose triple phenotype
deepen networks for monocular 3d object detection of melons and fruits
in ﬁelds, including four core components. First, we design a depth esti-
mation module, assisted by discrete depth labels, to mitigate the model's
limited understanding of depth from a single RGB image. Second, we
introduce a depth enhance module that reﬁnes depth feature represen-
tation by capturing weight information across spatial and channel di-
mensions. Third, the phenotype aggregation module leverages two cross-
attention mechanisms to capture the correspondence between image and
depth features, facilitating the fusion of image and depth information.
Finally, we propose a phenotype intensify module to generate compre-
hensive and high-quality feature representations through the utilization
of a self-attention mechanism. Extensive experiments have validated the
effectiveness of the proposed method, and our model achieve an AP3D
score of 63.13 % and an APBEV score of 63.61 % in the 3D object detection
task, representing improvements of 8.5 % and 4.87 %, respectively,
compared to other state-of-the-art methods.

 We conduct quantitative and qualitative experiments on proposed

effectiveness of TPDNet.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Dataset
In monocular 3D object detection, we typically use a single RGB
image as input to train the model and infer the object's 3D attributes, such
as position, size, and orientation. However, 3D datasets speciﬁc to crops
remain scarce, limiting the development of 3D object detection models
for real-world applications. To address this, we construct a dedicated 3D
dataset for wax gourd, which includes image data, point cloud infor-
mation, labels, and equipment parameters. The dataset comprises 943
scenes, with 5313 corresponding 3D and 2D labeled boxes.
2.1.1. Device and parameters
To simultaneously capture image and point cloud data in the same
scene, we need a device that integrates both a camera and a LiDAR
sensor, and can be suitable for use in outdoor environments. Therefore,
we chose the Neuvition Titan M1-A high-resolution solid-state LiDAR, as
it fulﬁlls the aforementioned requirements. The Titan M1-A supports up
to 700 laser scan lines, with 1750 pixels per line and a measurement
accuracy of 5 mm. It also features a built-in 2-megapixel camera sup-
porting up to 1080p resolution, allowing simultaneous capture of RGB
images and point cloud data. The accompanying software provided by
the manufacturer simpliﬁes device operation, including LiDAR control,
data processing, and point cloud display. Link to the manufacturer
(https://www.neuvition.com/zh-CN/products/titan-m1-a.html).
2.1.2. Dataset construction
As shown in Fig. 2, we build the wax gourd 3D object detection
dataset through three steps: Device Calibration, Data Collection, and
Bounding Box Labeling. The detailed process is as follows.
Device Calibration: The Neuvition Titan M1-A, a combined LiDAR-
camera system, simultaneously captures point cloud and image data,
integrating three coordinate systems: LiDAR, camera, and pixel. To
transform between these systems, we calibrate two matrices: the extrinsic
matrix (rotation matrix R and translation vector T) for mapping from the
LiDAR to camera coordinate systems, and the intrinsic matrix for map-
ping from the camera to pixel coordinates. As shown in Fig. 2(Device
Calibration), following method [30], we ﬁrst calibrate the camera's
intrinsic matrix. Then, we use Canny edge detection and an improved
Douglas-Peucker algorithm to extract image feature points, and Open3D
to select and locate the corresponding 3D point cloud features, providing
a robust foundation for high-precision data processing. Through the
above steps, we ultimately obtain the camera's intrinsic and extrinsic
parameter.
Data Collection: When selecting the crop type, we consider that
monocular 3D object detection technology is less sensitive to small ob-
jects. Therefore, we choose wax gourd as the object for data collection.
Data collection occurs in Guiyang, Guizhou Province, China (106.97 N,
26.92 E) between September and October 2023. Using the Neuvition
Titan M1-A, we gather data across different weather conditions and times
of day to ensure diversity and robustness. When conducting data
collection outdoors, due to the inﬂuence of varying weather conditions,
our dataset contains overexposed images. However, due to the absence of
relevant sensor devices during the data collection process, we are unable
to obtain environmental metadata. This diverse dataset improves the
model's adaptability and robustness in varying lighting conditions.
Bounding Box Labeling: Monocular 3D object detection relies on
supervised learning, making high-quality annotations essential. We
construct our dataset following the KITTI format, commonly used in
autonomous driving research. The label information includes the object

speciﬁcally for 3D object detection of wax gourd. This dataset consists of
943 data groups, with each group comprising point cloud ﬁles, RGB
images, detailed annotation information, and corresponding device pa-
rameters. The annotation information includes 3D bounding boxes for
spatial localization and 2D bounding boxes for image plane localization,
with a total of 5313 boxes, respectively. In the dataset split, we use 80 %
of the data as the training set and 20 % as the test set.
2.2. Method
The overall framework of the proposed TPDNet for monocular 3D
object detection is illustrated in Fig. 3. The framework primarily com-
prises ﬁve key modules: backbone, depth estimation module, depth
enhance module, phenotype aggregation module, and phenotype Inten-
sify module. The backbone, DAL-102 [33], pre-trained on the 3D Object
Detection Dataset (KITTI) [26], extracts multi-scale visual and pheno-
typic features from input images, which serves as the initial step toward
3D scene understanding. Following this, the depth estimation module
obtains depth features from the image data, addressing the inherent
limitation of monocular images, which lack direct depth information.
The depth enhance module further reﬁnes these depth features,
enhancing both spatial and channel-wise information to better represent
the depth characteristics of objects. Next, the phenotype aggregation
module fuses the image and depth features, allowing for a more
comprehensive representation of the object's spatial and phenotypic in-
formation. Finally, the enhanced features are passed through the
phenotype intensify module, which strengthens their interaction and
prepares them for the object detection head. The detection process is
anchored in both 2D and 3D bounding box regression, which outputs the
3D coordinates, dimensions, and orientations of detected objects. The
entire model is optimized using a custom loss function that combines
classiﬁcation loss, bounding box regression loss, and depth-assisted loss.
2.2.1. Backbone
The backbone in TPDNet is DAL-102 [33], a robust architecture
designed for deep feature extraction. DAL-102 employs deep layer ag-
gregation, which enhances the representation capabilities of deep neural
networks by effectively merging features from multiple layers. This
multi-layer aggregation strategy helps capture ﬁne-grained details and
global contextual information, which is crucial for monocular 3D object
detection, particularly in complex outdoor environments like agricultural
ﬁelds. The DAL-102 is pre-trained on the 3D object detection dataset
(KITTI) [26], which provides a foundational understanding of 3D object
perception. This pre-training allows DAL-102 to extract rich multi-scale
features from RGB images, offering a solid basis for the downstream
tasks of depth estimation and object detection.
2.2.2. Depth estimation
Monocular 3D object detection is inherently challenging due to the
absence of direct depth information in single-view images. To overcome
this limitation, we introduce a depth estimation module that generates
depth maps from image features. As shown in Fig. 3, the depth estimation

tures. First, to enable the depth estimation module to effectively estimate
depth information, we introduce a depth estimation auxiliary task into
the module. This auxiliary task supervises depth estimation using real
depth labels, which are obtained by projecting 3D point cloud data onto
the image plane. Second, since the depth estimation auxiliary task is a
regression task, which poses challenges for model ﬁtting, we transform
the regression task into a classiﬁcation task to make the depth estimation

2.2.4. Phenotype aggregation
In monocular 3D object detection, integrating heterogeneous fea-
tures, such as image and depth features, is critical for effectively
capturing the complex spatial relationships needed to estimate 3D object
information. These two types of features offer complementary informa-
tion—image features encapsulate visual context, while depth features
provide spatial understanding. However, image features and depth fea-
tures are two heterogeneous types, and capturing the potential corre-
spondences between them poses a challenge for effectively fusing these
features. To address this, we design a phenotype aggregation module to
fuse these features by leveraging cross-attention mechanisms, speciﬁcally
an image cross-attention mechanism and a depth cross-attention mech-
anism. A major limitation of traditional self-attention mechanisms is
their computational complexity, typically O(n2). Speciﬁcally, in tradi-
tional attention mechanisms, computing the attention weights is the
main part of the operation. The central step is to calculate QKT, Q 2 Rnd
and K 2 Rnd represent the query and key matrices. Therefore, the
complexity of the matrix multiplication is Q(n2d). Since n > >d, it is
approximately Q(n2). This scaling arises from the need to compute and
store attention matrices of size n2, leading to excessive memory con-
sumption and slower convergence. To overcome this, we utilize a linear
attention mechanism, which decomposes attention computations into
linear operations, reducing both computational complexity and memory
usage to O(n). This drastically improves resource efﬁciency and enables
the model to handle large datasets without sacriﬁcing performance.

similar to depth cross-attention to achieve an inverse fusion mechanism.
The query, key, and value representations are updated using the elu
activation function, and the Einstein summation convention is employed

accuracy and robustness of the monocular 3D object detection task,
particularly in challenging agricultural environments such as wax gourd
and fruit ﬁelds. By integrating these components into the TPDNet, we
signiﬁcantly improve the network's capability to predict accurate 3D
bounding boxes for objects, providing a more reliable solution for
monocular 3D object detection in precision agriculture scenarios.

but the extracted features remain relatively independent. Simply adding

their correlation and complementarity. Therefore, further effective deep
fusion of these two types of information is crucial for estimation in the

2.2.6. Detection head and loss function
Detection Head: Upon completion of the feature extraction process
through the various modules, we obtain the enhanced phenotype-
intensiﬁed feature representations. These representations are then fed
into detection heads equipped with predeﬁned 2D-3D anchor points for
object localization and bounding box regression. Each predeﬁned anchor
comprises parameters for both the 2D bounding box and the 3D bounding
box, and these anchor points serve as spatial references for the detection
of objects in the scene. During training, we compute the Intersection Over
Union (IOU) between the ground truth annotations and the predicted
bounding boxes, and anchors with an IOU greater than 0.5 are selected as
positive samples and used for model optimization. By using this
threshold, we ensure that the model focuses on bounding boxes that
closely match the ground truth. The use of anchors in this manner not
only aids in improving localization but also helps guide the network in
learning accurate object geometry and spatial relationships. To further
enhance the model's accuracy, the regression process for both 2D and 3D
bounding boxes is optimized based on these selected anchors. This allows
the model to iteratively adjust its predictions during the training phase,
leading to progressively better performance in terms of both 2D and 3D
object detection.

3.2. Implementation details
We conduct model training in a high-performance computing envi-
ronment powered by an NVIDIA A40 GPU. The 3D object detection
network undergoes training for 300 epochs using the Adam optimizer,
with a batch size of 3 and an initial learning rate of 0.0001. A cosine
annealing learning rate schedule is applied to ensure stable training dy-
namics. For each pixel on the feature map, 48 anchors are employed,
utilizing aspect ratios of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5, along with 12 distinct height
scales. During the inference phase, Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)
[felzenszwalb2010object] is employed with an IoU threshold of 0.4 to
eliminate redundant detection boxes and improve detection accuracy.
3.3. Quantitative results
We evaluate the performance of the proposed TPDNet against several
state-of-the-art monocular 3D object detection methods on wax gourd
dataset. The results, as shown in Table 2, reveal that while these models
perform well on the KITTI dataset, their performance in both 3D and BEV
detection on our dataset is signiﬁcantly lower compared to TPDNet. First,
TPDNet outperforms MonoDETR [37], MonoDistill [38], and MonoDTR
[36] by 12.06 %, 8.46 %, and 4.87 % in APBEV, respectively. This
improvement in APBEV is attributed to the depth enhancement module
(DEM), which augments depth feature expressiveness across both spatial
and channel dimensions, allowing the model to capture more precise
depth information. Second, TPDNet also surpasses MonoDETR [37],
MonoDistill [38], and MonoDTR [36] by 12.7 %, 16.89 %, and 8.5 % in
AP3D. This enhancement is a result of the phenotype aggregation module
(PAM) and phenotype intensify module (PIM), which leverage three
distinct attention mechanisms to effectively capture the complex in-
teractions between depth and image features, leading to a marked
improvement in AP3D. For AP2D, the performance is comparable across
models, as wax gourd, being relatively large objects, do not exhibit sig-
niﬁcant variations in 2D detection results.

3D Bounding Box Visualization: We can intuitively assess the per-

effective fusion of depth information and image features. This fusion

3.5. Ablation study
TPDNet comprises three key components: the depth enhance mod-
ule(DEM), the phenotype aggregation module(PAM), and the phenotype
Intensify module(PIM). We conduct an ablation study to evaluate the
contribution of each component, testing several model variants: TPDNet
without DEN (W/O DEN), TPDNet without PAN and PIN (W/O PAN-
&PIN), and TPDNet without all three (W/O DEN&PAN&PIN). The re-
sults, as shown in Table 3. First, the result indicates that when the depth
enhance module(DEM) is removed, the model's performance signiﬁ-
cantly declines, which relies solely on the initial depth features. This
underscores the importance of using the depth enhance module in the
model to enhance the expressiveness of depth features. Second, when the
phenotype aggregation module (PAM) and phenotype intensify module
(PIM) are removed, and depth features are fused with image features
using simple feature addition, the model's performance signiﬁcantly
declines. This clearly demonstrates that our the phenotype aggregation
module(PAM) and phenotype intensify module(PIM) effectively capture
the underlying correspondences between image features and depth fea-
tures, thereby enhancing the model's detection capabilities. Finally, the
removal of all three components demonstrates that relying solely on raw
features is insufﬁcient for achieving accurate object detection. These
ﬁndings underscore the synergistic contributions of each component,
which collectively enhance the overall performance of the model.

thereby improving overall detection performance.

3.8. The impact of overexposed images on model performance
During outdoor data collection, our dataset includes overexposed
images due to the inﬂuence of different weather conditions. This diverse
dataset helps improve the model's adaptability and robustness under
various lighting conditions. To assess the speciﬁc impact of overexposed
images on model performance, we conduct an experiment using the
counterfactual method: normal images serve as the training set, while
overexposed images serve as the test set. In our dataset, there are a total
of 23 overexposed images, accounting for approximately 2.5 % of the
entire dataset. As shown in Table 5, the experimental results indicate a
signiﬁcant decline in model performance. The primary reason for this
phenomenon is the notable difference in feature distribution between
overexposed and normal images, making it difﬁcult for the model to
accurately assess the spatial information of the target. These differences
cause the model to perform poorly when processing overexposed images,
thereby affecting the overall test results. This experimental result sug-
gests that enhancing the model's stability and robustness under different
weather conditions requires incorporating more data from diverse en-
vironments during training. This approach helps the model better handle
various complex scenarios in real-world applications.
3.9. Data partitioning and evaluation for model stability
To evaluate the stability and robustness of our model, we conduct a
comprehensive experiment to assess its performance across various data
splits. Model stability is crucial in real-world applications, where it is
essential that the model performs consistently regardless of how the data
is partitioned. This is particularly important when working with small or
imbalanced datasets, where variations in the training and testing data
could potentially lead to overﬁtting or unreliable performance. Thus, to
ensure a more reliable evaluation of our model's generalization ability,
we adopted a cross-validation approach.
The entire dataset is evenly split into ﬁve subsets, where the original
80 % training set is further divided into four subsets, while the remaining
20 % testing set serves as the ﬁfth subset. In each iteration, one subset is
selected as the test set, while the remaining four subsets are used for
training the model. This process repeats ﬁve times, ensuring that each
subset serves as the test set once, resulting in ﬁve independent model
training and evaluation cycles. As shown in Table 6, the model achieves
an average precision of AP3D ¼ 62.06, APBEV ¼ 62.52, AP2D ¼ 90.03. The
results indicate that the model maintains stable performance across
various data splits and exhibits strong generalization ability, consistently
achieving robust results in all cross-validation iterations.

4. Discussion
4.1. Prediction capability for different fruits
To validate the performance of our model on other crops, we ﬁne-
tuned TPDNet using a small amount of data and visualized the detec-
tion results along with the corresponding attention maps. The data fol-
lows the same format as the waxgourd dataset, including image data,
depth maps (generated from point cloud data), and label information. As
shown in Fig. 7, the detection results and attention maps can initially
indicate the locations of these objects. This ﬁnding suggests that with
appropriate ﬁne-tuning on relevant crop data, our model can be applied
to detection tasks for different types of crops. This discovery not only
expands the applicability of the model but also provides strong support
for further research into detecting more types of agricultural crops.
However, due to the limitations in the size of the dataset, the model
cannot converge during the ﬁne-tuning process, making it impossible to
perform quantitative and qualitative analysis with other models.
4.2. Monocular 3D object detection
In this study, we propose a network called TPDNet, which is designed
for monocular 3D object detection in waxgourd and is particularly suited
for large-volume object. To broaden TPDNet's applicability across various
crop scenarios, we reﬁne crop categorization through data expansion and
incorporate medium-volume crops (such as Hami melon) to optimize
detection capabilities for small object. The core challenge of monocular
3D object detection lies in efﬁciently inferring 3D spatial information
from RGB images, which depends on capturing subtle differences
between image features. Therefore, we integrate multispectral informa-
tion to enhance image features and improve the accuracy of 3D spatial
inference, which represents an important research direction. This study
holds signiﬁcant practical value for agricultural automation. First, by
providing precise object detection and 3D spatial localization, TPDNet
supplies reliable data support for automated agricultural equipment,
reducing reliance on manual inspections and labor-intensive operations
and effectively lowering labor costs. Second, this approach enhances the
efﬁciency and accuracy of automated machinery during harvesting,
thereby optimizing crop picking and management. With continuous ad-
vancements in data augmentation and multispectral integration, this
method extends to a wide variety of crops, offering robust technological
support for the intelligent and precise management of agricultural pro-
duction and driving the development of agricultural automation toward
more efﬁcient and intelligent systems.
4.3. Multimodal 3D object detection
Multimodal 3D object detection is an important area of future
research, as it combines data from various sources to enhance detection
accuracy. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding
of complex environments. Although the proposed TPDNet performs well
in monocular 3D object detection for large crops (such as wax gourd),
there are limitations with monocular detection methods for smaller crops
like apples and kiwifruit, where accurately estimating their spatial in-
formation in a 3D coordinate system remains challenging. To overcome
this limitation, future research can focus on multimodal 3D object
detection methods, as combining multiple data modalities (such as RGB,
depth, and LiDAR) can provide complementary information, enriching
feature representation. This integration helps the model better distin-
guish between objects and background, improving detection accuracy
and robustness in complex environments. Speciﬁcally, we plan to
enhance the model's perception of 3D information for small crops by
combining point cloud data with image data. Point cloud data provides
rich geometric structure information, while image data offers detailed
texture and color information. By integrating these two modalities, the
model will be able to more accurately recognize and localize the posi-
tions and shapes of small crops, thereby improving the precision and
robustness of detection.
