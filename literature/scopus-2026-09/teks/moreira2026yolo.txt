A B S T R A C T
The integration of Deep Learning techniques for grapevine yield estimation has led to significant advancements 
in Precision Viticulture. The accurate detection and counting of berries per bunch is a critical task that can 
explain up to 30% of yield variability, thereby enabling improved yield estimation. This study proposes a YOLO- 
based approach for the automated detection and counting of visible grapevine berries, using a dataset of more 
than 1500 images collected over three phenological stages. The selected YOLO models performed well in both 
detection and counting tasks, with all models achieving high detection accuracy (G-mAP > 0.95) and estimation 
of visible berries (R2 > 0.97). Among the evaluated models, YOLOv11n exhibited the highest detection per­
formance (F1-Score = 0.954, G-mAP = 0.962), while YOLOv10n demonstrated the most consistent and reliable 
counting accuracy (MAPE = 4.764, MSE = 12.203, RMSE = 3.493). Beyond overall performance, the analysis 
revealed that ampelographic features such as berry size, occlusion, and bunch morphology can influence ac­
curacy, although YOLOv10n showed no significant disparities across categories. To extend the scope, a com­
plementary analysis demonstrated a strong linear relationship (R2 = 0.860) between visible counts and the total 
number of berries per bunch, supporting the potential of correction models to address occlusion. By systemat­
ically evaluating model behaviour across diverse viticultural conditions and incorporating correlation with total 
berry counts, this study provides a deeper understanding of the robustness and limitations of Deep Learning 
models, offering critical insights for future applications in vineyard monitoring, yield estimation, harvest opti­
misation, and management.
1. Introduction
The wine industry is one of the most economically relevant and 
historically rich agricultural sectors worldwide. The global wine export 
value in 2023 reached 36.0 billion EUR, the second highest ever recor­
ded [34], with leading wine-producing regions, such as France, Italy, 
Spain, the United States, and Argentina, exporting millions of hectoliters 
annually (231 mhl total wine production mid-projection for 2024). In 
the European Union (EU) alone, vineyards cover approximately 3.3 
mha, representing nearly 45% of the world’s total vineyard area, and the 
total production, provisionally estimated at 139 mhl (2024), represents 
60% of the global total [33].
The quality and quantity of wine production are inherently linked to 
vineyard yield, which is highly dependent on external factors, influ­
enced by edaphoclimatic conditions, biotic and abiotic stresses, genetic 
factors (grape variety and rootstock) and cultural practices, which cause 
different physiological responses [26]. Wine grapes production requires 
careful balance between yield optimization and quality control. In the 
EU, for example, regulations and distinctive signs, such as the 'Desig­
nation of Origin' or 'Geographical Indications', dictate the maximum 
yield per hectare, as well as quality standards [13]. In this context, ac­
curate yield estimation plays a fundamental role in viticulture, as it 
directly impacts every stage of the winemaking process, from harvest 
planning, and resource allocation to pricing strategies, supply chain 
management and market distribution.
Traditional yield estimation methods rely on manual sampling and 
visual inspection of the so-called yield components, e.g. reproductive 
structures such as inflorescences, flowers, bunches or berries, which 
indicate on-going reproductive and yield development. Besides the 
number of bunches per vine, the number of berries per bunch has been 
identified as one major determinant of overall yield, which can explain 
up to around 30% of yield variability [10]. Therefore, accurately 
detecting and counting berries per bunch is essential for improving

However, these methods suffer from several major limitations, as they 
require extensive manual labor, making them costly and inefficient, 
especially in large-scale commercial vineyards. Manual counting of 
berries is a time-consuming and destructive yield estimation method, as 
the bunches must be removed from the vine, which is impractical for 
real-time decision-making. As a result, they often rely on small sample 
sizes, extrapolated to estimate yield across an entire vineyard, that can't 
fully capture the high spatial and temporal variability that grapevine 
yield exhibits. Furthermore, manual sampling is prone to human error 
and subjectivity, leading to inconsistent results.
The increasing adoption of digital technologies and data-driven ap­
proaches has transformed modern viticulture, in an attempt to solve the 
aforementioned challenges. Precision viticulture (PV) has gained signifi­
cant traction in recent years as a means to optimize vineyard management 
through data-driven decision-making [3]. The application of advanced 
sensors, remote sensing, Artificial Intelligence (AI), and Machine Learning 
(ML) techniques offers new opportunities to enhance yield estimation 
accuracy. Computer Vision (CV) and image processing represent the most 
common techniques for estimating yield as an alternative to traditional 
sampling methods [5]. The first method for counting berries in the field 
was introduced in 2007, relying on ellipse detection [38]. Since then, 
various counting techniques have been developed. Some of these methods 
utilize traditional image processing techniques, including segmentation, 
shape recognition, and feature extraction algorithms tailored to this 
specific task [5]. Emerging technologies, such as Deep Learning (DL), and 
the recent advancements in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have 
enabled fast and accurate detection of grapevine berries, providing 
granular, high-precision data that is objective, scalable, and repeatable, 
overcoming many limitations of traditional methods [29]. Several studies

segmentation, density map-based approaches have also been explored for

Despite its advantages, automating grape berry detection and yield 
estimation remains challenging due to vineyard-specific complexities. 
Intra-class variation in yield components is significant, fluctuating 
across phenological stages, viewpoints, and morphological traits [16]. 
Grape bunches and berries vary in size, shape, and color—both across 
and within varieties—and these features evolve during development, 
adding another layer of complexity. For instance, early-stage berries are 
especially difficult to count due to size and color similarity with foliage. 
Visibility issues also arise in unstructured vineyard environments, where 
lighting conditions, shadows, and background elements (e.g., soil, trel­
lises) interfere with image quality. Occlusions and berry overlaps further 
hinder detection, caused by dense bunch formations, surrounding plant 
structures, and vineyard management practices (G. F. [46].
Hardware limitations add to these difficulties. DL models require 
high computational power, which end-devices like mobile, ground, or 
aerial platforms often lack, resulting in slow inference and delayed 
decision-making [16]. While DL approaches outperform traditional CV 
methods, they are still under development. The field lacks standardized 
methodologies and performance metrics, limiting study comparability. 
A major constraint is the scarcity of large, diverse, annotated datasets 
with groundtruth data, such as berry counts. Most studies are focused 
narrowly—on specific grape varieties, stages, or conditions – making 
generalization difficult [29]. Additionally, practical deployment is often 
overlooked, as many models prioritize accuracy over computational 
efficiency necessary for real-world use.
By addressing these gaps, this research, built on previous work [30]
on grapevine inflorescence segmentation and visible flower estimation, 
proposes a lightweight YOLO-based approach for automated visible 
grapevine berry detection and counting, with a specific emphasis on 
evaluating the impact of ampelographic features on model performance. 
The study aims to: 
• Collection of a dataset of 1530 RGB images of nine grapevine vari­
eties across several Portuguese wine regions, under real vineyard 
environment conditions, throughout three phenological stages.
• Benchmark multiple lightweight YOLO variants (YOLOv8n, 
YOLOv9t, YOLOv10n, YOLOv11n) to assess detection accuracy and 
counting reliability in diverse vineyard conditions.
• Analyze the effects of grapevine variety, growth stage and bunch 
morphology (shape, length, and density) on detection performance 
to identify potential biases and limitations in DL-based models.
• Provide a systematic evaluation of DL models under real-world 
viticultural conditions, contributing to more robust, scalable, and 
precise yield estimation systems.
The findings of this study contribute to the development of scalable 
and adaptable DL solutions for PV. By systematically analysing how 
ampelographic traits influence model performance, this research pro­
vides new insights into the robustness and limitations of DL-based 
detection methods in vineyard environments. These insights will help 
refine future applications of AI in viticulture, improving yield estimation

2. Materials and methods
This study employs a modular pipeline for image-based grapevine 
berry detection and yield estimation under real field conditions, as 
illustrated in Fig. 1. The workflow commences with the construction of a 
RGB grapevine bunch images dataset collected across multiple wine 
regions, varieties and phenological stages. These images were subse­
quently used to train and benchmark DL models for single-image visible 
berry detection. A further analysis was conducted in relation to key 
ampelographic factors, including grapevine variety, phenological stage, 
and bunch morphological features, to assess robustness under diverse 
field conditions. Finally, a correlation-based modeling step was applied 
to estimate the total number of berries per bunch from the visible counts. 
This modular structure enables independent analysis of each component 
while supporting efficient and field-deployable yield estimation from 
single RGB images.
2.1. Data acquisition
A user-friendly Android application was developed and shared with 
several Portuguese winegrowers, allowing them to acquire images of 
vines and their yield components from various perspectives, associating 
a unique ID and the georeferenced location to each image (Fig. 2). A 
total of 1530 RGB images of grapevine bunches were collected from 
different vineyards in various demarcated wine regions across the 2022, 
2023 and 2024 seasons (Fig. 3).
After the flowering period, according to the extended Biologische 
Bundesanstalt, Bundessortenamt und CHemische Industrie (BBCH) scale 
[27], images of grapevine bunches were collected during the fruit 
development stage, from majority of berries touching (BBCH 79) and 
berries developing colour (BBCH 83) to berries ripe for harvest (BBCH 
89). The dataset comprises images of the following grapevine varieties, 
identified according to their Vitis International Variety Catalogue 
(VIVC) code (see https://www.vivc.de, accessed on 6 January 2025): 
Touriga Nacional (VIVC-12594), Viosinho (VIVC-13109), Tinta Roriz 
(VIVC-12350), Moscatel Galego (VIVC-8031), Touriga Franca (VIVC- 
12593), Jaen (VIVC-5652), Trajadura (VIVC-12629), Tinta Barroca 
(VIVC-12462), Cabernet Sauvignon (VIVC-1929).
Grapevine varieties demonstrate considerable diversity in bunch and 
berry morphology, encompassing disparities in length, shape, density, 
and colouration, which are additionally influenced by phenological 
development stages [32](Fig. 4). These variations present challenges 
that must be addressed to develop DL models capable of accurately 
detecting berries under a wide range of conditions. By including images 
of multiple grape varieties, the dataset encompasses a representative 
diversity of berry and bunch characteristics, mitigating potential biases 
and improving model performance in heterogeneous vineyard envi­
ronments. Furthermore, the inclusion of phenological diversity ensures 
that the models can detect berries at various stages of growth, thereby 
enhancing their utility for applications such as vineyard monitoring and 
yield estimation throughout the growing season. Additionally, capturing 
images under varying environmental and lighting conditions, including 
changes in shadows, backgrounds, and occlusions, further strengthens 
the model's robustness and adaptability to real-world scenarios. The 
diversity of the dataset enhances the scalability of the model, extending 
its applicability to a wider range of vineyards and viticultural regions 
while maintaining reliability across diverse operational contexts.

counting, a complementary experiment was conducted during the 2024 
growing season to investigate the relationship between visible berry 
counts and the total number of berries per bunch. Using the same mobile 
application, an additional set of images was collected at one of the

2.2. Deep learning-based approach
DL models were employed to detect and count visible berries in 
grapevine bunch images, as they have proven to be highly effective in 
recognising objects in complex images, even in scenarios involving small 
and densely packed objects. Four YOLO (You Only Look Once) model 
variants −
YOLOv8n, YOLOv9t, YOLOv10n, and YOLOv11n −
were

accuracy [20]. The smaller versions, 'nano' (n) and 'tiny' (t), of these 
models were chosen specifically because they offer lightweight archi­
tectures, which is a crucial consideration for our ultimate goal of 
deploying DL models in the developed Android application.
Each YOLO variant offers unique trade-offs between accuracy, speed, 
and computational efficiency, differing primarily in their architecture 
and optimization focus (Table 1). YOLOv10n and YOLOv11n incorpo­
rate architectural refinements over YOLOv8n, such as improved feature

to predict outcomes or classify inputs, a critical practice is partitioning 
the dataset to ensure the reliability and generalizability of the model's 
performance. Thus, the collected images were divided into distinct 
subsets for training (70%), providing the data necessary for the models 
to learn patterns and relationships, validation (10%), allowing for pe­
riodic evaluation of model performance during training, and test (20%), 
serving as an independent benchmark to evaluate the model's ability to 
make accurate predictions on unseen data. Table 2 provides more 
detailed information on the number of images per phenological stage 
and variety among the different subsets.

tions depending on the mobile device and acquisition conditions, all

corresponding to the fixed input resolution of the YOLO architectures

To introduce variability into the image data, essential for improving 
the robustness and performance of DL models, data augmentation 
techniques were used to artificially increase the Train set. Pixel-level 
(change just an input image) and spatial-level (simultaneously change 
both an input image and bounding boxes) transformations were applied 
using the open-source image augmentation library Albumentations 
[8,20], as it integrates directly into the YOLO training mode. The 
transformations applied during data augmentation are randomly 
decided during each batch loading, giving the model a slightly different 
version of the image each time. In this case, since the training sessions 
ran for 250 epochs, the models were trained with 267.750 images in 
total. Every time an image is loaded during training, a new version of 
this image will be generated, providing more variety that helps the 
models to generalize better. Table 3 outlines the effect of each 
augmentation, while Fig. 6 illustrates the transformations applied with 
the default values on an original image.
2.2.2. Models training
The models were pre-trained with Microsoft’s COCO (Common Ob­
jects in Context) dataset[25]which include 80 pre-trained classes. 
Through transfer learning, they were then fine-tuned for detecting 
grapevine berries. Using the YOLO pre-trained weights, the models were 
trained with similar hyperparameters: training sessions ran for 250 
epochs, with a batch size of 16 images, learning rate of 0.01 and an input 
image resolution of 640 × 640 pixels. The AdamW optimizer was used, 
with a momentum of 0.937, and a weight decay of 0.0005. All experi­
ments were performed on a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 graphics pro­
cessing unit (GPU) (see https://www.nvidia.com/en-eu/geforce/graphi 
cs-cards/40-series/rtx-4060-4060ti/, accessed on 10 January 2025) 
with 8 gigabytes (GBs) of available memory.
2.3. Models inference
2.3.1. Visible berries detection
Accurate detection is achieved when there is substantial overlap 
between predicted and groundtruth bounding boxes, commonly 
measured through the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric. The number 
of groundtruth objects is given by the sum of the True Positives and False 
Negatives (TP + FN), while the number of predicted objects corresponds 
to the sum of the TP’s and False Positives (TP + FP). Detection perfor­
mance was evaluated using Precision (1), ability to identify only the 
relevant objects, Recall (2), ability to detect all the relevant objects and 
F1-Score (3), first harmonic mean between Recall and Precision.

3. Results & discussion
3.1. Visible berries detection
The results presented in Tables 4 and 5 demonstrate that, while the 
four YOLO models deliver similar overall performance concerning 
grapevine berry detection, key differences in Precision, Recall and 
confidence thresholds distinguish their suitability for specific applica­
tions. YOLOv11n emerges as the top-performing model, achieving the 
highest F1-score (0.954) and G-mAP (0.962). Its high Precision (0.988) 
and confidence threshold (0.504) contribute to its superior ability to 
minimize FP's, making it highly reliable for accurate detections. In 
contrast, YOLOv9t, with a lower confidence threshold (0.455), attains 
the highest Recall (0.920), rendering it better suited for scenarios where 
maximizing TP's is critical, albeit with slightly reduced Precision 
(0.965). YOLOv10n achieves a more balanced trade-off between Preci­
sion (0.959) and Recall (0.919), operating with the lowest confidence 
threshold (0.405). This approach prioritises inclusive detection, poten­
tially at the expense of stricter certainty in predictions. YOLOv8n ex­
hibits a slightly lower Recall (0.905), yet it stands out for its high 
Precision (0.974), which ensures reliable identification of grapevine 
berries. The comparatively lower Recall is indicative of the model's 
tendency to overlook some TP instances, a phenomenon that can be 
attributed to its relatively higher confidence threshold (0.503), which 
serves to filter out detections of lesser certainty.
It is noteworthy that all models exhibit commendable performance, 
with G-mAP values exceeding 0.950 and inference times remaining

Systematically analysing TP, FP, and FN, is crucial for evaluating 
performance, as these metrics provide granular insights into the 
strengths and weaknesses of each detection model. The open source 
toolkit FiftyOne (see https://voxel51.com, accessed on 22 January 
2025) facilitates the visualisation of datasets and the analysis of CV 
models and was used for qualitative assessment, to better comprehend 
the circumstances under which FP and FN emerge and the rationale 
behind their existence. Mismatches between the groundtruth and the 
model's prediction bounding boxes are the main cause of the occurrence 
of FP and FN, due to the overlap of adjacent berries or other plant 
structures such as peduncles, which can cause the models to generate a 
bounding box that delimits only part of the berry. In cases where the 
overlapping area between the predicted and groundtruth bounding 
boxes falls below the established IoU of 60%, the prediction bounding 
box is considered an FP and the groundtruth one a FN, as if it were an 
undetected berry (Fig. 10a). FP also occurs in situations where the model 
detects a berry that has not been annotated as groundtruth, whether due 
to it appearing in the background or because of high levels of occlusion/ 
overlap that did not allow it to be identified in the manual annotation 
process, which is time-consuming, laborious and always prone to errors 
(Fig. 10b). However, even if the evaluation metrics are affected, the 
model's fundamental ability to detect berries remains unchanged. 
Adjusting the IoU threshold or refining annotations could improve the 
reported performance, reducing the appearance of FP and FN due to the 
last-mentioned scenarios.
Even so, there are specific instances where the model's ability to 
detect can be questioned. Some FP's occur because the models detect 
regions that resemble berries but are actually background elements, 
such as leaves or peduncles. This issue is particularly evident in images 
of white variety bunches or pre-ripened bunches, where there is a 
greater color similarity between these structures and the berries. 
Shadows can also contribute to FPs by creating low-contrast regions or 
dark patches that mimic the texture or shape of berries (Fig. 11a). On the 
other hand, FN's are primarily due to the model's difficulty in detecting 
small berries, which may be accentuated by the image resolution and 
distance from which was taken. Additionally, overlapping berries and 
occlusion effects caused by the aforementioned structures further 
contribute to FN (Fig. 11b).
The proposed method in this study, employing YOLO model variants, 
demonstrated superior performance regarding visible berries detection, 
particularly in terms of F1-Score and G-mAP metrics, outperforming 
several existing approaches.
Studies utilizing object detection architectures and other YOLO 
frameworks, such as YOLOv7 [4] and YOLOv5 [50,51] have reported 
mAP values of 0.835 and 0.877, respectively. Similarly, a study 
employing another one-stage object detector, a Retina-Net-based ar­
chitecture [22] reported an F1-score of 0.546, significantly lower than 
our best-performing model, YOLOv11n, which achieved an F1-score of 
0.954. This exact F1-Score was also achieved by Miao et al. [28]
improved HED network, which, despite being subjected to testing on 
bunches at varying stages of development, the findings were confined to 
a single variety. This approach was tested on fewer images (100) taken 
in different backgrounds and environments (natural and artificial), 
which, due to its two-step nature, was found to be approximately 5.8 
times slower than the YOLOv11n model.

3.2.1. Grapevine variety
While grapevine variety was not a significant factor in the overall 
interaction between models and this feature (p = 0.358), the YOLOv9t 
model showed a statistical variation in performance (p = 0.025) with a 
mean error for white grape varieties (3.989) significantly lower than for 
red varieties (5.106). This trend is observed in almost all models, except 
the YOLOv10n model. A key characteristic that differentiates red and 
white grapes, the berry color, only becomes apparent later in the 
ripening process. During the earlier growth stages, berries from both red 
and white varieties exhibit similar greenish coloration, and the models 
were therefore exposed to a large proportion of visually comparable 
berries. As ripening progresses, white grape varieties may develop 
yellowish or amber hues, but they generally lack the pronounced color 
transition characteristic of red varieties. Because the dataset spans 
multiple BBCH phenological stages, the models were exposed to the 
natural color evolution of berries, including in white varieties. Overall, 
the visual appearance of white berries across stages tends to be more 
stable in RGB imagery, which may contribute to more consistent 
detection performance, whereas red varieties present greater color 
variability during ripening, potentially introducing more heterogeneous 
visual features. This suggests that dataset composition and the timing of 
image acquisition play a crucial role in model accuracy.

3.2.3. Bunch length
Another cause of poor detection of small objects is image resolution 
[24], as the apparent bunch size in the image significantly affects the 
prediction errors of certain YOLO models. Berries resolution is low, and 
the proportion of pixels is small, limiting the extraction of adequate 
feature information. The Kruskal-Wallis test shows statistically signifi­
cant differences in error rates across bunch sizes for YOLOv8n (p =
0.004), YOLOv9t (p = 0.039), and YOLOv11n (p = 0.007). These models 
struggle more when the bunch appears smaller in the image, leading to 
higher errors. In contrast, YOLOv10n exhibits stable performance across 
different bunch sizes. The interaction between bunch length and model 
performance was not statistically significant (p = 0.816), however, these 
results highlight the importance of scale-aware training strategies. 
Maximizing the bunch length in the frame by capturing images from a 
closer distance can be an effective and straightforward method which, 
combined with multi-scale training and optimised anchor boxes [18],

3.2.4. Bunch density
A similar pattern is observed in the analysis of bunch density influ­
ence on the model’s errors. Despite the interaction between this feature 
and models did not prove to be statistically different (p = 0.166), while 
YOLOv10n remains the most stable model, bunch density significantly 
influences the performance of YOLOv8n (p = 0.008), YOLOv9t (p =
0.002), and YOLOv11n (p < 0.001), with loose bunches leading to 
higher errors. In loosely packed bunches, the total number of berries is 
generally lower compared to medium or dense bunches. Even if the 
absolute error (difference between predicted and ground truth count) 
remains similar across densities, the percentage error will be higher for 
loose bunches because it is calculated relative to a smaller total count. 
Furthermore, loose bunches are, in most cases, associated with earlier 
growth stages (greater colour correlation with the background) or 
smaller berries, which makes it easier to receive the influence of envi­
ronmental noise, thus generating more FN leading to higher errors.
3.2.5. Bunch shape
Bunch shape significantly influences error rates for YOLOv8n (p =
0.008), YOLOv9t (p = 0.002), and YOLOv11n (p = 0.025), with conical 
bunches leading to the highest errors. The high shape variability that can 
be found in grapevine bunches affects the spatial distribution of berries 
and can introduce different occlusion ratios. Despite the differences 
observed, unlike objective measurements such as bunch length in the 
image or bunch density, the classification of bunch shape relies on 
human perception, which can vary between observers and over time. 
This means that some bunches may not strictly fit these categories, 
especially in early growth stages, when bunches are still forming. 
Moreover, funnel-shaped bunches have characteristics of both conical 
and cylindrical structures, making their classification more ambiguous. 
Since bunch shape is a three-dimensional characteristic, image 
perspective can also influence the categorization of bunch shapes, 
adding another layer of variability to the analysis [42]. This could mean 
that the true impact of bunch shape on error rates is smaller than re­
ported, as some portion of the variability may come from miss- 
classification rather than actual model detection errors. Nevertheless, 
the YOLOv10n model maintains consistent error rates regardless of 
bunch shape and no statistically significant difference was found in the 
interaction between this ampelographic feature and the models (p =
0.832). More objective shape classification methods (e.g., width-to- 
length ratio, convex hull analysis) and the account for shape uncer­
tainty, reconsidering shape category definitions could help improve 
future analyses.
Overall, the results indicate that the YOLO models performed well 
across the different ampelographic features analyzed, with some varia­
tions in error rates depending on grape variety, growth stage, bunch 
length, density, and shape. Despite these differences, the models 
demonstrated robustness in visible berry counting, particularly 
YOLOv10n, which was not significantly influenced by any of the eval­
uated categories. The percentage error was recorded as no more than 5% 
across the evaluated models. While no universal error threshold exists 
for plant phenotyping tasks, errors in the range of 5–10% are commonly 
cited as ideal targets in vineyard yield estimation when compared to 
traditional manual approaches [45]. Although some models showed 
statistically significant variation between categories, this did not 
compromise overall performance. Improving dataset balance would 
mainly help reduce these intra-model differences and ensure a fairer and 
more representative evaluation in future work.

3.3. Visible vs total berry counts
A complementary analysis was performed on 110 bunches sampled 
during the 2024 growing season to investigate the relationship between 
visible berry counts from 2D images and the total number of berries per 
bunch. For this analysis, visible berry counts were obtained using the 
YOLOv10n model, which was selected as it achieved the lowest MAPE in 
the previous visible berry counting task and showed no statistically 
significant differences in error across ampelographic features. Since only 
one image per bunch was selected, part of each bunch is naturally hid­
den from view, thus the number of detected berries from a single image 
was consistently lower than the true total count. Nevertheless, the linear 
regression (Fig. 13) revealed a strong association (R2 = 0.860) between 
visible and total counts. The regression slope of 2.350 indicated that 
visible berries represented, on average, about 50% of the total berry 
number, reflecting the systematic underestimation caused by occlusion 
when relying on a single 2D perspective. Quantitative error metrics 
further illustrate the strength of this relationship within the analyzed 
dataset, with a MAPE of 20.807 and an RMSE of 21.246 berries per 
bunch. These values should be interpreted as in-sample indicators of 
consistency rather than predictive performance on unseen data. Taken 
together, these results confirm that while single-image detections 
inherently underestimate total berries, the underestimation follows a 
predictable linear pattern that can be corrected with simple statistical 
modeling.
These results align with previous findings in the literature. Torres- 
Lomas et al. [42] investigated grapevine bunch architecture and berry 
properties using detached bunches imaged in an artificial environment 
from four different angles. The authors observed systematic underesti­
mation of berry number, which varied with the imaging angle and 
averaged approximately 50% across their dataset. Importantly, they also 
showed that a simple linear regression model was sufficient to correct 
this underestimation, achieving an adjusted R2 of 0.872 with a 2.250 
slope when the cluster image with the maximum visible berries (from 
the four perspectives) was used.
Besides being consistent with the underestimation pattern reported 
in previous studies, the results presented also extend the analysis into 
real vineyard conditions, where image acquisition is considerably more 
challenging. Field-acquired images are subject to occlusions, non- 
uniform illumination, and restricted access to multiple viewing angles 
due to trellis and plant canopy architecture. Despite these constraints, 
the regression between visible and total berry counts remained strong, 
and importantly, it was based on detections from the YOLOv10n model, 
which had previously achieved the lowest MAPE in visible berry 
counting and showed no statistically significant sensitivity to ampelo­
graphic features. Moreover, while the model was trained and validated 
on nine grapevine varieties, the correlation analysis was performed on 
17 additional varieties not included in training. Such cross-varietal 
robustness greatly enhances the practical utility of the proposed 
framework, since viticultural applications often require deployment in 
vineyards with diverse varietal compositions without the possibility of 
retraining for each case.
The practical implications of these findings are noteworthy. 
Although single-image detections underestimate total berry counts, the 
consistent linear relationship suggests that a simple correction frame­
work could effectively transform visible detections into accurate esti­
mates of total berry number. This would significantly enhance the utility 
of visible berry detection methods for viticultural yield estimation, 
where the total number of berries per bunch is a key parameter. More­
over, relying on single 2D images offers advantages in terms of data 
collection efficiency, requiring less equipment and processing compared 
to multi-view or 3D reconstruction approaches, that rely on stereo 
vision, structure-from-motion, or point-cloud learning [39,49,23,54].
Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The 
strength of the correlation may vary depending on bunch compactness 
and phenological stage and future work should therefore focus on 
refining correction models — for example, incorporating phenological 
stage or bunch density as covariates — as well as exploring comple­
mentary strategies such as multi-view imaging or depth-sensing tech­
nologies to further mitigate occlusion effects.
4. Conclusions
This study proposes a YOLO-based DL approach for the automated 
detection and counting of visible grapevine berries, incorporating a 
comprehensive analysis of the influence of ampelographic features on 
model performance. Utilising a diverse dataset comprising grapevine 
bunch images from various Portuguese wine regions, a systematic 
evaluation was conducted to ascertain the impact of grape variety, 
phenological stage, bunch morphology (length and shape), and density 
on detection and counting accuracy for yield estimation.
The results demonstrate that the selected YOLO models performed 
well in both detection and counting tasks, with all models achieving 
high detection accuracy (mAP50 > 0.95) and estimation of visible berries 
(R2 > 0.97). Among the evaluated models, YOLOv11n exhibited the

highest detection performance (F1-Score = 0.954, mAP50 = 0.960), 
while YOLOv10n demonstrated the most consistent and reliable count­

strength of this work is the in-depth analysis of how ampelographic 
traits affect DL model's performance. While certain factors, such as 
loosely packed or conical bunches, introduced slight increases in error

conditions (MAPE < 7%). YOLOv10n model maintained a stable per­

across the different ampelographic features, confirming the suitability 
and adaptability of YOLO-based object detection models for real-word 
vineyard yield estimation applications, where varying grapevine con­
ditions present challenges for automated detection.
In addition to visible berry detection, a complementary analysis was 
conducted to explore the relationship between single-image visible 
berry counts and the total number of berries per bunch. Using 
YOLOv10n detections, a strong linear association was observed (R2 =
0.860), with visible counts corresponding to approximately 50% of total 
berries. Importantly, this correlation was obtained under field condi­
tions and across grapevine varieties not included in model training, 
highlighting both the robustness of the model and its ability to gener­
alize beyond the training set. These findings demonstrate that single- 
image detections, despite their inherent underestimation due to occlu­
sion, can serve as a reliable basis for estimating total berry numbers 
when coupled with a simple correction framework.
Overall, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of YOLO-based 
deep learning models for grapevine yield estimation, providing a 
strong foundation for AI-driven Precision Viticulture. By integrating 
ampelographic analysis into model evaluation, this work paves the way 
for more scalable and adaptable solutions in precision agriculture, ul­
timately supporting more efficient vineyard management and decision- 
making. Future research should focus on improving dataset balance 
across grape varieties, bunch morphology, and phenological stages to 
further enhance generalizability. A more detailed analysis of environ­
mental factors not directly linked to plant or fruit traits (e.g., variable 
light exposure, direction, and shadow conditions) would also be valu­
able to better understand their impact on detection and counting per­
formance. Additionally, expanding beyond visible berry detection to 
estimate the total number of berries per bunch, while also incorporating 
berry quality assessment through multi-class labeling or a post- 
processing step, would further increase the models' applicability to 
real-world yield estimation. This would require expanding the current 
annotation protocol to include additional berry condition classes (e.g., 
damaged, dehydrated, or diseased berries). Similar extensions of image- 
based plant phenotyping pipelines to trait-level analysis have been 
explored previously, indicating that the proposed detection framework 
is compatible with such developments [37]. Another important step is 
the deployment and evaluation of these models in real-world conditions, 
particularly on end-devices such as smartphones and robotic platforms, 
allowing for real-time inference.
