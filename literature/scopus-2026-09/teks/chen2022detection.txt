Detection of Oil Palm Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBS) with Computer Vision Models 
Zhiyuan Chen, Salma Elsaid, Doreen Ying Ying Sim, Tomas Maul, Iman Yi Liao   
School of Computer Science, Faculty of Science and Engineering 
University of Nottingham Malaysia 
Semenyih, Selangor, Malaysia 
Zhiyuan.Chen@nottingham.edu.my 
 
 
Abstract— Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs) are an important 
agricultural asset for countries such as Malaysia as they are 
used to obtain oil palm which contributes to a large portion of 
the countries’ GDP.  However, the current FFBs harvesting 
process requires a significant amount of manual labor and may 
cause injuries or damage to the FFBs which must be avoided to 
ensure the oil produced does not get affected. Therefore, an 
automatic harvesting system needs to be developed to help avoid 
such issues. An important part of the harvesting system would 
be a vision system for the detection and localization of the fruits 
and involves the usage of computer vision models. In this paper, 
a comparison is performed between four different models: 
Support Vector Machine (SVM) which uses the Histogram of 
Gradients (HOG), Faster R-CNN, YOLOv4 and YOLOv5. The 
models were tested by the metrics of mean average precision 
(mAP), precision, recall and F1 score and their detection speed. 
The results showed that the SVM + HOG model had the worst 
performance with a mAP of 89% and the YOLOv4 model had 
the best performance with a mAP of 98.5%. However, the 
YOLOv5 model provided the best results in terms of speed with 
a detection speed of 11.4 ms and its mAP was 96.4% making it 
the best model for use in a harvesting system for the detection 
of FFBs as it provides both speed and accuracy. 
Keywords- Object detection; oil palm fresh fruit bunch; fruit 
detection; SVM; Faster RCNN; YOLO 
I. 
 INTRODUCTION  
Oil palm is an important agricultural asset for countries 
such as Malaysia who is its second- largest producer after 
Indonesia. Oil palm is derived from fruit bunches of Oil Palm 
trees, commonly known as Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs). The 
current FFB harvesting process requires a significant amount 
of manual labor. The process may also cause injuries or 
damage to the FFBs which must be avoided to ensure the oil 
produced does not get affected [1].  
An automatic harvesting system can help prevent this. It 
can also enable industrial automation that would not require 
the use of manual labor and thus, can help save costs of FFB 
harvesting in general. It can ensure better consistency, 
accuracy, non-destructiveness, and quicker performance [2].  
In general, a fruit harvesting robot has two main functions: 
1) to detect and localize ripe fruits through its vision system 
and 2) moving the robotic arm to the position of the detected 
fruit and harvesting it through the end effector without causing 
it any damage [3]. The vision system plays a very important 
role for a harvesting robot as it is the first step used for the 
detection of the target fruit and so, if this step is performed 
correctly, it can help prevent errors in subsequent steps.  
    In recent years, several efforts have been made to 
develop vision systems for the detection of target fruits for 
harvest. Vision systems can be used to detect fruits via their 
external features such as color, texture, or shape or by using a 
mixture of them. [4], and [5] made use of a combination of the 
features of color and texture to extract features of FFBs using 
the statistical color features of histograms and the combination 
of different color spaces.  
Other features that can be used are the spectral reflectance, 
or the thermal signature of fruits. Spectral reflectance is the 
result of using a spectral camera that can capture wavelengths 
of light not visible to the human eye while the thermal 
signature of fruits is the result of using a thermal camera that 
can help differentiate a fruit from its surroundings through the 
difference in temperature. [6], and [7] developed algorithms 
to detect apples, and green citrus fruits respectively based on 
hyperspectral and multi-spectral image analysis while [8] and 
[9] made use of the thermal signature of immature citrus fruits 
to count the quantity of fruits in videos using fruit detection 
and tracking algorithms.  
    The usage of these conventional methods can lead to 
complex computations that make them impractical for use in 
real-time operations. Therefore, in the last decade, the trend 
has shifted towards the usage of machine learning computer 
vision models. Support Vector Machines (SVM) are one of 
the most widely used supervised machine learning classifiers. 
They have been used widely in different research for the 
detection and localization of fruits in harvesting robots such 
as their usage in [10], and [11] to classify plums and tomatoes 
respectively. SVMs make use of feature extraction techniques 
such as the Histogram of Gradients (HOG) which gives a 
representation of the structure of an image. The features 
extracted are used to train the SVM to identify whether an 
image contains the target fruit or not.  
SVMs use kernel functions that allow them to perform 
two-dimensional classification on a set of originally one-
dimensional data. The kernel functions project data from a 
low-dimensional space to a space of higher dimension. The 
most popular kernel functions used by SVMs are the linear 
kernel function, the polynomial kernel function, the Gaussian 
kernel function, the Gaussian Kernel Radial Basis Function 
(RBF) and the Sigmoid function. Each of these kernels 
transform the data space in a different manner and have their 
own sets of advantages and disadvantages [12]. 
SVMs categorize new data by selecting an optimal 
hyperplane through an implementation of the Structural Risk 
Minimization concept. In this way, SVMs have the advantage

Advancements in deep learning algorithms have given rise 
to a large variety of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) 
models. In comparison to fully connected networks, CNNs 
have the advantage of having fewer input parameters, which 
speeds up training.  
    CNN has many different variants such as Fully 
Convolutional Neural Networks (F-CNN), Regional CNN (R-
CNN), Fast R-CNN, and Faster R-CNN. The Faster R-CNN 
model has had several applications in fruit detection in robotic 
vision systems with high rates of accuracy, precision and 
recall and a lower processing time [14], [15], [16].  
    You Only Look Once (YOLO) is a CNN for performing 
object detection in real-time. Object detection in YOLO is 
performed as a regression problem and provides the class 
probabilities of the detected images. The advantages of the 
YOLO model are that it can perform detection with high 
accuracy and in real-time due to its speedy processing 
technique [17].  
    The YOLO model has different variants such as 
YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 which have been used in harvesting 
robots for the detection of different fruits such as apples [18] 
[19], pears [20], and FFBs [17]. While they are not the latest 
iterations of the YOLO model, they have been shown to be 
robust in their detection with high accuracies and speed and 
have had high compatibility with Robot Operating Systems 
(ROS) which is a core component of a robotic harvesting 
system.  
    The models mentioned above have yet to be compared 
against each other for the detection of FFBs in images. 
Therefore, in this paper, a comparison of the models was 
conducted to identify the best model that can be used in a real-
time robot harvesting system for the harvesting of FFBs in a 
plantation. The SVM + HOG model is a traditional computer 
vision approach while the deep learning models correspond to 
recent advancement in CNNs. 
    First, images were obtained from online datasets and 
images available via the search engine Google. Then, the 
images were pre-processed to make the models more robust 
and make the images more suitable for training. In the case of 
deep learning models, the images had to be annotated while in 
the case of the SVM model, the features of FFBs were 
extracted via the HOG. The image dataset was also divided 
into datasets for training, validation, and testing.  
    Following that, the models were trained by passing the 
training dataset through them or in the case of the SVM model, 
passing the features extracted via HOG. Finally, the models 
were validated and tested using the validation datasets and 
their performance was evaluated using the different metrics of 
mean Average Precision (mAP), Precision, Recall, F1 Score 
and detection time which is the time it took the models to 
detect FFBs in an image. The best model would be the one to 
provide both high accuracy and fast speed of detection as it 
would be implemented as a part of the vision system of a real-
time FFB harvesting robot. 
II. 
MATERIALS AND METHOD 
A. Data Acquisition  
Capturing images from an FFB plantation was not possible 
due to travelling restrictions. Therefore, images had to be 
obtained from different sources. The first source that was 
investigated were papers published in regard to developing 
vision systems for FFB detection in hopes that they would 
have published the datasets used in their research. This, 
unfortunately, did not yield any results so different sources 
which offer datasets for machine learning such as Papers with 
Code, Kaggle and UCI Machine Learning Repository were 
looked into.  
This resulted in obtaining two different datasets of oil 
palm fruit images from Kaggle. These images were not 
enough for training and included more images of oil palm 
fresh fruits than of fresh fruit bunches and so, following [21]’s 
approach, images were obtained by searching through Google 
Images via the keywords “Oil Palm Fresh Fruit Bunch”, “Oil 
Palm FFB”, “Fresh Fruit Bunch”, and “FFB”. 
In total, around 200 images were collected from these 
different sources out of which, only 169 were suitable for 
training. This means that those images were not too blurry or 
too crowded, did not have watermarks highly obstructing the 
FFB in the image and were of FFBs and not individual fruits.  
B. Pre-processing 
The SVM + HOG model’s data underwent a different pre-
processing process compared to the deep learning models to 
make them suitable for usage with the HOG. SVM is a 
classifier that can help identify whether an FFB is present in 
an image or not and uses HOG as a feature extraction method. 
HOG groups the pixels of an image into small cells. For each 
cell, it computes all the gradient directions and groups them 
into a number of orientation bins. Then, it sums up the 
gradient magnitude in each sample. Stronger gradients 
contribute more weight to their bins, and the effects of small 
random orientations due to noise are reduced.  
    This histogram gives us a picture of the dominant 
orientation of that cell. Doing this for all cells gives us a 
representation of the structure of the image. The HOG features 
keep the representation of an object distinct but also allow for 
some variations in shape. Fig. 1 shows an image of an FFB 
after its features have been extracted by HOG.

The SVM classifier requires at least two classes for 
training. Therefore, images of grass – on which it is assumed 
the FFBs will be on, on the ground – were also obtained from 
Kaggle. The image datasets of FFBs and grass were then 
divided into two sets: positive images and negative images. 
Positive images are those containing FFBs, and negative 
images were those without any FFBs in them.  
    The images were then resized into 96x64, converted into 
grayscale and the HOG was applied to them to extract their 
features. HOG was applied with the parameters of 9 
orientation bins, 16x16 pixels per cell and 2x2 cells per block. 
Finally, the images were divided by a ratio of 80:20 for 
training and testing respectively following [13]’s approach.

    Following the approaches in [24] and [25] where it was 
noted that data augmentation is needed to make a Faster R-
CNN model more robust, data augmentation was applied on 
the image dataset to be used for training the Faster R-CNN 
model only. The images were resized to 600x600, horizontal 
and vertical flips were applied by a factor of 0.3, random 
brightness contrast was applied by a factor of 0.1 and color 
jitter – a type of image data augmentation where the 
brightness, contrast and saturation of an image are randomly 
changed – was applied by a factor of 0.1. 
C. Training 
The device used for training and testing the models was 
equipped with an Intel Core i7-8550U Processor, and a 
Radeon RX 550 graphics card. The code for training and 
testing was implemented using Python version 3.9.6. 
The SVM model was created using the built-in function 
SVC() in the sklearn package in Python. The RBF kernel 
was used as it combines the advantages of the K-Nearest 
Neighbor 
algorithm 
while 
overcoming 
the 
space 
complexity problem. This is because the RBF Kernel only 
stores the support vectors during training rather than the 
entire dataset. 
A Cross Validation Grid Search was applied to find the 
best parameters for training the classifier. The model was 
then trained on the features extracted from the images. The 
process of reading images and extracting features took 20.5s 
while the process of training was completed in 102ms.  
The SVM model uses the concept of sliding windows and 
heatmaps to classify objects. For each of the regions of the 
window, the image classifier is applied to check if the FFB 
is detected in this region or not. After classification, there 
are overlapping detections and the false positive detections 
are spaced out. A heatmap is built to combine the 
overlapping detections and remove false positives. 
    For the deep learning models, pre-trained models were 
used with some modifications to their configuration files to 
save time of training the models from scratch and to 
accommodate the small size of the dataset. The training 
pipeline in general was as follows: 1) clone the Git 
repository containing the code of the pre-trained model, 2) 
install and import the necessary libraries such as PyTorch 
and numPy, 3) upload the custom labelled dataset in the 
correct format, 4) make the necessary changes to the 
configuration file such as the batch size, the number of 
iterations, and the number of epochs, 5) start the training 
process by passing the images through the model, 6) check 
the performance, and 7) test the models using the validation 
and testing datasets. 
    A high-resolution Faster R-CNN model with a 
MobileNetV3-Large FPN backbone was used. The training 
started with a batch size of 4 and 10 epochs and then 
gradually increased by 10 to test whether more epochs 
would provide better results. The training time was 23mins 
and 51s.  
    Pre-trained YOLOv4 weights with a model size of 
416x416 were used for the YOLOv4 model training. The 
model was trained for up to 1000 iterations. Every 100 
iterations, the mAP and average loss were observed, and the 
model weights were saved. Training stopped when there 
was no significant change in the mAP or loss values. The 
best weights were saved and used for testing. The training 
time was 3 hours 46 minutes. 
    Following the YOLOv5 documentation, the YOLOv5s 
pre-trained weights were used. The YOLOv5s model is the 
second smallest and the fastest model available making it 
best suited for mobile deployments such as usage on a real-

III. 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
A. Training Performance 
The models were tested on the testing dataset to observe 
their mAP, precision, recall and F1 Score. The data can be 
divided into four categories: true positive (TP), false 
positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN). 
The models' dependability is evaluated using the mAP, 
precision, recall, and F1-score. mAP is the average of the 
average precision (AP) over all detected classes. Precision 
is the proportion of positive predictions that are indeed 
correctly predicted positive classes. The proportion of 
correct positive class predictions made from all the correct 
positive cases in the dataset is known as recall. The F1-score 
offers a single score that simultaneously addresses precision 
and recall. The equations used to calculate mAP, Precision 
(P), Recall (R) and the F1 score are as follows:

B. Comparison of the different models 
The process of sliding windows and heat maps used by the 
SVM slows down the model during detection as it must pass 
the sliding window over each region in the image, classify it 
and build a heatmap. The model was able to successfully 
identify whether an FFB was in an image or not but when 
observing the bounding boxes drawn by the model, they were 
too loose, and the model identified multiple FFBs as one FFB 
(Fig. 2(a)). Therefore, with its low accuracy compared to the 
other models, its slow speed, and loose bounding boxes for 
detection, the SVM model would be impractical to use in a 
real-time scenario such as a harvesting robot.  
The Faster RCNN model had a high accuracy score, but a 
slow detection speed compared to the YOLO models. The 
model was able to identify FFBs in images with a confidence 
of over 80%, and the bounding boxes drawn were tight and 
covered the entire FFB in an image (Fig. 2(b)). The Faster 
RCNN model performs detection in two steps: classification 
and region proposal. This leads to high accuracy but also leads 
to a compromise on speed. On the other hand, the YOLO 
models are 1-stage-detectors which perform both steps 
simultaneously leading to faster detection rates while still 
maintaining accuracy [26].  
As real-time performance is a requirement for a harvesting 
robot, the best two models to consider would be the YOLOv4 
and the YOLOv5 models as the Faster R-CNN model does not 
meet the real-time requirement despite having a relatively 
high mAP.  
The YOLOv4 model had the highest mAP of 98.5% and a 
detection speed of 31.8 ms while the YOLOv5 model had a 
mAP of 96.4% and a detection speed of 11.4 ms which is 64% 
faster than the YOLOv4 model. The YOLOv4 model has a 
slightly higher accuracy rate than the YOLOv5 model with the 
YOLOv5 model outperforming it in terms of speed. This may 
be because the YOLOv4 model sorts out and tries all possible 
optimizations in the entire process and finds the best result in 
each permutation and combination [27]. Both models can 
identify multiple FFBs in images and draw tight bounding 
boxes over each of them with a confidence of over 90% (Fig. 
2(c) and 2(d)).

While the YOLOv4 model had the highest accuracy, 
YOLOv5 allows for rapid detection with a similar accuracy to 
YOLOv4. It has inherited the advantages of YOLOv4 while 
being more lightweight which makes it suitable for 
deployment on embedded devices to implement real-time 
detection. Additionally, it has a higher precision score than the 
YOLOV4 model meaning that the YOLOv5 model detects 
lesser false positives than the YOLOv4 model. According to 
the COCO standard, YOLOv5 is also superior to YOLOv4 in 
terms of latency [28].  
The difference between the mAP of the YOLO models is 
less than 3% and so, in this case, it would be best to use the 
YOLOv5 model for a real-time harvesting robot as it provides 
both accuracy and speed. However, to ensure the model is 
robust in a real work environment, it needs to be tested on an 
actual plantation to observe how effective it is in detecting 
FFBs in an uncontrolled environment.  
IV. 
CONCLUSION 
This paper presented a comparison of four different 
models, SVM + HOG, Faster R-CNN, Yolov4 and YOLOv5 
in the detection of oil palm Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs) in 
images. Images were collected from Google and Kaggle, the 
necessary pre-processing was applied and then training, and 
testing were performed to train the models on the image 
dataset and test their mean average precision (mAP), 
precision, recall and F1 score.  
The results showed that in terms of the mAP, the SVM + 
HOG model performed the worst with a mAP of 89% while 
the YOLOv4 model performed the best with an mAP of 
98.5%. However, in terms of speed, the YOLOv5 model 
performed the best with a detection speed of 11.4 ms making 
it the most suitable for real-time detection. While its accuracy 
score is slightly lesser than the YOLOv4 model at 96.4%, it is 
still sufficient for usage for the detection of FFBs. Therefore, 
with the requirements of both accuracy and speed in mind, the 
YOLOv5 model is considered the best option. 
In future works, the YOLOv5 model will be tested in the 
real environment of an oil palm plantation to test its reliability 
in an uncontrolled environment and whether it is suitable for 
use as a part of the vision system of a harvesting robot.
