A B S T R A C T
As a core driving force in the digital transformation of smart farming, 3D reconstruction technology has recently 
been shown to have substantial potential for agricultural environmental perception, precision operations, and 
decision-making support. This review summarizes recent advances in image-based 3D reconstruction from both 
methodological and application perspectives. Conventional methods, including voxel carving, Structure from 
Motion and Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS), and photometric stereo, are first reviewed, followed by recent de-
velopments in deep learning–based reconstruction, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), and 3D Gaussian Splatting 
(3DGS). These methods are discussed in relation to the major challenges of agricultural environments, including 
occlusion, heterogeneous illumination, and target motion. Representative applications in crop phenotyping, 
robotic perception and operation, and livestock monitoring are then analyzed, with comparison of technical 
systems in terms of accuracy, cost, and adaptability. Finally, current limitations related to real-time performance, 
robustness, and system integration are discussed, and future directions in lightweight modeling, multimodal data 
fusion, and edge deployment are outlined.
1. Introduction
As global population growth and resource constraints intensify, the 
precision and digitization of agricultural production are crucial to 
ensuring food security and sustainable development (Vinod Chandra 
et al., 2024). The application of advanced computer vision technology to 
agricultural production is gradually becoming a prominent research 
topic. Traditional agricultural monitoring methods, such as manual 
observation and 2D image analysis (Liu et al., 2018), suffer from limi-
tations such as low efficiency in data collection and analysis, a single-
dimensional perspective, and difficulty in accurately capturing the 3D 
morphological features of crops and livestock, which are crucial for 
assessing growth conditions (Xie and Yang, 2020). For example, two-
dimensional images cannot quantify the 3D structure of a crop can-
opy, and it is difficult to assess the behavioral patterns of livestock. In 
this context, image-based 3D reconstruction technology has emerged as 
a core tool in smart farming due to its non-invasive, high-precision, and
cost-effective benefits. It provides essential data for applications such as 
crop phenotyping (Zhu et al., 2020b), disease diagnosis (Wang et al., 
2024c), and body measurement (Ruchay et al., 2020), thereby pro-
moting smart farming to evolve towards dynamization and intelligence. 
3D reconstruction extracts geometric and textural information from 
data to create a 3D model of the target. According to the data acquisition 
method, 3D reconstruction techniques can be categorized into active 
and passive methods. Active 3D reconstruction methods use techniques 
such as LiDAR (Song et al., 2018) and structured light (Nguyen et al., 
2015) to scan the object and create the desired 3D model. Passive 3D 
reconstruction methods rely on visual sensors to capture image se-
quences (Yang et al., 2024a). In this review, image-based 3D recon-
struction refers to reconstruction methods that are primarily based on 
visual image observations, including both RGB image-based and RGB-D 
image-based approaches. RGB image-based methods recover three-
dimensional structure from monocular, binocular, or multi-view im-
ages using geometric constraints and matching algorithms, whereas

1.1. Scope and objective
This review paper aims to examine recent advancements in image-
based 3D reconstruction methods and their applications in smart 
farming, specifically covering crop phenotyping, robotic perception and 
operation in crop systems, and livestock monitoring applications within 
smart farming. With the continuous progress of 3D reconstruction 
technologies, this paper emphasizes their growing role in improving 
accuracy, real-time monitoring, and adaptability in agricultural envi-
ronments. By summarizing the experimental methods and application 
cases, this paper aims to provide theoretical references for the optimi-
zation and innovation of agricultural 3D reconstruction technology, 
contributing to the development of smart farming. It also highlights the 
challenges in adopting these technologies, such as the integration of 
multi-sensor data, dynamic environmental factors, and the computa-
tional challenges in deploying these methods in real-world applications. 
This review provides a structured overview of image-based 3D recon-
struction technologies in smart farming, summarizes recent methodo-
logical advances, and identifies current challenges and future research 
directions.
1.2. Analysis of related reviews
Although 3D reconstruction in agriculture has attracted considerable 
interest, existing reviews have been largely confined to specific sub-
domains or conventional geometric techniques. Specifically, Akhtar 
et al. (2024), Okura (2022), and Harandi et al. (2023) focused exclu-
sively on plant phenotyping, whereas Wang et al. (2023) concentrated 
solely on livestock. Regarding methodology, Yu et al. (2024) and Xiang 
and Wang (2023) predominantly examined explicit geometric methods, 
with limited attention given to recent AI-driven paradigms. Addition-
ally, Jurado et al. (2022) placed primary emphasis on remote sensing at 
aerial scales. Compared with these studies, the present review provides a 
more comprehensive perspective by jointly covering crop systems, 
livestock systems, and robotic perception and operation in smart 
farming, while also highlighting recent methodological advances in 
deep learning-based reconstruction, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), and 
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). A detailed comparison is presented in

2. Methodology
To provide a comprehensive overview of the advancements in image-
based 3D reconstruction for smart farming, a structured literature search 
was conducted. This section details the data sources, search strategies, 
and selection criteria used to identify the relevant studies reviewed in 
this paper.
A wide variety of research was reviewed in this study, drawing on the 
Web of Science and Scopus databases to identify peer-reviewed articles 
published between 2015 and 2025, with particular attention paid to 
high-impact articles published in the last three years (2022–2025) to 
capture the rapid emergence of deep learning-based methods. The 
search strategy was designed using Boolean logic to combine keywords 
associated with 3D reconstruction technologies and agricultural appli-
cations, including technology keywords (“3D reconstruction” OR 
“Structure from Motion” OR “Photogrammetry” OR “RGB-D” OR 
“Neural Radiance Fields” OR “3D Gaussian Splatting”) and application 
keywords (“Agriculture” OR “Phenotyping” OR “Livestock” OR 
“Greenhouse” OR “Orchard”).
The retrieved records were screened based on title, abstract, and full-
text relevance. To ensure that the focus remained on image-based

3. Methods of 3D reconstruction in smart farming
3.1. Data acquisition strategies
Agricultural 3D reconstruction is strongly influenced by how visual 
observations are acquired. In practice, image-based acquisition can be 
broadly organized into passive monocular, stereo, and multi-view con-
figurations, which differ in viewpoint coverage, depth recoverability, 
reconstruction completeness, and deployment cost. Separately, RGB-D 
systems provide an important depth-assisted alternative and are there-
fore discussed separately from purely passive image acquisition. Based 
on these acquisition characteristics, the following subsections summa-
rize the main sensing configurations used in smart-farming reconstruc-
tion tasks.
3.1.1. Passive image acquisition
Passive image acquisition is the basis of image-based 3D recon-
struction in smart farming. It can generally be classified into monocular, 
stereo, and multi-view setups, which differ in geometric constraints, 
reconstruction completeness, and deployment flexibility.
Monocular acquisition is low-cost and easy to deploy, but it usually 
suffers from scale ambiguity because depth is not directly observed. 
Stereo acquisition provides stronger depth cues through two-view ob-
servations and is more suitable for short-range reconstruction under 
controlled conditions, although it still depends on accurate calibration 
and sufficient texture. Multi-view acquisition offers richer geometric
constraints and is more favorable for high-accuracy reconstruction, but 
it requires better viewpoint coverage, greater image overlap, and higher 
computational cost.
In agricultural environments, the choice of acquisition configuration 
depends on the target and sensing conditions. Stereo and multi-view 
setups are generally more suitable for indoor or greenhouse scenarios, 
whereas orchards, open fields, and animal monitoring settings often 
present challenges such as occlusion, weak texture, wind disturbance, 
and non-rigid motion. Therefore, passive acquisition strategies should 
be selected by balancing reconstruction accuracy, robustness, and 
deployment practicality.
3.1.2. Depth-assisted and hybrid acquisition
In recent years, consumer-grade depth sensors, also known as RGB-D 
cameras, have attracted considerable attention because of their afford-
ability, high frame rates, and practical performance (Lao et al., 2023). 
An RGB-D camera simultaneously captures color imagery and depth 
information, thereby providing richer geometric cues than a conven-
tional RGB camera. In this review, RGB-D systems are treated as a depth-
assisted or hybrid acquisition strategy rather than a purely passive 
image-based method, because they combine image observations with 
explicit depth sensing.
Depth information in RGB-D systems is commonly obtained using 
technologies such as Time-of-Flight (TOF) and stereo-based depth 
sensing (Zanuttigh et al., 2016; Zollh¨ 
ofer et al., 2018). By directly 
providing depth measurements, RGB-D systems can simplify recon-
struction pipelines and improve robustness in short-range or relatively 
controlled scenarios.
In agricultural environments, RGB-D systems are particularly useful 
for crop phenotyping, precision livestock farming, and agricultural 
intelligent devices (Hu et al., 2018; Shuai et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 
2023). They are also frequently integrated into Simultaneous Localiza-
tion and Mapping (SLAM) frameworks to support robot navigation and 
perception in dynamic environments. However, their practical use may 
still be limited by sensing range, outdoor illumination, occlusion, and 
environmental noise.
3.2. Traditional geometric reconstruction methodologies
3.2.1. Multi-view reconstruction methods
Multi-view reconstruction methods recover 3D structure from im-
ages captured at multiple viewpoints. By exploiting inter-view consis-
tency, projection relationships, and geometric constraints, they 
generally provide more complete and accurate reconstructions than 
limited-view approaches. Representative classical methods include 
voxel carving and Structure-from-Motion combined with Multi-View 
Stereo (SfM-MVS).
Voxel carving iteratively removes voxels that are not photo-
consistent across visible viewpoints, resulting in a photo hull of the 
target (Kutulakos and Seitz, 1999). This method can accommodate 
scenes with arbitrary shapes and non-uniformly distributed camera 
viewpoints, and has been applied to crop phenotyping for reconstructing 
3D crop models from 2D images (Scharr et al., 2017; Tross et al., 2021; 
Feng et al., 2023). However, its performance depends strongly on image 
segmentation, viewpoint coverage, and appearance consistency. 
SfM-MVS is one of the most widely used multi-view reconstruction 
pipelines. SfM first estimates camera poses and reconstructs a sparse 
point cloud by matching feature points across overlapping images, after 
which MVS uses the recovered camera parameters to infer dense depth 
and generate a denser 3D representation (Pezzuolo et al., 2018; Meinen 
and Robinson, 2020; Chen et al., 2022). Owing to its relatively low cost 
and practical accuracy, SfM-MVS has been widely used in smart-farming 
applications. Wang et al. (2019) explored and compared three 3D data 
acquisition methods in maize plant phenotyping: 3D laser scanner, MVS 
and 3D digitization. The results show that MVS is suitable for the 3D 
reconstruction of small- and medium-sized plants, and that the image-

3.2.2. Limited-view reconstruction methods
Limited-view reconstruction methods recover three-dimensional 
structure from fewer viewpoints or more restricted observations than 
multi-view pipelines. In general, these methods are easier to deploy and 
involve lower acquisition complexity, but they also rely more strongly 
on imaging conditions, scene assumptions, and prior knowledge. 
Representative approaches in this category include binocular stereo, 
photometric stereo, and shape from shading.
Binocular stereo vision reconstructs 3D structure from two view-
points by estimating correspondences between stereo image pairs 
(Scharstein et al., 2001). Its reconstruction accuracy depends on reliable 
correspondence estimation, calibration quality, and scene texture, and 
may degrade under occlusion, repeated patterns, or reflective surfaces. 
In agricultural applications, it is mainly used in close-range scenarios 
where stable image matching can be achieved. The binocular stereo 
vision method has been successfully applied to field group crops 
reconstruction (Zou et al., 2023; Firmansyah et al., 2024), precision 
livestock farming (Shi et al., 2016) and agricultural intelligent devices 
(Vierbergen et al., 2023).
Photometric Stereo (PS) reconstructs surface geometry from images 
acquired under different lighting conditions (Woodham, 1989). By 
analyzing pixel-wise brightness variations under multiple light sources, 
it estimates surface normals and recovers local surface structure. This 
method has been used in crop phenotyping and has shown advantages in 
reconstructing detailed plant surfaces for automated identification and 
growth analysis (Smith et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018; Bernotas et al., 
2019).
Shape from Shading (SFS) is a technique for 3D reconstruction that 
utilizes changes in light and dark on the surface of an object in a single 
image to recover the relative heights or surface normal directions of 
points on the surface (Horn, 1970). Under assumptions about reflec-
tance, illumination, and surface smoothness, it estimates relative height 
or surface normal information from image intensity. In agricultural 
applications, SFS has been used for constrained object-level recon-
struction, such as single-view leaf reconstruction (Ren et al., 2021). 
Compared with multi-view methods, limited-view approaches are 
easier to deploy but more sensitive to image quality and prior assump-
tions. They are particularly useful in smart-farming scenarios that 
emphasize simple acquisition, close-range sensing, or local surface detail 
rather than full-scene completeness. However, challenges such as weak 
texture, illumination variation, occlusion, and non-rigid motion also 
highlight the need for more flexible deep learning-based methods.
3.3. Deep learning-based reconstruction methodologies
3.3.1. Non-radiance deep learning reconstruction methods
Non-radiance deep learning reconstruction methods learn the map-
ping from one or more input images to 3D structural representations 
through neural networks. Compared with traditional geometric recon-
struction pipelines, these methods rely less on manually designed cor-
respondence rules and can better exploit large-scale data, semantic 
priors, and task-specific regularization. Their outputs may include 
voxels, point clouds, meshes, depth maps, occupancy fields, or related 
geometric representations.
According to the underlying representation, these methods can be 
grouped into several categories. Voxel-based networks, such as 3D-R2N2 
(Choy et al., 2016), predict volumetric occupancy from one or multiple 
images. Point-based networks, such as PSGN (Fan et al., 2017), generate 
3D point clouds from image features. Mesh-based methods, represented 
by Pixel2Mesh (Wang et al., 2018), deform an initial mesh to recover 
surface geometry. Implicit occupancy or SDF methods, such as Occu-
pancy Networks, represent 3D structure in a continuous form. In addi-
tion, depth estimation and fusion frameworks, such as MVSNet (Yao 
et al., 2018), infer per-view depth and integrate it into a denser 3D
representation.
Recent studies have further explored supervised, self-supervised, and 
weakly supervised learning strategies, often combined with geometry-
aware constraints to improve structural plausibility and cross-view 
consistency. In agricultural applications, these methods are particu-
larly promising for targets with severe occlusion, weak texture, or strong 
morphological variation, such as plant organs, fruits, fish bodies, and 
livestock surfaces (Ubi˜ 
na et al., 2021; Hao et al., 2023). However, their 
practical performance is still constrained by training data availability, 
model generalization, and computational cost.
3.3.2. Deep learning-based monocular SLAM
Learning-based monocular SLAM extends image-based 3D recon-
struction from static offline modeling to sequential perception and on-
line mapping. By jointly estimating camera pose, scene structure, and 
depth from image streams, it is particularly relevant to agricultural ro-
bots and mobile monitoring platforms. Recent studies have shown that 
deep learning can enhance monocular SLAM through depth prediction, 
pose estimation, and geometry-aware constraints such as epipolar, 
photometric, and temporal consistency, thereby improving robustness 
in low-texture or dynamic environments (Bian et al., 2019). In smart-
farming scenarios, these methods are promising for field robots, green-
house sensing platforms, and livestock inspection systems, where map-
ping and measurement need to be performed during motion (K. Wang 
et al., 2024). However, their practical deployment remains constrained 
by scale drift, dynamic backgrounds, computational cost, and limited 
annotated agricultural data (Ding et al., 2022).
3.4. Neural scene representation and rendering methodologies
3.4.1. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) model a scene as a continuous func-
tion that maps 3D location and viewing direction to density and color. 
Using multi-view images and camera parameters as input, NeRF samples 
points along rays, predicts their density and color with an MLP, and 
synthesizes images through volumetric rendering. By minimizing 
rendering errors between synthesized and observed views, the model 
learns an implicit scene representation that supports both novel view 
synthesis and geometric reconstruction (Mildenhall et al., 2021).
The main strengths of NeRF lie in its high-fidelity continuous rep-
resentation and superior view synthesis quality. However, it is often 
computationally expensive, sensitive to acquisition quality and camera 
pose accuracy, and less robust in dynamic scenes. In agricultural ap-
plications, NeRF is promising for fine-grained reconstruction of orchard 
canopies, livestock surfaces, and indoor agricultural environments with 
complex geometry and occlusion (Jing et al., 2025; Saeed et al., 2023; 
Wang et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024). Nevertheless, wind-induced 
motion, animal movement, and real-time deployment constraints still 
pose major challenges to its broader practical use.
3.4.2. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) models a scene using a set of explicit 
3D Gaussian primitives and has recently emerged as an efficient alter-
native to radiance-field methods. Compared with NeRF, it offers a more 
favorable balance between rendering quality and computational effi-
ciency. A typical 3DGS pipeline starts from a sparse point cloud recon-
structed by SfM, which is used to initialize Gaussian primitives. The 
position, scale, rotation, color, and opacity of these Gaussians are then 
optimized through differentiable rendering, and image synthesis is 
performed by splatting the projected Gaussians onto the image plane 
(Kerbl et al., 2023).
The main strengths of 3DGS are its faster training and rendering 
speed and its suitability for high-quality visualization and detailed scene 
representation. However, it still depends on reliable initialization, suf-
ficient viewpoint coverage, and good input image quality, and its 
extension to dynamic scenes remains relatively immature. In

3.5. Summary and comparison of methods
Different 3D reconstruction methods have their own advantages and 
disadvantages in agricultural applications, with significant differences 
in data input requirements, computational complexity, accuracy, and 
applicable scenarios. Therefore, selecting the appropriate method based 
on the characteristics of the reconstruction technique and specific 
application requirements is crucial. Table 2 provides a detailed com-
parison of various methods in terms of their advantages, disadvantages, 
input requirements, and application areas.
The above methods differ substantially in terms of input re-
quirements, modeling assumptions, reconstruction accuracy, computa-
tional efficiency, deployment cost, and applicability to different 
agricultural scenarios. Traditional geometric methods are generally 
interpretable and effective under well-controlled imaging conditions, 
whereas deep learning-based methods offer greater flexibility in 
handling complex structures and incomplete observations. More recent 
neural scene representation methods, such as NeRF and 3DGS, further 
improve reconstruction fidelity and visual realism, but they also impose 
higher computational demands and stronger dependence on data 
quality.
From the perspective of application scenarios, controlled indoor, 
laboratory, and greenhouse environments are generally more suitable 
for stereo, multi-view, and RGB-D-based methods, because lighting and 
viewpoints can be more easily controlled. In contrast, outdoor fields and
orchards often involve illumination variation, occlusion, and wind-
induced motion, making robust multi-view or learning-based methods 
more advantageous. For livestock, aquatic organisms, and mobile 
sensing platforms, where non-rigid motion and continuous observation 
are common, learning-based reconstruction, monocular SLAM, and 
efficient scene representation methods may offer greater practical value. 
From the perspective of task requirements, high-accuracy static 
modeling is typically better supported by multi-view geometric pipe-
lines, NeRF, and 3DGS, whereas low-cost and rapid measurement is 
more compatible with monocular, stereo, or RGB-D configurations. 
Real-time mobile perception often benefits from learning-based 
monocular SLAM and lightweight reconstruction frameworks, while 
scenes with severe occlusion or irregular biological shapes may be better 
addressed by deep learning-based or neural rendering methods that can 
exploit stronger priors and more flexible representations.
Overall, agricultural 3D reconstruction has evolved from traditional 
geometric modeling toward learning-based approaches, from explicit 
geometric recovery toward implicit or rendering-based representations, 
and from offline processing toward more real-time, lightweight, and 
integrated deployment. This trend suggests that future smart-farming 
systems will increasingly rely on adaptive pipelines that balance 
reconstruction accuracy, computational efficiency, and scene robustness 
according to specific application needs.

4.1. Crop phenotyping
Crop phenotyping is one of the most important application domains 
of 3D reconstruction in smart farming. Compared with traditional 
manual measurement, image-based 3D reconstruction enables more 
efficient, non-destructive, and quantitative acquisition of crop structural 
traits. Existing studies mainly focus on crop reconstruction under 
different environments and spatial scales, including indoor single crop 
reconstruction, field single crop reconstruction, field group crops 
reconstruction, and organ-level reconstruction. Across these settings, 
the main objectives are to extract phenotypic traits related to plant ar-
chitecture, canopy structure, and organ morphology, while improving 
reconstruction robustness under different imaging conditions. Method-
ologically, the literature shows a gradual transition from traditional 
geometric methods, such as SfM-MVS, voxel carving, and stereo vision, 
toward learning-assisted, NeRF-based, and 3DGS-based reconstruction 
approaches. The application of 3D reconstruction techniques in crop 
phenotyping is illustrated in Fig. 2.
4.1.1. Indoor single crop reconstruction
Indoor single crop reconstruction mainly serves high-precision crop 
phenotyping under controlled imaging conditions. In this setting, 
studies generally focus on recovering individual plant architecture and 
extracting structural traits such as plant height, leaf number, leaf angle, 
and volume, while benefiting from stable lighting, simple backgrounds, 
and flexible viewpoint control (Xiao et al., 2020; Yang and Han, 2020; 
Rossi et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2023).
Existing studies in this scenario have mainly relied on SfM-MVS, 
voxel carving, RGB-D sensing, and, more recently, deep learning, 
NeRF, and 3DGS-based methods. SfM and MVS methods are widely used 
for 3D reconstruction across various crops. Zhu et al. (2020b) proposed 
an MVS-based method that combined “phenotypic fingerprinting” and a 
logistic growth model to successfully reveal the phenotypic change

further proposed a low-cost 3D reconstruction system based on SfM

an efficient solution for crop growth monitoring. Simultaneously, voxel 
carving techniques have also played an important role, particularly for 
volumetric representation of plant structures. Kunita et al. (2021) pro-
posed a visual volumetric intersection method based on voxel carving 
technique to realize 3D reconstruction and time-series scanning of
Arabidopsis strains. On this basis, Feng et al. (2023) further optimized 
the voxel carving method, proposing a probabilistic voxel carving-based 
algorithm that enables efficient reconstruction of maize plants and 
automatic extraction of leaf features.
Researchers continuously improve the accuracy and adaptability of 
3D reconstruction by optimizing system design and data acquisition 
protocols to extract crop phenotypic information more efficiently. Yang 
and Han (2020) proposed an SfM-based method for 3D reconstruction 
via smartphone video recording, enabling the measurement of pheno-
typic parameters such as height, number of leaves, leaf length, and leaf 
angle of leafy vegetable plants. Rossi et al. (2020) further proposed a 
high-throughput phenotyping platform based on SfM, which enabled the 
extraction of phenotypic parameters for maize, tomato and olive tree. 
Wu et al. (2022b) introduced MVS-Pheno V2, enabling high-throughput, 
high-precision data acquisition and automated phenotypic parameter 
extraction for low-growing plants such as wheat. Roshan et al. (2024) 
combined Agisoft PhotoScan software with optimized SfM techniques to 
achieve 3D modeling of tomato seedlings, enhancing the 3D model 
quality and measurement accuracy through optimized imaging param-
eters and camera angles. These studies indicate that, in addition to al-
gorithm design, imaging configuration and acquisition strategy are also 
important factors affecting reconstruction quality and phenotypic 
measurement accuracy. Zhao et al. (2022) proposed P3ES-Net, a deep 
learning-based 3D reconstruction method using single RGB images, 
enabling the effective estimation of crop phenotypic parameters. Yao 
et al. (2025) combined RGB-D cameras with the FPFH-ICP algorithm to 
develop a 3D reconstruction method for tomato plants, demonstrating 
high accuracy during the seedling, flowering, and fruiting stages, 
thereby offering an effective tool for tomato growth monitoring. 
Driven by deep learning, the accuracy limitations of traditional 
methods have been effectively addressed, especially in complex scenes 
with dense structures or insufficient texture. Liu et al. (2023) proposed a 
reparameterized self-supervised 3D reconstruction algorithm RepC-

collards and tussocks, Yang et al. (2024a) proposed an improved SfM-
MVS algorithm IVOP & AKAZESFM, which achieved high-precision, 
automated and integrated 3D reconstruction. Recently, NeRF-based 
reconstruction methods have gained significant attention. Saeed et al.
(2023) proposed the PeanutNeRF method for high-precision detection 
and segmentation of peanut pods, which was capable of achieving low-
noise and high-resolution 3D reconstruction on deleafed and leafed 
peanut plants. Hu et al. (2024) further proposed a high-fidelity plant 3D 
reconstruction framework based on the NeRF method, which realized 
the acquisition of multiple phenotypic data representations such as 
multi-view images, point clouds and grids from a single data acquisition, 
and dramatically improved the efficiency and quality of plant pheno-

4.1.2. Field single crop reconstruction
Field single crop reconstruction extends crop phenotyping to more 
realistic agricultural environments, where plants are imaged under 
natural and less controllable conditions (Bao et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 
2020; Marks et al., 2022). Compared with indoor reconstruction, this 
scenario places greater emphasis on robustness to illumination varia-
tion, wind-induced motion, partial occlusion, and background
complexity, while still aiming to recover key structural traits at the 
individual-plant level.
Existing studies have mainly adopted traditional geometric pipe-
lines, portable phenotyping platforms, and, more recently, learning-
assisted reconstruction methods to improve field applicability. Overall, 
these studies mainly seek to balance reconstruction accuracy, robust-
ness, and acquisition efficiency under field conditions. Sodhi et al. 
(2017) established an SfM-MVS-based framework for phytomer unit 
segmentation in sorghum plants to enable high-throughput plant phe-
notyping in the field under uncontrolled environments. Bao et al. (2019) 
developed a binocular stereo matching algorithm to overcome the lim-
itations of dense planting scenarios, enabling efficient reconstruction of 
sorghum morphological parameters. Xiang et al. (2020) proposed the 
PhenoStereo system to improve the accuracy of stem diameter mea-
surements to the millimeter level, marking a breakthrough in stereo 
vision for field micro-phenotyping analysis. Additionally, Xiao et al. 
(2020) used the SfM-MVS method for 3D modeling of field sugar beet 
and the automatic extraction of phenotypic features, providing a valu-
able tool for crop growth monitoring. For leafless fruit trees, Lavaquiol 
et al. (2021) accurately acquired a 3D model of a leafless fruit tree based 
on the SfM-MVS method, offering quantifiable morphological informa-
tion for orchard management. Fern´andez-Sarría et al. (2022) further 
applied the SfM method to accurately estimate the geometric parameters 
of walnut trees at a lower cost.
With the increasing demand for high-throughput and low-cost field 
measurements, researchers have developed portable and automated 
field phenotyping systems. Wu et al. (2020) proposed a portable and 
low-cost phenotyping platform, MVS-Pheno, to achieve high-throughput 
and high-precision phenotypic feature extraction in maize under field 
conditions. In a follow-up study, Wu et al. (2024) completed the auto-
mated extraction of 44 phenotypic features from 495 maize inbred lines 
and genetic analysis using this platform. Additionally, Gao et al. (2023) 
combined SfM with the improved QuickShift algorithm to extract the 3D 
morphology of individual maize plants from field data acquired by 
UAVs, improving the accuracy of single plant segmentation. Paturkar 
et al. (2020) developed a non-destructive and low-cost pepper growth 
monitoring system using the SfM algorithm with a smartphone camera, 
enabling the measurement of plant structural parameters under field 
conditions. In a follow-up study, Paturkar et al. (2022) proposed an SfM-
based 3D reconstruction method for field pepper growth monitoring 
using only a single mobile phone camera. These studies suggest that 
portable platforms and simplified acquisition strategies are important 
for improving the practicality of field phenotyping.

4.1.3. Field group crops reconstruction
Field group crop reconstruction focuses on canopy- or population-
level structural characterization rather than isolated individual plants.

scene scales, stronger overlap between neighboring plants, and higher 
demands on sensing efficiency, making it particularly relevant to canopy 
analysis, biomass estimation, and yield-related phenotyping (Che, 2020;

3D reconstruction method based on SfM, achieving accurate estimation 
of phenotypic parameters in crop rows. Zhu et al. (2020a) investigated 
light interception in the canopy of monocrop and intercrop crops using

the high-precision measurement of leaf length and width in wheat. Zou 
et al. (2023) proposed a 3D reconstruction method for wheat spikes by 
combining binocular stereo vision with the improved Quick-Shift clus-
tering algorithm, successfully estimating spike density with high preci-
sion, providing crucial support for crop yield prediction. Firmansyah 
et al. (2024) introduced a binocular stereo vision 3D reconstruction

4.1.4. Crop organ-level reconstruction
Crop organ-level reconstruction aims to recover fine-scale geometry 
of specific plant organs, such as roots, stems, leaves, fruits, and seeds 
(Boogaard et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2021a; Masoudi, 2024). Compared with 
whole-plant reconstruction, this task requires higher spatial resolution 
and more precise geometric recovery, because it is often used to quantify 
organ-specific traits related to morphology, growth, and yield. Accord-
ingly, existing studies have mainly focused on improving measurement 
accuracy, surface detail recovery, and trait extraction for organs with 
diverse shapes and scales.
3D reconstruction techniques based on traditional geometric 
methods have been widely applied in organ-level reconstruction, 
demonstrating excellent accuracy in measurement and adaptability for 
leaf, fruit, and root systems. Tross et al. (2021) developed an automated 
sorghum leaf angle measurement technique using the voxel carving 
method, identifying genetic loci associated with leaf angle variation. 
Gen´e-Mola et al. (2021) proposed an SfM-MVS-based 3D reconstruction 
method for apples, enabling the automatic estimation of apple size and 
visibility by evaluating four different size estimation algorithms. 
Masoudi et al. (2024) introduced a method for 3D reconstruction of 
smooth fruits, such as tomatoes, by combining SfM and granular film 
techniques, achieving accurate measurements of fruit surface area, 
volume, and chromaticity index. Hong et al. (2024) proposed a real-time 
3D reconstruction method based on multiple RGB-D cameras, enabling 
the real-time measurement and analysis of morphological features in 
oriental melon fruits. In addition, Shao et al. (2025) proposed a 3D 
reconstruction method based on SfM-MVS for extracting 3D models of 
cucumber, pepper, and tomato seeds, optimizing the parameters of the 
discrete-element model using the GA-BP-GA algorithm, which success-
fully predicted seed stacking angles with high precision. These studies 
indicate that traditional geometric methods remain effective for organ-
level reconstruction when high measurement precision is required.
For root reconstruction, Sunvittayakul et al. (2022) developed an 
MVS-based method that enabled the high-quality and rapid acquisition 
of 3D cassava root crown models, providing technical support for the 
phenotypic analysis of tuberous root crops. Wu et al. (2023) combined 
an automated rotary imaging system with a high-throughput root 3D 
reconstruction method, enabling dynamic measurement of root struc-
ture from seedling to mature stages. Xiao et al. (2021) used the SfM-MVS 
approach for automated phenotypic reconstruction of sugar beet roots 
and successfully predicted economic benefits based on phenotypic traits 
using the PSLR model. Additionally, Lu et al. (2021a) proposed a self-
supervised learning network that fused SfM and Inertial Measurement 
Unit (IMU) to enhance the completeness of plant root point cloud 
reconstruction. Xu et al. (2025a) further proposed a Vid2Curve-based 
3D reconstruction method for root systems, utilizing multi-view image 
sequences to achieve efficient and accurate 3D reconstruction and 
phenotypic measurements of the root system. These studies show that 
root reconstruction increasingly combines geometric methods with 
learning-based strategies to improve reconstruction completeness and 
throughput.

5.1. Body measurement and weight estimation
Body measurement and weight estimation are closely related appli-
cations of 3D reconstruction in livestock systems. In many studies, 3D 
reconstruction is first used to recover body shape and extract geometric 
traits, which are then further used for quantitative estimation such as 
body size measurement or weight prediction. This application direction 
therefore links geometric reconstruction with practical livestock and 
aquaculture management.
5.1.1. Body measurement
Body measurement is one of the most direct applications of 3D 
reconstruction in livestock systems. Using RGB-D cameras, SfM, binoc-
ular stereo vision, and more recently NeRF-based approaches, re-
searchers can recover animal body shape and extract morphometric 
traits such as body length, height, width, girth, and volume (Marinello 
et al., 2015; Shuai et al., 2020; Fan and Cheng, 2024). This application is 
particularly valuable because it enables non-contact and potentially 
more repeatable measurement under practical farming conditions. 
Representative studies in this direction have mainly focused on RGB-
D-based point cloud reconstruction and multi-view alignment for live-
stock body measurement. Marinello et al. (2015) proposed a 3D recon-
struction method based on RGB-D cameras using triangulation to 
achieve rapid 3D reconstruction of the body of dairy cows, enabling the 
estimation of body parameters. Subsequently, Ruchay et al. (2019, 
2020) proposed a scanning system based on three RGB-D cameras and 
the ICP algorithm through a series of studies, significantly improving the 
accuracy and stability of 3D reconstruction of live cows. Similarly, Fan 
and Cheng (2024) proposed a 3D model reconstruction method for sheep 
based on RGB-D cameras and used the ICP algorithm for accurate 
alignment to build the 3D model, enabling the estimation of body size 
parameters. Shuai et al. (2020) proposed a 3D surface reconstruction 
and body measurement method for pigs based on a multi-view RGB-D 
camera, enabling the accurate measurement of key parameters of pigs 
under free-walking conditions. With the maturity of multi-sensor fusion 
technology, Li et al. (2022a), Li et al. (2023a) constructed a real-time 
point cloud system with five RGB-D cameras, and proposed an 
attitude-adaptive PMA model to overcome the measurement error 
problem of beef cattle under dynamic attitudes. In terms of low-cost 
solutions, Yang et al. (2022) proposed an SfM-based method to recon-
struct a 3D point cloud of a cow using a smartphone, providing effective 
technical support for dairy cattle breeding and production. Pezzuolo 
et al. (2019) compared and verified the balance between Kinect v1 and 
SfM technology in terms of cost, accuracy, and applicable scenarios, 
providing a theoretical basis for technology selection. Sun et al. (2025) 
employed a tri-camera Kinect DK to capture real-time point clouds of
sheep in a passageway, enabling non-contact body measurement of 
goats in arbitrary postures. These studies show that RGB-D-based multi-
view reconstruction remains the dominant solution for livestock body 
measurement because it provides a practical balance between accuracy, 
cost, and deployment feasibility.
Recent studies have increasingly incorporated point-cloud learning 
and model-based reconstruction to improve measurement accuracy in 
more complex scenarios. Hao et al. (2023) proposed a point cloud seg-
mentation model based on an RGB-D camera and improved 
PointNet++, enabling the accurate segmentation and measurement of 
different body parts of pigs in various postures. Similarly, Wei et al. 
(2025) introduced an automated sheep body measurement method 
combining RGB-D cameras with PointNet++ point cloud segmentation, 
enabling contactless, low-cost, and precise acquisition of sheep body 
metrics. Jin et al. (2024) proposed a 3D automatic body measurement 
method using an RGB-D camera and an improved PointStack approach, 
enabling high-precision, non-contact measurement of goat body di-
mensions. Additionally, Lu et al. (2025) proposed an automatic coarse-
to-fine method based on an improved Graph Convolutional Network 
(GCN) and a 3D parametric model, measuring nine body dimensions of 
cattle on a reconstructed mesh. Zhou et al. (2025) combined the 
FGPointKAN++ pixel-level point cloud segmentation with the adaptive 
key cutting plane recognition method using a five-view Kinect DK to 
achieve body measurement of lactating dairy cows. These studies indi-
cate that body measurement is gradually evolving from geometric 
reconstruction alone toward reconstruction pipelines integrated with 
segmentation, pose adaptation, and model-based analysis.
In recent years, body measurement applications have also expanded 
toward NeRF-based reconstruction and aquatic scenarios. Jing et al. 
(2025) proposed a non-contact 3D volumetric measurement technique 
based on NeRF, efficiently and accurately acquiring cow volume data 
without additional equipment. The application scenarios are also 
expanding. Ubi˜ 
na et al. (2021) proposed a convolutional neural 
network-based underwater 3D object reconstruction method, enabling 
3D size estimation of underwater fish at high frame rates through stereo 
matching and video-interpolated CNN. Meanwhile, Gao et al. (2024) 
proposed a 3D reconstruction method based on underwater binocular 
vision, combining voxel downsampling and Poisson surface recon-
struction to generate point clouds and triangular mesh models of the fish 
body, enabling high-precision measurements of the fork length and body 
width of the fish. Overall, recent studies suggest that livestock body 
measurement is expanding from terrestrial farm animals to more diverse 
species and scenarios, while also moving toward more automated and 
non-contact reconstruction frameworks.

5.1.2 wt. estimation
Weight estimation is closely related to 3D body reconstruction 
because reconstructed geometry and derived morphometric traits can 
serve as informative predictors of body weight. Existing studies have 
applied RGB-D point cloud reconstruction, binocular stereo vision, SfM, 
and, more recently, NeRF-assisted reconstruction to achieve non-contact 
weight prediction in livestock and aquaculture systems.
Representative studies in this area have mainly focused on 
combining reconstructed body geometry with statistical or learning-
based estimation models. Shi et al. (2016) constructed a binocular ste-
reo vision system based on LabVIEW to achieve automatic and accurate 
estimation of pig weight under indoor farm conditions, preliminarily 
verifying the feasibility of computer vision in livestock weight estima-
tion. Subsequently, Pezzuolo et al. (2018) proposed a method based on 
SfM photogrammetry, enabling automatic estimation of camera pa-
rameters and 3D reconstruction without the need for pre-calibration, 
significantly improving the deployment efficiency of the system. With 
the maturity of depth sensing technology, Kamchen et al. (2021) suc-
cessfully estimated the body weight and morphometric parameters of 
Nellore heifers using the Intel RealSense D435i depth sensor in combi-
nation with image analysis techniques, verifying the potential of depth 
sensors for high-accuracy body weight estimation. Based on this, Kwon 
et al. (2023) further proposed a real-time pig weight estimation method 
based on an RGB-D camera and deep learning, which quickly recon-
structed a mesh model from point cloud data and utilized deep neural 
networks for weight prediction, enabling efficient, automated estima-
tion. Nguyen et al. (2023), on the other hand, explored the application of 
a handheld RGB-D camera for rapid pig weight estimation by extracting 
potential features from 3D point cloud data through a deep learning 
model and combining them with a regression model to improve the
accuracy of body weight prediction, thus making the application of 
portable devices in animal husbandry possible. In terms of 3D recon-
struction algorithm optimization, to address the point cloud noise 
problem, Kwon and Mun (2022) proposed an iterative offset-based mesh 
model reconstruction method, achieving accurate reconstruction of the 
3D shape of a pig in the presence of a large number of noisy or missing 
data points. Xu et al. (2025b) proposed a stepwise hole filling method 
based on RGB-D cameras. After obtaining a watertight model, the vol-
ume was estimated to calculate the weight, which was applied in multi-
view Kinect DK data acquisition for pasture fence obstructions. These 
studies indicate that improving reconstruction completeness and 
reducing point cloud noise are important for enhancing the reliability of 
downstream weight estimation.
Recent studies have further extended this direction to more complex 
dynamic scenarios, especially in aquaculture systems. Notably, Wang 
et al. (2024a) proposed a combined NeRF-supervised stereo matching 
and 3D reconstruction method to achieve accurate weight estimation of 
underwater, free-swimming fish. This study not only improves depth 
estimation and 3D reconstruction accuracy in complex dynamic scenes 
but also provides a new paradigm for non-contact weight estimation. 
Building upon this foundation, Wang et al. (2025a) proposed a light-
weight EViT-CARAFE-SimAM instance segmentation and stereo match-
ing framework. This work innovatively introduced a “surface area-body 
weight” three-dimensional correlation, enabling the precise calculation 
of the body weight of freely swimming bass underwater. These studies 
suggest that weight estimation is gradually expanding from relatively 
controlled livestock scenarios toward more dynamic and visually chal-
lenging environments.

5.2. Body condition assessment and health evaluation
Beyond geometric measurement and weight estimation, 3D recon-
struction has also been increasingly used for body condition assessment 
and related health evaluation (Xiao et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). In 
this context, the goal is not only to recover body shape, but also to infer 
nutritional, physiological, or welfare-related conditions from recon-
structed morphology. Compared with traditional visual assessment, 3D 
reconstruction offers a more quantitative and potentially more objective 
basis for animal condition monitoring.
Existing studies have mainly focused on livestock body condition 
scoring based on RGB-D-derived surface geometry. Maki et al. (2018) 
pioneered the use of multiple Kinect sensors to simultaneously capture 
depth maps of cows from different viewpoints and reconstruct their 3D 
models for estimating body condition scores in cows, validating the 
feasibility of 3D vision in cow body condition assessment. On this basis, 
Salau et al. (2017) further proposed a scanning system based on an RGB-
D camera, enabling high-precision measurements of body features such 
as teat length and sciatic node height in the cow's resting and walking 
states, providing richer morphological parameters for automated 
assessment of cow body condition. Subsequently, Zhang et al. (2023) 
proposed a 3D shape analysis method based on an RGB-D camera, 
quantitatively analyzing the body condition of livestock of different 
species and enabling cross-species body condition score assessment, 
further expanding the scope of application of the technique. These 
studies indicate that RGB-D-based surface reconstruction provides an 
effective basis for quantitative body condition assessment, especially 
when body-shape differences can be clearly captured.
In addition to livestock condition scoring, 3D reconstruction has also 
been used in poultry health monitoring. Aydin (2017) proposed a 
monitoring method based on RGB-D cameras, enabling an objective 
assessment of the activity level and lameness of broilers by automati-
cally detecting the number of laying events and laying latency, verifying 
the potential of 3D vision in poultry behavior monitoring. With the 
growing demand for real-time monitoring, Xiao et al. (2019) developed 
a 3D reconstruction system for caged chickens based on a binocular 
vision system, capable of real-time monitoring of chickens' health and 
accurately identifying changes in feeding and drinking behaviors, thus 
improving the level of fine management in poultry farming. On this 
basis, Zhang et al. (2021) further integrated binocular stereo vision and 
thermal imaging data, utilizing PMS algorithms to fuse multimodal in-
formation and generate a more complete point cloud of the chicken
body, providing solid technical support for accurate health management 
in smart farming. These studies suggest that 3D reconstruction is grad-
ually extending from body condition scoring to broader health- and 
behavior-related monitoring tasks.

Overall, 3D reconstruction provides a promising basis for more 
quantitative and objective body condition assessment and health eval-
uation in livestock systems. However, practical performance still de-
pends on stable body-shape recovery, robust handling of posture 
variation and surface coverage, and the reliability of reference labels 
used for supervision or evaluation.
6. Challenges and future trends
In recent years, 3D reconstruction has been increasingly applied in 
agricultural scenarios and has shown considerable potential in crop 
phenotyping, robotic perception and operation, and livestock moni-
toring. However, due to the complexity of real-world agricultural en-
vironments and the unique characteristics of biological entities, current 
3D reconstruction techniques still face significant challenges in agri-
cultural applications.
1) Technology Adaptability: Most existing 3D reconstruction 
methods have been developed and validated under relatively controlled 
conditions. Their data collection processes are time-consuming and 
highly dependent on lighting conditions and fixed camera configura-
tions. Agricultural settings, however, are characterized by high dyna-
mism and complexity. Issues such as uneven outdoor lighting and severe 
occlusions significantly reduce model accuracy, making it difficult to 
address real-world challenges like wind-eroded crops or moving live-
stock. The fusion of multi-sensor data involves high computational 
complexity and low fault tolerance, limiting its practical application. 
Future research should therefore focus on improving robustness in dy-
namic environments, for example through lightweight learning-based 
models, temporal consistency constraints, and more efficient scene 
representation methods such as NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2021) and 
3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023). Such advances may help extend agricultural 
3D reconstruction from static scene recovery toward more adaptive and 
dynamic perception.
2) Economic Feasibility: Although multi-camera RGB-D systems and 
UAV-based platforms have demonstrated good performance in experi-
mental studies, their deployment at commercial farm scale remains 
costly.

3) Cross-Domain Collaboration: The advancement of smart farming 
requires close integration among agriculture, computer vision, robotics, 
sensing, and data science. However, practical collaboration across fields 
like agricultural technology, computer science, robotics, and data sci-
ence faces significant challenges. First, knowledge barriers and technical 
coordination difficulties between disciplines often lead to inefficient 
collaboration. For instance, the fragmented standards of diverse agri-
cultural data (e.g., meteorological, remote sensing, sensor data) hinder 
direct compatibility and effective integration. Second, the seasonal and 
dynamic nature of agricultural scenarios demands rapid iteration and 
adaptation, yet technology development and validation cycles typically 
span months for adjustments and optimization. This scenario of “high 
coordination costs and low integration efficiency” hinders the rapid 
construction and widespread adoption of smart farming ecosystems. 
Future research should therefore strengthen cross-domain collaboration 
by promoting standardized agricultural data formats, improving tools 
for multi-source data conversion and fusion, and building more inte-
grated evaluation frameworks for real-world deployment. With 
continued advances in sensors, learning algorithms, and 3D recon-
struction methods, such efforts are expected to improve the practicality, 
scalability, and long-term impact of smart farming systems.
7. Conclusion
This review systematically summarizes recent advances in image-
based 3D reconstruction for smart farming from both methodological 
and application-oriented perspectives. Methodologically, the field has 
progressed from conventional geometric pipelines to deep lear-
ning–based and neural representation–based frameworks. Classical 
methods, including Structure from Motion and Multi-View Stereo (SfM-
MVS), photometric stereo, and voxel-based reconstruction, remain 
important for agricultural structure recovery under controlled or 
moderately complex conditions. More recently, learning-based methods, 
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) have 
improved reconstruction fidelity, robustness, and scene representation 
capability in complex agricultural environments. At the same time, ad-
vances in image acquisition, feature extraction, and matching strategies 
have enhanced the reconstruction of weakly textured organs, occluded 
structures, and other challenging targets.
From the application perspective, image-based 3D reconstruction 
has shown broad utility across crop phenotyping, robotic perception and 
operation, and livestock monitoring. In crop systems, it supports multi-
scale analysis ranging from individual organs to whole plants, canopy 
architecture, and field-level structures. In robotic perception and oper-
ation, 3D scene understanding contributes to target localization, navi-
gation, and precision harvesting in complex environments. In livestock 
systems, image-based reconstruction has enabled non-contact and dy-
namic assessment of body size, weight-related traits, and body condition 
through multi-view imaging, RGB-D sensing, and learning-based 
models. These developments highlight the value of 3D reconstruction 
as a core enabling technology for precise and automated agricultural 
management.
Despite this progress, several challenges still limit large-scale 
deployment in real agricultural settings. Reconstruction performance 
remains sensitive to illumination variation, occlusion, background
complexity, and target motion. In addition, the computational cost and 
hardware requirements of advanced frameworks may constrain adop-
tion, especially in resource-limited farming scenarios. Another impor-
tant limitation is the insufficient integration of reconstruction methods 
with agronomic knowledge, robotic platforms, and downstream 
decision-making systems. Future research should therefore focus on 
improving robustness and generalization in dynamic environments, 
developing efficient and low-cost acquisition–reconstruction pipelines, 
and strengthening multimodal data integration for comprehensive 
agricultural scene understanding. Continued progress in these directions 
will support the more intelligent, precise, and sustainable development 
of smart farming.
