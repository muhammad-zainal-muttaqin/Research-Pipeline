A B S T R A C T  
Accurate detection of both immature and mature apples in orchard environments is essential for early crop load 
management. A near real-time method is proposed in this study for detecting green (early-stage), green–red- 
mixed (mid-stage; red varieties), or red apples (harvest-stage; red varieties). Both the number of fruits and fruit 
size were estimated for the entire tree with a single image captured by a low-cost smartphone using two different 
imaging methods (oblique and panorama modes). An attention mechanism module called the convolutional 
block attention module (CBAM) was added to the generic YOLOv4 detector to improve the detection accuracy by 
only focusing on the target canopies. Furthermore, an adaptive layer and larger-scale feature map were included 
in the modified network structure, enabling it to adapt to various characteristics of fruits and canopies during the 
entire growing season, such as different fruit colors and sizes, dense-foliage conditions, and severe occlusions. To 
verify the effectiveness of the proposed method, we compared our improved model, canopy-attention-YOLOv4 
(or CA-YOLOv4), with other commonly adopted models available in the literature, such as the original 
YOLOv4, Faster R-CNN, and single-shot multibox detector (SSD). Two commonly planted apple varieties, “Envy” 
and “Scifresh”, were used in the study. The results showed that the proposed CA-YOLOv4 detector performed the 
best among all the algorithms, with up to ~3% improvement in terms of fruit counting over the original YOLOv4. 
With the “Envy” variety, fruit detection accuracies were 86.2%, 87.5%, and 92.6% for the early-, mid-, and 
harvest stages, respectively, whereas the same were 71.0%, 83.6%, and 86.3% for the “Scifresh” variety, which 
has denser canopy foliage. Both imaging methods proposed in this study only needed one/single shot targeting 
the entire fruiting tree, which can be highly efficient for real-world applications in crop load management. 
Finally, CA-YOLOv4 estimated fruit sizes were compared to manual measurements, achieving up to R2 values of 
0.68 in fruit height and 0.66 in fruit width estimations.   
1. Introduction 
Apple fruit production is an essential part of tree fruit production 
worldwide. The United States produced approximately 10.3 billion 
pounds of apples in 2020, where Washington State alone contributed 
more than 67% of the entire national production (USDA, 2020). For 
most tree fruit crops, crop load estimation is one of the most critical 
procedures that allows for timely orchard and canopy management by 
growers, such as green fruit thinning and orchard yield forecasting, 
leading to more flexible market strategies. It also helps with the 
arrangement of agricultural workers and orchard equipment manage­
ment (Koirala et al., 2021). Both fruit number and fruit size need to be 
estimated to ensure an accurate crop load estimation. Knowing such 
information will help growers balance the allocation of tree nutrition to 
produce high-quality fruits (e.g., more fruits of smaller size and fewer 
fruits of larger size) that are preferred by the market (M´esz´aros et al., 
2021). However, manual counting and size measurement can be costly, 
time-consuming, and error prone. With the rapid advancement in arti­
ficial intelligence (AI) tools and computational technology, AI-driven 
and sensor-based (e.g., color imaging) field operations have become 
crucial agricultural components (Tyagi, 2016). The adoption of AI- 
enabled processing pipelines has shown promise in solving several as­
pects of farming problems to improve orchard profitability, including 
monitoring crop status, pest and disease control, and precision canopy

reliably monitoring crop conditions and estimating crop load (Dorj et al.,

Efficient and accurate fruit detection or recognition in an actual or­
chard environment is one of the most crucial steps for accurate crop load 
estimation in tree fruit crops. Early approaches for fruit detection were 
based on simple visual features, including color-based techniques, such 
as color thresholding (Gongal et al., 2015); shape-based techniques, 
such as the circular Hough transform (CHT) (Bargoti et al., 2017); and 
some local texture features (Sengupta and Lee, 2014), to distinguish the 
fruit from its background. Although these efforts can often achieve fast 
computing performances, they suffer from low accuracy under chal­
lenging conditions, such as crop variability, multi-object detection, 
illumination changes, and occlusion problems (Lin et al., 2019; Vasco­
nez et al., 2020). Linker et al. (2012) proposed a method for detecting 
apples under natural lighting, where both color and smoothness features 
were used to detect a set of pixels with high probability that belongs to 
apples to form a “seed area”. The algorithm then determined whether 
that specific region contains an apple based on the coincidence ratio 
between the “seed area” and an apple model previously developed. The 
results showed that this method can effectively detect a region that 
contains an individual apple. However, it caused considerable errors 
when the fruits were only partially visible due to dense foliage and/or 
fruit clusters. Lu and Sang (2015) similarly used the color and contour 
information-based method for citrus fruit recognition under varying 
illumination conditions in the field. Their method can adapt to the 
natural environment with complex illumination and background, but 
the detection performance is undesired when citrus is heavily occluded 
in the acquired images. 
In recent years, machine learning technologies, including deep 
learning, have been widely investigated and applied in various in­
dustries, such as autonomous driving (Bersani et al., 2021) and face 
recognition for online payment (Liu & Tu, 2021) and well agriculture 
(Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018). Compared to traditional ma­
chine vision techniques, deep learning-based image processing is 
directly driven by data. Its self-learning characteristic is used to express 
relationships between the input and output that can solve a series of 
problems, which are usually challenging to solve using traditional 
methods (Nasiri et al., 2019). In the agricultural domain, among various 
other applications, numerous efforts have been made for real-time or 
near real-time fruit recognition using various deep learning algorithms 
(Koirala et al., 2019a; Zhuang et al., 2018). 
Among others, the convolutional neural network (CNN) framework, 
represented by Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2015) and YOLO (or you only 
look once) (Redmon et al., 2016), is undoubtedly the most widely used 
for object detection tasks with different encoding and/or decoding 
backbones. Overall, Faster R-CNN provided satisfactory performances in 
detecting a single object and multiple objects simultaneously (Bargoti 
and Underwood, 2017a; 2017b; Inkyu et al., 2016). However, Faster R- 
CNN consists of two parts: region proposal networks (RPNs) and clas­
sification networks. Thus, the detection speed is comparatively slow, 
which may cause problems when dealing with high-resolution images in 
real-time tasks (Tian et al., 2019). The YOLO framework, in contrast, 
does not require RPN, and it directly performs regression to detect the 
objects in the target image, which generally completes the detection 
tasks faster. In addition to the original YOLO framework, Koirala et al. 
(2019b) presented a modified framework, MangoYOLO, to identify 
mango fruit in an orchard environment. They considered the impact of 
changing lighting conditions in a complex orchard environment and 
achieved up to 98% detection accuracy. Furthermore, Xiong et al. 
(2020) proposed the Des-YOLOv3 network, which is also modified from 
the original YOLOv3 network for detecting mature citrus fruit at night 
with an average accuracy of 90.75% and a detection speed of 53 frames 
per second (fps). 
One of the greatest challenges in fruit recognition is caused by oc­
clusions from neighboring objects, such as branches, leaves, and fruit 
clusters. To improve the detection efficacy under this situation, Kang 
and Chen (2019) used a Kinect V2 to detect and segment apple fruits and 
branches in an orchard environment in real-time. They developed a 
lightweight backbone network model, lw-net, based on the DaSNet 
network architecture. The results indicated that the detection accuracies 
were 86.5% and 75.7% for apples and branches, respectively. Gao et al. 
(2020) proposed an improved Faster R-CNN multiclass fruit detection 
method to detect apples under four different canopy occlusion condi­
tions. The average accuracy was approximately 0.88, and the average 
processing time was approximately 0.24 sec per image. In addition, 
Chen et al. (2020) developed an advanced YOLOv4 model (Bochkovskiy 
et al., 2020) for citrus detection. The average detection accuracy of 
citrus fruits at different growth stages in the orchard environment was 
~96% by adding a feature detection layer and combining the residual 
network structure with the densely connected network. Suo et al. (2021) 
proposed a method for avoiding detecting kiwifruit hidden by branches/ 
wires to obtain only the accessible target. In their work, the generic 
YOLOv4 was also employed to classify the fruits into different types 
according to the robot’s picking strategy and canopy obstruction, which 
can potentially help to avoid damage to the hardware of the robot. 
However, none of them evaluated the performances of their models in 
terms of counting the number of fruit and fruit size estimation over 
entire tree canopies. 
The object detection algorithms worked well for scenarios with 
distinctive features and simple backgrounds. In the orchard environ­
ment, the image background can be very complex with changing sun­
light direction and intensity. Varying the size of the target and fruit 
occlusion also cause substantial difficulties in identifying those fruits.

Current object detection studies in tree fruit crops have achieved limited 
success in early crop load estimation tasks. For example, (i) Zhao and 
Chen (2021) found that fruits can generally be detected more accurately 
during the late-growth stage (mature fruit) than during the early growth 
stage (immature fruit) using the same model, and (ii) fruit detection and 
fruit size measurement have not been estimated simultaneously using 
the same model (Costa et al., 2021). Considering these challenges, deep- 
learning-based methods can provide a good balance between accuracy 
and computational speed. Therefore, we propose an improved YOLOv4 
detector (i.e., CA-YOLOv4) in this work for near real-time detection of 
apple fruits in various growth stages and for estimating fruit size in 
orchard environments. The major goal of this study was to investigate 
the potential of early crop load estimation in apple orchards based on 
both fruit count and fruit size. The specific objectives of this study were 
1) to improve the original YOLOv4 network architecture with spe­
cific attention to the canopy of apple trees (called CA-YOLOv4); 
2) to compare the performance of CA-YOLOv4 with other commonly 
used deep neural networks, including Faster R-CNN, single-shot multi­
box detector (SSD), and original YOLOv4; and 
3) to compare the performance of two different imaging methods 
(the panorama and the oblique modes) in terms of fruit counting and 
fruit size estimation over three growth stages. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Test orchards 
Simple, narrow, accessible, and productive (SNAP) fruit tree archi­
tecture is being widely adopted for fresh market apples in the Northwest 
Pacific (NWP) region in the United States. In this study, two different 
commercial apple orchards were selected to conduct the field trials, 
including a V-trellis-trained “Envy” (Fig. 1a) and a vertical-trellis- 
trained “Scifresh” (Fig. 1b) near Prosser, WA, USA. The primary 
branches of the fruit trees in both orchards were trained to seven hori­
zontal tiers with a spacing of ~0.5 m between trellis wires. Primary 
branches in the vertical trunks were fastened to the horizontal tiers by 
tapes. The vertical tree trunks were allowed to grow up to ~4 m on 
average. The row spacing and tree spacing were 3.5 m and 1.4 m, 
respectively, for “Envy” and 2.7 m and 1.5 m, for “Scifresh”. The canopy 
depth was approximately 0.3 m for “Envy” (single side of the V-canopy) 
and 0.5 m for “Scifresh” (canopy). The specific ranges of fruit number 
per branch were 1–26 for “Envy” and 1–42 for “Scifresh” (Zhang et al., 
2020). Due to the specific planting pattern of the trees, canopy layout, 
and rootstocks used, both orchards presented dense-foliage canopy 
conditions. Such high-density foliage conditions present more chal­
lenges to machine vision systems, as objects of interest (e.g., fruit and 
branches) are heavily occluded. 
Specifically, for “Envy” and “Scifresh” apples in Washington State, 
we collected imagery data from August 13 (early stage with green fruits 
only; Fig. 2a) through the growth period (midseason when only a 
portion of fruits were matured with both green and red fruits; Fig. 2b) to 
October 7 (commercial harvest season with red fruits only; Fig. 2c) in 
2019. Therefore, our imagery dataset covered large variations in fruit 
conditions from early-, middle-, to late-season (or harvest-stage) sce­
narios. When the fruits were still green, it was challenging to detect 
them, as the fruits have colors similar to the canopy background 
(leaves). However, fruit detection in the early stage can help growers 
estimate the crop load in a timely manner to adjust market strategies. 
During the data collection process, we consistently used two white bars 
to separate the target tree from others. This physical barrier was useful 
to collect accurate ground-truth data and to compare the automatically 
estimated results against the ground-truth data within the same target 
region. For “vertical” tree architecture (“Scifresh”), we manually coun­
ted every fruit on individual trees (both frontside and backside), while 
for “V” tree architecture (“Envy”), we counted every fruit on one of the 
V-sides of individual trees.

smartphone (Galaxy S9, Samsung Electronics, South Korea). The 
smartphone contains a 12-megapixel rear camera with an image pixel 
resolution of 5312 × 2988. Conventionally, the whole tree was divided 
into three parts (i.e., upper, middle, and lower) to collect the image data

central line, close to the tree trunk, to maintain good image quality. It

image distortion, which can negatively affect the fruit size estimation in 
this study. Such influences induced by image distortion are fully dis­

ages in oblique mode, the smartphone was held still to capture the image 
at a certain angle (Fig. 3b). This mode was adopted because the orchard 
row spacing was not sufficient to capture the entire tree height in a 
single frame using the front view. With such imaging methods, the in­
formation of entire trees could be captured as a single image, thus 
avoiding explicit image stitching. For panorama mode, the distance of 
the smartphone and the target canopy was approximately 2.7–3.5 m 
(close to the row spacing of each orchard); for oblique mode, the dis­
tance was approximately 3.1–3.9 m. We collected 480 raw apple tree 
images with each imaging method during the three growth stages dis­
cussed above (i.e., early, mid, and harvest stages). Table 1 summarizes 
the datasets acquired in each of the oblique and panorama modes for all 
growth stages. For “Envy” trees, only the single side of the V-canopy was 
targeted for image data acquisition. 
2.3. “Debar” algorithm development 
To compare the detection results achieved with the selected net­
works against the ground-truth data (manually estimated/measured 
fruit count and fruit size in the field), we used two white bars to separate 
the target tree regions from the rest of the tree canopies (i.e., one tree at 
a time). The white bars were only adopted to assist in evaluating the 
performance of the fruit detection models by indicating the start/end 
points of individual trees. Once our proposed CA-YOLOv4 was verified 
to achieve a certain level of accuracy and precision, white bars are no 
longer needed in the actual orchard environment, as growers can target 
the desired canopies by holding the smartphone at different angles. 
When the ground-truth data were collected, only the fruit between the 
two bars was counted. Therefore, it was essential to develop an algo­
rithm to automatically recognize the position of the white bars in the 
images and remove the area outside the designated detection area – the 
entire process was called “debar”. The detailed flowchart of the “debar” 
algorithm is shown in Fig. 4. Major steps of the “debar” algorithm 
include 1) using hue-saturation-value (HSV) color space, 2) using the 
Otsu method (Qi et al., 2019) to adaptively determine the segmentation 
threshold of each H, S, and V component and perform segmentation, 3) 
performing probabilistic Hough line detection (Matas et al., 2000), 4) 
using convex hull algorithm for convex point detection (Song et al., 
2013), 5) adopting mean clustering (Vora and Oza, 2013) to determine 
the number of white bars, and 6) performing the area selection and 
morphological dilation. Furthermore, the “debar” process is visualized 
in Fig. 5, where Fig. 5a-5b shows examples of original input images 
using the two imaging modes, Fig. 5c-5d shows the corresponding inter- 
product rendering, and Fig. 5e-5f shows the final product rendering.

There are three major improvements from the original YOLOv4 to 
CA-YOLOv4, as discussed below: 
i) The convolutional block attention module (CBAM) attention 
mechanism (Woo et al., 2018) was introduced to the original YOLOv4 
network. There are diverse apple fruit characteristics in the dataset, such 
as different shapes, sizes, colors, and occlusion conditions under 
changing lighting situations in various growth stages. Therefore, the 
CBAM attention mechanism (canopy-attention or CA in this work) can 
learn target features well and suppress nontarget features to improve 
detection accuracy; 
ii) An adaptive layer and a larger-scale feature map were added. The 
adaptive layer retains the target features obtained by the CBAM and 
adapts the feature map to the dimensions of the output layer of different 
scale features. The original three-scale detection is extended to four- 
scale detection in the scale detection module at the same time; and 
iii) The densely connected network structure was integrated, and the 
regression box loss function was optimized. Considering the differences 
in apple characteristics, the integration of a dense connection structure 
in the network can combine the characteristics of the deep network with 
the shallow network for accurate apple detection. Furthermore, the 
optimization of the regression prediction box function can further 
improve the apple information predicted by the network. 
2.4.1.1. Convolutional block attention module (CBAM). The attention 
mechanism is a type of resource allocation mechanism that redistributes 
originally evenly distributed resources according to the importance of 
the attention object. It is widely used in natural language processing 
(NLP), image classification, and speech processing tasks (Takalkar et al., 
2021). On this basis, we introduced the CBAM (Woo et al., 2018) into the 
YOLOv4 detector, which was expected to make the network focus more 
on the tree canopy instead of the whole image. Specifically, CBAM is an 
attention mechanism module combining the channel and space infor­
mation. The channel attention module first compresses the feature map 
in the spatial dimension and then performs the operations after 
obtaining a one-dimensional vector. Once processed by two pooling 
functions (average and maximum pooling), a total of two one- 
dimensional vectors can be obtained. Global average pooling has feed­
back for every pixel on the feature map, while gradient feedback exists 
only in places where the response is largest in the feature map when 
global maximum pooling is used for gradient backpropagation calcula­
tions. Equation (1) was used to calculate the channel feature map as

tion over union between the predicted box and the ground-truth box, ρ 
represents the Euclidean distance, and c represents the diagonal length 
of the minimum bounding box between the predicted and ground-truth 
boxes.

2.5. Fruit size estimation 
Once an apple in the image was successfully detected by the network, 
a bounding box at the position of the apple was generated using the 
smallest bounding rectangle containing the apple fruit (Fig. 9a). We 
segmented each detected bounding box out as a new sub-image ensuring 
that the number of sub-image pixels was the same as reflected in the 
original image (Fig. 9b-9c). The pixel value and number of sub-images 
were then recorded. Finally, the Otsu-based method (Otsu, 1979) was 
used to perform the best global threshold processing on the sub-images 
to remove the background information, such as occluded fruits and 
leaves (Fig. 9d-9e). 
To best estimate the apple fruit size, we placed a red artificial apple 
(width of 90 mm and height of 80 mm) as the reference in the middle of 
the target area during the data collection stage. It is worth mentioning 
that because of the relatively small size of the reference apple compared 
to the entire canopy, we assumed that its exact location would not 
impact the fruit size estimation process. The reference fruit was also 
labeled separately during the image annotation process. To further 
compare with the ground-truth data, 30 trees in the early and harvest 
stages were randomly selected for manual measurement (with a Vernier 
caliper) of actual fruit size. The range of height and width of the sampled 
apples were 39.3–54.0 mm and 40.8–54.7 mm, respectively, in the early 
growth stage and 61.0–86.2 mm and 64.9–86.9 mm, respectively, in the 
late-growth stage. Estimating the size of the fruit (Heightest(Hest) and 
Widthest(West) in mm) required knowing the fruit height and width in 
pixels (px) in the images (Hpx and Wpx) and the reference fruit’s actual 
height and width in mm (Href and Wref) and pixels (px) in the images 
(Href px and Wref px). Equations (13–14) can be used for the calculation:

square (R2), Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r), and root mean square 
error (RMSE) were used to compare the correlations between estimated

The four networks (i.e., Faster R-CNN, SSD, original YOLOv4, and

The training results showed that the proposed CA-YOLOv4 achieved the

3.1.2. Ablation experiment 
The above result showed that the proposed CA-YOLOv4 achieved the 
most accurate results in detecting apples over different growth stages. As 
mentioned earlier, we made three major improvements to the original 
YOLOv4, including some structural changes to the network (e.g., adding 
a convolutional block attention module (CBAM), adaptive layer, and 
larger-scale feature map; described in subsection of 2.4.1.1 and 2.4.1.2) 
and loss function improvements (e.g., regression box loss; described in 
subsection of 2.4.1.3). Therefore, we further conducted an ablation 
experiment to confirm which modification contributed the most to the 
proposed CA-YOLOv4 network, enabling better detection capability in 
this work. Table 5 shows the results of the ablation experiment, where 
the “loss function of YOLOv4” refers to only the loss function of YOLOv4 
being modified. Similarly, the network structure of YOLOv4 refers to the 
situation when only the network structural changes were made on top of 
the original YOLOv4. Clearly, although both modifications contributed 
to the improved performance of CA-YOLOv4, the structural change 
made more substantial improvements to the network, with better pre­
cision (92.37%), recall (89.22%), and F1-score (90.86%), compared to 
the network performance when only the loss function was improved (the 
numbers were 91.52%, 87.49%, and 89.59, respectively, for the same). 
Due to the nature of structural modification, the model size was slightly 
larger (292 megabytes or MB) than the original YOLOv4 (250 MB). As 
expected, CA-YOLOv4 performed the best in this ablation experiment, as 
it combined both structural and loss function modifications. Due to the 
superiority of our improved CA-YOLOv4 network, the original YOLOv4 
network was not further compared in the following content. 
3.2. Fruit detection at three different growth stages with the CA-YOLOv4 
model 
Four trained networks were tested using the test dataset, which 
showed that, as expected, the CA-YOLOv4 network performed the best 
in detecting apples (Table 6) at different growth stages with both “Envy” 
(Fig. 10) and “Scifresh” (Fig. 11) apple cultivars. For both cultivars, 
apple fruits were relatively small and heavily occluded by the sur­
rounding objects, such as leaves and shoots, during the early growth 
stage (Fig. 10a-10c and Fig. 11a-11c). In addition, apples were mostly 
green at this stage, which presented color features similar to those of the 
background in the canopy (mainly leaves). Therefore, the number of 
apples labeled in the acquired images (62–94 apples) during the anno­
tation process was less than the actual number of apples counted in the 
field (78–124 apples; Table 6). As shown in Table 5, the precision and 
recall of the three detection networks were all relatively lower in the 
early stage, demonstrating the challenges in early crop load estimation 
in tree fruit crops. Among all three networks, the proposed CA-YOLOv4 
achieved the highest detection accuracy of 86.17% (up to 85 apples 
detected) compared to the ground-truth fruit count. 
During the growth stage with mixed-color fruit (red and green), 
apples grew larger and were less occluded by the leaves. However, 
because fruits only partially turned red, the mixed-color fruit still pre­
sented major challenges in detecting them with neural networks. 
Comparatively, the number of apples detected by the networks 
increased, reaching as high as 87.5% using the CA-YOLOv4 model. Only 
82.29%-82.97% of fruit were successfully detected using Faster R-CNN 
and SSD models (Fig. 10d-10f and Fig. 11d-11f).

3.3. Effect of imaging methods on model performance 
Furthermore, we compared the detection results achieved by the CA- 
YOLOv4 network with two different imaging methods: oblique 
(Fig. 14a) and panorama (Fig. 14b) imaging modes. Table 7 shows that a 
better detection result was achieved with the oblique imaging method 
compared to the panorama imaging method, most likely due to the 
image distortion caused by panorama mode. True positive detection was 
2.52% higher with the oblique imaging method, whereas false positive 
detection was 3.28% lower and true negative detection was 2.52% lower 
than the same achieved with panorama imaging. Although the front 
view (i.e., camera horizontally facing the fruiting wall) might be the 
optimal approach to accurately count the apples in an entire tree using a

3.4. Fruit size estimation 
After detection, fruit size (height and width) was postprocessed using 
the results of CA-YOLOv4. Fig. 15 shows the calibration lines of fruit size 
estimated by the model and measured in the field as the ground-truth for 
both oblique and panorama imaging methods based on Eqs. (13) and 
(14). Overall, both imaging methods tended to overestimate or under­
estimate the actual fruit size, mostly due to the image distortion induced 
by the oblique or panorama mode. With oblique mode, the R2 and RMSE 
were 0.64 and 11.01 mm, respectively, for fruit height estimation 
(Fig. 15a), while the numbers were 0.65 and 10.79 mm for fruit width 
estimation (Fig. 15b). Similar patterns were found with the panorama 
mode, where the R2 and RMSE were 0.68 and 10.41 mm for fruit height 
estimation (Fig. 15c) and 0.66 and 10.34 mm for fruit width estimation 
(Fig. 15d). Thus, it was clear that the estimated fruit size deviated from 
the ground-truth measurement. However, it was also interesting to 
reveal that the overestimated and underestimated fruit sizes compen­
sated for each other for all scenarios, resulting in a near 1:1 slope 
(regression slopes ranged from 0.93 to 1.00) between the CA-YOLOv4 
estimated and actual measured fruit sizes.

dense-foliage V-trellis and vertical-trellis, covering early-, mid-, to late- 
growth stages (August 13–October 7, 2019, for “Envy” and “Scifresh” in 
Washington State, specifically), where both immature and mature (i.e.,

for image acquisition with two imaging techniques of oblique view and 
panorama to capture the entire tree in a single shot. Based on the results, 
the following specific conclusions can be made from this work. 
1) Our proposed CA-YOLOv4 includes three major improvements: i) 
the CBAM attention mechanism was introduced to the original YOLOv4, 
ii) an adaptive layer and larger-scale feature map were embedded, and 
iii) the densely connected network structure was integrated, and the

verified the superiority of CA-YOLOv4 over the original YOLOv4 with

2) CA-YOLOv4 also outperformed the other two commonly adopted

testing results showed that the “Scifresh” fruit detection accuracy for 
CA-YOLOv4 was 6.86%–10.45% higher than that of Faster R-CNN and 
5.49%–11.29% higher than that of SSD in the early-, mid-, and late

denser foliage canopies (“Scifresh”) using CA-YOLOv4; and

mode achieved better performances in terms of TP detection accuracy of 
84.25% (2.52% higher), FP of 10.18% (3.28% lower), and TN of 15.75%

YOLOv4 over 30 in-field manually measured apple fruits. The results 
showed that the R2 was up to 0.68 in fruit height estimation and 0.66 in 
fruit width estimation. 
Overall, this study verified the superiority of the proposed CA- 
YOLOv4 detector for better fruit counting and accurate fruit size esti­
mation in dense-foliage apple crops during the entire growth stage, 
particularly in the early stage for timely crop load estimation. CA- 
YOLOv4 can perform in near real-time applications with an average 
detection time of 0.1 s per image with a high resolution using the 
hardware described in subsection 2.4.2. Future work will focus on 
developing a mobile application (app) using the trained CA-YOLOv4 
detector for practical deployment targeting fruit growers as end-users 
for better strategic orchard management.
