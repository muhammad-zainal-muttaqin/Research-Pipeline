A B S T R A C T  
The implementations of deep learning combined with other methods such as transfer learning and data 
augmentation in oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) ripeness classification have been researched throughout the 
years. However, most of the methods require devices with high computational resources which could not be 
implemented in mobile applications. To overcome this problem, this research would focus on creating a mobile 
application to classify the ripeness levels of oil palm FFB using lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). 
In this research, we implemented ImageNet transfer learning on 4 lightweight CNN models with a novel data 
augmentation method named “9-angle crop”, which would be further optimized using post-training quantization. 
Transfer learning with 3 unfrozen convolution blocks and 9 angle crop successfully increased the classification 
accuracy on MobileNetV1, and when it was compared to other lightweight models, EfficientNetB0 performed 
best with 0.898 test accuracy on Keras. Float16 quantization also proved to be the most suitable post-training 
quantization method for this model, halving the size of EfficientNetB0 with the least increase in image classi­
fication time and an accuracy drop of only 0.005 after the model was converted to TensorFlow Lite interpreter. In 
conclusion, the best model created in this research is EfficientNetB0, trained with the combination of transfer 
learning, 9 angle crop, and float16 quantization, which enabled it to achieve an overall test accuracy of 0.893 on 
TensorFlow Lite with 96 ms classification time per image, far surpassing the other 3 compared models with the 
second-best model being MobileNetV1 with 0.811 accuracy. The model itself was able to achieve similar results 
when it was implemented on an Android application to classify the ripeness levels of oil palm FFB images ob­
tained through live camera input.   
1. Introduction 
Oil palm is considered as a highly influential plant in the fields of 
agriculture with a continually increasing production rate since 2012 
(Shahbandeh, 2021). Countries such as Indonesia, Malaysia, and 
Guatemala have exported large quantities of oil palm fruits throughout 
the world (Index Mundi, 2021). This huge number of production rates, 
along with a wide variety of derivative products from oil palm plants 
indicates that oil palm is a highly prominent international commodity. 
Demands may continue to rise and oil palm farmers will require methods 
to facilitate more efficient productions of oil palm Fresh Fruit Bunches 
(FFB), as one of the current existing problems is the usage of manual 
ripeness sorting, which can be considered time-consuming and less 
efficient. Farmers may have limited access to reliable classification 
systems. Studies have been conducted to address this challenge, 
including by simplifying of ripeness grading process using computer 
vision. 
In oil palm fruit ripeness classification, various methods including 
image processing, machine learning, and deep learning methods have 
been implemented to achieve high classification accuracy using image 
data. Some implementations include the usage of image segmentation 
using Canny edge detection algorithm in classifying oil palm fruits 
(Septiarini et al., 2020). In 2014, machine learning using Multi-Layer 
Perceptron (MLP) was used to classify images of oil palm FFB 
captured using spectral cameras (Bensaeed et al., 2014). Despite of the 
high accuracy, this method requires the usage of a spectral camera to 
capture individual oil palm images and various preprocessing process 
(background removal, pixel discrimination, and noise reduction). Other 
studies involve the usage of MLP with Principal Component Analysis 
(PCA) and Stepwise Discriminant Analysis (SDA) to classify the ripeness

However, some drawbacks exist in the usage of machine learning. 
One such drawback is the need of feature extraction process outside of 
the model, which means that researchers need to identify the important 
classifying features first and implement a program to extract these fea­
tures (Dargan et al., 2019). This method would consume a lot of time to 
select influential features of the oil palm fruits. As classifying oil palm 
FFB would be challenging due to its complex features, such as color 
gradations among the fruits, the thorns, and the shape of the FFB, deep 
learning would be more efficient in terms of implementation. Ibrahim 
et al. have also compared the performance of deep learning and machine 
learning in classifying oil palm FFB, in which deep learning method 
managed to outperform machine learning methods (Ibrahim et al., 
2018). Another shortcoming in previous studies for this topic is the 
requirement of high-tech devices in supporting the computer vision 
methods, such as spectral camera or other input devices for specific data. 
Such method may prove less reliable as these devices may not always be 
available for oil palm farmers, and external feature extraction program 
may take more time to produce outputs. Therefore, we narrowed the 
scope of our proposed method, using simple ubiquitous mobile devices 
which take more general forms of real-time input such as images from 
camera as these devices are not only easy-to-use and easy-to-find, but 
also capable of executing computer vision based tasks. An example of 
such devices includes smartphones. 
Unlike machine learning, deep learning utilizes the concept of rep­
resentation learning which enables the models to use raw input data in 
various tasks, including classification. This means that deep learning 
models extract input features inside of the model itself to form high-level 
abstraction form of data. Convolution and pooling are used to extract 
these features, and the output is fed into neural networks. This archi­
tecture is called Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) (LeCun, Bengio, 
& Hinton, 2015). For computer vision-based classification task in mobile 
devices, this method is more suitable for its simplicity which does not 
necessarily require data preprocessing. Previous studies with CNN 
managed to produce practical classification models to be used in mobile 
applications by taking real-time inputs for the model to present real-time 
outputs, such as in classifying freshwater fishes (Suharto et al., 2020) 
and plant disease (Syamsuri & Kusuma, 2019). 
Deep learning has also been used several times in past studies on oil 
palm FFB ripeness classification. Several CNN architectures have been 
implemented and tested, such as AlexNet (Ibrahim et al., 2018), ResNet- 
152 (Harsawardana et al., 2020), and DenseNet (Herman et al., 2020). 
Ibrahim et al. managed to achieve 100% accuracy using AlexNet on their 
test dataset. In 2019, Harsawardana et al. implemented ResNet-152 on a 
smart crane grabber for classifying palm oil ripeness levels with 0.7134 
F1 score. In 2020, Herman et al. applied residual attention on DenseNet 
and obtained 0.6929 F1 score in palm oil ripeness level classification. In 
these past experiments, transfer learning and data augmentation were 
used and managed to improve classification results. However, these 
models cannot be considered lightweight due to their huge number of 
parameters. In other words, the used models are potentially unable to be 
used in simple devices with low computational resources, such as 
smartphones. In order to solve this problem, our research focused on 
training lightweight CNNs to classify ripeness levels of oil palm FFB. 
One notable lightweight CNN architecture called MobileNet has been 
used in various research fields involving deep learning as an architecture 
of CNN requiring light computational resources and excellent results. It 
was first introduced in 2017 as a lightweight CNN for mobile applica­
tions with approximately 4.2 million parameters (Howard et al., 2017). 
This MobileNet architecture would be known as MobileNetV1. In 2018, 
a newer version of MobileNet was introduced as MobileNetV2. This 
version of MobileNet implemented the usage of bottleneck blocks, 
consisting of convolution with ReLU6 (Rectified Linear Unit 6), depth­
wise convolution, and 1x1 convolution without non-linearity, with less 
(approximately 3.4 million) parameters and fewer computational cost 
(Sandler et al., 2018). These 2 versions of MobileNet have been widely 
implemented in various fields, such as recognizing American Sign Lan­
guage (ASL) (Rathi, 2018) and handprint recognition system (Michele 
et al., 2019). However, it is yet to be tested in the fields of agriculture. 
Other lightweight CNN architecture such as EfficientNetB0 (Tan & Le, 
2019) and NASNet (Neural Architecture Search Network) Mobile (Tan 
et al., 2019) can also be studied for this field as they also possess similar 
number of parameters to those of MobileNet and are comparable in 
terms of performance (Saxen et al., 2019). 
One shortcoming of deep learning methods is that it requires a vast 
amount of training data in order to achieve high accuracy and avoid 
overfitting (a condition where a neural network performed too well on a 
particular set of data, and otherwise on other sets). To deal with this 
weakness, transfer learning can be utilized, as it has been proven 
capable of enhancing performance of CNN in cases where large amounts 
of data is unavailable (Hentschel et al., 2016; Alzubaidi et al., 2020). 
Transfer learning is done by copying a base knowledge, or previously 
trained weights of a neural network in the case of deep learning, to the 
used model’s weights and freezing several of the layers, or in other 
words, rendering the layers’ weights untrainable (Torrey and Shavlik, 
2010). In particular, ImageNet has been used in transfer learning to 
transfer knowledge of common objects (Deng et al., 2009). This way, a 
CNN would have basic knowledge about object appearance. MobileNet 
transfer learning with ImageNet has proven to be highly accurate in 
various studies, including in classifying brain Magnetic Resonance Im­
aging (MRI) (Lu et al., 2020), welding defects (Pan et al., 2020), and 
even skin cancer (Velasco et al., 2019). 
Another method that may be used to avoid overfitting is data 
augmentation. It is a technique to expand the size of the used datasets 
and improve their quality by creating additional data from existing data. 
There are various methods of augmentation such as geometric trans­
formations (translation, rotation, flipping, etc.), kernel filters, random 
erasing, generative adversarial networks (GAN), and many more. 
Among these methods, cropping has proven capable of achieving 
distinctively higher accuracy than other simple geometric trans­
formations (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, 2019). In previous studies, crop­
ping techniques such as Ten Crop was used to improve CNN 
performance in classifying oil palm FFB ripeness level and achieved high 
accuracy (Harsawardana et al., 2020; Herman et al., 2020). 
This paper focuses on creating a lightweight CNN to classify oil palm 
FFB by their ripeness levels which is usable in devices with low 
computational resources, particularly smartphones. In this paper, a 
novel data augmentation method which we named “9 Angle Crop” is 
proposed to be implemented with transfer learning on lightweight 
CNNs. The CNNs are then compared to discover the best model in terms 
of accuracy and speed in Keras, and further optimized using post- 
training quantization before being compared in TensorFlow Lite. Af­
terwards, the best models were implemented in a mobile application. All 
in all, the main contributions of this research include:  
– testing the effect of transfer learning and the number of unfrozen 
blocks on oil palm FFB classification using lightweight CNN,  
– introducing a novel image augmentation method names “9 angle 
crop” and testing its influence in deep learning for oil palm FFB 
classification,  
– comparing the effects of post-training quantization methods to 
optimize the models on changes in the models’ accuracy, speed, and 
size,  
– comparing the performances of 4 lightweight CNN architectures with 
the proposed method to determine the best model to be deployed in a 
mobile application,  
– creating a mobile application to classify ripeness levels of oil palm 
FFB

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Dataset 
The dataset consisted of 653 images taken from an oil palm mill in 
Central Kalimantan during the grading process. Samples of images from 
each category are presented in Fig. 1. The images were taken from 6 
categories of oil palm FFB, which are: unripe (Fig. 1(A)), under-ripe 
(Fig. 1(B)), ripe (Fig. 1(C)), over-ripe (Fig. 1(D)), abnormal (Fig. 1(E)), 
and empty fruit bunch (EFB) (Fig. 1(F)) using smartphone digital camera 
in various outdoor locations as background during sunny days. Speci­
fications of the smartphone and its camera settings can be viewed in 
Table 1 and Table 2 respectively. The images were saved in JPG (Joint 
Photographic Group) format with 3120 × 4160 pixels resolution and 
labelled manually from the mill. There were 110 images for each cate­
gory with the exception of abnormal class, which had 103 images. For 
the dataset, the images are split with a ratio of 5:2:3 for training, vali­
dation, testing sets respectively. 
2.2. The proposed method 
The proposed method consisted of 2 main steps and 1 implementa­
tion step. The 2 main steps consisted of data pre-processing and deep 
learning steps while the implementation step involved the development 
of an android application for the created CNN model to be used in real 
life. An illustration of the proposed method can be seen in Fig. 2 which 
describes the whole process of the proposed method. 
2.2.1. Pre-processing 
Overall, there are 4 steps involved in pre-processing: (1) localization 
and square cropping, (2) Gaussian blur, (3) 9 angle crops, and (4) 
resizing. Localization was used to ensure that the features in the images 
would mostly belong to the fruits. It was done manually by cropping the 
images in a 1:1 aspect ratio and excluding the background as much as 
possible. The next process was image filtering with Gaussian blur which 
was done to the whole dataset. Blurring was used to reduce image noise 
or degradations from image acquisition, transmission, or storage. Blur is 
one of the most common image degradation phenomena from cameras. 
As the objective of the research is to build a mobile application for image 
classification, mobile devices used may not have the best camera qual­
ity, as such the CNN needed to train with blurred images (Dodge & 
Karam, 2016). Blurring was also used to soften the images before they

2.2.2. Deep learning 
2.2.2.1. Architecture of the proposed model. The proposed model con­
sisted of 3 parts: (1) feature extraction, (2) Global Average Pooling 
(GAP), and (3) Fully Connected Layer. For the feature extraction, 4 
different CNN models from Keras were deployed and compared: (1) 
MobileNetV1, (2) MobileNetV2, (3) EfficientNetB0, and (4) NASNet 
Mobile. These models were used for feature extraction, and their outputs 
were passed through a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer and a fully 
connected classifier. We replaced the original classifier of the Keras 
models with a custom classifier as the original model was trained to 
classify images from ImageNet dataset with 1 Dense layer. The modified 
classifier consisted of 2 hidden Dense layers, 1 output Dense layer, and a 
Dropout layer between the 2 hidden layers. The hidden layers contained 
432 and 216 neurons respectively while the output layer contained 6 
neurons which represented the 6 ripeness levels. The dropout layer has a 
rate of 0.5. Architecture of the proposed deep learning model is pre­
sented in Fig. 4. 
The input layer was set to 224 × 224 × 3 for all models in reference 
to the original MobileNet and EfficientNet architectures (Howard et al., 
2017; Sandler et al., 2018; Tan & Le, 2019). For the feature extraction 
process, NASNet Mobile and EfficientNet were chosen to be used as their 
number of parameters are much closer to those of MobileNet compared 
to other CNNs. A comparison of the 4 models can be viewed in Table 3. 
All of the chosen lightweight models have less than 5 million parame­
ters. In other words, they would not take too much inference time and 
memory usage on the device (Reddy et al., 2018). As such, MobileNetV1 
may be faster than NASNet Mobile and EfficientNetB0, while Mobile­
NetV2 may be even faster. In this research, this hypothesis will also be 
tested. 
Afterwards, the extracted features will be sent as input in the size of 
1024 to a GAP layer. GAP was used in order to reduce the number of 
parameters used, capture possible discriminative features, and prevent 
overfitting (Zhou et al., 2016). The pooling would return 1024-sized 
parameters to be classified by the fully connected layer. In the fully 
connected layer, 2 hidden layers were deployed in order to enable the 
CNN to not only represent linear decision, but also estimate functions 
with continuous mapping from one finite space to another (Heaton, 
2008). The usage of 2 hidden layers have been proven to work better 
than 1, but too many hidden layers may result in the CNN being too 
complex (Thomas et al., 2017). The usage of a Dropout layer between 
the 2 hidden layers was implemented in order to help the network 
prevent overfitting (Srivastava et al., 2014). The output of the proposed 
deep learning model is a list of 6 scores, each representing the confi­
dence score of the input image belonging to a category. The index of the 
maximum confidence score would be used to label oil palm FFB images 
in the mobile application.

We also utilized early stopping to reduce the possibility of over­
fitting. This method means that the training process would be halted 
when a halting condition has occurred before the maximum number of 
epochs. When a neural network is trained, a phenomenon where the 
training loss continually decreases while validation loss suddenly in­
creases may occur. Such cases is an example of overfitting and it is the 
reason behind the usage of validation dataset, which is to monitor the 
training process and identify the signs of overfitting. In some early 
stopping methods, validation loss is used as an indicator to halt the 
training process by ending the training process if the validation loss does 
not increase after a specified number of epochs. If this criteria occurs, 
the weights of the model from the epochs with the lowest validation loss 
would be restored to the model (Prechelt, 1998). 
Aside from validation loss, different combinations of train accuracy, 
train loss, or validation accuracy can also be used for the stopping 
criteria. In order to ensure that only the best models on both datasets are 
used, we implemented early stopping with both training and validation 
loss as the indicators with a patience of 10 epochs. In other words, 
suppose that we have trained a model for n epochs and both losses does 
not improve after another 10 epochs, the training process would be 
stopped and the model’s weights would be restored to its weights during 
the n-th epoch. This way, neither too much computational resources nor 
training time would be wasted when the model has converged before the 
final epoch. 
2.2.2.3. Model optimization. As the model would be used in mobile 
devices, optimization methods, such as pruning, quantization, knowl­
edge distillation, etc., had to be considered due to the limited compu­
tation resources in such devices. Among these optimization methods, 
quantization has been widely used and known to be hardware-friendly 
(Chen et al., 2020). By default, Keras models in TensorFlow used 32- 
bit floating point numbers as parameters or weights. Quantization was 
done by converting the model’s parameters to lower bit-depth repre­
sentations such as float16 or int8, which resulted in the model having 
smaller size and less memory usage at the cost of a drop in its accuracy. 
Generally, there are 2 usable methods in quantizing models: (1) Post- 
training quantization and (2) Quantization-aware training. In some 
cases, post-training quantization may be preferable than quantization- 
aware training because the latter method would require a model anno­
tation and another training process on the converted model. 
The techniques that can be used for post-training quantization are 
float16 quantization and dynamic range quantization. Float16 quanti­
zation was capable of halving the model’s size, while dynamic range 
quantization would compress the model to a quarter of its original size 
albeit it disabled GPU acceleration for the model (TensorFlow, n.d.). 
This means that when models converted with float 16 quantization were 
tested in a PC with GPU acceleration, they would be faster than models 
converted with dynamic range quantization, although the same thing 
may not be concluded on mobile devices. In this experiment, we 
implemented and compared the results of float16 quantization and dy­
namic range quantization as quantization-aware training consumes 
more time in training the quantization-aware models. 
2.2.3. Mobile application development 
The trained models were converted to TensorFlow Lite models called 
interpreter with the format of “.tflite” files to enable them to be used on 
mobile devices. The mobile application was developed for Android OS 
using Kotlin programming language with TensorFlow Lite library to 
enable the usage of TensorFlow Lite models in mobile phones. Addi­
tionally, RenderScript was used to denoise input images. These 2 li­
braries were implemented in order to enable the application to take live 
camera image feeds, preprocess them, and use the interpreter to classify 
them. 
The first step in developing the application was preparing the models 
and labels. The TensorFlow Lite interpreters were first loaded in the 
application. The Keras base model’s output was a list of 6 confidence 
score, each implying how likely the image belongs to each class. The 
same output was produced by the interpreter when it took an image as 
input. The application would take the index of the highest confidence 
score and display the label with the same index. In order to process the 
images more efficiently, the images were converted to byte buffers 
before being fed into the interpreters. 
The next step was to construct the process flow. As the interpreter 
required some time to classify the inputs, a selection process needed to 
be used on the image input stream from the camera. This selection was 
done using a flag variable. If an image is still being processed, the 
captured frames from the camera input stream would be skipped and not 
used as input for the interpreter although they are still displayed on the 
application. By default, the images captured by the camera were rep­
resented by YUV channel pixel values. Therefore, images that passed 
through the selection process would be converted to RGB values. 
Denoising was then applied to these images using ScriptIntrinsicBlur 
module from RenderScript, which is similar to Gaussian blurring. Af­
terwards, the image was resized to 224 × 224 using nearest-neighbor 
interpolation to meet the interpreter’s input size, and converted to

3. Results and discussions 
3.1. Evaluation method 
Performance of each model was compared by 2 specific metrics, 
which are: (1) classification accuracy and (2) image classification time, 
in spite of the fact that the former would be more influential in selecting 
the best model. The classification time metric would be observed for the 
final step of evaluation. Overall, there were 4 steps of experiment and 
evaluation to test the impact of transfer learning, data augmentation, 
and quantization for oil palm FFB classification using deep learning. 
Based on these data, the best model in this research was determined. 
Detailed information of the experiment process is written in Table 6. 
The first step of the experiment involved 6 models of MobileNetV1 
with different numbers of unfrozen convolutional blocks after transfer 
learning with ImageNet. This CNN architecture was chosen as the base 
model due to its high number of usage in studies involving CNN in 
mobile applications, such as classifications of skin diseases (Velasco 
et al., 2019), freshwater fishes (Suharto et al., 2020), welding defects 
(Pan et al., 2020), and breast cancer (Ansar et al., 2020). This step was 
done in order to analyze the effect of transfer learning and how the 
number of unfrozen blocks affect a model’s performance. The Mobile­
NetV1 model used in this research consisted of 13 layers and the 
experiment was conducted on 6 models of this type, with 13, 12, 9, 6, 3, 
and 0 unfrozen convolution blocks respectively. For most of the models, 
some of the blocks were left unfrozen in order to enable the model to 
adapt to the used dataset in extracting features and not be solely focused 
on extracting features from ImageNet dataset. As such, the model with 
0 unfrozen convolution blocks was also used in order to evaluate the 
validity of this theory. All of the models were tested on a test dataset 
without data augmentation. 
Results of the first step, specifically the number of convolution blocks 
that should be left unfrozen, were used in the next steps. In the second 
step of the experiment, MobileNetV1 was also used as the base model in 
evaluating the effects of the image augmentation methods. In this step, 
the novel 9 angle crop was tested using the best model from the first step, 
with the former being compared to another augmentation method, 
namely 5 crop with additional rotation augmentation. Afterwards, the 
best method would be used to augment the dataset in the third step of 
the experiment, which is comparing the results of MobileNetV1 with 
MobileNetV2, NASNet Mobile, and EfficientNetB0 for this problem. The

3.3. The developed application 
Since each of the models have different advantages and disadvan­
tages in terms of accuracy and classification speed, all four of the models 
were implemented inside the application. We used the float16 quantized 
models as the interpreters with the exception of NASNet Mobile, which 
achieved higher test accuracy using dynamic range quantization. Users 
would be able to choose which interpreter to be used. Screenshots of the 
Android application is presented in Fig. 9. When the application is 
launched, 4 buttons will be displayed, each having the name of each 
model for the user to choose (Fig. 9 (A)). 
After users have selected a model, they will need to aim the camera at 
an oil palm fruit that they want to classify. The labels and confidence 
level of the classification process would be displayed in the panel at the 
bottom of the screen (Fig. 9 (B,C,D,E)). The confidence level is shown in 
percentage, which implies the image’s likeliness of being in the dis­
played class. If a captured frame has not been classified by the inter­
preter, all of the frames captured by the camera would be skipped and 
not fed into the interpreter. 
3.4. Conclusion 
This research produced an Android application capable of classifying 
the ripeness levels of oil palm FFB in 6 categories (unripe, under-ripe, 
ripe, over-ripe, abnormal, and Empty Fruit Bunch). The deep learning 
models used were trained using transfer learning and a novel data 
augmentation method called “9 angle crop”. Both of these methods had 
proven capable of increasing the accuracy of MobileNetV1 when they 
were tested on validation and test dataset. Transfer learning with 3 
unfrozen blocks allowed MobileNetV1 to reach 0.777 validation accu­
racy and 0.806 test accuracy, with a further increase to 0.915 accuracy 
and 0.811 test accuracy using 9 angle crop. The novel 9 angle crop also 
helped in preventing overfitting on the model, as it reduced the differ­
ence between train and validation accuracy on MobileNetV1 by 0.074. 
Further experiments on MobileNetV2, NASNet Mobile, and Effi­
cientNetB0 brought about the conclusion that EfficientNetB0 was su­
perior to the other 3 models with 0.898 test accuracy on Keras. 
The models were further optimized using post-training quantization, 
with dynamic range quantization providing the best result for NASNet 
Mobile and float16 quantization providing the best results for Mobile­
NetV1, MobileNetV2, and EfficientNetB0. Float16 quantized Effi­
cientNetB0 managed to reach the highest accuracy, achieving 0.893 
accuracy score on test dataset. On the other hand, MobileNetV1 had 
proven not only to be the most efficient CNN requiring only 39 ms image 
classification time, but also to be highly accurate, with 0.811 test ac­
curacy. Despite MobileNetV2 having the least parameter, it was a bit 
inferior to MobileNetV1 in this research, both in terms of accuracy and
