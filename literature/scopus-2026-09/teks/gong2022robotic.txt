Abstract
Occlusion is one of the key factors affecting the success rate of vision‐based fruit‐
picking robots. It is important to accurately locate and grasp the occluded fruit in
field applications, However, there is yet no universal and effective solution. In this
paper, a high‐precision estimation method of spatial geometric features of occluded
targets based on deep learning and multisource images is presented, enabling the
selective harvest robot to envision the whole target fruit as if its occlusions do not
exist. First, RGB, depth and infrared images are acquired. And pixel‐level matched
RGB‐D‐I fusion images are obtained by image registration. Second, aiming at the
problem of detecting the occluded tomatoes in the greenhouse, an extended Mask‐
RCNN network is designed to extract the target tomato. The target segmentation
accuracy is improved by 7.6%. Then, for partially occluded tomatoes, a shape and
position restoration method is used to recover the obscured tomato. This algorithm
can extract tomato radius and centroid coordinates directly from the restored depth
image. The mean Intersection over Union is 0.895, and the centroid position error is
0.62 mm for the occluded rate under 25% and the illuminance between 1 and
12 KLux. And hereby a dual‐arm robotic harvesting system is improved to achieve a
picking time of 11 s per fruit, an average gripping accuracy of 8.21 mm, and an
average picking success rate of 73.04%. The proposed approach realizes a high‐
fidelity geometrics reconstruction instead of mere image style restoration, which
endows the robot with the ability to see through obstacles in the field scenes and
improves its operational success rate in its result.

INTRODUCTION
When a fruit‐picking robot is deployed, its visual ability is a critical
factor for successful operation. Machine vision and its precise posi-
tioning have been proved as the bottleneck in their commercial ap-
plication (Tang, Wang, et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2019). Yang et al.
developed a mushroom picking robot and achieved a F1 score of
0.951 and an average 3D localization error of 2.43 mm. The targets
are in the plane and the experimental scenario is simple (Q. Yang
et al., 2020). In 2015, Feng et al. (2015) developed a tomato‐picking
robot, which combined structured lighting and HSI color threshold to
detect tomatoes. The execution time was about 24 s/f (second per
fruit) and the success rate had reached 83.9%. The main reason for
picking failure is due to the overlapped or deformed fruit that is not
identified. In 2016, Yaguchi et al. (2016) designed a tomato‐picking
robot system having binocular vision combined with HSI color space
thresholding and clustering. It realized 23 s/f execution efficiency in
practice. In 2017, Lili et al. developed a greenhouse tomato‐picking

The failure is attributed to the tomato of interest being sheltered by
obstacles such as immature fruits, stems, and leaves (L. Wang
et al., 2017). However, some research had indicated that binocular
vision often has poor performance when the fruits are clustered. It is
due to the failure of feature point matching.
Some recent studies have begun to adopt pattern recognition
methods that can address the problem in vision‐based fruit‐picking
robotic systems. Lin et al. proposed a fruit‐recognition algorithm that
combines partial shape matching, probabilistic Hough transform, and
SVM. It was applied to the identification of citrus images and reached
a precision of 0.783 and a recall of 0.802 (Tang, Zou, et al., 2020).
Kurtulmus et al. (2014) used statistical classifiers and neural networks
to detect peaches with a correct rate of 84.6%, although some highly‐
overlapped peaches could not be successfully detected. In 2018, a
humanoid dual‐arm tomato‐picking robot was developed with va-
cuum cup grasping and wide‐range cutting. The success rate achieved
87.5% and the positioning error of the end‐point was less than
10 mm. Picking failure is often due to excessive overlapping of the
target tomato (Ling et al., 2019).
It can be found that recent research on fruit‐picking robots are
using more and more advanced detection methods to improve de-
tection accuracy and efficiency, however, the overlap problem has
not been solved yet (Pastrana & Rath, 2013; Y. Zhao, Gong,
et al., 2016). Related studies indicate that in the real‐world scenario,
only 60% of strawberries are intact (including adjacent situations) in
picking robot's visual fields (Hayashi, 2005). On the other hand, the
tomato plant is densely composed and occluded by branches
(X. Chen, Chaudhary, et al., 2015), according to our statistics, intact
tomato only accounts for about 50% in total.
In the agricultural environment, there are often overlapped and
occluded cases such as tomatoes behind its leaves, which results in
large errors in positioning and hampers the harvest success rate. The
major difficulty of the tomato‐picking robot is to find the shape and
position of the tomatoes. In previous studies, there exist three types of
shape and position estimation methods. The first type is the mor-
phological method. Song had used mathematical morphology and the
convex hull of the object boundary to extract the contours (Song
et al., 2012). The disadvantage rests on its low accuracy due to the lack
of color and spatial information. The second type is the image in-
painting
method.

is
roughly
categorized

(Bertalmio et al., 2000; D. Liu et al., 2007) and patch‐based (Huang
et al., 2014) algorithms. However the inpainting method is less ef-
fective in handling images with large missing parts or large structures.
In recent years, some researchers used deep learning based generative
methods to fill the missing parts. Generative adversarial networks
(GAN) and convolutional neural network (CNN) are the two basic
networks. GAN keeps style consistency but is not suitable for images
with complex structures (Lahiri et al., 2020). CNN may generate dis-
torted textures due to the lack of direct supervision on attention
scores (Zhou et al., 2020). The third type is the three‐dimensional (3D)
object reconstruction method. C. Wang et al. (2019) introduced a
method to extract geometric features from RGB and depth (RGB‐D)
images. This method obtains 3D bounding boxes instead of accurate
positions and cannot be used to navigate the picking robot. These
methods, developed for recovering RGB images are not able to re-
construct real targets. Meanwhile, the goal of these tasks is to restore
images for visual naturalness but not for the shape and position esti-
mation. Notably, we refer to the style restoration from the image as an
inpainting method while the precise shape and position as a high‐
fidelity reconstruction approach. “high‐fidelity” refer to sound and
researchers extended it to the image area to describe the high‐quality
restoration of image (Chang et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2020).
This study takes tomato as the experimental object and aims at
solving the target positioning problem of the greenhouse tomato‐
picking robot. The following issues are considered:
(1) Low detection rate for excessively occluded tomatoes;
(2) Difficulty in determining the contours of occluded tomatoes;
(3) Positioning error of the target tomato due to partial occlusion.
It is common sense that humans may conjure up the whole body
of a partially visible object precisely according to their life experience.
Inspired by nature, we mimic this human capability with a learning
and generative approach. We propose an instance segmentation
CNN enhanced by multisources including RGB, depth, and infrared
(RGB‐D‐I) images. Furthermore, a neural network for restoring the
complete shape and position (S‐P) information of occluded tomato is
designed and trained. Finally, we implement the whole visual scheme
that can precisely locate the overlapped or occluded tomato and
experimented with in‐field data. Interestingly, the proposed approach
provides the fruit‐picking robot with an augmented reality environ-
ment, where the robot can see through the obstacles to figure out
the shape and position of the occluded fruits for the picking decision
making as if the foreground obstacles do not exist.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the
experimental equipment, the data set preparation, and the frame-
work of the proposed method. Section 3 presents the effect of the
extended Mask R‐CNN algorithm, compares the proposed SPR model
with other inpainting algorithms. Then the position accuracy of this
algorithm for different occluded rates and different lighting is in-
troduced. The Field experimentation is introduced in Section 3.5.
Camera calibration and hand‐eye calibration of the robot system,
gripping accuracy and picking success rate are described. Lastly, a
conclusion is presented in Section 4.

Data preparation
To train and test the proposed tomato S‐P restoring neural network,
two image data sets are used. The first data set is the open‐sourced
fruits‐360 image set (Mureşan & Oltean, 2018), which contains
49,561 images of 74 kinds of fruits. The second data set (RGB‐D‐I
tomato set) has 2000 pairs of RGB‐D‐I images (RGB image, the depth
image, and the infrared image, captured at the same time by Kinect‐

Agriculture Base. Each pair contains one RGB‐D‐I image of an intact
tomato and several RGB‐D‐I images taken when the aforesaid tomato
is occluded by leaves or other tomatoes. All the image pairs are
captured by keeping the target tomato's position fixed. The depth

Vision solution
Starting with the original RGB‐D‐I channel image acquired by the
Kinect‐V2 camera, it takes three phases to extract the tomato co-
ordinates, as shown in Figure 4. In the first stage, the target tomato,
Figure 4e, is segmented from the original image, Figure 4a–d, by
using the extended Mask R‐CNN model. In the second stage, with
the mask generated in the first stage, the depth information of the
complete tomato is recovered using the shape and position re-
construction (SPR) model. In the third stage, the centroid and the
diameter of the complete tomato in camera coordinates are extracted
from the reconstructed depth image.

Phase 1: Instance segmentation
Many of the previous methods can realize semantic segmentation,
that is, mark all the targets in an image with the same label. Then each
object is individually annotated according to the separation of image
regions. This method is unable to distinguish each other when the
fruits overlap. However, different from semantic segmentation,
instance segmentation requires individual annotation for each object
of the target category. In the development of deep learning,
Mask‐RCNN is one of the most popular open‐source models. It works
in various scenarios with high precision and fast computation speed
(5 fps). However, when Mask‐RCNN is applied in the fruit‐picking
robot system, the boundary of the overlap fruits is still difficult to be
segmented accurately. This is because the segmentation relies on
texture features, while the texture of sticky fruit is very smooth and
uniform in color.
Although the overlapped tomatoes are highly similar in the RGB
space, they can be easily distinguished in the depth (D‐channel) im-
age and infrared (I‐channel) image, as shown in Figure 5. To make it
more informative, we displayed the depth image as a heatmap, with
different colors indicating different depths. The depth image is not
affected by illumination and can distinguish between different in-
dividual tomatoes by depth information, and the bright center and
dark surroundings of the infrared image are useful for extracting edge
features. Previous research also proved that multisource images can
improve segmentation accuracy (Gupta et al., 2014). Based on the
above considerations, we introduce the depth channel image and
infrared channel image into the instance segmentation process.
As shown in Figure 6, our method uses Mask‐RCNN as a basic
segmentation network, then modifies the dimension of input data
and randomly initializes parameters of the network's first layer. By
this strategy, the network can be trained to utilize the information of
the RGB‐D‐I images. Moreover, only the first layer of Mask‐RCNN is
changed,
which

increasing
the

pixel‐level mask of the target object. As the input images were
changed from three‐ to five‐dimensional, the shallow network
structure of ResNet was modified and the parameters of the modified
network were initialized with the Gaussian random. The model was
trained with an initial learning rate of 0.001 for the head network
layer, and then the whole network layer was trained with a learning

Phase 2: Shape and position restoration
Neural networks are becoming the focus in several areas of
computer vision and image processing such as in image restora-
tion (H. Zhao, Gallo, et al., 2016). Image restoration is the process
of estimating uncorrupted images from noisy or blurred ones
(Y. Chen, Yu, et al., 2015). Particularly, what we are interested in
is how to estimate a true intact image from an image with an
incomplete object. In recent years, many deep learning models
have demonstrated encouraging image inpainting performance,
such as complementing face images by given an incomplete one.
However, the inpainting image was often found to be quite
different from the original image and the results tend to be fuzzy
(Li et al., 2018).
The existence of this situation is mainly due to the following
reasons:
1. Most of the recent image inpainting networks are GAN based
(Bao et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Yeh et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2019). As a
generative model, GAN does not update directly with data examples.

Algorithm's shape and positioning error
analysis
The shape and position restoration effect of the SPR algorithm
proposed is affected by many factors, such as masking areas, light
intensity, and so on. High precision restoration indicates the high
accuracy of the boundary and the position. This section discusses the
boundary contour accuracy and surface point cloud position accuracy
of masked‐restored tomato fruits under different occluded rates and
different lighting conditions.
3.4.1
|
Shape and positioning error for different
occluded rate
The centroid localization error of the entire vision system is de-
rived from the error of image acquisition equipment, image re-
construction error, and coordinate extraction algorithm error.
Since the error scale is small (<10 mm) and the centroid is an ab-
stract concept, the localization error is difficult to be measured
directly in an unstructured environment. Accordingly, we focus on

the occluded image (Figure 16). It takes 80 ms per image for the
SPR algorithm. Finally, the centroid coordinate described in
Section 2.3.3 is used to calculate the centroid coordinates corre-
sponding to the real sample and the reconstructed sample.

Discussion
The study of the field harvesting experiments provides valuable
insights into automated harvesting of the greenhouse tomatoes,
especially
the
detection
and
positioning
of
branch‐occluded
tomatoes.
For the detection problem of the shaded fruits, the extended
Mask R‐CNN is used to segment the tomato from the background.
The unique is using RGB‐D‐I images as the input to the network. The
depth and infrared features are added to make the model more ef-
fective. The detection accuracy is improved by 7.6% compared to the
RGB‐based Mask R‐CNN. But the average detection time for a single
image increases by 159.5% due to the increased computational work.
For the localization problem of occluded fruits, the SPR model is
proposed to estimate the color and depth information of the occluded
part. The centroid error and mean IoU of the reconstructed point cloud
are used as the evaluation metrics. The model effects under different
occlusion rates and different light conditions are discussed. The smaller
the occlusion rate is, the better will the reconstruction effect be.
When the occlusion rate reaches 75%–100%, the centroid error is
12.94 mm, the mean IoU is only 0.653. For different lighting condi-
tions, too strong light intensity leads to the decrease of centroid
localization accuracy and surface contour accuracy, and the best effect
is achieved in an overcast day with enough light. The contour error
(mean IoU) improves by 12.5% and the centroid error reduces by
87.1% compared with the noon in direct sunlight.
Automated harvesting experiments were tested in a tomato
greenhouse. The robot was able to replace humans in harvesting
mostly, with a single fruit harvesting time of 11 s, average gripping
accuracy of 8.21 mm, and average picking success rate of 73.04%.

CONCLUSIONS
The paper provides an end‐to‐end approach for harvesting the oc-
cluded fruits in the field scenes, that is, the robot immediately obtains
the picking decision‐making parameters from the image.
A vision system that can accurately extract the occluded tomato
from the background is established. Compared to the original open‐
source network, the extended Mask‐RCNN network can effectively
utilize the information of multisource images, finally achieve an IoU
score increase of 0.065 on the test set. It runs at 456 ms per image.
The basis of the proposed shape and position restoration method
is the ability of the human to predict the invisible part of a tomato
according to the information in visible parts, such as texture, shape,
and infrared value. The neural network is also able to possess
this ability by reasonable design and training, which is proved by
experiments. In the condition that the target tomato has an occlusion
rate of less than 50%, the tomato centroid position error caused by
the reconstruction process is less than 2 mm.
Moreover, automated harvesting experiments were conducted in
the field. The average picking time per fruit is 11 s. The average
success rate is 73.04%. Failure was mainly due to the thick stem
obstruction and the structural defect of the gripper.
The experiment results show that the proposed method can obtain
the position of the target tomato, while its positioning accuracy for the
occluded tomato is much higher than the traditional methods. Further-
more, this method does not rely on artificial parameters such as color
threshold, which often perform unsatisfactorily in the reality scene.
