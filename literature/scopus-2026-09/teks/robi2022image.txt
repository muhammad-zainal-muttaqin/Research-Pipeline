Abstract—Many computer vision approaches, such as deep
learning and machine learning, can be employed in agriculture
in this era of artificial intelligence (AI). These computer vision
techniques are frequently utilized in product categorization,
identification, and estimation. Implementing computer vision in
agriculture will boost agricultural output and quality by assisting
farmers in monitoring agricultural activities. Currently, the oil
palm harvester judges oil palm maturity manually using natural
signs like oil palm colour appearance and the number of oil
palm loose fruit drops under the tree. This paper focuses on
developing automated detection systems using a deep learning
model to detect and classify oil palm fruit bunch based on their
ripeness level. The ripeness of oil palm can be classified into
four maturity levels: unripe, under ripe, ripe, and over ripe.
There are two approaches in this paper namely detection of oil
palm fruit bunches and classification of oil palm fruit bunches.
This paper employes several types of YOLO algorithms such as
YOLOv3, YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv4, and YOLOv4-tiny to compare
the performance and accuracy of different versions of YOLO.
It is shown that YOLOv4 has a higher accuracy of 98.70% in
detecting and classifying oil fruit bunches based on their ripeness
level compared to YOLOv3, YOLOv3-tiny, and YOLOv4-tiny.
Index Terms—deep learning, machine learning, oil palm, de-
tection, classification
I. INTRODUCTION
The oil palm business has grown to be one of Southeast
Asia’s most important industries, particularly in Malaysia. In
2019/2020, Malaysia was the second-largest palm oil producer
and the second-largest palm oil exporter [1]. The quality and
general marketability of palm oil are determined by the age or
the ripeness of the oil palm fruits [2], [3]. There are four types
of ripeness in oil palm: unripe, underripe, ripe, and overripe.
The current application for harvesting oil palm fresh fruit
bunch (FFB) is manually harvested by counting the number of
loose fruits [4]. It is done by counting the number of free fruits
on the ground or the number of loose fruit sockets on the FFB
before it is cut. This method of harvesting may be inaccurate.
This is because loose fruits may fall under a different tree,
become lodged in the fronds, be washed away by heavy rain,
or be eaten by animals in the oil palm plantation.
Nowadays, many computers vision approaches, such as
colour vision, deep learning, and machine learning, can be
implemented in agriculture. These computer vision techniques
are frequently utilized in product estimate, categorization, and
identification. Computer vision in agriculture would improve
agricultural output and quality by assisting farmers in monitor-
ing agricultural activities [5]. Several studies have explored the
effects of colour vision in agriculture. Colour space selection
is critical in identifying the maturity of oil palm FFBs using
colour vision to enable proper analysis. The RGB colour space
has been frequently employed in oil palm FFB categorization
[6]. This colour space may not be ideal for assessing the
maturity of oil palm FFBs using colour vision when examining
photos in a natural light setting. The RGB pixel values rise as
the light intensity increases [7]. Next, another colour system,
hue, saturation, and intensity (HSI), was employed in specific
research. The hue value was discovered to have little influence
on illumination intensity changes [8].
Therefore, many different solutions have been put forward
for this colour vision problem. There have been several at-
tempts to use machine learning in agriculture activities. The
study in [9]–[11] proposed machine learning techniques to
detect and classify different types of fruits. There are several
types of machine learning algorithms, such as Support Vector
Machine (SVM) for fruit recognition [9], K-Nearest Neighbor
(KNN) for classification of cape gooseberry [10], Artificial
Neural Network (ANN) [12] for oil palm grading. A fuzzy
classification approach based on colour features was used
in another study to predict apple ripeness [13]. Crop detec-
tion mentioned above was accomplished using hand-crafted

Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have achieved
outcomes for object detection in terms of accuracy and detec-

in machine vision applications [14]. The most recent and
advanced theory proposed by [15], [16] offers a new approach

rotation, Gamma correction, and noise rejection. Then, CNN
is applied during the simulation.
On the other hand, single-stage detectors approach object
detection as a direct and straightforward regression problem
that takes the full image as input and generates several
bounding boxes and class probabilities simultaneously [17].
Multiple previous attempts have been applied to the You Only
Look Once (YOLO) model in various applications. YOLO
has been used for object detection in shelf images [18],
real-time object detection using Microsoft Common Objects
in Context (COCO) dataset [19]–[22], and fruit vegetables
detection using the YOLO model [16], [23], [24]. Hence, it
has been shown in [12] that the YOLO model method can
be extended for object detection in crop harvesting systems.
In [12], they proposed object detection for crop harvesting
systems in oil palm plantations. They used the YOLO-P model
and YOLOv3-tiny to detect and localize three objects at a palm
oil plantation: fruit bunch, grabber, and palm tree.
Against this background, we extend the work [12], [24]
to detect and classify the oil palm FFB based on their
ripeness level. We implement image classification based on
deep learning algorithms for oil palm bunches and modify the
YOLO model using our dataset. To the best of our knowledge,
no approach has been proposed to detect and classify oil
palm plantations based on their ripeness level using YOLO
algorithms as a deep learning model. We used our dataset of
oil palm images to create the training models. The paper is
organized as follows. Section II is about related work that
has been done in detection and classification. In Section III,
we presents the proposed solution for this paper. Section
IV presents the experiment and evaluation index. Section V
discusses the experiment and results analysis. Finally, Section
VI concludes the paper.
II. RELATED WORKS
The study in [12] proposed the object detection model for
crop harvesting systems in the oil palm plantation. They used
YOLO models to classify objects in the oil palm plantation,
such as FFB, grabber, and palm tree. They used YOLOv2,
YOLOv3, YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv3-dense, and YOLO-P. Then,
they also classify oil palm fruit based on their ripeness using
the YOLO model. The highest accuracy for the detection and
classification is by using YOLO-P. YOLO-P is a modified
YOLOv3-tiny which employs DenseNet201 architecture as
a feature extractor, multi-scale detection and clustering of
anchor boxes. However, for unmodified YOLO, the highest
accuracy for detection and classification of oil palm is 97.17%
using YOLOv3. There is some limitation in [12] where the
classification of oil palm is not for oil palm fruit bunch. They
only considered the oil palm that had already fallen from the
tree as their dataset. Only three ripeness levels are considered
in the study: ripe, under ripe, and over ripe. Conceptually
similar work has also been carried out by [23], [25] using
YOLO models to detect tomatoes and figs. The detection
concept for tomatoes and fig fruits is like oil palm fruit bunch.
The detection is on the tree and may have some situations,
such as fruit overlap and being obscured by leaves.
In agriculture, YOLO has precisely been used in several
applications [23]–[25]. Two approaches have been used in
the past. The first approaches are detection and recognition.
The second approach is classification. In [25]–[27], they used
YOLOv2, YOLOv3, and YOLOv4 models to detect fruits such
as figs, apples and vegetables. From previous work, the highest
accuracy is from YOLOv4 models compared to YOLOv3 and
YOLOv2 from one class of detection [25].
After considering all the advantages of YOLO models, we
decided to use YOLO algorithms as our deep learning mod-
els. YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 are chosen because in previous
studies, these models have the highest accuracy and precision
result compared to other YOLO models. The lighter ver-
sion of YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 which are YOLOv3-tiny and
YOLOv4-tiny is used to compare the performance between the
tiny version and the original version. Usually, this tiny version
is used for a lightweight target detection algorithm applied to
embedded platforms. Therefore, we extend and modify the
YOLO model of the prior work used in other paper to detect
and classify the oil palm fruit bunch based on their ripeness
level.
III. THE PROPOSED SOLUTION
A. Network Model
This paper used YOLOv3, YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv4 and
YOLOv4-tiny as our primary deep learning models. YOLO is a
new approach for object detection systems. Classifiers are used
in existing solutions to object detection problems. However
YOLO frames it as a regression problem by marking bounding
boxes that are geographically separated and assigning class
probability values in one step, making its functioning ex-
tremely similar to how humans recognize objects [24]. Using
a single neural network, YOLO is one of the deep learning
strategies. Compared to all the RCNN family, YOLO is the
fastest algorithms for real-time object detection (45 frames per
second). There are many versions of YOLO such as YOLOv1,
YOLOv2, YOLOv3, and YOLOv4. For smaller detection,
YOLO also provide tiny version such as YOLOv3-tiny and
YOLOv4-tiny.

B. Oil Palm FFB Training and Detection
Step-1 Dataset Collection 175 images are collected from
internet sources. There are several oil palm species globally,
but the most common species in Malaysia is from hybrid
species between dura and pisifera. Fig. 2 shows the example
of the oil palm dataset collected from internet sources.
Step-2 Dataset Augmentation Dataset augmentation is
significant to help prevent overfitting and help the model
become more stable. The augmentation involved different
transformations such as rotation, width shift, rescale, and
horizontal flip. Each original image produced one new image
and made 350 images for the oil palm dataset.
Step-3 Annotation of the images The labelling process
involves drawing a bounding box at the FFB to distinguish
the fruit bunch from the tree. For this project, 70% of the
images are used for training, 20% are used for verification,
and 10% are used for testing.
Step-4 Training Network Model Fine-tuning is one of the
common migration learning approaches. Fine-tuning involves
changing the structure of the pre-training network model,
selectively loading the model’s weight, and then retraining the
model with our dataset. This work used pre-trained YOLOv3
and YOLOv4 weights. There are two classes involved during
the training. One class detection only detects the FFB, and four
class detection classified the oil pam based on their ripeness

accuracy of deep learning models. As the algorithms become
more sophisticated, the image recognition accuracy of oil palm
fruit bunches or one-class detection will improve.

that rely on machine vision.

detection is 98.97%, and the accuracy for four class detection
has the highest accuracy, 77.20%. Furthermore, this work
is also limited by considering the number of the dataset
compared to [23]. This is in contrast with previous studies
and can be explained by several reasons, such as the lack
of the number of images for FFB. We used Google Images
to collect and build our dataset. The imbalanced number of
images between the classes also plays an essential role in
training deep learning. Other than that, in [23], they only
considered the detection of tomatoes, but in this paper, we
consider the detection and classification of FFB.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
This paper employs the deep learning approach using trans-
fer learning with YOLO algorithms to detect and classify the
oil palm FFB based on their ripeness level. Other YOLO-
based models have been compared to the proposed model. The
experimental findings have revealed that the proposed model,
which is the YOLOv4 model, performs well in detection, with
an mAP of 98.97%, an F1-score of 0.96, and an average IoU of
68.80%, the highest among the models for one class detection.
For four class detection, YOLOv4 show the highest accuracy
with an mAP of 77.20%, and F1 score of 0.7 and an average
IoU of 51.68%. This research is the first step toward a more
profound understanding of using deep learning in agricultural
activities. This paper is limited to oil palm detection and
classification but could be extended to the other agriculture
sectors in the future.
