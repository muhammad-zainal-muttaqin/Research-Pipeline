A B S T R A C T
Table grape thinning requires selective removal of 20–40% of berries from dense clusters. In practice, workers 
decide which berries to remove by considering both the approximate berry count and local 3D spatial charac­
teristics such as crowding and relative positioning. Automating this task is challenging because conventional 2D 
image-based approaches suffer from occlusion-related counting errors and lack explicit 3D spatial information 
necessary for reliable manipulation. We propose a robot-integrated vision pipeline that reconstructs grape bunch 
structure from posed multi-view RGB images. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) is used to learn a volumetric scene 
representation, from which a dense, low-noise point cloud is extracted via depth back-projection, and RANSAC- 
based geometric fitting models individual berries and stems, enabling berry-level segmentation and orientation 
estimation for manipulation planning. The perception pipeline uses an eye-in-hand RealSense D405 camera 
mounted on a Fanuc CRX-5iA collaborative robot. Camera poses are derived from the robot kinematic chain, 
allowing the reconstructed point cloud and detected berry centers to be expressed directly in the metric robot 
base frame without external scale recovery. In robot-mounted RealSense D405 experiments on 10 grape bunches, 
RANSAC sphere fitting achieved a counting MAE of 0.50 berries, RMSE of 0.71 berries, and mean center 
localization error of 2.71 mm. On a 52-bunch benchmark, RANSAC sphere fitting outperforms the learning-based 
SoftGroup++ method for 3D berry instance segmentation (92.1% vs 82.8% average precision) without requiring 
annotated training data. In 35 manipulation trials, the system achieved an 85.7% pre-grasp reachability rate and 
an 83.3% conditional target success rate demonstrating an end-to-end pipeline from neural reconstruction to 
manipulation-ready berry poses.
1. Introduction
Berry thinning is a critical horticultural practice in table grape 
cultivation that involves strategic removal of berries during early bunch 
development to improve fruit quality for better market value. This 
operation provides adequate spacing for remaining berries to expand, 
improves airflow within the bunch to reduce disease pressure from 
pathogens, and optimizes bunch compactness for premium market 
presentation. The impact of proper berry thinning on final product 
quality is substantial and multifaceted. Berry thinning prevents exces­
sive bunch compactness which is one of the most important physical 
barriers to avoid fungal infections (Roberto et al., 2015). Thinning 
treatments have been shown to increase berry weight, total soluble 
solids, sugar content (fructose and glucose), berry firmness, and mineral 
contents, while decreasing titratable acidity and improving the ratio of 
sugar to acid (Jia et al., 2023). The timing of thinning is equally critical. 
Early berry thinning (7 days after flowering) is most effective in 
reducing bunch compactness and achieving optimal bunch quality 
(Ivaniˇsevi´c et al., 2020; Roberto et al., 2015). Research has demon­
strated that appropriate thinning reduces grape yield but increases mean 
cluster weight, total phenols, anthocyanins, and individual berry size, 
while the bunch becomes more open and less inclined toward disease 
development (Karoglan et al., 2014). Beyond fruit quality, controlled 
bagging treatments combined with proper thinning timing can effec­
tively regulate light exposure, influencing sugar metabolism and 
anthocyanin accumulation during berry maturation (Zha et al., 2019). 
These quality improvements directly affect market price, making berry 
thinning economically essential despite its labor intensity.
However, berry thinning faces critical operational constraints in 
commercial production. The suitable period for this operation is limited

The practice of high-quality berry thinning by skilled farmers relies 
on understanding both the spatial arrangement and developmental po­
tential of berries within the bunch. Accurate berry counting serves as a 
key information for effective thinning decisions. Farmers must first ac­
cess the initial berry count (typically 60–80 berries in early bunch 
development) to determine the removal target (typically 35–40 berries 
for premium table grapes) based on variety-specific quality standards. 
As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the temporal transformation from pre-thinning 
to harvest demonstrates why geometric and developmental assessment 
is critical: an initial bunch of 70 berries at early development (left) 
undergoes strategic thinning during the narrow 2–3 weeks post- 
flowering window to approximately 35 berries (middle), which then 
expand over 2 months to produce marketable full-grown bunch (right). 
This progression reveals that farmers must assess not only the current 
berry count but also predict the 3D spatial arrangement evaluating 
whether remaining berries will have adequate spacing to expand 
without physical interference from neighbors. The target bunch archi­
tecture after thinning (Fig. 1b) follows a hierarchical structure: a total 
length of 7–8 cm with berries distributed across multiple lateral sub- 
branches, typically maintaining 4–5 berries per 2 sub-branches in the 
upper region, 3 berries per 5 sub-branches in the middle section, and 2 
berries per 5 sub-branches in the lower portion, yielding target counts of 
28–40 berries. This spatial organization serves multiple agronomic ob­
jectives: adequate spacing allows each berry to expand to target 
marketable size without physical interference from neighbors, improved 
airflow between berries reduces humidity and fungal disease pressure, 
and the open architecture facilitates uniform light exposure promoting 
anthocyanin development and sugar accumulation that characterizes 
premium table grapes. This geometric reasoning about 3D structure, 
spacing adequacy, and growth potential cannot be reliably inferred from 
single 2D images due to occlusion and perspective ambiguity. For ro­
botic execution, this task further demands accurate 3D localization of 
individual berries in metric coordinates compatible with robot motion 
planning, along with collision-aware approach.
2D image-based methods achieve fast berry detection and counting 
using deep learning with hand-crafted features or end-to-end neural 
networks (Buayai et al., 2021; Buayai et al., 2023b; Du & Liu, 2023; Woo 
et al., 2023a). Early work employed regression model based on hand 
crafted features (Buayai et al., 2021), while recent approaches use 
convolutional neural networks for predicting berry counting from single 
image (Woo et al., 2023a) or instance segmentation using advanced 
architectures like Swin transformer with adaptive spatial feature fusion 
(Du & Liu, 2023). While instance segmentation methods like AS-SwinT 
achieve impressive 2D detection performance (AP > 90%), these 
methods suffer from fundamental limitations that make them inade­
quate for precision berry thinning. First, they exhibit view-dependent 
predictions: the same bunch yields different berry counts from 
different viewpoints due to occlusion. Reported counting errors increase 
systematically with bunch density, reaching mean absolute errors of

cannot provide geometric reasoning about actual 3D inter-berry dis­

Accurate 3D positioning for robotic manipulation requires depth 
information that 2D method cannot provide. Single-view depth sensing 
approaches attempted to bridge this gap by integrating RGB-D cameras 
or stereo vision for 3D positioning, Buayai et al. (2023a) combined 
depth sensing technology with neural networks to control robotic arm 
end-effector for grape berry approaching, integrating 2D berry detection 
with depth maps to estimate 3D target coordinates and training neural 
networks to map detected berry positions to feasible robot end-effector 
poses. However, single-view depth sensing achieves only 20% approach 
success due to challenges for outdoor agricultural applications: noisy 
depth estimates under variable lighting. Furthermore, single-view depth 
provides no geometric consistency across viewpoints, depth estimates 
for the same berry differ depending on viewing angle and cannot handle 
severe occlusion where berries are completely hidden from the sensor’s 
line of sight. These depth errors propagate directly to collision checking 
and trajectory planning limiting reliability for safe manipulation in 
cluttered bunches.
Multi-view reconstruction methods offer the potential for com­
plete geometric understanding. Classical Structure-from-Motion (SfM) 
and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) have been applied to grape bunch 
reconstruction (Woo et al., 2023b). However, photometric stereo 
matching faces significant challenges for grape berries. This approach 
relies on identifying corresponding texture features across multiple 
views. Grape berries present two key challenges for this approach: (1) 
surface texture-poor that provides few distinctive matching points, and 
(2) small physical size (typically 10–20 mm diameter) relative to prac­
tical reconstruction resolution. These factors result is sparse, incomplete 
reconstruction where many berries are missed or only partially 
captured.
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) (Mildenhall et al., 2020) overcome 
these texture-matching limitations through learned volumetric repre­
sentations from multi-view images to render photorealistic images from 
arbitrary viewpoints. Unlike classical MVS methods that relies on 
explicit feature matching and produce sparse point clouds, NeRF learns 
continuous volumetric scene representations without requiring explicit 
depth supervision (Li et al., 2025). Recent agricultural applications 
demonstrate NeRF’s capability for high-fidelity crop reconstruction of 
small and complex crop structures: rice panicles achieving mean F1 
score of 86.9% and IoU of 79.8% (Yang et al., 2024), cherries (Hu et al., 
2024), general plant phenotyping (Smitt et al., 2023), and tomato 
morphological analysis (Choi et al., 2024).
However, existing agricultural NeRF works focus on visualization 
and phenotyping rather than robotic manipulation, revealing critical 
gaps when extended to manipulation tasks. Three fundamental limita­
tions persist: 2D mask fusion approaches (Meyer et al., 2024; Smitt et al., 
2023) project neural network detections onto reconstructed geometry, 
inheriting view-dependent errors and requiring heuristic merging rules 
that introduce inconsistencies when berries appear differently across 
views. Shrestha et al. (2025) render novel views for training pose esti­
mation networks but require separate networks per object category and 
provide no geometric primitives for collision checking. Phenotyping 
pipelines (Choi et al., 2024) require separate calibration procedures 
with known-dimension reference objects to recover metric scale and, 
critically, do not validate end-to-end manipulation.
NeRF was originally proposed for novel view synthesis by learning a 
continuous volumetric scene representation from multi-view images to 
render photorealistic images from arbitrary viewpoints. However, the 
learned density field implicitly encodes scene geometry, enabling 
extraction of dense 3D point clouds through ray-based depth estimation 
and back-projection. We leverage this geometric byproduct for down­
stream segmentation and manipulation rather than rendering capability 
itself. Critically for our application, NeRF’s volumetric learning does not 
require explicit feature matching between views, making it well suited 
to texture-poor grape surfaces where classical multi-view stereo 
methods produce sparse, incomplete reconstructions. By extracting in­
dividual berry instances from reconstructed point cloud requires seg­
mentation. For instance-level segmentation of 3D point clouds, two 
primary approaches are commonly adopted: learning based approaches 
and geometry-based approaches. Learning-based 3D instance segmen­
tation method such as SoftGroup (Vu et al., 2022) directly segmenting 
3D point clouds using neural networks. These methods require extensive 
manually annotated 3D training data, which is costly to produce. 
Furthermore, when berries are small and densely clustered with signif­
icant occlusion, learned segmentation can fail to reliably separate in­
dividual instances.
In contrast, geometry-based approaches rely on explicit shape priors 
and parametric primitive fitting. In this work, we adopt a RANSAC based 
geometric primitive fitting strategy that leverages the strong prior that 
grape berries are approximately by spherical. The method exploits the 
geometric constraint: berry surfaces are well approximated by spheres, 
whereas stem structures are more accurately modeled by connected 
cylindrical primitives. This distinction enables robust separation 
through primitive fitting without dependence on annotated training 
data. Recent work has demonstrated RANSAC’s robustness to imaging 
noise and outliers in agricultural point clouds, achieving R2 > 0.90 for 
diameter and angle measurements even with up to 10% outlier

combined with RANSAC shape detection (Schnabel et al., 2007) in 
controlled settings (Mack et al., 2018).
This work builds on established components, including NeRF-based 
reconstruction, RANSAC primitive fitting, skeletonization, and robotic 
motion planning, and focuses on the integration into a robot-ready 
perception-to-manipulation pipeline for grape berry thinning. Existing 
agricultural NeRF studies mainly focus on visualization, trait measure­
ment, or synthetic data generation, whereas robotic berry thinning re­
quires relative berry positions to robot coordinate system, local stem- 
aware approach orientations, and collision-aware motion execution. 
To address these requirements, our system integrates robot-derived 
camera poses, NeRF-based volumetric reconstruction, depth-based 
point cloud extraction, RANSAC geometric primitive fitting, stem skel­
eton extraction, and motion planning into a complete pipeline that 
produces berry-level 3D positions and 6-DoF approach poses for robotic 
execution. The key insight is that neural volumetric reconstruction and 
geometric shape detection are complementary: NeRF learns a contin­
uous scene representation from multi-view RGB images, from which a 
dense point cloud is extracted through depth rendering and back- 
projection, while RANSAC sphere fitting provides robust, training-free 
berry segmentation by exploiting spherical geometry priors. By 
deriving camera poses from robot kinematics, the extracted grape bunch 
point cloud is expressed directly in the metric robot base frame, enabling 
end-to-end validation from perception to physical manipulation.

develop an integrated eye-in-hand multi-view reconstruction 
framework in which RGB images from a robot-mounted RealSense 
D405, and camera poses derived from robot forward kinematics

2. Materials and methods
This section presents our complete pipeline for grape bunch recon­
struction and robotic manipulation through multi-view neural recon­
struction and RANSAC fitting. Fig. 2 illustrates the five-stages approach: 
(1) multi-view data acquisition using an eye-in-hand camera mounted 
on industrial manipulator (Section 2.2), (2) NeRF-based 3D recon­
struction trained from 360-degree view images with metric-scale pres­
ervation through robot-derived camera poses (Section 2.1), (3) 
RANSAC-based primitive detection to segment individual berries and 
stems in the point cloud (Section 2.3), (4) grape bunch structure 
extraction from extracted primitives and 6-DoF berry pose optimization 
that aligns end-effector approach direction with berry-stem attachment 
geometry (Section 2.4), (5) robot motion planning and execution using 
optimized berry poses as manipulation targets (Section 2.5).

2.2. Data acquisition
The images used to train the NeRF model for the manipulation 
pipeline were captured with an Intel RealSense D405 camera mounted 
on a Fanuc CRX-5iA robot with an eye-in-hand configuration. This 
arrangement supports repeatable viewpoint sampling and provides a 
consistent relationship between camera and the robot end-effector, 
which is required to express reconstructed geometry in the robot base 
frame.
2.2.1. Image capture
RGB images were recorded at a resolution of 840 × 480 pixels using 
only the RGB sensor of an Intel RealSense D405 mounted on Fanuc CRX- 
5iA with custom end-effector (Fig. 3a). The D405 depth channel was not 
used for NeRF reconstruction or point-cloud generation. To ensure 
photometric consistency across viewpoints and minimize auto-exposure 
artifacts that degrade NeRF training quality, camera settings were held 
fixed during acquisition: ISO was set to 100, auto-exposure was disabled 
with a fixed exposure time of 20 ms, and white balance was locked to 
4000 K to compensate for greenhouse lighting conditions.
The robot was commanded through a complete 360◦circular tra­
jectory around each target grape bunch. The trajectory was planned in 
the robot joint space, with the camera positioned at a working distance 
of approximately 300 mm from the bunch center. Total of 279 images 
were captured per acquisition sequence, corresponding to 1.29◦angular 
increment between consecutive frames. This sampling density provides 
substantial overlap between consecutive views and was further evalu­
ated through the view-sampling ablation study reported in Section 3.2.
During acquisition, each saved RGB image was paired with camera 
pose obtained from the robot kinematic chain through ROS 2 TF2 at the 
time of saving (Fig. 3b). The transform base link to D405 frame was 
converted to the OpenGL camera convention allowing NeRF recon­
struction to be expressed directly in the robot base coordinate frame. 
The resulting posed image sequence therefore allowed NeRF recon­
struction to be expressed directly in the robot base coordinate frame. 
Fig. 3c shows a representative circular D405 acquisition trajectory and 
the corresponding camera viewpoints around the target bunch.
Although the proposed reconstruction pipeline does not use active 
depth sensing, RGB image quality remains important because NeRF 
optimization depends on multi-view photometric consistency. The effect 
of RGB acquisition quality was therefore evaluated separately using a 
paired robot-mounted D405/iPhone comparison, as reported in Section 
3.4. The D405 depth channel may still be useful in future deployment for 
coarse target-bunch localization, initial bunch-center estimation, scan- 
trajectory initialization, ROI placement, and workspace-level collision 
awareness.

Camera extrinsic poses, the 6-DoF position and orientation of the 
camera for each captured image are conventionally estimated using 
structure-from-motion (SfM), with COLMAP (Schonberger & Frahm, 
2016) being the de facto preprocessing tool in NeRF workflows. COL­
MAP performs feature extraction, cross-view matching, and bundle 
adjustment to recover camera poses from image correspondences. 
However, this offline processing adds computational overhead (typically 
several minutes for hundreds of images) and, more critically, produces 
pose estimates in an arbitrary coordinate frame with unknown metric 
scale. In our robotic pipeline, we bypass COLMAP entirely by deriving 
camera poses directly from robot’s kinematic chain via the ROS 2 TF2 
(Transform Framework). For each captured image, its timestamp was 
recorded, and the corresponding camera pose was queried from TF2 at 
that timestamp by computing the transform chain from the robot base 
frame to the camera optical frame. This approach provides two advan­
tages: (1) it eliminates the computational cost and potential failure 
modes of feature-based pose estimation, and (2) it produces camera 
poses in the metric robot base coordinate system, enabling the recon­
structed point cloud to be used directly for robot motion planning 
without additional frame transformations or scale calibration.
2.2.3. Environment filtering
Before berry detection, we remove background points outside the 
region of interest to eliminate false positive detection from environ­
mental clutter. The NeRF reconstruction produces a dense point cloud of 
entire visible scene, including the target grape bunch, neighboring 
bunch, canopy, and distance object.
Our filtering strategy leverages the known camera trajectory geom­
etry from eye-in-hand acquisition setup. During data acquisition the 
camera was positioned at a fixed working distance of 300 mm from the 
estimated bunch center throughout the 360◦circular trajectory. We 
define a spherical region of interest (ROI) centered at this target position 
with radius of 200 mm (diameter 400 mm). This radius was chosen to 
provide sufficient margin beyond typical grape bunch dimension.
As shown in Fig. 4, this spherical filtering effectively isolates the 
grape bunch while removing the environment objects in the raw 
reconstruction and provides a cleaner input for RANSAC-based berry 
detection. This design assumes that the target bunch has been selected 
before reconstruction and that the objective is to recover berry-level 
geometry for one bunch at a time. Multi-bunch and canopy-level 
reconstruction would require additional target-bunch detection, 
bunch-level instance separation, and adaptive scan planning, which are 
discussed as future work in Section 4.
2.3. Berry segmentation using RANSAC geometry fitting
The geometric characteristics of grape bunch point clouds motivate 
the adoption of RANSAC-based sphere detection for berry segmentation. 
As reported by Mack et al. (2018), berry surfaces exhibit approximately 
spherical and smoothly curved geometry in laser-scanned point clouds, 
whereas stem structures are more accurately described by connected 
cylindrical primitives. This distinction in local surface geometry makes 
geometric primitive fitting well suited for separating berries from stem 
structures. In particular, the spherical shape of berries supports robust 
detection through sphere fitting, while the geometry of stems is natu­
rally excluded under appropriate geometric constraints.
Grape berries are approximately spherical and therefore amenable to 
detection via geometric primitive fitting. We employed the RANSAC 
algorithm for robustly identify multiple spherical structures in the 
presence of noise and outliers (Schnabel et al., 2007). Each sphere is 
parameterized by a center c ∈R3 and a radius r > 0. The algorithm 
operates iteratively by randomly sampling minimal point sets from the 
point cloud, fitting sphere hypotheses to these samples, and determining 
the subset of points that support each hypothesis. Sphere hypotheses are 
generated by randomly sampling four points from the point cloud and 
computing an initial estimate of the sphere center c = (x, y, z)⊤and 
radius r. For each hypothesis, an inlier set I is defined as the set of 
points whose distance to the sphere surface is below a predefined 
tolerance ∊. A point p is classified as an inlier if |‖p −c‖ −r| ≤∊. If the 
number of inliers exceeds a minimum threshold Nmin and rmin ≤r ≤rmax, 
the sphere parameters are refined using least-squares fitting over all 
inliers and the hypothesis is accepted; otherwise, it is rejected. This 
procedure is repeated for a maximum of Kmax iterations to identify the 
sphere with the largest inlier support. Since grape bunch point clouds 
typically contain multiple berries, sphere detection is performed itera­
tively. After each successful detection, the corresponding inlier points 
are removed from the point cloud, and the detection process is applied to 
the remaining points. Iteration continues until either an insufficient 
number of points remains, or no further valid sphere hypotheses can be 
identified. To avoid biologically implausible detections, candidate 
spheres are constrained to an admissible radius range [rmin, rmax] derived 
from known berry sizes of the target grape observed from the dataset in 
Section 3.5.1. These parameters are summarized in Table 1.

each berry so that the end effector can align its tool axis with the

3D berry center coordinate alone is insufficient for reliable manipulation 
because it defines only the position of the target, leaving end-effector

located deep inside a dense cluster cannot be reliably approached using

detected berry by leveraging the spatial relationship between the berry

First, we extract a branch and stem skeleton from the residual point 
cloud obtained after removing berry inliers from the RANSAC sphere 
fitting stage. Second, we optimize each berry’s orientation such that its 
approach axis is consistent with the local stem attachment direction 
inferred from skeleton. The resulting rotation matrix, together with the 
berry center, defines 6-DoF target pose used to compute a pre-grasp pose 
and subsequent approach motion.
2.4.1. Grape bunch skeletonization
To recover the supporting structure of the bunch, we apply skeleto­

number of remaining points after excluding all berry inliers. We 
employed Laplacian-based contraction (LBC) (Cao et al., 2010) to iter­

can be discontinuous due to occlusion, varying point density, and partial 
incompleteness, we further impose connectivity by constructing a 
minimum spanning tree (MST) over the skeleton points (Meyer et al., 
2023). In Fig. 6c, the resulting graph provides a coherent representation 
of the branch and stem structure and serves as a geometric reference for 
estimating berry attachment directions in the subsequent orientation 
optimization.

2.5. Robot control
Robot motion is executed by a ROS 2 motion node integrating TF2 for 
frame management, MoveIt 2 (Coleman et al., 2014) for motion plan­
ning and trajectory execution, and a gripper controller for end effector 
actuation. At initialization, the node loads the set of optimized berry 
poses computed in Section 2.4. Each berry instance j is represented by a 
target 6 DoF pose Ttarget
j 
and a corresponding pre grasp pose Tpre

Fig. 9 illustrates the manipulation pipeline. Fig. 9a shows the gripper 
coordinate frame definition. The gripper local z axis defines the 
approach direction and is aligned with the optimized berry orientation. 
During execution, the gripper approaches along the negative z direction, 
with the jaws-oriented perpendicular to the approach vector to mini­
mize disturbance to neighboring berries.
Pre grasp poses are generated by offsetting 80 mm along the negative 
approach direction from each target pose. Fig. 9b visualizes the pre- 
grasp frames for an approach target berry. The diversity of orienta­
tions reflects the geometric optimization described in Section 2.4.2, 
where approach directions are aligned with local stem attachment 
structure rather than uniformly outward.

2.6. Implementations
NeRF training and point cloud export were performed on a work­
station with an AMD EPYC 7543P CPU (32 cores, 64 threads), with GPU 
acceleration provided by an NVIDIA A100 (80 GB PCIe). The system 
used NVIDIA driver 565.57.01 and CUDA 12.7 to accelerate NeRF 
optimization and dense point sampling.
For robotic experiments were executed using ROS 2 Humble running 
on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB as the robot control computer. The 
manipulator platform as Fanuc CRX-5iA (6-DoF). An Intel RealSense 
D405 RGB-D camera was used for scene capture. The end-effector was a 
custom RGIC gripper, and gripper actuation was limited to open-close 
motion with a closing width constrained to the berry size to avoid 
excessive contact during approach validation.

3.2. View-sampling requirement for NeRF reconstruction and berry 
detection
To clarify the inference-stage input requirement, we conducted a 
view-sampling ablation study using the full 360◦acquisition sequence as 
the reference. The original sequence contained 279 posed RGB images 
captured at approximately 1.29◦angular increments. We generated 
reduced training sets by uniformly subsampling the original trajectory at 
angular intervals of 3◦, 9◦, 12◦, 20◦, and 25◦, and trained a separate 
NeRF model for each setting using the same reconstruction and point- 
cloud export parameters.
Fig. 11 shows the sampled camera viewpoints for each angular in­
terval. As the angular interval increases, the number of training views 
decreases and the overlap between neighboring views become smaller. 
The 25◦settings therefore represent the sparest sampling condition, 
while the 3◦settings represent the densest reduced-view condition. This 
experiment evaluates the trade-off between acquisition density recon­
struction fidelity, and downstream RANSAC-based berry detection.
For each sampling condition, we compared the reconstructed point 
cloud with the full-view 279-image reconstruction. Let A denote the 
point cloud reconstructed from sampled views and B denote the point 
cloud reconstructed from the full 279-view sequence. We computed 
bidirectional nearest-neighbor distance between A and B. The A→B di­
rection measures how closely the sampled reconstruction agrees with 
the full-view reference, whereas the B→A direction measures how 
completely the sampled reconstruction preserves geometry present in 
the full-view reconstruction. For each direction, we report the mean and 
95th percentile of the nearest-neighbor distances as robust geometric 
discrepancy metrics. In addition, we report the symmetric Hausdorff 
maximum, Hmax = max(maxdA→B, maxdB→A), as a conservative worst- 
case deviation. Because Hmax is sensitive to isolated floating points, it 
is interpreted together with the mean and 95th percentile distances 
rather than as the sole reconstruction-quality indicator.
Fig. 12 shows rendered images and point cloud distance maps for 
each sampling condition. Quantitative results are summarized in 
Table 3. The geometric distance increased as the angular sampling 
became sparser. At 3◦sampling, the sampled reconstruction was very 
close to the full-view reference, with mean distances of 0.286 mm for A 
→ B and 0.281 mm for B → A. At 25◦sampling, the mean distances 
increased to 0.801 mm and 0.824 mm, respectively. The 95th-percentile 
distance showed the same trend, increasing from 0.697/0.667 mm at 3◦
to 2.338/1.9262 mm at 25◦. This confirms that denser view sampling 
produces point clouds that are geometrically closer to the full 279-view 
reconstruction, while the sparsest 25◦condition produces the largest 
deviation.
Downstream berry detection followed the same trend. The ground- 
truth berry counts for this bunch was 55. The 25◦and 20◦settings 
detected only 45 and 48 berries, respectively, indicating that sparse 
angular sampling loses local berry surface structure needed for reliable 
RANSAC sphere fitting. The 12◦settings improved detection to 53 
berries, while the 9◦and 3◦settings detected all 55 berries. Rendering 
metrics were also highest at 9◦and 3◦sampling, with the 9◦settings 
achieving PSNR of 18.33 dB and SSIM of 0.61, and the 3◦setting 
achieving PSNR of 18.0 dB and SSIM of 0.59.
Overall, this ablation shows that the proposed method requires a 
posed multi-view image sequence rather than a single image. In this 
representative case, 9◦and 3◦angular sampling detected all 55 berries, 
while 12◦, 20◦, and 25◦sampling progressively lost berries. Thus, 
approximately 9◦angular spacing can be interpreted as an empirical 
lower bound for this bunch. Nevertheless, the main pipeline uses the full 
279-view sequence because the objective of robotic berry thinning is not 
only correct counting, but robust reconstruction of berry surfaces, 
occluded regions, and fine stem geometry for downstream segmentation 
and manipulation planning.

3.5.1. Dataset overview
After validating the proposed berry detection pipeline using robot- 
derived-pose D405 reconstruction in Section 3.3, we further evaluated 
the RANSAC sphere fitting method on a larger high-resolution bench­
mark dataset. This dataset comprised 52 grape bunches captured during 
March-April 2024 using an iPhone 15 at 3840 × 2160 pixels and 60 fps. 
Each bunch was recorded as a 360◦handheld video, from which 80–120 
frames were extracted using FFmpeg to provide sufficient view overlap 
for multi-view reconstruction (in Fig. 18).
This dataset was used only for large-scale berry instance segmenta­
tion and counting evaluation. It was not used for robot control because 
camera poses were estimated using COLMAP's structure-from-motion 
pipeline. NeRF models were trained following Section 2.1 methodol­
ogy. Since COLMAP produces scale-ambiguous reconstructions, metric 
scale was recovered using a reference calibration tag (45.0 mm height) 
positioned adjacent to each bunch during capture (in Fig. 26). The 
reconstructed tag height determined a per-bunch scale factor applied 
uniformly to all coordinates.
Berry diameters range 10.2–24.8 mm (mean: 17.43 mm) as shown in 
Fig. 19a with bunch sizes ranging from 12 to 89 berries (mean: 53.5 ±
18.2), illustrated per sample in Fig. 19b.
3.5.2. Instance-level segmentation metrics
Instance-Instance-level berry segmentation was evaluated using the 
3D IoU defined in Section 3.3.1. For both RANSAC and SoftGroup++, 
each predicted berry instance was represented as a parameterized 
sphere over the evaluated point cloud. For SoftGroup++, the mask 
corresponds to the predicted instance output. For RANSAC, the mask 
corresponds to the set of points assigned to each fitted sphere.
Predicted and ground-truth berry instances were matched using one- 
to-one IoU-based matching. A prediction was counted as a true positive 
when its point-mask IoU with an unmatched ground-truth instance 
exceeded the evaluation threshold. Unmatched predictions were coun­
ted as false positives, and unmatched ground-truth instances were 
counted as false negatives. Average precision and recall were reported at 
IoU thresholds of 0.25 and 0.50, denoted as AP@0.25, AP@0.50, 
AR@0.25, and AR@0.50. Because berry was the only evaluated object 
class, all reported metrics correspond to single-class berry instance 
segmentation performance.
For matched true-positive instances, berry-center localization error 
was computed using the same definition as in Section 3.3.1.

center localization error remains within a few millimeters, and radius

3.6. Bunch structure reconstruction
Direct ground-truth evaluation of grape bunch skeletonization is not 
feasible, as no physical reference centerline can be reliably obtained, 
and manual annotation of branch centerlines in occluded point clouds 
introduces unacceptable subjectively. We therefore adopt a two-level 
evaluation strategy applied to the same subset of 8 bunches 
comprising 491 berries: first, an indirect geometric consistency evalu­
ation that quantifies how accurately the extracted skeleton captures the 
observed stem point cloud geometry; second, a topological assignment 
evaluation that assesses how reliably each berry is assigned to its current 
parent stem segment.
Geometric consistency evaluation. The evaluation pipeline is 
illustrated in Fig. 21. The residual point cloud obtained after removing 
RANSAC berry inliers (Fig. 21a) was first contracted into a 1D skeleton 
via Laplacian-based contraction (LBC), then connected into a tree to­
pology via minimum spanning tree (MST) construction (Fig. 21b and c). 
Each MST edge was replaced by a fitted cylinder with fixed radius. The 
resulting cylinder surfaces were densely sampled (Fig. 21d) to generate a 
synthetic point cloud Ṕstem that is geometrically comparable to the 
original residual cloud Pstem.
Geometric fidelity is evaluated using directional Hausdorff distances 
between the synthetic cylinder point cloud Ṕstem (Fig. 22a) and the 
original residual point cloud Pstem (Fig. 22b). The cylinder fit direction 
(Ṕstem → Pstem) measures how closely fitted cylinders approximate 
observed stem geometry, while the branch coverage direction (Pstem → 
Ṕstem) measures how completely the cylinder model captures the original 
stem structure. Results are summarized in Table 8.
Both directions achieve sub-millimeter mean accuracy, confirming 
that the Laplcian-based contraction produces geometrically faithful 
skeleton representations. The per-point distance map (Fig. 22c) shows 
that errors are spatially concentrated at fine secondary pedicels and 
peripheral branch tips, while the main rachis and primary lateral 
branches exhibit close agreement (predominantly blue, < 1 mm). The 
elevatedṔstem → Pstemmaximum of 15.17 mm reflects isolated MST edges 
bridging spatial gaps at branching junctions; this affects fewer than 1% 
of points (99th percentile: 2.08 mm) and does not propagate to berry 
orientation estimates, which depend on the direction to the nearest 
skeleton point rather than global surface accuracy.
Topological assignment evaluation. Geometric accuracy alone 
does not guarantee that each berry is assigned to its anatomically correct 
parent stem. To evaluate topological correctness, we visualize berry-to- 
stem correspondences by colorizing each berry’s point cloud according 
to its assigned stem segment, as shown in Fig. 23a. This visualization 
enables human inspection of assignment quality across the full bunch 
without requiring explicit ground-truth labelling.
However, topological accuracy cannot be assessed from geometric 
distance alone, as two distinct failure modes produce incorrect assign­
ments despite geometrically plausible skeleton representations. These 
are illustrated in Fig. 23b and c.
The first failure mode, shown in Fig. 23b, arises from biological 
ambiguity: berries from adjacent sub-branches physically penetrate 
neighboring clusters, causing a berry’s nearest skeleton point to belong 
to an adjacent branch rather than its true anatomical parent. The 
orange-highlighted berries indicate shared assignments to stem.
The second failure mode, shown in Fig. 23c, arises from insufficient 
reconstruction resolution: fine sub-branches that is insufficient recon­
struction resolution are absent from the skeleton, causing the berry to be 
assigned to a geometrically closer but anatomically incorrect stem 
segment. In Fig. 23c contrasts the actual stem attachment direction with 
the incorrectly assigned closest reconstructed segment.
Both failure cases produce shared berry-to-stem assignments where a 
single stem segment is matched to two or more berries but arise from 
fundamentally different causes. To quantify this, a shared-assignment 
rate error was defined as a stem segment matched to two or more 
berries across the same 8-bunch evaluation subset. The mean error rate 
was 14.1 ± 9.3% (range: 4.3–28.2%), with two bunches exceeding 25%. 
Most shared assignments involved pairs of berries mapped to the same 
stem, with no triple assignments observed. A moderate positive corre­
lation between bunch size and the absolute number of shared berries

4. Limitations and future work
The proposed system demonstrates an end-to-end perception-to- 
manipulation pipeline for robotic grape berry thinning, but several 
limitations remain before production deployment. First, the current 
implementation requires a posed multi-view scan of one target bunch at 
a time. The view-sampling ablation indicates that overly sparse angular 
coverage can degrade reconstruction quality, berry counting, and berry 
localization, which may affect downstream thinning decisions such as 
local spacing estimation and berry removal selection. Therefore, the 
present system prioritizes dense 360◦acquisition for reliable berry-level 
reconstruction. Although this target-bunch-level formulation is appro­
priate for robotic thinning, production environments often contain 
multiple neighboring bunches, leaves, and canopy structures. In this 
study, these surrounding structures were intentionally removed using 
spherical region-of-interest filtering, and multi-bunch or canopy-level 
reconstruction was not fully evaluated. Future extension could

5. Conclusions
This paper presents an integrated perception-to-manipulation pipe­
line for robotic grape berry thinning, combining NeRF-based 3D 
reconstruction, RANSAC geometric segmentation, and structure-aware 
pose optimization. Three principal findings emerge from the evaluation.
First, deriving camera poses directly from robot forward kinematics 
via ROS 2 TF2 eliminates the scale ambiguity inherent in COLMAP- 
based NeRF workflows, producing metric-coordinate point clouds in 
the robot base frame without additional calibration. Scale verification 
confirmed sub-millimeter accuracy (45.9 mm reconstructed vs. 45.0 mm 
reference), establishing that reconstructed berry positions are directly 
usable for motion planning.
Second, RANSAC sphere fitting on NeRF-exported point clouds 
achieves 92.1% average precision and MAE of 1.1 berries, out­
performing the learning-based SoftGroup++ baseline (82.8% AP) and 
reducing counting error by 52% compared to state-of-the-art 2D 
methods, without requiring any annotated training data. The geometry- 
based approach generalizes across bunch densities and berry sizes where 
learned methods exhibit systematic under-segmentation.
Third, stem-aligned 6-DoF pose optimization yields an end-to-end 
grip success rate of 68.6%, compared to 51.4% under a fixed-direction 
baseline — an absolute improvement of 17.2 percentage points. The 
ablation study reveals that orientation constraints reduce pre-grasp 
reachability by 14.3% due to stricter IK requirements, but this cost is 
recovered through a substantially higher collision-free grip rate (96% vs. 
78.3%), confirming that structure-aware orientation estimation is 
essential for reliable manipulation in dense grape clusters.
The primary remaining limitation is IK infeasibility under strict 6- 
DoF orientation constraints, which accounts for all target-stage fail­
ures. This stems from optimizing only the approach axis direction while 
leaving gripper roll unconstrained, occasionally producing wrist con­
figurations near joint limits. Joint-limit-aware pose optimization that 
jointly considers approach direction and wrist configuration represents 
the most direct path to improving end-to-end success. Computationally, 
NeRF training requires approximately 167 s per bunch; acceleration via 
Instant-NGP or 3D Gaussian Splatting is promising but requires careful 
validation to ensure sub-millimeter geometric accuracy is preserved, as 
manipulation success is sensitive to precise berry localization.
