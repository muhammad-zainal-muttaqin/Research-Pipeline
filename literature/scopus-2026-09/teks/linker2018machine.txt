A procedure for identifying apples in night-time orchard images was developed and tested
on two datasets totalling over 550 images of Golden Delicious trees captured on two years
with different cameras and lighting systems. The analysis started by detecting specular
reﬂection highlights and extracting sub-images (101 by 101 pixels) centred at those local
maxima. Each sub-image was reduced to a 676 Upright Speeded Up Robust Features (U-
SURF) vector. Close to 20,000 sub-images from one dataset were manually labelled as
“apple” or “not apple”. The latter group included parts of leaves, branches and other objects
which exhibited strong specular reﬂection. Seventy-two classiﬁers were trained with the
number of “apple” and “not apple” training samples ranging from 500 to 2000 and from
5000 to 10,000, respectively, and with vocabulary size ranging from 500 to 10,000. Mis-
classiﬁcations occurred mostly in dark and low contrast regions, which led to developing
alternate models based on the posterior probability that the classiﬁcation result was cor-
rect taking into account the sub-image entropy or intensity. Yield models were calibrated
for each dataset, using 20 random trees. For both datasets the overall yield estimate was
within 10% of the actual yield, and the standard deviation was around 30% of the average
tree yield. These results are similar to those reported in previous studies, but while these
previous studies used procedures calibrated and tested with images from the same dataset,
in the present study the classiﬁer trained with images from one dataset was successfully
applied to the second dataset.

1.
Introduction
Estimation of fruit load is one of the key elements required to
improve orchards' management. Knowledge of fruit load
spatial distribution within the orchard throughout the season
would allow the grower to allocate resources (water, fertiliser)
more efﬁciently, while estimation of the expected yield would
be useful for managing manpower and (post-) harvest logis-
tics. Since an automated system for performing such a task is
currently not available, growers typically rely on manual
sampling of a small number of trees, which is labour-
intensive, not necessarily representative and prone to large
errors. In recent years this challenging task has been consid-
ered in a number of studies, most of them involving imaging
as the main, and in most cases only, sensing platform (Bargoti
& Underwood, 2017; Dorj, Lee, & Han, 2013; Dorj, Lee, & Yun,

A large number of studies have investigated the use of
computer vision for fruit detection in general, as detailed in the
reviews of Jimenez, Ceres, and Pons (2000), Kapach, Barnea,
Mairon, Edan, and Ben Shahar (2012) and Gongal, Amatya,
Karkee, Zhang, and Lewis (2015). While the potential of
“advanced” technologies such as thermal imaging, hyper-
spectral imaging or time-of-ﬂight 3D imaging has been
demonstrated in some studies (Bulanon, Burks, Alchanatis,
2008, 2009; Gongal et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2016; Okamoto
& Lee, 2009; Stajnko, Lakota, & Hocevar, 2004; Stein, Bargoti,
& Underwood, 2016; Wachs, Stern, Burks, & Alchanatis, 2010),
the high cost of such type of equipment, and in some cases the
high level of training required in order to operate it or analyse
the data, limits its chance of adoption by growers. In this
respect, a system based on affordable, easy to operate colour
cameras would likely be more appealing to growers.
Image analysis can be conducted primarily via segmenta-
tion or via extraction of key-points around which some
analysis is conducted. The ﬁrst step of image segmentation is
pixel classiﬁcation, using colour and/or texture information
(e.g. Dorj et al., 2016; Jidong, De-An, Wei, & Shihong, 2016;
Linker, Cohen, & Naor, 2012; Liu et al., 2016; Payne et al., 2014;
Yamamoto, Guo, Yoshioka, & Ninomiya, 2014; Zhou, Dam-
erow, Sun, & Blanke, 2012). Bargoti and Underwood (2017)
recently showed that adding metadata (pixel location, row
number or sun azimuth) could improve signiﬁcantly this initial
segmentation. Next, the resulting probabilistic map is con-
verted into a binary map by applying some threshold and blob
classiﬁcation is achieved, either by comparing the blob shape
to a model of the fruit (e.g. Jidong et al., 2016; Linker et al., 2012;
Qureshi et al., 2017) or by using a classiﬁer that relies on
explicit blob features such as shape, colour, texture and size
(e.g. Yamamoto et al., 2014; Bargoti & Underwood, 2017). When
dealing with fruit that grows in clusters, if the goal is to
determine the number of individual fruit, an additional step is
usually required in order to separate overlapping fruit.
By using key-points as starting points for the analysis, one
avoids having to perform classiﬁcation of all the pixels in the
image, and the analysis is restricted to areas which have a
high probability of containing a fruit. In the studies of Nuske,
Achar, Bates, Narasimhan, and Singh (2011), Linker and
Kelman (2015) and Linker (2017), key-points were detected
by exploiting the radial symmetry of the light proﬁle around
specular highlights that occur on fruits which are approxi-
mately spherical. Alternatively, Sengupta and Lee (2014)
identiﬁed key-points by applying the circular Hough Trans-
form to detect fruit contours, while Kelman and Linker (2014)
analysed the light proﬁle along segments anchored at edge
pixels. In the present paper we opted for the option of using
specular highlights as key-points, and investigated the use of
machine-learning for classifying sub-images centred at those
key-points.
2.
Material and methods
2.1.
Image datasets
Two datasets of Golden Delicious mature trees images ac-
quired at night with artiﬁcial lighting were used in this study.
Both datasets were acquired at orchards located at the Mat-
ityahu Research Station in Northern Israel.
The ﬁrst dataset consisted of 316 images of 79 trees ac-
quired on July 4, 2016 using a Canon EOS50D camera equipped
with a 18e55 mm lens (set to 18 mm) and an external ﬂash
(Nikon SB-20 operated in “Wide” mode). The camera was
placed on a tripod at a height of 1.60 m approximately 3.6 m
from the tree trunks and both “landscape” and “portrait” im-
ages were captured. The 4852 by 3168 pixels images were
saved in jpeg format. The apparent diameter of most apples in
these images ranged from 50 to 100 pixels. Each tree was
photographed from both sides, and typical images are shown
in Fig. 1. Sixty three of these trees were picked individually
and their yield was recorded.
The second dataset was the “July 2014” dataset used in
Linker (2017). This dataset included 258 images which had
been captured using cameras of a different type (Canon
PowerShot G7) and a lighting system consisting of eight LED

2.2.
Image analysis
The ﬂowchart of the procedure is shown in Fig. 2. The ﬁrst step
consisted of ﬁnding key-points, i.e. pixels which are likely to
belong to apples (Step 1). This step was inspired from Linker
(2017) who noted that specular highlights (bright spots) are
present on most apples. Such bright spots were detected by
ﬁnding local intensity maxima in the grey-scale image I.
I ¼ 0:2989,R þ 0:5870,G þ 0:1140,B
where R, G and B denote the red, green and blue values in the
original image. A sub-image centred at the local maximum
was extracted at
each key-point,
and U-SURF
(Upright
Speeded Up Robust Features) descriptors were computed (Step
2). The size of the sub-image was 101 pixels by 101 pixels,
which was slightly larger than the size of typical apples (see
Fig. 3 below). U-SURF descriptors are an adaptation of the
SURF descriptors developed by Bay, Tuytelaars, and Gool
(2006, 2008), in which the orientation of the sub-image is not
computed (Bay et al., 2006, 2008). U-SURF descriptors are not
rotation-invariant but their computation is faster than that of
SURF descriptors and the loss of rotation-invariance is not an
issue when analysing images such as the ones considered in
the present study. The U-SURF descriptors were calculated on
an 8 by 8 grid at 4 scales (1.6; 3.2; 4.8 and 6.4), leading to 676
(132*4) descriptor vectors per sub-image. At the training stage
the descriptor vectors of labelled sub-images were used to
form a bag of visual words and train a Support Vector Machine
(SVM) classiﬁer (Csurka, Dance, Fan, Willamowski, & Bray,
2004) (Step 3a) while at the testing stage this classiﬁer was
used to classify the sub-image as “apple” or “not apple” based
on its representation using the same visual words (Step 3b).
Only 20 images selected randomly from the 2016 dataset
were used to develop and calibrate the whole procedure.
Altogether 19,854 local maxima were found in these 20 images
and the resulting sub-images were labelled manually as
“apple” (3419 sub-images) or “not apple” (16,435 sub-images).
Note that such labelling is inherently biased and different
observers tend to label images differently (Linker et al., 2012).
In the present study all images were labelled by a single
observer to maintain consistency as much as possible.
Selected sub-images are shown in Fig. 3. A sub-image was
labelled as “apple” whenever the human observer was able to
detect an apple in the sub-image, which led to labelling as
“apple” sub-images with severe occlusion (Fig. 3d and e). The
sub-images labelled as “not apple” corresponded to leaves,
branches and other objects which exhibited strong specular
reﬂection (Fig. 1fej). Of the labelled sub-images, 1296 “apple”
and 6024 “not apple” sub-images were selected randomly and
set aside as test set, while classiﬁers were calibrated using
some of the 12,534 remaining images (see details below).
Visual inspection of the classiﬁcation results for the cali-
bration images indicated that false positive and missed de-
tections occurred mostly in dark and/or low contrast regions
of the images. This was conﬁrmed by calculating the following
rates of correct classiﬁcation:

3.
Results and discussion
3.1.
Classiﬁer performance
Three factors inﬂuence the performance of the classiﬁer: the
number of “apple” training samples, the number of “not
apple” training samples, and the number of “words” included
in the “bag” (so-called vocabulary size). In order to determine
the inﬂuence of these factors, 72 classiﬁers with the following
characteristics were trained and tested:
 number of “apple” training samples: 500, 1000, 1500 or 2000
 number “not apple” training samples: 5000, 7500 or 10,000
 vocabulary size: 500, 1000, 2500, 5000, 7500 or 10,000.
The performance of these classiﬁers (all on the same test
set of 7320 images) is summarised in Fig. 4 which shows the
so-called Receiving Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, i.e.
the True Positive Rate ðTPRÞ (also called sensitivity or recall
rate) and False Positive Rate ðFPRÞ calculated according to:
TPR ¼ number of correct “apple” detections
number of “apple” detections
FPR ¼ number of “non  apple” objects classified as “apple”
number of “non  apple” objects
(8)
Regardless of the number of training samples, increasing
the vocabulary size reduced both TPR and FPR, meaning that
fewer apples were correctly detected and fewer non-apple
objects were erroneously identiﬁed as apples. However, the
impact of the vocabulary size was much more pronounced
when the number of “apple” training samples was small (500
or 1000, blue and red curves, respectively). Furthermore, when
the number of “apple” training samples was small (500 or
1000), the number of “not apple” training samples also had
some impact, especially on TPR (curve shifting down with
increasing number of “not apple” training samples). When the
training set included 1500 or more “apple” samples, the
number of “not apple” samples had very little impact, and the
vocabulary size inﬂuenced mostly FPR. Using 2000 “apple” and
5000 “not apple” training samples with the vocabulary size
equal to 500 led to the highest TPR (93.2%), which was also
associated with the highest FPR (8.4%). Increasing the vocab-
ulary size to 7500 decreased FPR appreciably (to 6.1%) with
only negligible reduction of TPR (to 91.5%). This classiﬁer was
considered to provide the best trade-off between missed de-
tections and false detections and was selected for the subse-
quent analysis.
3.2.
Calculation of the posterior probability distributions
of correct classiﬁcation
Figure 5a and b shows the number of training sub-images
classiﬁed as “apple” and “not apple”, both as a function of
the sub-image property q “entropy”. Figure 5c and d shows the
percentage of training “apple” (resp. “not apple”) sub-images
correctly classiﬁed (Eq. (1)). Combining this information led
to the posterior probability distributions (Eqs. (2) and (3))
shown in Fig 5e and f. It can be seen that the probability that
the classiﬁer result was correct depended on the sub-image
entropy, especially if the result of the classiﬁer was “apple”.
In this case this result had a ~90% probability of being correct
if the sub-image entropy was above 6.5, but the probability of
being correct was less than 20% if the sub-image entropy was
5.5 or less. Figure 6 shows the posterior probabilities obtained
when using “intensity” as the sub-image property q. The
dependence between the probability of correct classiﬁcation
and this property was both more gradual and more pro-
nounced than when using entropy. Also, it must be noticed
that the training sub-images did not cover the whole dynamic
range, and in particular there were no “apple” images with
intensity higher than 220 or “not apple” images with intensity
higher than 190.

3.3.
Estimation of the number of apples in images
3.3.1.
2016 dataset
The number of apples was counted in 12 images selected
randomly (but not included in the calibration set). The
straightforward and adjusted estimates (Eqs. (5) and (4),
respectively) obtained for these images are shown in Fig. 7. It
can be seen that the adjustment based on posterior proba-
bilities improved signiﬁcantly the linear correlation between
the actual and estimated number of apples in the images, both
in term of R2 value and in term of reduced bias. There was very
little difference between the results obtained using entropy or
intensity as the basis for the adjustment.

Discussion
In terms of fruit detection, the performance of the present
approach can be compared to that of three approaches which
were previously used to analyse the 2014 dataset. This com-
parison, which is summarised in Table 5, shows that no
improvement
was
achieved
with
the
machine-learning
approach developed in the present study. However, it must be
emphasised that whereas in the three previous studies the
apple detection procedures were calibrated using images from
that dataset, the present results were obtained using a classiﬁer
and posterior probabilities calibrated with images captured on
a different year, with a different lighting system and with
different cameras. The only “local calibration” consisted of
establishing the linear relationship between the number of
detections classiﬁed as “apple” and the number of apples ac-
cording to visual count (Fig. 8a). Note however that this
relationship was required only in order to analyse the perfor-
mance of the procedure at the image level. In order to obtain
yield estimates, it is only necessary to calibrate models such as
Eq. (6) or (7), which can be based on the number of detected
objects rather than on estimates of the number of apples.
In terms of yield estimation, considering that the current
approach did not result in more accurate estimations of the
number of apples in individual images than the approach re-
ported in Linker (2017), it is not surprising that the accuracy of
the yield estimate at the tree level was not improved either.

consisting of images collected in 15 rows with an unspeciﬁed
number of trees with red, pink-green and green fruit. Both
studies reported actual and estimated yield only at the row
level. Based on all 15 rows, Hung et al. (2015) reported a cor-
relation (R2) between the algorithm count and the actual
number of apples equal to 0.81. Although the standard devi-
ation of the row-count estimates was not reported in the
paper, it can be estimated from their Fig. 7: 1850 apples/row,
which corresponds to ~20% of the average row yield. The
study of Bargoti and Underwood (2017) reported a slightly
higher coefﬁcient of correlation (R2 ¼ 0.85) and lower standard
deviation of the row-count estimates (~1000 apples/row,
inferred from their Figure 13). However, it must be noted that
in order to minimise double-counting, Bargoti and Underwood
(2017) deﬁned manually the region of interest in which apples
were counted in each image. The study of Wang et al. (2013)
included
670 green-variety trees (14 rows) which were
grouped in 224 sections of three trees each. Ten random sec-
tions were used to calibrate a linear relationship between
number of detections and actual yield. Applying this model to
the other sections led to yield estimation error of ~2% at the
row level. The standard deviation at the row level was ~12%.
Unfortunately, these results at the row level can not be readily
compared to the tree-level results reported here. Furthermore,
although it terms of illumination the images of Wang et al.
(2013) were closest to the ones used in the present study, it
must be noted that the average fruit load in Wang et al. (2013)
study was ~23 apples/tree (inferred from their Figure 11),
compared to 400e500 apples/tree in the present study.
5.
Conclusion
Machine learning appears to be an attractive approach for
detecting fruit in canopy images. One of the main advantages
of this approach is that it does not require tedious labelling of
single pixels or accurate delineation of training objects.
Although a large number of training sub-images is required
(~10,000 in the present study), as long as there are no extreme
variations in terms of illumination or object colour and size,
these training samples can be extracted from a small number
of images (20 in the present case). Labelling these sub-images
can be completed very rapidly. Another advantage of this
approach is its robustness, which was demonstrated by the
good results obtained when using the classiﬁer with images
captured on a different year, with a different lighting system
and
with
different
cameras.
However,
applying
post-
classiﬁcation adjustment based on sub-image intensity or
entropy was not as robust and improved only the results
related to estimation of the number of fruit per image in im-
ages very similar to the ones used to calibrate the correction
procedure. Considering the limited robustness of the post-
classiﬁcation adjustment approach adopted in this study, it
would be worthwhile to consider enhancing the image before
starting the analysing instead. However, although local-
enhancement methods produce images which are “more
pleasing to the eye”, such enhancement may cause undesir-
able side-effects such as loss of texture. Testing such pro-
cedures and determining which one(s) would be appropriate
for the type of images relevant to the present application was
beyond the scope of the present study but should be consid-
ered in the future.
One of the limitations of machine learning is that this
approach is suitable only for analysing sub-images around
key-points which have been previously identiﬁed. It is likely
that the detection of such key-points could be improved by
using other features than specular highlights and alternative
approaches should be investigated. In particular, more com-
plex approaches inspired from automated object detection
algorithms, and which do not exploit a single characteristic of
the image but rather combine several cues, should be
considered (Puttemans, Vanbrabant, Tits, & Goedeme, 2016).
Using a different method for detecting key-points would also
extend the applicability of the proposed approach, which is
currently restricted to fruit/illumination combinations that
result in signiﬁcant specular reﬂection.
