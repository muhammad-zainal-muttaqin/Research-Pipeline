A B S T R A C T  
Rapid and non-destructive automatic statistics of cherry tomatoes at different ripeness stages help better manage 
resources during harvesting, storage, and transportation processes. Currently, the inspection of cherry tomatoes 
(ripeness assessment and counting) still faces challenges, such as excluding background cherry tomatoes, 
detecting heavily obscured ones, and tracking similar feature extraction across frames. This study presented a 
statistical algorithm for cherry tomatoes with different ripeness. Firstly, a complete field of view was achieved by 
stitching images from dual cameras. Then, during the detection phase, the proposed depth information mapping 
and morphological operations were employed to filter out background cherry tomatoes effectively. Secondly, the 
SimAM attention module was introduced to enhance the focus of the YOLO v7-tiny model on small and occluded 
targets. The ReID feature extraction model was replaced with the lighter and more powerful MobileNeXt model, 
with the input resolution adapted to 64 × 64 based on the morphological characteristics of cherry tomatoes. 
Finally, the statistics of cherry tomatoes at different ripeness levels were conducted using the improved Deep­
SORT algorithm. The ablation experimental results prove the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm. The 
improved YOLO v7-tiny has a mAP of 87.3 % on the dataset, combining depth information mapping with 
morphological operations. Compared with the original DeepSORT algorithm, the improved DeepSORT algorithm 
has RMSE decreased by 15.46 to 3.11, and R2 increased by 0.071 to 0.998. The statistical algorithm enables real- 
time statistical of the number of cherry tomatoes at different ripeness levels during inspection.   
1. Introduction 
Tomatoes are one of the people’s favorite fruits, and they are widely 
cultivated worldwide (Gehlot et al., 2023). They are the world’s second 
most economically valuable horticultural crop, following only potatoes 
(Ma et al., 2023). Tomatoes are widely grown in greenhouse environ­
ments to meet long-term demand. Cherry tomatoes, suitable for raw 
consumption, have become the new favorite for tomato consumption 
due to their small fruit size, vibrant color, and excellent taste (Lipan 
et al., 2021). In a greenhouse, cherry tomatoes ripen quickly, requiring 
regular harvesting of ripened fruits every few days. Therefore, the rapid 
and non-destructive automatic statistical of tomatoes at different ripe­
ness stages helps better manage resources during harvesting, storage, 
and transportation processes. The statistics of cherry tomatoes involve 
two tasks: judging cherry tomato ripeness and counting the number of 
cherry tomatoes ready for harvest. Without the support of an automated 
system, the statistics of cherry tomatoes is typically done manually. 
Manual statistical involves randomly selecting areas for counting and 
extrapolating the results to the entire orchard. However, this method is 
time-consuming, labour-intensive, and prone to errors (Ampatzidis 
et al., 2020; Misra et al., 2020). Generally, supervised learning yields 
better results for tomato counting compared to handcrafted features 
(such as color, texture, shape, etc.) (Koirala et al., 2019a). In recent 
years, with the rapid development of computer technology and deep 
learning method, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been 
widely used in agriculture, such as pest classification (Setiawan et al., 
2022), yield estimation (Parr et al., 2023), harvesting (Zhang et al., 
2023), plant protection (Wang et al., 2020). The artificial intelligence

Regarding judging the ripeness of crops, the deep learning method 
has achieved good results. Suharjito et al. (2021) used the Effi­
cientNetB0 network to complete the ripeness classification on the oil 
palm fresh fruit bunch, which enabled it to achieve an overall test ac­
curacy of 0.893. Yang et al. (2023) proposed to integrate the LW-Swin 
Transformer based on YOLOv8s algorithm to complete strawberry 
ripeness detection, achieving 94.4 % detection precision. Zhu et al. 
(2024) proposed a novel Olive-EfficientDet to detect the maturity of 
multi-cultivar olive fruits in orchard environments. The mean average 
precision (mAP) of fruit maturity detection reached 94.60 %, 95.45 %, 
93.75 %, and 96.05 % for olives of the cultivar “Frantoio”, “Ezhi 8″, 
”Leccino“, and ”Picholine“. The aforementioned studies all employed 
deep learning techniques to accomplish crop ripeness classification tasks 
in real agricultural environments. 
In agricultural counting, researchers have conducted extensive 
studies, which can be mainly categorized into two methods: object 
detection and object tracking. The object detection method involves 
capturing images by moving the camera at a constant speed. An 
appropriate sampling frequency is used to ensure that there are no 
overlapping regions in each frame. The number of crops detected in each 
image is multiplied by the number of frames to achieve crop counting. 
Khaki et al. (2022) proposed a novel deep learning model WheatNet, 
which utilized point-level annotations for detecting and counting wheat 
in images. The model achieved MAE and RMSE of 3.85 and 5.19, 
respectively. Koirala et al., (2019b) proposed the MangoYOLO model for 
detecting mango and estimating the fruit load in orchards based on the 
count of detected fruits. The MangoYOLO model achieved an F1 score of 
0.968 and an Average Precision of 0.983 on a test set. However, methods 
relying solely on object detection for crop counting face challenges in 
setting the camera’s movement speed and sampling frequency. Over­
lapping regions can lead to significant duplicate counting. Additionally, 
various factors, such as occlusions and lighting changes, can result in 
many objects not being detected. Therefore, crop counting relying solely 
on detection is not reliable. 
The Multiple Object Tracking (MOT) approach addresses the issue of 
duplicate counting by detecting the number of crops in an image and 
tracking multiple objects in each frame. This method ensures that the 
same crop is not counted multiple times by consistently tracking objects 
across frames (Wang et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2022). The 
DeepSORT algorithm is one of the fastest and most accurate algorithms 
for multiple object tracking (Villacr´es et al., 2023; Wojke, 2017). Due to 
its stable performance and strong robustness, the DeepSORT algorithm 
has been widely used in agriculture. Villacr´es et al. (2023) utilized 
DeepSORT for apple counting tasks in orchards and achieved the best 
results compared with Kalman Filter, Kernelized Correlation Filter, 
Simple Online Real-Time Tracking, and Multi Hypothesis Tracking, with 
a MATO of 93 %. Parico et al. (2021) utilized the combination of 
YOLOv4 and DeepSORT to accomplish citrus fruit counting tasks, and 
F1count reached 87.85 %. However, cherry tomato counting using 
DeepSORT still faces some challenges. First, when tomatoes are densely 
planted in multiple rows, background tomatoes in neighbouring rows 
may appear in the camera’s field of view, affecting the performance of 
statistical algorithms, as shown in Fig. 1(a). Rong et al. (2021) and Rong 
et al. (2023) have also highlighted this issue multiple times in their 
research. Second, the narrow passage of the inspection robot leads to a 
short distance between the camera and the cherry tomato, and a single 
camera cannot obtain visual information of the entire tomato growing 
area, as shown in Fig. 1(b)(c). Third, the characteristics of cherry to­
matoes are similar. When using the DeepSORT algorithm for multiple 
object tracking, frequent ID switching is easy to occur. Fourth, cherry 
tomatoes are small objects compared to the entire image, and high 
detection accuracy is still a challenge. 
This paper presented a real-time statistical algorithm for cherry to­
matoes with different ripeness based on depth information mapping, 
utilizing DeepSORT as the foundation. First, the whole plant field of 
view is obtained through multiple cameras, and morphological opera­
tion is applied to compensate for the limitations of depth cameras. Then, 
the valid depth information is mapped to the color image, thus forming a 
masking effect on the background tomatoes. Object detection is per­
formed on the depth mapping image to eliminate interference from 
background tomatoes. The SimAM attention module, belonging to the 
non-parametric attention category and introducing 3-D weighted 
attention, exhibits superior performance. The detection model is an 
improved YOLO v7-tiny using the SimAM attention module (Yang et al., 
2021) By incorporating attention modules, the model enhances its focus 
on small objects, such as cherry tomatoes, thus improving detection 
accuracy. The ReID model, which solely employs residual modules, has 
been replaced with the more powerful and light-weight MobileNeXt 
model (Zhou et al., 2020) for feature extraction within the bounding 
boxes, thus enabling the extraction of more meaningful features. The 
MobileNeXt model requires less calculation than the ReID model and is 
more suitable for deployment in embedded and mobile devices.

2.2. Depth information mapping 
Due to the interference caused by background tomatoes in multirow 
cultivation, both detection and counting are severely affected. This 
study proposed a method that relied on depth information to map color 
images in order to filter out background tomatoes. The inspection robot 
traveles on a track, and the tomato cultivation follows a standardized 
pattern, resulting in a roughly constant distance between the camera 
and the target tomatoes, approximately 30–50 cm. The effective depth 
information is mapped back to the color image, while the invalid depth 
is replaced with 0 in the red (R), green (G), and blue (B) channels of the 
color image. As shown in Fig. 3, (a) is the original image, (b) is the mask 
of effective depth information, and (c) is the effective color image after 
depth information mapping. This method not only eliminates the 
interference caused by background tomatoes during the detection stage 
without determining the exact depth of each tomato, but also simplifies 
the background environment to a certain extent, making detection 
easier. Ultimately, an effective color video is formed based on the 
mapping of depth information. 
The depth information from the Realsense D435, which is a struc­
tured light camera, can be affected by lighting conditions. Some areas 
may not have valid depth information, which prevents mapping to the 
color image and affects the accuracy of detection. In fact, TOF cameras, 
stereo cameras, and structured light cameras are all susceptible to 
environmental influences and cannot capture complete and valid depth 
information. To address the aforementioned issue, this study applies 
image processing techniques to valid depth images, specifically 
morphological operation, as shown in Fig. 4(b). Due to the instability of 
depth at the edges of RGB-D cameras, the color pixels at the edges of 
tomatoes might be missing, impacting the detection accuracy. The 
closing operation in the morphological operation can smooth the 
boundary, and the dilation operation can expand the effective depth 
area, eliminating the invalid depth, as shown in Fig. 4(c). 
2.3. Stitching of upper and lower camera images 
In this study, we attempted to extract feature points from both im­
ages using SIFT (Lowe, 1999) (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform), 
SURF (Bay et al., 2006) (Speeded-Up Robust Features), ORB (Rublee 
et al., 2011) (Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF), and SuperPoint 
methods (DeTone et al., 2018). We also explored feature point matching 
using KNN (K-Nearest Neighbors) matching, FLANN (Muja and Lowe, 
2009) (Fast Library for Approximate Nearest Neighbors) matching, 
brute force matching, and SuperGlue (Sarlin et al., 2020). However, all 
these attempts failed. This is primarily due to the camera’s proximity to 
the tomato plant, resulting in significant parallax, making it difficult to 
perform image matching. Since the distance between the two cameras is 
fixed, and the positions of the cameras with respect to the tomato plants 
are also fixed, the fixed pixel parameters can be utilized to stitch the two 
images together. The complete tomato growing area is obtained through 
two cameras. In this greenhouse, the fixed pixels of this data set are 470. 
The lower camera has reduced 470 lines of pixels, and then directly 
stitches the processed image with the one obtained from the upper 
camera. Stitched images will not have duplicated tomatoes, thus 
avoiding any impact on the counting results. As can be seen from Fig. 5, 
some repeated cherry tomatoes are circled in (a) and (b), but they do not 
appear in (c), which proves the effectiveness of our method.

2.5. Improved YOLO v7-tiny model 
2.5.1. YOLO v7-tiny model 
YOLO v7 (Wang et al., 2023) is one of the most advanced models in 
the YOLO series, demonstrating excellent performance in both detection 
speed and accuracy. The YOLO v7-tiny model is a light-weight version of 
YOLO v7. During the inspection process, model detection speed is 
crucial, so the YOLO v7-tiny model is selected. The architecture of YOLO 
v7-tiny is similar to YOLO v5, as shown in Fig. 8, and consists of four 
main components: Input, Backbone, Neck, and Head. In the input part, 
the input image size is resized to 640 × 640 × 3. The backbone part 
primarily extracts features, the neck part is mainly responsible for 
feature fusion, and the head part is primarily responsible for model 
classification and regression output. In this study these components are 
selected as CBL (Conv + Bn + LeakyReLU) module, ELAN’ (efficient 
layer aggregation networks) module, SPPCSPC module, Maxpool layer, 
and UPSample layer. The CBL module is mainly used for feature 
extraction. The ELAN’ module is composed of stacked convolution 
blocks and controls the shortest and longest gradient paths, allowing 
deeper networks to learn and converge effectively, thereby improving 
the model’s learning ability. The SPPCSPC module is an extension of the 
SPP module, incorporating the idea of the CSPLayer by adding a residual 
edge and stacking it with the feature maps outputted after Maxpool 
operations. 
2.5.2. SimAM attention module 
Cherry tomatoes are small and mostly shaded. This requires the 
model to pay more attention to the tomatoes themselves while ignoring 
background information such as stems and leaves. Attention modules 
can enhance focus on crucial regions by assigning different weights to 
feature maps. The SimAM attention module was proposed by Sun Yat- 
sen University at the 2021 ICML conference based on some well-

feature vectors. These feature vectors are then used for cascaded 
matching. If a successful match is found, the matched target is inputted

calculates the predicted physical variables and the observed value of the 
object at the current time using predefined weighted variables. Finally, 
the algorithm obtains the corrected value at the current time k through 
the Kalman filtering algorithm. The parameters in the Kalman filtering 
algorithm are then updated.

2.6.3. Dataset for feature extraction model 
The dataset for the feature extraction model was constructed using 
Dataset 3. The training dataset was used to build a dataset comprising all 
cherry tomato images to train the feature extraction model. This dataset 
includes 1368 images of ripe cherry tomatoes, 555 images of semi-ripe 
cherry tomatoes, and 2882 images of unripe cherry tomatoes. The 
training of the feature extraction model follows the same procedures as 
training the classification model. During inference in DeepSORT, the 
classification layer is removed, and only feature vectors are extracted. 
The ReID model, originally designed for Pedestrian Re-identification, 
has an input image resolution of 64 × 128 pixels. Cherry tomatoes 
possess their morphological characteristics. Therefore, we plotted the 
width and height of all cherry tomatoes in the training dataset, as shown 
in Fig. 11. Red color represents dense points, with the reddest regions 
distributing near the 1:1 line, in which both width and height are around 
43. As a result, image resolutions of 64 × 64 and 128 × 128 are 
considered to be better choices for input image sizes. In the experimental 
section, experiments were conducted using different input image sizes to 
determine the optimal image size. 
2.6.4. Optimization method of DeepSORT algorithm 
The improved DeepSORT algorithm is shown in Fig. 12. When 
dynamically counting the cherry tomatoes, they are occluded by leaves 
and neighboring cherry tomatoes. Therefore, it is crucial to choose a 
detection model that has high accuracy and fast speed. In this study, the 
improved YOLO v7-tiny is adopted as the detection model to achieve 
cherry tomatoes detection and maturity estimation. The improved YOLO 
v7-tiny enhances its focus on small and occluded objects by incorpo­
rating the SimAM attention module. The ReID module has been replaced 
with the MobileNeXt model, which has stronger feature extraction ca­
pabilities. The optimal input image size is chosen for tomato feature 
extraction within bounding boxes, improving inspection accuracy.

3. Result 
3.1. Ablation experiment of detection model 
3.1.1. Ablation experiments of depth mapping and morphological 
operations 
As described in Section 2.4, this study constructed three datasets and 
employed the YOLO v7-tiny model for training. The experimental results 
are shown in Table 4. Dataset 2, which integrates depth mapping, shows 
a 3 % higher mAP compared to Dataset 1, which directly uses color 
images. This demonstrates that depth mapping contributes to the per­
formance improvement of the detection model. Additionally, Dataset 3, 
which incorporates depth mapping and morphological operations, ex­
hibits a 2.5 % higher mAP than Dataset 2, which solely uses depth 
mapping. This further illustrates that morphological operation enhances 
the detection model’s performance. 
3.1.2. Ablation experiments of attention module 
The attention module could be added to both the backbone and neck 
parts of the YOLO v7-tiny model, and different positions for adding the 
attention module could yield different effects. In this study, we cate­
gorize the positions for adding the attention module into three types: 1) 
adding it to the backbone part; 2) adding it to the neck part; 3) adding it 
to both the backbone and neck parts. Training and testing are conducted 
on Dataset 3 to demonstrate the optimal placement. As shown in Table 5, 
when the SimAM is added to both the backbone and neck parts, it 
achieves the best results, with a mAP of 87.3 %. This represents a 2.5 % 
improvement in mAP compared to not using any attention modules. CA, 
NAM (Liu et al., 2021) (Normalization-based Attention Module), and 
Shuffle (Zhang and Yang, 2021) attention modules are frequently 
employed as effective means to enhance the accuracy of detection net­
works, particularly in the agricultural field. When compared to them, 
SimAM outperforms best, underscoring the superiority of the SimAM 
attention module and its optimal placement. 
As described in Table 6, in the four experiments, the detection ac­
curacy for ripe and unripe tomatoes is higher than that for semi-ripe 
tomatoes. This is because the color and other features of semi-ripe to­
matoes fall between ripe and unripe tomatoes, making them more prone 
to misclassification. The incorporation of attention modules in both the 
backbone and neck parts partially alleviates the above-mentioned issue, 
and the average precision (AP) for ripe and semi-ripe tomatoes signifi­
cantly increases to 87.9 % and 86.5 %, respectively.

3.1.3. Attention visualization 
The Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Selvaraju et al., 
2017) (Grad-CAM) technique is employed to generate activation heat 
maps of objects within the image. These heat maps provide an intuitive 
representation of the pixels that hold more significant information 
during the model’s image processing. The redder the color, the more 
attention is paid to this pixel. As shown in Fig. 13, by adding attention 
modules to both the backbone and neck of YOLO v7-tiny more attention 
is paid to ripe and semi-ripe tomatoes, which is consistent with our 
experimental results. Compared to the original YOLO v7-tiny model, the 
addition of an attention module has led to an increased focus on to­
matoes, particularly on ripe tomatoes and occluded targets. Experi­
mental results and attention visualizations both confirm the 
effectiveness of incorporating the SimAM module. 
3.2. The impact of morphological operations on statistical algorithm 
Experiments were conducted on Dataset 2 and Dataset 3 using the 
YOLO v7-tiny model as the detector in the DeepSORT algorithm. 
Experimental results are shown in Table 7. When using DeepSORT with 
Dataset 3, the algorithm achieves counts of 45 ripe tomatoes, 29 semi- 
ripe tomatoes, and 152 unripe tomatoes in the algorithm evaluation 
videos. Keeping other conditions consistent, using Dataset 3 results in a 
lower RMSE, a decrease of 4.14, and a higher R2 value, an increase of 
0.036, compared to using Dataset 2. The use of morphological opera­
tions could effectively enhance the performance of the detection model, 
thereby improving the statistical algorithm. 
3.3. The impact of feature extraction model and input resolution on 
statistical algorithm 
In this study, in order to extract more crucial features for object 
tracking tasks, it is proposed in Section 2.6 to replace the original ReID 
model with the MobileNeXt feature extraction model. Additionally, a 
new input image resolution is suggested based on the biological char­
acteristics of tomatoes. In the experiments, three different input image 
resolutions were attempted, and comparisons were made between the 
excellent light-weight models such as MobileNeXt, Espnet v2 (Mehta 
et al., 2019), MobileNet v3 (Howard et al., 2019), GhostNet (Han et al., 
2020), and the original ReID model. Due to the model’s inherent 
downsampling, a resolution of 64 × 64 could not be applied to the 
original ReID model, so no comparison is made. The experimental re­
sults, as shown in Table 8, reveal that the MobileNeXt model achieves 
the best results with an input size of 64 × 64, yielding an RMSE of 11.21 
and an R2 value of 0.973. Compared to the original ReID model using 64 
× 128 input images, the MobileNeXt model exhibits a 7.36 reduction in 
RMSE and a 0.046 increase in R2, along with a significant decrease in 
computational complexity. Across all experiments, using a 64 × 64 input 
image resolution consistently outperforms 64 × 128 and 128 × 128, 
yielding the best results. 
3.4. Improved DeepSORT algorithm performance experiment 
Compare the improved DeepSORT algorithm with the original 
DeepSORT algorithm on Dataset 3. The experimental results are shown 
in Table 9, RMSE decreases by 15.46, and R2 increases by 0.071. The 
inference time of the improved YOLO v7-tiny and MobileNeXt_64 × 64 
models is 16.5 ms and 9.8 ms respectively. The counting performance 
test video requires approximately 33 s to complete the counting of 262 
tomatoes. Therefore, the average counting performance of this model is 
7.9 tomatoes per second. The proposed method successfully completes 
the statistical task of cherry tomatoes. As shown in Fig. 14, this is a 
visualization of statistical algorithm applications. 
4. Discussion 
In Section 3.1.1, due to deep mapping that simplifies the image 
backgrounds, the detection model performs better on Dataset 2 than on 
Dataset 1. Additionally, the morphological operations successfully 
mitigate the impact of insufficient camera performance, resulting in 
more complete tomato contours. Consequently, the detection model 
performs better on Dataset 3 than on Dataset 2. Simple background and 
complete target information are beneficial to feature extraction during 
model training, thereby improving model performance. 
In Section 3.1.2, after adding the attention module, compared with 
the original YOLO v7-tiny, except for adding the SimAM attention 
module to the backbone and neck, all other methods exhibit the char­
acteristic of P rising and R falling. This is because incorporating the 
attention module causes the model to prioritize what it deemed 
important targets while neglecting other objects, resulting in increased 
precision and decreased recall. When the SimAM attention module is 
applied to both the backbone and neck, such a phenomenon does not 
occur. However, the improvement in R is not substantial, primarily due 
to a significant increase in P. After these modifications, the enhanced 
YOLO v7-tiny model experiences a 4.4 ms decrease in speed compared to

In Section 3.3, all feature extraction models perform worse when the

the feature extraction model, utilizing R2 and RMSE to reflect the ac­

improvement in ID switching accuracy.

found that the RMSE was less than 3 mm in the range of 0.6–1.5 m.

greenhouse does not exceed 1 m. At the same time, due to hanging vine 
planting, the distance between tomatoes is greater than 10 cm. This 
camera error does not affect the determination of background tomatoes. 
At the edge of the cherry tomato target, this error may be large, which 
will cause the cherry tomato pixels to be missing. However, we have 
made up for this problem through morphological operations.

errors in distance calculation. In summary, our solution exhibits certain 
advantages when compared with some existing research. 
The proposed method has completed the statistical task of cherry 
tomatoes very well, but it still has some shortcomings and limitations. 
There are certain errors in the experimental results: 1) Repeated 
counting caused by failed cross-frame matching and uncounted caused 
by severe occlusion cancel each other out. 2) Ripeness is only catego­
rized into three classes. 3) During the inspection process, each cherry 
tomato is guaranteed to appear at least four times in the field of view. 4) 
The image stitching algorithm cannot completely eliminate duplicate 
pixels, there will be some overlap. However, the number of pixels in the 
area where the tomato belongs is more than the error pixels. Therefore, 
after the stitching process, the resulting image will not have duplicated 
tomatoes, thus avoiding any impact on the counting results. 
In future research, we plan to refine the categorization of ripeness 
levels and integrate factors such as soil fertility, temperature, humidity, 
and lighting to construct a ripeness prediction model. This ripeness 
prediction model will allow us to predict the ripening time for each 
cherry tomato more accurately. 
5. Conclusions 
High-precision detection and tracking of cherry tomatoes at different 
stages of ripeness in target rows is of great significance to statistical 
algorithms. In this research, background tomato interference is elimi­
nated during the detection phase through deep mapping and morpho­
logical operations. A complete field of view is obtained through multi- 
camera image stitching. Detection performance for small and occluded 
targets is enhanced using the SimAM attention module in the YOLO v7- 
tiny model. Target tracking accuracy is improved by replacing the 
feature extraction model and adjusting image resolution. Using deep 
mapping and morphological operations on the dataset improves the 
mAP of YOLO v7-tiny by 5.5 %. After incorporating the SimAM attention 
module, the improved YOLO v7-tiny achieves a 2.5 % increase in mAP 
compared to the original YOLO v7-tiny on Dataset 3. Replacing the ReID 
model with the MobileNeXt model and adjusting the input resolution to 
64x64 results in a 7.36 reduction in RMSE and a 0.046 increase in R2. 
Finally, the improved DeepSORT algorithm reduces RMSE by 15.46 and 
increases R2 by 0.071 compared to the original DeepSORT algorithm on 
Dataset 3. These experiments provide strong evidence for the effec­
tiveness of our proposed improvements and successfully accomplish the 
statistical task of cherry tomatoes. They provide the necessary theoret­
ical foundation and technical support for developing cherry tomato in­
spection robots.
