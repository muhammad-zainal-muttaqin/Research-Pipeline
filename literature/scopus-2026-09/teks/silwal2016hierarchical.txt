A HIERARCHICAL APPROACH TO APPLE IDENTIFICATION  
FOR ROBOTIC HARVESTING 
A. Silwal,  M. Karkee,  Q. Zhang 
ABSTRACT. Identifying fruits with limited visibility and fruits in clusters has been a challenging problem in machine vision 
systems used in robotic tree fruit harvesting. Issues with clusters and partial to full occlusion of fruit could be minimized by 
strategically harvesting the most visible fruits first. This work presents a hierarchical method for apple identification suita-
ble for robotic harvesting. Test images were acquired from six overlapping sections of tree canopies trained in fruiting wall 
architecture. An image processing method consisting of circular Hough transform (CHT) and blob analysis was applied in 
an iterative fashion. Clearly visible fruit identified by CHT were preferred to partially visible apples for initial harvesting. 
These prioritized apples were then manually picked to prove the concept of hierarchical fruit identification. As images were 
taken again after harvesting the well-exposed apples, partially or fully occluded apples were better exposed in successive 
iterations. This iterative process was applied over every image acquired from both sides of tree canopies until no apples 
were identified. In total, 740 images were taken from 240 canopy sections of 20 trees where 1789 apples were identified out 
of 1827 manual counts. On average, this method achieved 98% identification accuracy. In addition, 80% of the apples were 
detected and harvested with images taken from one side of the canopy, and the remaining apples were harvested from the 
opposite side. Although this process is simple and intuitive, the work provided a unique and novel insight into the fruit 
identification and harvesting accuracy achievable with such an approach in a field environment and showed the huge po-
tential of this machine vision system for robotic harvesting of apples trained in fruiting wall architectures. 
Keywords. Apple harvesting, Fruit identification, Fruit occlusion, Iterative approach, Machine vision, Over-the-row plat-

roduction of fresh market fruit crops such as apples 
and pears requires a large number of semi-skilled 
seasonal laborers. Harvesting is a time-sensitive task 
that requires the right number of workers with the 
right skills at the right time during the harvest season. In 
Washington State alone, apple harvesting requires an addi-
tional 30,000 workers, costing approximately $1,100 to 
$2,100 per acre per year (Gallardo et al., 2009; Galinato and 
Gallardo, 2010). To reduce the dependency on and cost of 
such a seasonal labor force, research and development of ro-
botic tree fruit harvesting systems have received a renewed 
focus in recent years. One of the key tasks for robotic harvest-
ing of tree fruit crops such as apples is to identify and locate 
apples in the tree canopies using different types of sensors 
(Bulanon et al., 2002; Baeten et al., 2008; Ji et al., 2012). 
Over the past several decades, researchers have devel-
oped and implemented various machine vision-based meth-
ods for fruit identification. With increasing sensing and com-
putational capability, a large number of fruit identification 
work has been reported in the last decade. Bulanon et al. 
(2004) used an artificial neural network (ANN) and a deci-
sion theoretic classifier to identify apples for robotic harvest-
ing. Both methods achieved a similar accuracy of 80%. Tabb 
et al. (2006) developed a method of global mixture of Gauss-
ians (GMOG) based on the principles of mixture of Gaussi-
ans (MOG) for identification of red and yellow apples. 
Stajnko et al. (2009) used color and texture-based segmen-
tation with circular Hough transform (CHT) to identify ap-
ples in canopy images in an outdoor environment. Linker et 
al. (2012) used color and smoothness to create seed areas on 
apple surfaces that were then expanded to generate simple 
models of apples, achieving an accuracy of 85% in identify-
ing green apples. Supervised classification techniques based 
on soft-computing methods such as support vector (Wang et 
al., 2009; Ji et al., 2012) have used color and shape proper-
ties to recognize apples. 
Most of these studies reported that variable lighting con-
ditions, clustering, and occlusion pose challenges for accu-
rate fruit identification in orchard environments (Plebe and 
Grasso, 2001; Hannan and Burks, 2004; Zhao et al., 2005; 
Stajnko et al., 2009; Kurtulmus et al., 2011; Rakun et al., 
2011; Ji et al., 2012; Linker et al., 2012). To address the issue 
of fruit clustering, Zhao et al. (2005) developed a method to 
separate connected apples in the binary image using texture-

Researchers have been improving existing methods and 
investigating new methods to address the issues of variable 
lighting conditions, fruit clustering, and occlusion for im-
proved fruit identification. Meanwhile, the performance of 
machine vision systems for robotic harvesting could be im-
proved with a simpler yet practical solution. In this work, 
issues with clustering and partial to full occlusion of fruit in 
an apple orchard were minimized by strategically harvesting 
the most visible apples first. As images were taken again af-
ter harvesting the fruit with good visibility, the partially or 
fully occluded apples and apples in clusters were better ex-
posed. 
DESIGN SPECIFICATIONS, OBJECTIVES, AND GOALS 
Vision is an essential task for a robotic harvesting system. 
Developing a machine vision system for fruit identification 
and localization in orchard environments is a challenging 
problem and is often considered a bottleneck for developing 
commercial robotic harvesting systems. The fundamental re-
quirements for a machine vision system for fresh fruit har-
vesting can be summarized as follows: 
 Identification: Identify clearly visible, individual fruit 
within clusters as well as occluded fruit from images 
acquired in different lighting conditions. 
 Localization: Estimate the three-dimensional position 
of each fruit to be harvested. 
 Prioritization: Create and apply rules for optimizing 
the harvesting sequence. 
 Obstacle detection: Detect obstacles for collision 
avoidance. 
The specific objective of this work is to evaluate the per-
formance of a hierarchical method for apple identification 
through an iterative process of imaging and prioritizing fruit 
for harvesting. In this work, the above guidelines were used 
with the following assumptions. The potential for obstacles 
between fruit and the robotic harvester (i.e., obstacle detec-
tion) was neglected, leading to the assumption that all iden-
tified apples were harvestable. These apples were then har-
vested by hand for the demonstration of the hierarchical ap-
proach (i.e., localization was not required). Clearly visible 
fruits were prioritized over occluded fruit for initial harvest-
ing (i.e., prioritization). This work resulted in important 
quantitative information on harvest accuracy, which helps to 
prove that occlusion problems can be strategically solved 
and very high accuracy in harvesting and identification can 
be achieved using a simple approach in fruiting wall apple 
orchards. To the best of our knowledge, this information is 
unknown to the scientific community prior to this work. 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
STUDY SITE 
Implementation of a robotic system to harvest apples in 
an unstructured outdoor environment requires complex ob-
stacle avoidance and maneuvering capability that could be 
computationally expensive and challenging (Bac et al., 
2013). However, modern apple tree architectures such as V-
trellis (fig. 1a) and vertical (fig. 1b) are trained, pruned, and 
thinned to grow apples in a vertical fruiting wall, which pre-
sents an easier work environment for robotic harvesting. All 
research activities were conducted in a commercial orchard 
(Allen Brothers, Inc., Prosser, Wash.) trained in a vertical 
fruiting canopy architecture.

DATA ACQUISITION AND IMAGING PLATFORM 
Color images of Jazz apples in tall spindle architecture 
(2.74 m row spacing and 1.17 m inter-tree spacing) were ac-
quired in a commercial orchard during the 2014 harvest sea-
son. These images were acquired using a CCD camera (Pro-
silica GigeE 1290C, Allied Vision, Exton, Pa.). This camera 
used an f1.4/6 mm lens with a 43.6° (horizontal) by 33.4° 
(vertical) field of view (FOV). With the camera located ap-
proximately 1 m away, apples of 6 to 8 cm diameter were 
mapped to approximately 35 to 70 pixels in the 1280  
960 images. Table 1 summarizes the major physical param-
eters of the cultivar system. Briefly, average tree height was 
about 427 cm, which was vertically supported by seven trel-
lis wires, and the tree spacing was 140 cm. The average fruit 
density was 90 apples per tree, and fruits were present within 
60 cm from the center of the tree trunks along the rows. 
An over-the-row platform (2.9 m L  1.8 m W  3.6 m H) 
with a tunnel structure designed by Gongal et al. (2016) was 
used in this study to minimize variability in lighting condi-
tions during imaging. This rectangular structure has opaque 
curtains blocking direct sunlight onto the tree canopy (fig. 2a), 
which helped us acquire images with minimal specular reflec-
tion even during broad daylight. A semi-controlled lighting 
environment was created within the platform using a set of 
diffused white LED lights (Trilliant 36 light emitting diodes) 
for imaging during the day as well as at night. Images acquired 
during both day and night were used in this work. 
The camera was placed at the center of the longitudinal 
section of the sensing platform with a vertically sliding 
mount (fig. 2b). Images were acquired at six overlapping 
sections along the vertical axis of the canopy to cover entire 
trees. Once repeated imaging and harvesting were completed 
on all six sections of a side of the tree and the machine vision 
algorithm showed no apples, the camera and the sliding 
mechanism were moved to the other side of the canopy to 
repeat the process. On average, the camera was roughly 1 m 
away from the central trunk of the canopy being harvested. 
The platform was mounted on a tractor and moved between 
tree rows for imaging different trees (fig. 2a). Images were 
captured when the sensing platform was stationary. 
HIERARCHICAL FRUIT IDENTIFICATION 
A generalized block diagram of the hierarchical fruit 
identification and harvesting method is shown in figure 3. It 
is an iterative method that ends only when no apples are 
identified in the images. The image processing method for 
apple identification, developed by Silwal et al. (2014), con-
sisted of CHT and blob analysis (BA). Clearly visible fruits 
were identified by CHT, and partially occluded apples were 
detected using BA. An example of the algorithm’s output is 
shown in figure 4, where circled apples are the result of CHT 
and apples enclosed in rectangles are the result of BA. This 
apple identification method was applied to images immedi-
ately after they were acquired in the field. The results were 
then displayed to human operators in near-real time. The hu-
man operators then carefully picked the prioritized apples, 
which was the final step in the loop. The prioritization pro-
cess is described in detail in the following paragraph. 
This research was conducted on apple trees trained in a 
fruiting wall architecture where the major tree trunk grew 
vertically and lateral branches were tied to trellis wires to 
grow horizontally. Qualitative observation of the images re-
vealed that a good proportion of fruit were growing individ-
ually with clear visibility. However, there were also fruit 
growing in clusters and located deeper in the canopy, where 
they were obscured by branches, leaves, or other apples. This 
qualitative information was used to develop the apple iden-

A sequence of images resulting from the hierarchical im-
aging and harvesting approach is shown in figure 5. This it-

in the images. In total, 740 images were taken in 240 canopy 
sections of 20 Jazz apple trees. A PC configured with a 
3 GHz Intel i7 processor, 16 GB RAM, and 64-bit Windows 
7 operation system was used for processing the machine vi-
sion algorithm. On average, calculation time per image in the 
first iteration was approximately 6 s. As the number of ap-
ples decreased with successive harvesting, the calculation 
time per image dropped to less than 1 s for images with no 
apples. Because the algorithm was implemented in Matlab 
(R2014a) without any optimization, it is expected that a sub-
stantial improvement in computational speed may be possi-
ble with optimization of the code and programming plat-
form, as well as the hardware and software architectures. 
However, the goal of this work was to assess the potentially 
achievable harvest accuracy with the hierarchical approach; 
optimizing computational speed was outside the scope of 
this work.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
An example of the in-field fruit identification and hierar-
chical harvesting process on both sides of a canopy is shown 
in figure 5. As discussed earlier, apples identified only with 
CHT were considered for the first round of harvesting (figs. 
5a and 5b). As these clearly visible apples were harvested, 
the image taken for the second iteration revealed previously 
hidden apples. An example of a newly revealed apple is in-
dicated by the arrow in figure 5b. After the first iteration, the 
combination of CHT and BA was used to minimize the num-
ber of iterations required per canopy section (fig 5c). The 
iterative approach was applied until the algorithm could not 
locate any apples in the image (fig. 5d). Figures 5e and 5f 
show the results of iterative imaging and harvesting on the 
other side of the canopy for the same section of the tree. The 
method was tested with more than 740 canopy images from 
240 sections of 20 Jazz apple trees. Table 2 summarizes the 
apple identification accuracy with this method. 
Of the 1827 apples present in 20 trees, the algorithm suc-
cessfully detected 1789 apples, leading to an identification 
accuracy of 98% (true positives) with false negatives of 2% 
(38 apples) and false positives of 7% (130 counts). False 
negative errors mainly occurred because some of the apples 
were not visible from either side of the canopy. Additionally, 
a few apples were completely green, which the image pro-
cessing algorithm was not able to identify. The false posi-
tives mainly included multiple counts of single apples. Such 
errors could be reduced by using 3D location information for 
apples to detect and remove more than one instance of an 
apple if the multiple instances are located closer to each 
other than their average diameter. Qualitative observation 
and earlier study (Robinson et al., 2013) of planar canopies 
such as the tall spindle architecture used in this research 
showed that the canopy depth is no more than three feet for 
better distribution of light. Because the canopies are very 
shallow, more than 50% of the fruit were visible and could 
be accessible from either side. Dual-side imaging allowed 
apples that were not visible from one side of the canopy to 
be visible from the other side. When obstacles were not con-
sidered (an assumption in this work), it was found that 80% 
of the fruit could be harvested from a single side of the can-
opy. This implies that a relatively large amount of fruit could 
be harvested from a single side. 
To prove the concept of hierarchical fruit identification, 
the apples identified and prioritized by the algorithm were 
picked manually. In an integrated harvesting system, fruit 
picking would be performed by a robotic end-effector, which 
would have certain limitations compared to human picking. 
Such limitations could be in assessing fruit maturity and in

provided a better R2 value than a linear model. The positive 
instantaneous slopes of the regression line within the range 
of the manual fruit count showed the increasing trend in the 
number of iterations with the increase in manual count. This 
trend was expected because occlusion is more severe in trees 
with large numbers of apples where additional apples be-
come more visible with iterative harvesting and imaging. 
Fruit visibility was also influenced by the presence of foliage 
and branches, which seems to be the major cause of the non-
linearity. The field trials showed that the presence of foliage 
caused variations in fruit visibility irrespective of the num-
ber of apples present in the scene. This led to a higher num-
ber of iteration for some of the tree sections with relatively 
fewer of apples and more foliage. Similar observations were 
noted where fewer harvesting iterations were required to de-
tach fruits with relatively less vegetation and more fruit in 
the canopy. On average, three iterations per section were 
needed to harvest all detected apples in every section of the 
tree canopies.

harvesting. Further improvement in the performance of this 
method could come from a few different areas. First, im-
proved training, pruning, and thinning of apple trees could 
further improve the visibility of apples and thereby improve 
the overall apple identification and harvest accuracy. Formal 
tree canopies, such as the tall spindle architecture used in this

human input to improve the machine’s performance. Fur-
thermore, a real-time environment with a number of robotic 
devices assisted by a single operator may need to be studied 
for efficient human-machine collaboration. Use of multiple 
robotic devices with overlapping reach would also provide 
multiple views of the same canopy section, potentially al-
lowing access and the opportunity to harvest apples that 
could not be achieved with a single view. 
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION 
A machine vision algorithm designed to identify red ap-
ples in a field environment was used for hierarchical fruit 
identification and harvesting. Apples identified by the ma-
chine vision system were harvested manually to establish the 
accuracy achievable with this method. For the first iteration 
of harvesting, apples with clear visibility (identified by cir-
cular Hough transform) were considered, while all identified 
apples were considered in subsequent iterations. On average, 
the iterative approach of identifying, prioritizing, and har-
vesting apples achieved 98% harvest accuracy (of 1827 total 
apples, 1789 were identified and harvested). There were 7% 
falsely identified fruit, whereas only 2% (38 apples out of 
1827) remained in the trees after harvesting. The false nega-
tives from completely hidden and greenish apples were more 
challenging to correct than the false positives. Modification 
of the machine vision algorithm could be explored to reduce 
both false positive and false negative identifications. Hu-
man-machine collaboration is another approach that could 
be explored for improved performance of the machine vision 
system. 
It could be hypothesized that an iterative imaging and har-
vesting process increases the proportion of fruit that can be 
detected and harvested by a robotic harvester from an apple 
tree canopy. However, this hypothesis was not explicitly 
stated in this research, as we formulated our objectives to 
fulfill a need. Rather than proposing a hypothesis followed 
by statistical inference, the performance of the iterative 
method was interpreted using identification accuracy, a rel-
atively simpler approach. The identification accuracy of 
nearly 98% achieved in this work clearly shows that the it-
erative imaging and harvesting process has potential to 
achieve a very high fruit harvesting accuracy by gradually 
reducing the canopy complexity. Although this process was 
intuitive and simple, the work provided a unique and novel 
insight into the fruit identification and harvesting accuracy 
achievable with such an approach in fruiting wall orchards. 
The results, therefore, showed the huge potential of this ma-
chine vision system, which addresses one of the bottlenecks 
in developing commercial robotic harvesting systems.
