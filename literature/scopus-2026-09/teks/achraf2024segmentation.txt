Abstract— In this study, we propose an innovative approach to 
segment images of olive tree branches taken by smartphones to 
detect olives and leaves. The methodology relies on the self-
creation of a labelled database (e.g., olives, leaves, and others) 
using Grounding-Dino and SAM for detection and segmentation, 
respectively. We exploit the performance of DINOv2 to compute 
embeddings for each class. These embeddings are then used to 
train a Support Vector Machine (SVM) that classifies objects in 
photos (olives/leaves). Finally, the image is segmented into objects 
using SAM, and each object is classified by the trained SVM to 
determine their respective classes. The proposed approach not 
only ensures the automatic construction of our specific database 
but 
also 
demonstrates 
high 
performances 
that 
surpass 
RESNET50.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Olive tree cultivation is an important economic sector in 
many countries of the Mediterranean region. The estimation of 
olive yields is crucial for farmers, not only to assess their income 
and negotiate selling prices but also from a socio-economic 
perspective due to the significance of olive-derived products, 
such as oil. Traditionally, this estimation relies on the visual 
observation of the entire olive grove, a task requiring 
considerable experience and remaining subjective. 
In the current era, artificial intelligence has significantly 
evolved, offering various approaches for object detection and 
segmentation in images [1]. These technological advances have 
paved the way for diverse applications, ranging from object 
recognition to computer vision. Recent approaches, like the 
Grounding DINO open-set object detector [2], have emerged, 
leveraging a combination of the Transformer-based detector 
DINO with Grounded pretraining. DINO, recognized for its 
state-of-the-art object detection performance, allows the 
incorporation of multi-level text information into its algorithm 
through Grounded pre-training. This innovative fusion results in 
the creation of Grounding-DINO, enabling the detection of 
arbitrary objects with human inputs, such as category names or 
referring expressions. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) [3] 
stands out as a novel AI model from Meta designed to segment 
objects in images with high accuracy, even in scenarios where it 
has not encountered specific training data. On a related note, 
DINOv2 [4] employs a self-supervised training method for 
computer vision models, utilizing a dataset of 140 million 
unlabelled images. The embeddings generated by DINOv2 find 
applications across various domains, including classification, 
image 
retrieval, 
segmentation, 
and 
depth 
estimation. 
Noteworthy among the various AI models are those founded on 
convolutional neural networks (CNN), such as ResNet [5], 
YOLO (You Only Look Once) [6], Faster R-CNN [7], and 
Detectron2 [8]. These models are trained on a labeled dataset 
containing images with annotated objects. During training, the 
model adjusts its weights to minimize the error between 
predictions and annotations. It is also possible to use pre-trained 
architectures such as ResNet, often employed as a pretrained 
base architecture for various computer vision tasks due to its 
robust performance. Pretrained weights on massive datasets, 
such as ImageNet [9], are available, enabling effective transfer 
learning for specific tasks with more targeted datasets. However, 
whether training a model on a specific labeled dataset or fine-
tuning a pre-trained model, creating a comprehensive labeled 
dataset demands significant human effort and expertise. It 
involves annotating images with precise labels that capture the 
intricacies of the target objects or classes. This process is labor-
intensive, time-consuming, and requires domain-specific 
knowledge to ensure the model's robustness across various 
conditions. 
The approach proposed in this paper introduces, on one 
hand, a novel methodology to create an automatic database from 
smartphone photos taken in olive fields using the Grounding-
DINO and Segmenting Anything Model (SAM) models. On the 
other hand, it exploits the constructed database, DINOv2, 
Support Vector Machines (SVM) [10] and SAM to segment 
olive branch images. 
II. DEVELOPPED APPROACH  
Our approach focuses on the segmentation of regions or 
objects identified in smartphone-captured photos of olive 
branches within an olive grove. The primary objective is to 
categorize these segmented regions into three classes: olives, 
leaves, and other areas devoid of both olives and leaves (e.g., 
sections of the sky, soil, branches, etc.). Figure 1 illustrates the 
key steps of our methodology, which include database 
construction, embeddings computation, and classification.

A. Automated Database Construction 
To construct classes for olives and leaves, we begin by 
detecting these objects within a given image. To achieve this 
goal, we employed the Grounding DINO. In our application, we 
use the referring expressions "Olive" and "Leaf" along with a 
confidence coefficient indicating the level of trust in the detected 
object. As depicted in Figure 1 (Detection a), we exploit this 
criterion by setting it relatively high to ensure the accurate 
detection of the right objects (e.g., olive or leaf). For instance, 
Figure 2 (a) shows that not all olives are detected, but only those 
with high confidence. This approach allows us to build two 
classes: olives and leaves. The third class contains all segments 
that are not olive nor leaf, it is obtained by removing all possible 
objects that could be olive and leaf. To do it, in Figure 1 
(Detection b), a lower threshold is used, aiming to detect all 
possible objects that could be in the first classes. Figure 2 (b) 
illustrates that all objects assumed to be olives (with false 
detections) are detected.  After the detections step, we proceed 
to segmentation by applying SAM to each detected object. 
Hence, from the output of Detection (a), the segmented olives 
and leaves are retrieved and stored within their respective 
classes. For Detection (b), where our main objective was to 
recover the maximum possible number of detected olive or leaf 
objects, and after segmenting them, these segmented items are 
subtracted from the original image, resulting in obtaining the 
third class consisting of objects other than olives or leaves, such 
as parts of the sky, branches, understories, etc.

B. Embeddings and Classification 
The Embeddings step serves as a preparatory stage for the 
classification algorithm, with a focus on leveraging DINOv2. 
Firstly, our constructed database will be input into DINOv2 to 
compute specific embeddings for olives, leaves, and the third 
class. These embeddings will subsequently form a dataset for 
training the well-established SVM. Finally, by applying the 
SAM model to the image, all segmented objects will undergo 
SVM classification, assigning these objects to our target classes.

III. RESULTS 
In this paper, we present two distinct sets of results. The first 
set pertains to the performance of our approach, evaluated using 
the constructed database and subsequently compared to the well-
established ResNet50 algorithm. The latter algorithm replaces a 
part of our processing chain regarding the leaning after the 
automatic creation of the database. The second set visualizes the 
classification outcomes directly from photos taken with a 
smartphone in an olive grove. 
A. Performances of the Approach Applied to the Database 
By leveraging our previously described approach, we have 
compiled a database comprising 1205 images of olives, 1341 
images of leaves, and 1130 images of other objects. The 
database is divided into two sets, with 80% of the data dedicated 
for computing embeddings during the initial training of SVM. 
Following this, the same dataset is employed for fine-tuning the

IV. CONCLUSION 
We proposed in this paper to develop a customized approach 
employing AI to detect olives and leaves in smartphone photos. 
Our approach has the advantage that it permits the self-
construction of a database using the Grounding-DINO model, 
followed by the SAM model. The first model detected olives and 
leaves, while the former segmented these objects and then 
constructed the database. This process was applied with two 
confidence thresholds. A high threshold was used to reliably 
detect sought-after objects (i.e., olives or leaves), and a second, 
relatively low threshold was used to detect every object 
presumed to be olives or leaves (e.g., with possible false 
detection). This part allowed us to construct the third class 
consisting of objects detected other than olives or leaves. Given 
the database, it becomes possible to train a learning technique. 
We applied DINOv2 to the different classes in our database to 
provide an embeddings that were used to train an SVM. Finally, 
each smartphone photo was segmented by the SAM model, and 
subsequently, each segmented object was classified using the 
trained SVM. 
We demonstrated that this approach not only provided 
automation in creating the database but also achieved 
classification performance better than ResNet50 trained our 
created database. Additionally, the showcased results of our 
approach applied to smartphone photos have proved the 
performances of our approach. 
In future, our approach will be used to estimate the olive tree 
yield and it will be extended to other tree species.
