A B S T R A C T
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have been widely adopted for reconstructing high-quality 3D scenes from 2D RGB 
images. However, achieving accurate 3D object segmentation within these reconstructed scenes remains chal­
lenging. Existing NeRF-based segmentation methods either rely on post-processing (SA3D), which produces noisy 
point clouds due to the absence of density field optimization, or employ joint training with additional seg­
mentation heads (FruitNeRF), which can lead to suboptimal performance due to conflicting learning objectives. 
In this work, we propose InvNeRF-Seg (Input-substitution NeRF for Segmentation), a two-stage fine-tuning 
strategy for 3D object segmentation that preserves the original NeRF architecture and loss function entirely. We 
first train a standard NeRF on RGB images and then fine-tune it using 2D segmentation masks formatted as RGB- 
like inputs, without introducing any architectural modifications or additional loss functions. This input- 
substitution approach reshapes the density field to align with object regions while suppressing background 
density. We validate InvNeRF-Seg through comprehensive ablation studies examining the roles of density and 
color MLPs, loss function choices, and training strategies. Field density analysis reveals consistent semantic 
refinement: densities of object regions increase while background densities are suppressed. Experiments on 
synthetic fruit datasets and real-world soybean imagery demonstrate that InvNeRF-Seg produces cleaner 3D 
segmented point clouds compared to both SA3D and FruitNeRF, enabling more accurate downstream object 
counting. The method is further validated on a self-collected soybean dataset to demonstrate its applicability in 
real-world agricultural scenarios. Our code is available at https://github.com/ZJiangsan/InvNeRF-Seg.
1. Introduction
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have demonstrated remarkable suc­
cess in reconstructing photo-realistic 3D scenes from 2D images by 
learning a volumetric scene representation [1–3]. However, while 
NeRF-based models have been widely adopted for view synthesis, 
research into object-level 3D segmentation within reconstructed NeRF 
scenes remains relatively limited [1,4,5].
Accurate 3D segmentation is essential for downstream applications 
such as robotic perception [6], augmented reality (AR) [7], autonomous 
driving [8], medical imaging [9], and plant phenotyping in agriculture 
[4,10]. However, existing approaches to 3D segmentation either rely on 
fully annotated point clouds—requiring substantial manual labeling 
[11]—or introduce significant architectural complexity by coupling 
additional semantic branches or loss functions into NeRF models [4,5]. 
Alternatively, post-processing-based methods like SA3D [1] perform 3D 
segmentation by back-projecting 2D masks, but suffer from noise and 
lack of density refinement. These limitations hinder the adoption of 
NeRF-based segmentation in real-world applications where robustness 
and ease of implementation are critical.
A key observation motivating this work is that directly training NeRF 
on binary segmentation masks—which lack the smooth photometric 
gradients essential for geometry learning—leads to complete failure, 
producing entirely black, degenerate outputs (Fig. 1). However, when a 
NeRF model has already learned accurate geometry from RGB images, 
fine-tuning on binary masks can effectively reshape the density field to 
focus on foreground objects while suppressing background density.
Motivated by these challenges, we introduce InvNeRF-Seg, a simple 
yet effective two-step fine-tuning method for NeRF-based 3D object 
segmentation. The method maintains the original NeRF architecture and 
loss function entirely unchanged. The first step involves training a high- 
quality NeRF model on multi-view RGB images using a standard NeRF

2. Related work
Classical deep learning methods for 3D segmentation, such as 
PointNet [11] and PointNet++ [12], have achieved remarkable success. 
However, their reliance on labor-intensive and time-consuming 3D point 
cloud annotation remains a significant bottleneck, limiting their scal­
ability and applicability across domains [13]. Additionally, these 
methods often struggle in scenarios with noisy or incomplete point 
clouds, especially in complex environments with occlusions [14,15].
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) have transformed 3D reconstruction 
through learning implicit neural representations of scenes from multi- 
view RGB images, enabling photo-realistic novel view synthesis [2]. 
Building on this foundation, several approaches have been developed on 
top of NeRF to achieve 3D segmentation with the help of segmented 2D 
segmentation masks. Among these, two prominent categories have 
emerged: Segment Anything in 3D (SA3D) [1] and FruitNeRF [4].

3. Proposed approach
3.1. Motivation
NeRF models rely heavily on smooth photometric gradients—such as 
color, shading, and texture—to infer scene geometry [2]. As a result, 
directly training NeRF using binary segmentation masks, which lack 
these properties, typically leads to failure. The model produces entirely 
black outputs with no meaningful geometry (Fig. 1), because binary 
masks provide insufficient gradient signals for learning a coherent 
density field from scratch.
However, a NeRF trained on RGB images already encodes accurate 
geometry. We hypothesized that fine-tuning the model on 2D binary 
masks—without changing the model or loss functions—could shift the 
density field to focus on foreground objects. This simple fine-tuning 
strategy proved effective, resulting in high-quality segmentation in 
both rendered 2D masks and 3D point clouds.

The model architecture and loss functions are unchanged compared

masked object regions, since only those regions contribute to the loss. 
This formulation preserves the original NeRF architecture, training 
pipeline, and loss function, but shifts the model’s learning objective via 
input substitution alone. Because Stage 2 fine-tunes the entire network 
on masks formatted as RGB inputs, the resulting checkpoint is a dedi­
cated segmentation model: its rendered output encodes foreground 
probability rather than scene appearance, and the original colour, 
shading, and texture are intentionally overwritten. The two stages are 
stored as separate checkpoints. The Stage-1 RGB model is retained both 
as the initialization for fine-tuning and for any task that still requires 
photometric rendering, while the Stage-2 model is used exclusively for 
segmentation and point-cloud extraction. This separation is by 
design—appearance and semantics are represented by two specialized 
models rather than one multi-task network—which is precisely what 
allows each to be optimized without compromise.
4. Experiments and results
4.1. Dataset
We used the apple and peach datasets from synthetic fruit trees in the 
publicly available FruitNeRF Dataset as testing examples (Fig. 2a and b). 
Details about the data creation can be found in FruitNeRF [4]. Since the 
dataset includes segmentation masks, camera intrinsics and poses, no 
additional processing was required.
To further challenge the models, we collected a real-world soybean 
dataset specifically for this study. Video recordings of a soybean plant 
was captured using an iPhone 12 Pro under natural outdoor lighting 
conditions, with bright sunlight to reflect realistic field environments 
(Fig. 2c and d). The soybean plant was in a mature stage with clearly 
visible pods. The video was captured by manually moving a handheld 
camera around the soybean plant placed on the ground, ensuring multi- 
view coverage from different angles. Standard preprocessing was per­
formed, including frame extraction from video using FFmpeg [16] at 5 
frames per second and followed by camera pose estimation using COL­
MAP [17]. After discarding motion-blurred frames from the handheld 
capture, 149 clean RGB images of the plant were retained for recon­
struction; camera poses were estimated with COLMAP, and this single 
reconstruction was used for both the single-pod and double-pod seg­
mentation tasks, which share the same RGB images and differ only in 
their target masks (a single-pod mask or a double-pod mask). To 
generate pod segmentation masks, we used the AnyLabeling annotation 
tool with Segment Anything (MobileSAM) [18] integrated for automatic 
mask generation. Two tasks were defined using this dataset: (1) binary 
instance-level 3D segmentation of a single soybean pod, and (2) 
multi-instance 3D segmentation of two pods from the same plant. Two 
pods were selected as a controlled proof of concept for the 
multi-instance setting rather than as an exhaustive plant-level count; 
annotating every pod on the plant was not the objective of this feasibility 
validation and is constrained by severe pod-to-pod occlusion in a single 
handheld capture.

The RGB training stage was performed for 4000 epochs using

1 × 10⁻⁴ for the camera optimizer. The subsequent fine-tuning stage was

NeRF was trained for a total of 6000 epochs to match the overall training 
budget.
4.2.2. SA3D implementation
We implemented two variants of SA3D for comparison. The original 
weight-based SA3D back-projects 2D segmentation masks into 3D using 
volume-rendered weights with a threshold of τ = 0.1, producing surface- 
like point clouds. In addition, we implemented a density-based variant 
that thresholds raw field density (σ ≥70) combined with 2D mask 
filtering, resulting in denser volumetric point clouds that better capture 
object interiors. This density-thresholded variant extracts points directly 
from the unmodified NeRF density field, followed by the same DBSCAN 
clustering pipeline used for all methods. It therefore serves as a 
geometry-based NeRF + DBSCAN baseline without any density-field 
refinement.
Both variants operate on the pretrained RGB NeRF model without 
any additional training or fine-tuning. The density-based variant is 
included to provide a more favorable setting for clustering-based 
downstream tasks, as volumetric representations are better suited for 
density-based clustering methods such as DBSCAN.
4.2.3. Clustering and counting
All methods used an identical clustering pipeline to ensure fair 
comparison: DBSCAN clustering (eps=0.0065, min_samples=3), fol­
lowed by recursive splitting of large clusters using KMeans based on size 
and volume statistics. This ensures that counting accuracy differences 
reflect segmentation quality rather than post-processing choices.
4.3. Evaluation metrics
Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) [20] and Intersection over Union 
(IoU) [21] were used to evaluate the quality of the rendered 2D RGB 
images and masks, respectively, during training. The accuracy of fruit 
counting was used to evaluate the quality of generated 3D segmentation 
point clouds. A two-tailed t-test was performed using SciPy (v1.15.2) 
[22] to evaluate statistical significance.
4.4. Experimental environment
All experiments were carried out on a Windows 11 Pro platform with 
Intel Core i9 5 GHz × 16 processor (CPU), 64 GB of RAM, and NVIDIA 
RTX A5500 GPU with 16 GB of RAM.
4.5. Results on synthetic datasets
4.5.1. Rendered image and mask quality
The quality of the rendered RGB images for both apple and peach 
datasets was comparable between InvNeRF-Seg and FruitNeRF, as 
indicated by the non-significant differences in PSNR values—despite 
slightly higher scores from InvNeRF-Seg (Fig. S1). This outcome is ex­
pected, as both models share the same architecture for RGB-based 3D 
reconstruction.
The IoU of the rendered masks for both apple and peach datasets was 
significantly higher in InvNeRF-Seg compared to FruitNeRF, with p 
values below 0.001—highlighting the superior segmentation accuracy 
of InvNeRF-Seg (Fig. 3). This is expected, as FruitNeRF remains 
dependent on RGB supervision, treating segmentation as a secondary 
task. In contrast, InvNeRF-Seg fine-tunes the density field directly using 
ground truth masks, allowing for more precise alignment between the 
learned radiance field and object boundaries.
4.5.2. 3D point cloud quality
The clearest difference between methods is visible in the 3D 
segmented point clouds, apple (Fig. 4) and peach (Fig. S2). InvNeRF-Seg 
produces clean, well-separated fruit-shaped blobs with minimal noise. 
The instances in Fig. 4 are sufficiently well separated that a simple non- 
learned clustering method (DBSCAN) recovers near-exact fruit counts 
(282 vs. 283 for apple and 155 vs. 152 for peach) without requiring a 
learned detector. This demonstrates one practical advantage of reshap­
ing the density field into discrete, well-separated object instances. 
FruitNeRF generates recognizable but fragmented fruits with scattered 
points around object boundaries. SA3D (both variants) produces heavily 
noisy point clouds with extensive scattered points throughout the scene. 
This quality difference is primarily attributable to the refined density 
field: InvNeRF-Seg’s fine-tuning suppresses background density while 
reinforcing object density, whereas SA3D operates on an unmodified 
density field and FruitNeRF’s joint training compromises density field 
quality.

4.6.2. Component analysis: density vs. color MLP
Nerfacto’s architecture consists of a density MLP (mlp_base, 12.2 M 
parameters) and a color MLP (mlp_head, 39 K parameters). We isolated 
the contributions of each by selectively freezing one during fine-tuning. 
The qualitative differences in 3D segmentation under different training 
strategies are illustrated in Fig. 5, where InvNeRF-Seg produces cleaner 
and more coherent point clouds compared to partial fine-tuning or joint 
training approaches.
Fine-tune density only (color frozen). Training progresses slowly, 
taking roughly 3000 epochs before IoU begins to improve. This is likely 
due to the large number of parameters in the density MLP, which makes 
optimization under mask-only supervision difficult. Nevertheless, 
modifying the density field is essential for forming clean and well- 
separated 3D point cloud structures.
Fine-tune color only (density frozen). Training converges quickly, 
reflecting the small size of the color MLP. However, since the geometry 
is fixed, the model produces spatially biased masks and flat depth maps. 
As a result, the extracted point clouds contain noticeably more noise.
Fine-tuning both (InvNeRF-Seg). Jointly updating both compo­
nents leads to faster convergence and higher IoU, indicating that 
adapting color and density together is necessary for the best overall 
performance.
Counting results further validate this analysis (Table 2). While 
density-only fine-tuning achieves near-perfect counting on apple (281/ 
283), color-only fine-tuning significantly overcounts (294/283), indi­
cating that density refinement is critical for producing well-separated 3D 
clusters suitable for downstream tasks.
4.6.3. Loss function: MSE vs. BCE in joint training
To isolate whether the advantage of InvNeRF-Seg comes from the 
two-stage strategy or the loss function, we trained FruitNeRF with MSE 
loss (replacing its default BCE) on the segmentation head. FruitNeRF 
with MSE achieved comparable final IoU to BCE but with slower initial 
convergence. Since both loss functions yield similar results under joint 
training, yet InvNeRF-Seg outperforms both, this demonstrates that the 
two-stage fine-tuning strategy—not the loss function choice—is the 
primary driver of InvNeRF-Seg's advantage.

4.7. SA3D variant comparison
We compared the original weight-based SA3D with our density- 
based variant. The density-based variant produces cleaner, denser 
point clouds because raw density captures object interiors rather than 
surfaces only. Despite this more favorable formulation for SA3D, 
InvNeRF-Seg still substantially outperforms both SA3D variants in 
downstream counting (Table 1), confirming that the advantage comes 
from density field refinement through fine-tuning, not from the point 
cloud extraction strategy.

4.9. Extension to multi-instance segmentation
Although InvNeRF-Seg was originally designed for binary segmen­
tation, we further tested its ability to handle multi-instance segmenta­
tion by separating two distinct pods from the same soybean plant. 
Despite using the same MSE loss and unchanged model architecture, the 
method produced promising results for double-pod segmentation. 
However, its performance was slightly lower than in the binary case, as 
evidenced by some misclassified points near the instance boundaries 
(Fig. 9). SA3D also performed reasonably well in this task, although its 
outputs exhibited noticeable noise across both object and background 
regions.
4.10. Field Density Change Analysis
The volumetric field density before and after fine-tuning was 
analyzed to better understand the internal behavior of InvNeRF-Seg. 
Targeted object regions consistently show increased density after fine- 
tuning, leading to sharper and more localized peaks along sampled 
rays (Fig. 10a-f). In contrast, non-targeted objects and background areas 
exhibit field density suppression or small fluctuations around zero, 
which results in minimal accumulated weights and effective background 
removal (Fig. 10g-l). These patterns confirm that InvNeRF-Seg seman­
tically reshapes the radiance field without requiring architectural 
changes.

5. Discussion
Our experiments across both synthetic and real-world datasets 
demonstrate that InvNeRF-Seg, a two-step fine-tuning strategy that le­
verages a pre-trained RGB NeRF, consistently outperforms existing 3D 
segmentation methods—namely SA3D [1] and the joint training-based 
FruitNeRF [4]—in terms of segmentation accuracy, 3D point cloud 
quality, and object-level counting performance.
Decoupling geometry and semantics. The fundamental insight is 
that geometry learning and semantic learning should be decoupled. 
NeRF models rely on smooth color gradients in RGB images to learn 
scene geometry; binary segmentation masks with hard transitions pro­
vide limited geometric cues and can hinder training if used too early. 
Joint learning, as in FruitNeRF, forces the model to optimize for con­
flicting objectives—RGB fidelity and semantic separation—using shared 
features, which can lead to suboptimal performance on both tasks [23]. 
In contrast, InvNeRF-Seg preserves geometric integrity by first opti­
mizing for RGB reconstruction, and only then introduces semantic su­
pervision during fine-tuning.
Nature of the contribution. InvNeRF-Seg is not a new network 
architecture but a training strategy that exploits an empirical property of 
converged NeRF models: once a smooth density field has been learned 
from RGB images, it can be reshaped toward object regions by continued 
optimization of the unchanged MSE objective on mask-substituted in­
puts. The contribution is therefore twofold—an empirical finding about 
the adaptability of pretrained radiance fields, and a simple, architecture- 
invariant recipe that turns this finding into a practical 3D segmentation 
tool. We make no claim of a new loss or module; the value lies in 
showing that none is needed.
Why input substitution reshapes the density field. The mecha­
nism follows from the volume-rendering objective. Under MSE super­
vision,

density redistribution observed in Fig. 10. Starting from a pretrained 
field is essential: the gradients sharpen an existing surface rather than 
having to discover geometry from a near-binary signal, which is why 
training from scratch collapses (Fig. 1). This view also predicts when 
substitution fails—when foreground and background project onto the 
same rays (thin or heavily occluded structures), when 2D masks are 
inconsistent across views (the field cannot satisfy contradictory targets 
and blurs the boundary), and when the sampling volume greatly exceeds 
the object, so that most samples receive an uninformative background 
target and the effective foreground gradient is diluted, the same effect 
addressed by the far-plane setting discussed below.
Why density adapts rather than colour. A natural question is why, 
under the unchanged MSE objective, the network satisfies the mask 
target by reshaping the density field rather than by simply having the 
colour MLP emit white on foreground samples and black elsewhere. 
Both pathways receive gradients, but they are not equally effective. The 
colour MLP can only set the emitted value of a sample that is already 
being rendered; it cannot remove the contribution of background or 
occluding matter along the ray, because that contribution is governed by 
density and transmittance rather than colour. To render a clean binary 
image consistently across all views, the network must therefore 
concentrate accumulated weight on the object surface and drive it to 
zero elsewhere—an operation only the density field can perform, since a 
purely colour-based solution would have to encode a consistent fore­
ground/background label for every 3D sample under every viewing di­
rection, which the geometry represents far more parsimoniously. The 
ablations confirm this: colour-only fine-tuning leaves the density field 
diffuse and overcounts (294 versus a ground truth of 283), whereas 
density adaptation yields the clean, separable clusters required for ac­
curate counting (Fig. 5; Table 2).

reveal that both density and color MLPs play complementary roles 
during fine-tuning. The density MLP reshapes the volumetric structure, 
which is critical for clean 3D point cloud extraction—as evidenced by

Volume control is critical. Setting an appropriately small far plane 
is essential for stable fine-tuning, especially when the target object oc­
cupies a small portion of the scene. For instance, fine-tuning for single- 
pod segmentation consistently failed when using a large far plane (e.g., 
1000), but succeeded reliably when the far plane was reduced to 2. The 
far plane must be consistent between RGB NeRF training and fine- 
tuning. The reason is that a large far plane places most sampled points 
in empty background whose mask target is zero; the background term 
then dominates the loss and the sparse foreground gradient cannot build 
a coherent density peak, which is why single-pod fine-tuning failed at a 
far plane of 1000 but succeeded at 2. In practice the far plane need not 
be found by trial and error: it can be set directly from the reconstruction 
geometry—for example from the extent of the COLMAP sparse point 
cloud or the depth range rendered by the Stage-1 model—choosing a 
value that just encloses the object of interest. The apple and peach scenes 
required no adjustment because the fruit-bearing tree fills the recon­
structed volume, so the default far plane already concentrates sampling 
on foreground.
Relation to direct 3D reconstruction. InvNeRF-Seg does depend on 
2D masks, but this is a mild requirement in practice: 2D segmentation is 
mature and can be produced automatically with foundation models such 
as SAM, whereas direct 3D segmentation would require annotated point 
clouds—the very bottleneck this work aims to avoid—or a separately 
reconstructed and registered 3D model that still has to be segmented. By 
lifting inexpensive 2D masks into a geometry-consistent 3D field, the 
method converts a well-supported 2D task into 3D labels with no manual 
3D annotation. The trade-off is that segmentation quality is upper- 
bounded by the multi-view consistency of the input masks and by the 
fidelity of the underlying reconstruction.
Choice of baselines. SA3D and FruitNeRF were selected because 
they represent the two established paradigms for NeRF-based 3D 
segmentation—training-free post-processing of a fixed field (SA3D) and 
joint RGB-plus-semantics training (FruitNeRF)—and together bracket 
the design space that InvNeRF-Seg occupies. We additionally evaluated 
a density-thresholded SA3D variant (raw NeRF density with 2D-mask 
filtering, clustered with DBSCAN) as a stronger volumetric baseline. 
Feature-field distillation methods that lift 2D foundation-model features 
into NeRF are a promising further comparison, but they target open- 
vocabulary querying rather than the single-object, count-oriented 
setting studied here; a broader benchmark against them is left to future 
work.
Limitations. InvNeRF-Seg assumes access to accurate multi-view 
RGB images and corresponding 2D segmentation masks. The multi- 
instance extension using intensity-encoded masks shows boundary am­
biguity due to the regression nature of MSE [24]. Incorporating 
class-aware losses such as cross-entropy may improve multi-instance 
performance, although this would break the “zero-change” design. The 
method has been validated primarily on agricultural datasets; evalua­
tion on general-purpose scenes with more diverse object categories re­
mains future work. Our soybean masks were generated with MobileSAM 
and manually verified. More broadly, the proposed two-stage segmen­
tation is, by design, dependent on two high-quality inputs—a 
high-fidelity RGB NeRF reconstruction and accurate 2D segmentation 
masks—both of which are standard, well-supported components 
obtainable with current NeRF pipelines and foundation-model seg­
menters, and are treated here as prerequisites of the method rather than 
variables it must be robust to. Characterizing the precise input-quality 
threshold below which the pipeline fails is not well defined, since 
neither reconstruction nor segmentation quality reduces to a single 
tunable axis and the tolerance depends on scene geometry, object scale, 
occlusion, and view distribution; we therefore scope the method to the 
good-quality-input regime and make no claim outside it. Point-cloud 
quality is reported here through downstream counting accuracy, as 
dense 3D metrics such as Chamfer distance or 3D IoU require per-point 
ground truth, which neither the FruitNeRF synthetic data (annotated by 
fruit count) nor the field-collected soybean data provide. Finally, the 
real-world validation is deliberately small in scale—one plant and one to 
two pods—and is intended to demonstrate feasibility under field con­
ditions rather than large-scale performance; because no dense 3D 
ground truth exists for the field plant, its reconstructions are assessed by 
rendered-mask IoU/PSNR during training and by qualitative 3D in­
spection, and evaluation on multi-plant, densely-podded canopies re­
mains future work.
6. Conclusions
We proposed InvNeRF-Seg, a two-step, zero-change fine-tuning 
strategy for NeRF-based 3D object segmentation that preserves the 
original network architecture and loss function. Through systematic 
ablation studies, we demonstrated that: (1) pretrained geometry is 
essential—training on masks from scratch fails completely; (2) both 
density and color MLPs must co-adapt during fine-tuning for optimal 
performance; and (3) the two-stage approach outperforms joint training 
regardless of loss function choice (MSE or BCE).
InvNeRF-Seg consistently produced cleaner 3D segmented point 
clouds than the compared methods, enabling accurate downstream ob­
ject counting with minimal post-processing tuning. Field-level analysis 
reveals reinforced density in target regions and suppression of back­
ground content, confirming effective semantic refinement within the 
radiance field. Moreover, the method extends naturally from binary 
instance-level to multi-instance segmentation tasks, demonstrating 
generalizability across scenes of varying complexities.
This zero-change, modular strategy provides a practical architecture- 
invariant framework for bridging 2D segmentation and 3D semantic 
reconstruction.
