A B S T R A C T  
Increasing farm labour and input costs and the requirement for more orchard data are leading to rapid advances 
in technology to improve management systems in fruit production. The study aimed to (i) validate a sensorised 
platform to estimate fruit number, peel colour and blush coverage in pear orchards with different pear selections 
and tree architectures, (ii) establish relationships between fruit number, peel colour and canopy geometry fea­
tures, and (iii) evaluate the platform for mapping and digitising orchard features. The study was carried out over 
two years in an experimental ‘ANP-0131′ orchard and in one year in two commercial pear orchards (‘ANP-0131′ 
and ‘PremP009’). Predictions of fruit number and blush coverage were compared in traditional three- 
dimensional (3D) and modern high-density two-dimensional (2D) training systems. Overall, prediction errors 
for fruit number were < 6.5 % in all the training systems, but improved performance was achieved in vertical 2D 
configurations (% standard errors = 2.2 %). Fruit number and blush coverage per unit of leaf area were higher in 
2D compared to 3D training systems. Blush coverage predictions in ‘ANP-0131′ were reliable (R2 = 0.67, RMSE 
= 3.70 %). Accurate predictions of blush coverage classes were achieved by modifying sample variance. Fruit 
number and blush coverage were negatively affected by increasing canopy size. The platform proved useful for 
mapping and digitisation. Spatial heatmaps of orchard features provided a valuable visual aid to identify zones 
for priority interventions for peel colour enhancement.   
1. Introduction 
Current and future developments of precision orchard management 
require incremental technological uptake within farms. Obtaining ac­
curate and scalable data from the orchard should be a prerequisite for 
fruit growers to accept and incorporate technology in their business 
models. Orchard operations such as crop load and fruit quality man­
agement often rely on small subsamples of fruit or subjective visual 
observations of fruit size, number and colour carried out by seasonal 
workers with limited experience. In addition to the low-sample and 
high-subjectivity limitations, the cost of farm labour is increasing 
worldwide, leading to Australian fruit growers struggling to maintain 
profitability (Amin-Chaudhry et al., 2022). It is therefore pivotal to 
reduce orchard costs by, amongst others, increasing technology adop­
tion and replacing expensive untrained seasonal workers with the 
long-term goal to maintain fruit production sustainability in the region. 
Technology to detect fruit in the orchard is now widely available at 
experimental, prototype-release, and commercial stages, and it repre­
sents a large topic in horticulture research (Ukwuoma et al., 2022). 
Currently, the majority of technology used for fruit detection in orchards 
rely on the integration of machine vision and deep learning algorithms 
that can often achieve accuracy over 95 % in experimental conditions 
(Anderson et al., 2021; Koirala et al., 2019). Detection errors are typi­
cally caused by factors such as changes in illumination, foliage occlu­
sions, fruit overlaps and next-row detections (Tang et al., 2023). As a 
consequence, due to better visibility, fruit detection accuracy in tree 
canopies with two-dimensional (2D) configurations improve compared 
to three-dimensional (3D) systems, as demonstrated in apple (Bortolotti 
et al., 2021) and stone fruit (Islam et al., 2022). Accurate estimates of 
fruit number at different stages of the fruit growing season—i.e., from 
fruit set to harvest—can empower fruit growers to take decisions for 
fruit thinning requirements (e.g., labour for hand thinning; type of

Tree canopy architecture parameters can be measured using 
different technologies. Although attempts to accurately reproduce 3D 
vegetation structures with machine vision and ultrasounds have previ­
ously been carried out (Colaço et al., 2018; Kolhar and Jagtap, 2023; 
Zaman and Schumann, 2005), Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) 
generates significantly improved accuracy (Dandois and Ellis, 2010) and 
has become a widely accepted technology in horticultural industries. 
Important architectural parameters such as canopy height, width, den­
sity, volume and area, leaf area and leaf area index can be derived from 
LiDAR-3D cloud point imagery by applying geometrical concepts to the 
voxels (Escol`a et al., 2017; Rosell Polo et al., 2009; Sanz-Cortiella et al., 
2011; Sanz et al., 2013; Underwood et al., 2016). In addition, it was 
demonstrated that canopy light interception can be estimated or 
modelled from LiDAR-derived canopy geometry features (¨Orn, 2016; 
Scalisi et al., 2021a; Westling et al., 2018, 2020). 
The Australian pear industry is currently threatened by concomitant 
increasing costs (mostly labour) and reduced domestic demand (Aus­
tralasian Agriculture Equities, 2021). To counter reduced demand, 
breeding programs and marketing campaigns need to target selections 
that produce fruit that are appealing for consumers and withstand long 
cold chain storage, while adapting to modern high-density orchards that 
maximise productivity and fruit quality. Worldwide, breeding programs 
have recently favoured the selection of pear fruit with red pigmentation 
(blush or full coverage) over traditional green pears. For example, an 
Australian breeding program recently developed and patented the two 
blush pear cultivars ‘ANP-0131′ (Turpin, 2015a) and ‘ANP-0118′ (Tur­
pin, 2015b) that are currently marketed as Ric´o™ and Lanya™, 
respectively, while a New Zealand breeding program patented the fully 
red cultivar ‘Prem009’ (White, 2015) registered as Piqa® Boo®. 
Technology capable of block scale estimations of crop load, peel 
redness and blush coverage, fruit size and canopy size could enhance the 
implementation of precision management strategies to improve overall 
marketable yield, reduce within-orchard variability, and reduce costs 
associated with labour and manual operations. However, to date, there 
is no clear evidence that shows the reliability of remote or proximal 
sensing to accurately, and consistently, predict tree features at large 
scale in pear orchards with diverse training system configurations. A 
successful attempt to measure spatial variability of yield and tree vigour 
in a pear orchard was previously carried out by Perry et al. (2010) using 
UAV imagery. The accuracy of a commercial sensorised platform for 
predictions of (i) tree geometry, light interception, flower and fruit 
number in apple (Scalisi et al., 2021a), (ii) fruit number, size and colour 
in stone fruit (Islam et al., 2022), and (iii) fruit size in pears (Scalisi et al., 
2023b) has been recently validated. Reliable digitisation of orchard 
features will allow in-depth understanding of temporal crop responses to 
numerous factors and is paramount to support the growth of orchard 
profitability and maintain long term sustainability. 
Previous research unveiled underlying relationship trends between 
fruit number and peel colour development in pear cultivars (Scalisi 
et al., 2023a). The blush redness of ‘ANP-0131′ was reduced in larger 
fruit and in trees with large canopies, whereas colour development in 
full-red ‘PremP009’ pears was only marginally affected by fruit size and 
canopy size, and was likely to be more influenced by air temperature. 
This might explain why pear growers in the Goulburn Valley (Australia) 
reported lack of peel redness development in ‘PremP009’, as opposed to 
growers in cooler regions (New Zealand or Northern Italy). This phe­
nomenon can also be explained by the different light environments in 
the growing regions. Some effects of temperature on the blush colour of 
‘ANP-0118′ pears have been recently reported (McClymont et al., 2023). 
Relationships between canopy parameters, crop load and fruit 
quality need to be further studied in trees with different canopy archi­
tectures to generate effective orchard management practice guidelines 
for growers. This study aimed to determine the reliability of a sensorised 
platform to estimate fruit number and peel colour in pear orchards with 
different training system configurations, and to study their relationship 
with canopy geometry features. The research attempted to compare 
prediction reliability in traditional 3D and in modern high-density 2D 
training systems. The hypothesis was that machine vision performance 
for fruit detection and their feature estimation deteriorates in 3D canopy 
scans compared to 2D systems due to missing detections. In addition, the 
research aimed to determine the accuracy of blush coverage classifica­
tion in a modern blush pear to inform supply chain and forecast packout, 
and to evaluate the use of the platform for mapping and digitisation of 
orchard features for precision agriculture applications. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Orchards and experimental design 
The study was carried out over the two summer seasons 2020–21 
(year 1) and 2021–22 (year 2) in the Goulburn Valley (Greater Shep­
parton, Victoria, Australia). Greater Shepparton is the largest pear 
production region in Australia (72.9 %) (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 
2022). 
2.1.1. Experimental orchard 
An experimental ’ANP-0131′ pear orchard located at the Tatura 
SmartFarm (36◦, 26′, 19.97′’ S and 145◦, 15′, 54.04′’ E, 114 m above sea 
level) was used to obtain measurements in years 1 and 2. The orchard 
was planted in 2013 and consisted of three traditional training sys­
tems—i.e., spindle (SP), central leader (CL) and vase (VA)—and two 
modern high-density systems: a multileader vertical trellis (MLVT), and 
a multileader open Tatura trellis (MLOT). The orchard has 4.5 m row 
spacing and various tree spacings. Both MLVT and MLOT trees consisted 
of two horizontal cordons that had leaders (i.e., uprights) with 50 cm 
spacing, and equivalent to ~ 4444 and ~ 8889 leaders/ha for MLVT and 
MLOT, respectively. SP, CL, VA and MLOT systems were considered to 
have 3D canopies, whereas MLVT systems were classified as 2D fruiting 
walls. 
The experiment was designed as a split-plot randomised complete 
block with three replicates of each treatment, except for MLOT and 
MLVT, which had 9 replicates. Each plot was 14 m along the tree row 
(north–south) with a central measurement row and two guard rows (east 
and west). Orchard management operations such as soil management, 
irrigation, nutrition, integrated pest and disease management and weed 
management followed commercial practices. Additional information on 
the experimental conditions at the Tatura SmartFarm is available in 
recent literature (McClymont et al., 2021). 
2.1.2. Commercial orchards 
Two commercial orchards (Calimna Orchard, Ardmona, Victoria, 
Australia) were used for technology validation during Year 2. The two 
blocks had different modern cultivars—‘ANP-0131′ and ‘PremP009’.

2.1.3. Cultivar description 
This study involved the use of the two modern cultivars ‘ANP-0131′ 
and ‘PremP009’. ‘ANP-0131′ (Pyrus communis) was developed and 
patented in Australia (Turpin, 2015a). ‘ANP-0131′ is a bicolour pear 
with a green background and a dark-red overcolour (blush) in the peel. 
‘ANP-0131′ was derived from crossing ‘Corella’ (seed parent) and 
‘Doyenne du Comice’ (pollen parent) and is marketed with the name 
Ric´o™. According to the Australian Apple and Pear industry (Apple and 
Pear Australia Ltd), blush coverage (%) represents one of the most 
important fruit quality characteristics to determine packout of Ric´o™ 
fruit. 
‘PremP009’ is an interspecific pear (Pyrus communis × Pyrus pyrifolia 
× Pyrus bretschneideri) developed and patented in a New Zealand 
breeding program (White, 2015). ‘PremP009’ has uniform dark-red 
colouration and is commercialised with the name Piqa® Boo®. The in­
tensity of red colouration represents an important quality parameter for 
Piqa® Boo® pears. 
Both ‘ANP-0131′ and ‘PremP009’ are under industry evaluation for 
large-scale production in order to meet the high demand from the south- 
east Asian market. 
2.2. Scanning platform and technology 
The sensorised mobile platform Cartographer (Green Atlas, Alexan­
dria, NSW, Australia) was utilised for the collection of raw RGB images 
and to apply deep-learning models for the prediction of fruit number and 
colour. Concomitantly, light detection and ranging (LiDAR) point cloud 
images were logged by the platform to generate canopy architecture 
parameters. The platform utilised in this study included two RGB cam­
eras, four custom-built strobe lights for canopy illumination, a LiDAR 
scanner (orientated to scan the vertical profile of the trees while pass­
ing), and a global navigation satellite system (GNSS). The reliability of 
this technology was recently validated on apples (Scalisi et al., 2021a) 
and stone fruit (Islam et al., 2022). 
A phone-interface was used to control logging and record metadata 
to aid retrospective identification. Logging was switched on a few me­
tres prior to the start of the measurement section and off a few metres 
past the end of the measurement section. Measurements were done early 
in the morning—07:00 am Australian Eastern Daylight Time (AEDT)— 
in line with a previous study on stone fruit (Scalisi et al., 2022) that 
demonstrated that early morning or late afternoon scans can achieve 
statistically similar fruit colour estimates to nocturnal (ideal) 
measurements. 
Fig. 1 shows examples of tree shape transects of the five systems 
under study (i.e., SP, CL, VA, MLVT and MLOT) obtained with LiDAR 
scanner mounted on the scanning platform. The orchard had retractable 
overhead hail netting, as shown in blue (Fig. 1). 
The scans obtained in the ‘ANP-0131′ experimental site at the Tatura 
SmartFarm were done driving Cartographer at an average speed of 4.4 
and 8.2 km h−1 in 2021 and 2022, respectively. The low speed utilised in 
the first season was precautionary due to limited experience. The driving 
speed determined the final image sample size, as Cartographer logged 
images at an interval of 5 images/s. The sample size generated with 
2022 scans was considered adequate for this experiment (n > 200, 
Table 2). MLOT and MLVT systems had a number of images approxi­
mately three-fold higher than other systems, as they included plots with 
tree spacings aligned to spacings in SP, CL and VA systems, albeit with 
leader density maintained constant. 
2.3. Fruit number 
The Green Atlas protocol involves calibration to obtain absolute fruit 
number per tree or per hectare. In our case we calibrated detections per 
image against fruit number per hectare as our experiment included trees 
at different spacings and a calibration to obtain fruit number / tree 
might have been misleading due to variable number of trees visible in 
each image collected using Cartographer. 
Mobile scans of both sides of short row sections (7–10 m) were used 
to obtain counts of fruit (detections per image) in the experimental 
‘ANP-0131′ orchard at the Tatura SmartFarm. Ground truth counts of 
fruit were obtained in the same row sections with a commercial fruit 
grader equipped with optical sensors (InVision 9000; Compac Sorting 
Equipment Ltd, Colliver, Victoria, Australia) at harvest in years 1 and 2. 
Ground truth counts were used to calibrate detections per image and 
predicted counts were expressed in number per hectare (Table 2) to 
simplify interpretation due to variable tree spacing amongst training 
systems.

2.4. Peel colour 
Peel colour redness was estimated using the colour development 
index (CDI) derived from hue angle values, as demonstrated in previous 
work on peach and nectarine (Islam et al., 2022; Scalisi et al., 2022), 
measured by the mobile platform Cartographer. CDI was estimated 
within the central pixels of the detection boxes generated after 
deep-learning recognition of pear fruit. CDI conveniently requires no 
calibration although association with a visual colour scale is helpful for 
interpretation. Due to cultivar-specific peel colour features, the inter­
pretation of CDI changes between blush pears and fully red pears. While 
in ‘PremP009’ CDI represents a powerful redness indicator as it is, in 
‘ANP-0131′ it was important to unveil its association with blush 
coverage. 
Cartographer scans for CDI were obtained at harvest in year 1 and 2 at 
the experimental ‘ANP-0131′ orchard, whereas ground truth measure­
ments of blush coverage (%) were obtained after harvest using the fruit 
grader. A relationship between CDI and blush coverage in ‘ANP-0131′ 
was established. Normal distributions of blush coverage classes (five 
histogram bins: 0–20, 20–40, 40–60, 60–80 and 80–100 %) estimated 
using the commercial grader and predicted using Cartographer were 
compared and classification errors were calculated (% absolute error). 
The sample obtained with Cartographer scans was composed of images, 
whereas the fruit population measured with the fruit grader was 
composed of individual fruit. Since in each image, the average CDI of 
detected fruit is returned, it is expected that the histograms obtained 
from Cartographer data are narrower (i.e., smaller variance) than those 
obtained on the whole fruit population. Therefore, data of predicted 
blush coverage histogram was transformed by increasing residuals to 
determine the lowest error in classifying blush coverage. Three pre­
dicted histograms were compared to the observed histogram—(i) an 
untransformed histogram derived from Cartographer data, (ii) a histo­
gram derived from doubling residuals (Eq. (1)), and (iii) a histogram 
derived from tripling residuals (Eq. (2)).

Validations of CDI predictions in the commercial ‘PremP009’ or­

appearance, the four calibration zones were classified as (A) orange–red, 
(B) red–rust, (C) green–rust, and (D) orange–rust. 
2.5. LiDAR-derived measures of canopy geometry 
Canopy geometry parameters calculated from LiDAR data included 
canopy height, canopy area, canopy density and cross-sectional leaf area 
(CSLA). Canopy area (m2) represented the area of the polygon drawn 
around the LiDAR-generated points in the scanned transect, excluding 
the trunk (green polygons in Fig. 1). Canopy density represented the 
ratio between the number of light beams generated by the LiDAR that 
bounces back to the light source and the total number of emitted light 
beams within the canopy area. CSLA was calculated as the product of 
canopy area and canopy density and is equivalent to the area of the 
points (comparable to leaves) within the canopy area polygon in the 
scanned transect, as shown in a previous work (Scalisi et al., 2021a). 
Canopy geometry parameters were measured before harvest in year 2. 
2.6. Relationships between fruit number, peel colour and canopy geometry 
Relationships between fruit number, blush coverage and canopy 
geometry features were investigated in the training systems under study 
in the ‘ANP-0131′ experimental trial before harvest in year 2. 
The relationships obtained in the ‘ANP-0131′ experiment were 
compared to those obtained in the commercial ‘ANP-0131′ orchard in 
the same season. To extract calibrated fruit number and blush coverage 
in the commercial block, a grid of 12 m (3 × row spacing) × 15 m (10 ×
tree spacing) pseudo-plots was overlaid, as per Scalisi et al. (2023a). In 
addition, two rows of pseudo-plots along the 4 edges of the orchard 
block were removed from the crop load relationship analysis as they 
were considered “buffer” plots that were subject to factors external to 
the orchard block, as typically done in experimental designs. Relation­
ships in the commercial ‘ANP-0131′ orchard were extracted from a total 
of 126 pseudo-plots.

3. Results 
3.1. Fruit number 
3.1.1. Experimental orchard 
The relationships between fruit number detections using the mobile 
platform Cartographer and observed fruit number counted using the 
commercial fruit grader were different in the five training systems under 
study at the ‘ANP-0131′ experimental orchard at the Tatura SmartFarm 
(Fig. 2). Different calibration factors (slopes of the linear regressions) 
were obtained for each training systems. In year 1 (Fig. 2a) and year 2 
(Fig. 2b), Cartographer detected the highest and lowest number of fruit 
per unit of ground-truth counts in MLVT and MLOT trees, respectively. 
When years 1 and 2 were pooled together, the same clear visual sepa­
ration between detections in MLOT and MLVT systems was measured. In 
years 1 and 2, detections in VA, SP and CL had similar relationships to 
observations obtained with the fruit grader. 
Linear regression slope comparison carried out with ANOVA tests 
confirmed visual observations—detections in MLOT and MLVT systems 
were significantly (p < 0.05) the lowest and the highest, respectively, 
and statistically differed (p < 0.05) from each other and from VA, SP and 
CL (Table 3). In year 1, detections in VA were significantly higher (p <
0.05) than SP and CL. In year 2 the same difference was not confirmed. 
In fact, when years 1 and 2 were pooled together, detections in VA trees 
were significantly lower (p < 0.05) than those in SP trees. 
Regression models revealed that, with the exception of SP trees in 
year 1, calibration had%SE below 6 % in all the training systems 
(Table 3). In year 1, detections in SP trees had%SE = 10.3 %, although 
an error above 10 % was not confirmed in year 2. Overall, when years 1 
and 2 were considered together, detections in MLVT had calibration 
errors of 2.2 %, whereas detections in the remaining systems had%SE 
ranging from 4.0 to 6.4 %. 
3.1.2. Commercial orchards 
Regression models in commercial orchards with tree canopies 
trained to Tatura trellis (Y) revealed higher Cartographer detection rates 
per unit of observed fruit number counted with click counters in 
‘PremP009’ (slope coefficient = 0. 588) than in ‘ANP-0131′ (slope co­
efficient = 0.497) (Fig. 3). 
The calibration errors for ‘PremP009’ and ‘ANP-0131′ were 4 
(Fig. 3a) and 1 % (Fig. 3b), respectively; thus, it was confirmed that even 
in commercial orchard conditions, fruit number detections can be cali­
brated with errors below 5 %. 
3.2. Peel colour 
3.2.1. Experimental ‘ANP-0131′ orchard 
The scatterplot of the relationship between CDI measurements ob­
tained using Cartographer and the blush coverage percentage obtained at 
harvest with the fruit grader revealed no visual separation between 
training systems (Fig. 4) and ANOVA comparison between linear slopes 
showed no statistical separation (p > 0.05, data not shown). Thus, a 
linear fit was interpolated through the scatterplot points of all the 
training systems (Fig. 4) and a significant positive relationship (R2 =
0.666, p < 0.001) was obtained. 
Histograms of blush coverage predicted using the equation in Fig. 4 
for years 1 and 2 pooled together showed that untransformed pre­
dictions had narrower distribution compared to observations (Fig. 5a). 
Although the overall predicted mean blush in untransformed predictions 
and observations was similar (Table 4) and the overall prediction error 
of average blush coverage in the block was low (< 1.0 %), within-class 
errors were relatively high—i.e., 40 % error in the 20–40 % blush 
coverage class, 21 % error in the 0–20 % class and 5 % error and in the 
40–60 % class, respectively (Fig. 5a). With the transformations carried 
out by doubling and tripling residuals, mean values remained approxi­
mately identical to untransformed data (Table 4). When residuals were 
tripled, the mean value slightly increased due to the rounding of values 
< 0 and > 100 % blush coverage, leading to a coincidental match

3.2.2. Commercial ‘PremP009’ orchard 
The validation of CDI predictions in the commercial ‘PremP009’ 
orchard revealed that the ground-based platform Cartographer generated 
estimates of peel redness with good accuracy and precision (rc = 0.794)

Standard deviations in predicted CDI were lower than in observed

deviations of observed values and predicted values resulted in a variance 
2.4-fold larger in the former.

3.4. Relationships between fruit number, peel colour and canopy geometry 
3.4.1. Experimental ‘ANP-0131′ orchard 
Relationships between fruit number and canopy geometry parame­
ters were established for all the training systems pooled together 
(Fig. 7), as separating them in different groups deteriorated the quality 
of the relationships. Fruit number had poorly significant positive cor­
relation with canopy height (Fig. 7a) and negative correlation with 
canopy width (Fig. 7b) and had no significant correlation with canopy 
density (Fig. 7d). Fruit number appeared to be subjected to a stronger 
negative effect of canopy area (Fig. 7c) and cross-sectional leaf area 
(Fig. 7e). 
Blush coverage appeared to be affected by canopy geometry features 
similarly to how fruit number was affected. Increasing canopy height 
and width produced significant but poor (i) improvement of blush 
coverage for the former and (ii) deterioration of blush coverage for the 
latter (Fig. 7f,g). Canopy area did not significantly affect blush coverage 
(Fig. 7h). Tree canopies with higher canopy density and cross-sectional 
leaf area had a significantly lower blush coverage (Fig. 7i,j). 
Overall, in year 2 fruit number per hectare in MLVT trees was the 
highest, although it did not statistically differ (p > 0.05) from CL and SP 
trees (Fig. 8a). MLOT and VA trees had the lowest fruit number per 
hectare. When fruit number was expressed per unit of cross-sectional 
leaf area, differences between training systems remained similar, as 
MLVT yielded the highest number of fruit per unit of cross-sectional leaf 
area, whilst MLOT and VA produced the lowest (Fig. 8c). 
Only CL had significantly higher blush coverage than MLOT while no 
other differences were found (Fig. 8b). However, when blush coverage 
was expressed per unit of cross-sectional leaf area, MLVT had the best 
performance closely followed by CL trees (Fig. 8d). MLOT trees had the 
lowest efficiency amongst training systems.

from MLVT experimental plots clearly showing more fruit number

represented reduced blush coverage and were found over MLOT plots, in

4. Discussion 
The scientific validation of technology is a prerequisite to allow 
reliable upscaling and trustworthy integration with front-end manage­
ment automation. Such a scalable approach will boost orchard digiti­
sation and build historical databases to support long-term sustainability 
of orchard businesses. 
In this study, it was demonstrated that the number of fruit detections 
per unit of ground-truth counts is lower in denser canopy configurations 
(3D) compared to narrow vertical 2D systems (Fig. 2, Table 3). This is 
likely due to fruit being increasingly occluded by foliage in visible 
portions of the canopy (Koirala et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023). Never­
theless, when considering the two years of study, predictions errors after 
applying a calibration factor remained low in all the 3D architectur­
es—range between 4.0 and 6.4 % (Table 3). In the same time range, 
errors in MLVT trees were below 2.5 %. Crop load prediction errors 
remained below 5 % in commercial orchard tests carried out in year 2 
(Fig. 3). Similarly, fruit detections in stone fruit canopies previously 
showed better machine vision performance in trees trained on trellises, 
as opposed to trees with free standing large canopies (Islam et al., 2022). 
Improved performance of machine vision on 2D canopy systems was also 
measured in apples (Bortolotti et al., 2021). 
There is a worldwide trend for orchard intensification that has seen a 
significant increase of pear high density plantings (Musacchi et al., 
2021; Sansavini and Musacchi, 2002). Narrow-wall, multileader 
training systems have shown great potential to increase productivity and 
fruit quality (Dorigoni and Micheli, 2018), as trees with large canopies 
produce fruit with highly variable maturity and quality (Zhang et al., 
2016). While increasing orchard intensification is not primarily driven 
by improved efficiency of automation, robotics and technology over­
all—rather by factors such as labour cost (Musacchi et al., 2021), spray 
volume (Sansavini et al., 2008), and packout (Dorigoni et al., 2011)— 
the improved machine vision performance for crop load estimations in 
2D systems provides indications that these systems are the most suitable 
tree architectures for accurate future orchard digitising and phenotyp­
ing, and precision horticulture applications. 
Despite different canopy geometry features in the training systems 
under study (Table 5), estimates of blush coverage derived from CDI 
were consistent in SP, CL, VA, MLOT and MLVT trees (Fig. 4). This is 
likely because measurements of CDI were carried out early in the 
morning and the strobe lights on Cartographer that continuously flashed 
canopies guaranteed a standardisation of the colour appearance and 
light environments under diverse canopy conditions. Blush coverage is a 
very valuable parameter for ‘ANP-0131′ pears in the Australian context. 
Similarly, CDI predictions in a commercial ‘PremP009’ block had good 
accuracy and precision (rc = 0.794,%SE = 1.09 %) in a scenario in which 
operators visually assessing peel colour failed to clearly identify sub­
stantial differences between zones. 
Although block- or zone-scale blush coverage averages have good 
accuracy (Figs. 4 and 6), classification of fruit into blush coverage his­
togram classes using untransformed data revealed failure to represent an 
accurate spread of data (Fig. 5a). In detail, the errors for the percentage 
of fruit with blush coverage in the 20–40 and 0–20 % classes were 40 %. 
Nonetheless, transformation of data by tripling the residuals of the raw 
data revealed a much improved performance, with within-class errors 
reduced to ≤2 % (Fig. 5c, Table 4). This was expected, as the sample 
obtained with Cartographer scans was composed of images, whereas the 
fruit population measured with the fruit grader was composed of indi­
vidual fruit.

netting, biostimulants) in the orchard to improve packout and inform 
the supply chain in a timely manner (Scalisi et al., 2022). The avail­
ability of temporal data can also support harvest logistics and reduce 
waste. It is equally important to be able to accurately predict peel colour 
histogram classes, as shown for fruit size in recent studies (Apolo-Apolo 
et al., 2020; Gongal et al., 2018; Scalisi et al., 2023b). While several 
research studies have focused on large-scale crop load and yield pre­
dictions, only few attempts have been done to estimate the spatial 
variability of peel colour in orchards (Scalisi et al., 2022). While hand­
held colourimeters or operator visual assessments remain the most 
adopted methods to assess peel colour in situ (Scalisi et al., 2021b), 
machine vision has great potential to upscale data collection on much 
larger sample sizes, although its use is still limited due to the significant 
effects of external light on pixel colour (Mirbod et al., 2021; Silwal et al., 
2021). The findings obtained in this study provide confidence on the 
reliability of ground-based platforms for large-scale estimations of peel 
colour. 
The canopy geometry parameters obtained from LiDAR images 
generated from Cartographer returned valuable indicators to character­
ise tree architectures in different pear training systems (Table 5). 
Obtaining key canopy features is essential to precisely manage or 
automate operations such as variable-rate spraying (Escol`a et al., 2013; 
Li et al., 2018; Seol et al., 2022; Sultan Mahmud et al., 2021), pruning 
and defoliation (Westling et al., 2021), and irrigation (Escol`a et al., 
2017), as point clouds may be used to derive variable crop coefficients 
that can be incorporated in crop water requirement models and preci­
sion irrigation scheduling. In addition to practical management opera­
tions, LiDAR cloud points have been used to model or estimate light 
interception (¨Orn, 2016; Scalisi et al., 2021a; Westling et al., 2018). 
However, further studies are needed to estimate within-tree light dis­
tribution at lateral shoot levels or near fruit to achieve improved fruit 
quality.

Fruit number and blush coverage were both influenced by canopy 
geometry features (Fig. 7) when data from different canopy architec­
tures were pooled together. It would be better to separate training sys­
tems to develop independent relationships between canopy features and 
crop load and quality, although datasets should be collected over several 
years to remove seasonal effects as in this study no separation was 
recommendable based on visual inference. The negative effects of can­
opy width, area and cross-sectional leaf area on fruit number (Fig. 7b,c, 
e) might have been caused by reduced light penetration that led to low 
bud initiation. In addition, low fruit number might have resulted in an 
overall increased vigour. These findings suggest that vegetative growth 
and reproductive (fruit) activity might have been unbalanced towards 
the former, and improved pruning management strategies will need to 
be implemented (Musacchi, 2021). The detrimental effects of increasing 
canopy density and cross-sectional leaf area observed on the blush 
coverage of ‘ANP-0131′ (Fig. 7i,j) were consistent with previous findings 
that showed negative effects of reduced exposure of fruit to light on 
colour formation in the same cultivar (Peavey et al., 2020), in 
‘ANP-0118′ (Peavey et al., 2020, 2022), and in ‘d’Anjou’ (Zhang et al., 
2016). High shading and poor light environment in internal positions of 
the canopy is well known to cause reduced colour formation and fruit 
quality in general. In this study, it was observed that MLVT trees had the 
highest fruit number and blush coverage per unit of cross-sectional leaf 
area, indicating that they could be exposed to an optimal light envi­
ronment. Due to their 2D configuration, as seen above, MLVT trees are 
the most suited for the application of robotics, but it is important to 
consider that, in some cases and in the first years after planting, the 
management of these canopies might require more specialised work and 
higher costs compared to traditional large canopy systems (Musacchi 
et al., 2021). 
Findings on negative effects of cross-sectional leaf area on fruit 
number and blush coverage were confirmed in a commercial orchard 
(Fig. 10). In this case the effects were more robust as the orchard was 
larger and not characterised by a pool of different architectures. In 
addition, the relationships between canopy size, fruit number and blush 
coverage in the experimental ‘ANP-0131′ trees were influenced by 
overhead netting, as opposed to unnetted commercial trees. Georefer­
enced colour- and size-coded maps (Fig. 11) provide a valuable tool to 
visualise overlaid canopy information and fruit parameters (e.g., num­
ber and colour) to easily identify zones that necessitate priority in­
terventions and to tailor precision management practices to specific 
requirements. 
Advanced phenotyping of tree architectures can be achieved by 
integrating LiDAR and machine vision (Huang et al., 2020; Rosell and 
Sanz, 2012; Underwood et al., 2016). The integration of LiDAR and 
machine vision has important implications for breeding programs. Their 
combination supports the characterisation of selections that are best 
suited for 2D planting and for phenotyping key tree architectural and 
reproductive traits for pear production systems, such as branch angle, 
fruit-leaf ratio, fruiting behaviour, fruit clustering, as summarised in 
detail in available literature (Costes et al., 2004, 2010; Lauri et al., 2002; 
Musacchi, 2021). 
5. Conclusions 
The platform used in this study had consistent and reliable perfor­
mance, placing it in an optimal position for scientific data acquisition in 
research experiments, while maintaining its prerogative of being a 
commercially valuable tool for growers to measure and map spatial in­
formation and digitise fruit and tree metrics. Digitising orchards and 
crop characteristics is a required step towards automation and data- 
driven precision farming. Building accurate historical datasets of or­
chard blocks and farms will allow a better understanding of the business 
by enabling financial analysis and sustainability studies over time based 
on objective data. The implications of digitised orchards for the auto­
mation of orchard practices, from planting to managing and harvesting, 
are vast and require vertical integration of follow-up technologies that 
can take data-driven decisions to optimise production. Data predicting 
packout, based on fruit number, size and colour, is highly sought by the 
supply chain and could be leveraged by growers and packhouses to
