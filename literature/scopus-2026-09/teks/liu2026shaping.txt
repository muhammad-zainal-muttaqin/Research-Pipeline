A B S T R A C T
Accurate yield forecasting enables growers and the citrus industry to optimize resource allocation, mitigate 
economic risks, and enhance supply chain efficiency. However, existing studies remain fragmented, with sub­
stantial variation in input features, data acquisition platforms, preprocessing strategies, and predictive algo­
rithms, limiting comparability and the development of common best practices. This review systematically 
synthesizes the state of the art in citrus yield prediction from four perspectives: (i) input features influencing 
yield; (ii) data acquisition platforms and feature extraction methods; (iii) prediction models and analytical ap­
proaches; and (iv) complementary yield-related methods, including estimation, monitoring, and measurement. 
Comparative analysis of published studies highlights consistent trends, such as the importance of fruit counts and 
canopy spectral indices for short-term predictions, the critical but underexplored role of mid-term forecasting in 
bridging imaging and climate-based data regimes, the reliance on climatic and soil variables in long-term 
forecasting, and the increasing use of machine learning to capture complex, nonlinear interactions. Despite 
notable progress, critical challenges remain, including limited availability of long-term and large-scale datasets, 
occlusion and variability in image-based methods, and poor model transferability across orchards, varieties, and 
growing regions. To address these barriers, future research should prioritize standardized and harmonized 
feature sets, robust multi-source data integration, interpretable and hybrid modeling frameworks that incorpo­
rate biological knowledge, and scalable pipelines capable of supporting operational decision-making. By 
consolidating fragmented knowledge, this review provides both the current state of citrus yield prediction and a 
roadmap for advancing toward reliable, transferable, and sustainable forecasting systems.
1. Introduction
Citrus is one of the most economically important fruit crops world­
wide, and accurate yield prediction has received growing attention due 
to its direct relevance to orchard management, harvesting logistics, and 
market planning (Trentin et al., 2024). Reliable yield prediction can 
help optimize resource allocation, guide fertilization and irrigation 
strategies, and reduce uncertainties in the supply chain. Traditionally, 
citrus yield has been estimated using manual and experience-based 
methods such as fruit sampling, canopy volume measurement, or 
grower intuition.
In Florida and other major citrus-producing regions, for example, a 
common practice involves selecting a small set of representative trees 
across the field, typically categorized as small, medium, and large trees 
(Vijayakumar et al., 2023). Growers (or consulting companies) harvest 
all fruit from these sampled trees, at the green or immature stage, weigh 
them, and extrapolate the results to the entire orchard using historical 
inventory data on tree size distribution. Although this approach is 
simple and has been widely adopted, it suffers from several drawbacks. 
First, the need to harvest immature fruit leads to unnecessary crop loss, 
which reduces potential profits. This is particularly critical in Florida, 
where citrus production has already been severely impacted by Huan­
glongbing (HLB, or citrus greening disease) for more than a decade, 
making every unit of yield valuable (Tang et al., 2020). Second, the 
inherent variability among trees can result in significant sampling errors 
when scaling up from a small set of trees to the entire orchard. Finally, 
these traditional methods cannot provide timely or spatially detailed 
insights into yield distribution within the field, limiting their usefulness 
for precision orchard management. These limitations highlight the need 
for developing automated, data-driven approaches capable of predicting

Unlike annual row crops such as wheat, corn, or soybeans, citrus and 
other perennial tree crops have long lifespans whose growth, fruiting, 
and yield potential are shaped by cumulative multi-year effects of 
environment, management, and stress. Individual trees differ widely in 
canopy size, fruit distribution, and health status, requiring yield pre­
diction to account for multi-scale characteristics from individual trees to 
entire blocks. Among perennial tree crops, apple yield prediction has 
received substantial research attention, benefiting from relatively uni­
form canopy architecture and well-established thinning practices 
(Cheng et al., 2017). However, transfer to citrus is limited by citrus's 
greater susceptibility to biotic stress and more irregular canopy geom­
etry. Mango shares citrus's sensitivity to disease pressure and irregular 
flowering but differs in its more pronounced alternate bearing and lower 
amenability to high-density sensing systems (Torgbor et al., 2023). Olive 
yield prediction is dominated by climate-driven models leveraging 
multi-year temperature records (Ramos et al., 2025), which transfer 
partially to long-term citrus forecasting but are insufficient for short- 
and mid-term predictions where fruit-level sensing is required. Across 
these crops, transferable insights include the value of multi-year training 
datasets, tree-level rather than field-level modeling, and UAV-based 
canopy metrics as yield proxies.
Citrus-specific challenges further distinguish it from other tree crops 
and necessitate specialized prediction methodologies. Biennial bearing 
patterns (Nawaz et al., 2020) and cumulative multi-year effects on fruit 
set introduce temporal dependencies that single-season models cannot 
capture. More critically, citrus yield is highly sensitive to biotic stresses: 
HLB, citrus canker, and citrus leaf miner have devastating impacts on 
fruit set and productivity (Abdulridha et al., 2019), while fruit drop 
induced by HLB or extreme weather events can remove a substantial 
portion of yield within weeks of harvest. Refined data acquisition is 
therefore required, involving RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, ther­
mal, and LiDAR sensing at both ground and aerial scales, with canopy 
volume, fruit counts, and nutritional status serving as essential model 
inputs. While advances in computer vision have enabled early stress 
detection (da Cunha et al., 2024; Hariharan et al., 2023), their inte­
gration into comprehensive yield prediction frameworks remains 
limited.
Several reviews have advanced crop yield prediction, yet none pro­
vides a citrus-specific synthesis. As summarized in Table 1, existing re­
views either cover multiple crops without a citrus focus (Van 
Klompenburg et al., 2020; Muruganantham et al., 2022; Jabed and 
Murad, 2024) or treat multiple tree crops without isolating citrus as a 
distinct case (He et al., 2022; Trentin et al., 2024); none address pre­
diction lead time, biotic stress, or citrus-specific biological features. This 
gap is increasingly consequential: over the past five years, advances in 
UAV platforms, multimodal sensing, and AI-based modeling have 
brought citrus yield prediction to a stage where systematic synthesis and 
standardized frameworks are not only possible but necessary to prevent 
continued fragmentation of the field. To address this gap, this review 
systematically organizes and analyzes the citrus yield prediction litera­
ture, structured around four categories of study: (i) input features 
related to yield, (ii) data acquisition and feature extraction methods, (iii) 
yield prediction models, and (iv) other yield-related methods such as 
monitoring, estimation, and measurement. By offering, to the best of our 
knowledge, one of the first systematic, citrus-specific syntheses, this 
review fills a critical gap in the literature and establishes a foundation 
for developing more accurate, scalable, and resilient yield prediction 
frameworks to meet the pressing needs of the citrus industry.
Before proceeding, it is useful to distinguish four related, but distinct 
concepts used throughout this review. Yield prediction refers to fore­
casting future yield at an early or mid-growth stage using statistical or 
machine learning models. Yield estimation refers to assessing expected 
yield closer to harvest based on current observable data such as fruit 
counts or canopy indices. Yield monitoring involves continuous obser­
vation of crop development over time, while yield measurement pro­
vides the actual harvested yield as ground truth. This review focuses 
primarily on yield prediction, while estimation, monitoring, and mea­
surement are discussed as complementary methods in Section 3.
2. Methodology of literature selection
A systematic search was conducted in the Scopus database, restricted 
to publications from 2005 to 2026 within the subject areas of Agricul­
ture, Engineering, and Computer Science, using keywords combining 
citrus crop types, yield-related terms, and sensing or machine learning 
techniques. The initial search yielded 1,007 articles, of which 273 were 
selected as highly relevant. Studies were emphasized in the synthesis 
based on three criteria: dataset scale (number of trees, seasons, or or­
chards covered), validation rigor (use of cross-validation, independent 
test sets, or multi-year data), and reproducibility (availability of open 
datasets or sufficient methodological detail for replication). The quali­
tative synthesis was conducted by organizing studies according to six 
dimensions: prediction lead time (short-, mid-, and long-term), sensor 
platform and modality, model type and paradigm, crop variety and 
growing region, spatial scale of prediction (tree-, block-, or orchard- 
level), and evaluation metrics reported. No meta-analysis was per­
formed, as the substantial heterogeneity in research objectives, data 
scales, sensor configurations, and evaluation metrics across studies 
makes quantitative pooling inappropriate and potentially misleading. 
The geographical and temporal distributions of the reviewed studies are 
shown in Fig. 1a and b. Selected studies were categorized into four 
groups (Fig. 1c): (1) input features related to yield, (2) data pre­
processing and feature extraction, (3) yield prediction methods, and (4) 
other yield-related approaches including estimation, monitoring, and 
measurement. The complete selection process is summarized in Fig. 2
(PRISMA 2020 flow diagram).

4. Input features for citrus yield prediction
Accurate yield prediction depends critically on the selection of input 
features that are both measurable at scale and meaningfully correlated 
with yield outcomes. Although many studies have explored the influ­
ence of biological characteristics and environmental factors on citrus 
yield, many of these findings have not been incorporated into existing 
prediction frameworks. This disconnect arises in part because plant and 
agricultural scientists and engineers often work within separate do­
mains, with limited exchange and integration of knowledge. Bridging 
this gap is critical, as the integration of biological and agronomic in­
sights into engineering-based approaches can lead to more accurate and 
generalizable yield prediction models. A synthesis of 144 studies on 
biological features associated with citrus yield indicates that these fea­
tures can be classified into six categories: rootstocks, tree structure 
traits, physiological processes, nutritional characteristics, agronomic 
practices, and biological stresses. Representative examples are summa­
rized in Table 3.
Although rootstock is rarely used as a direct model input, it 
substantially influences tree growth patterns and yield potential (Yildiz 
et al., 2013; Ampatzidis et al., 2019). Citrus trees grafted onto different 
rootstocks should therefore be treated as separate subgroups in yield 
modeling, with rootstock-specific canopy traits emphasized as key pre­
dictors. Tree structural traits, including canopy volume, height, and 
crown density, are among the most informative inputs for yield pre­
diction (Levy et al., 2023). Automated quantification via UAV imagery 
and LiDAR scanning has made these parameters increasingly accessible 
at orchard scale (Trentin and Ampatzidis, 2024; Zhou et al., 2025), and 
their extraction methods are discussed further in Section 5. Physiolog­
ical processes such as photosynthesis and water transport directly in­
fluence fruit set and yield but are rarely measured directly in field-scale 
studies. Instead, proxy indicators such as NDVI, canopy temperature 
from thermal imaging, and leaf gas exchange indices are used as model 
inputs, enabling physiological information to be incorporated into yield 
prediction frameworks at orchard scale (Poudyal et al., 2023; Teshome 
et al., 2023).
Nutritional indicators, particularly leaf nitrogen content and chlo­
rophyll indices derived from SPAD meters or hyperspectral imaging, 
serve as early, non-destructive predictors of yield trends. Remote 
sensing and machine learning now enable large-scale, automated esti­
mation of these variables (Costa et al., 2022). The same shift applies to

but how to fuse biological, structural, and stress features into a single

5. Data acquisition and feature extraction
Translating the input features described in Section 4 into usable 
model inputs requires reliable data acquisition and feature extraction 
pipelines. While manual measurements remain a baseline, they are 
labor-intensive, slow, and susceptible to observer bias, which are the 
limitations that have driven the adoption of imaging and sensing tech­
nologies including RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, thermal infrared, 
and LiDAR, combined with computer vision and 3D reconstruction 
methods. Across the reviewed studies, feature extraction can be orga­
nized into three levels: fruit-level quantification, which provides direct 
count-based inputs; tree-level structural phenotyping, which captures 
canopy geometry and architecture; and nutrition and health modeling, 
which monitors physiological and disease status. Together, these three 
categories supply the core input features required across different pre­
diction horizons and are summarized in Table 4 and Table 5. Fig. 3
presents a comparison of data collection platforms and their respective 
strengths and limitations.
5.1. Fruit-level quantification
Fruit count is the most direct and widely used input for short-term 
yield prediction. Ground-based platforms provide higher resolution 
and stability for fine-scale detection, while UAVs enable rapid orchard- 
wide coverage but struggle with fine-scale fruit-level tasks due to flight 
constraints and environmental sensitivity. Among sensors, RGB cameras 
dominate due to accessibility, but hyperspectral (Ding et al., 2018), 
thermal (Gan et al., 2018), and depth cameras (Liu and Ampatzidis, 
2026) improve robustness under occlusion and variable illumination, 
conditions that directly inflate yield estimation error if unaddressed. 
Algorithmically, deep learning detectors, such as YOLO (Redmon et al., 
2016) and the R-CNN series, have largely replaced traditional image 
processing pipelines (Azizi et al., 2024), which degrade significantly on 
green fruits or under variable lighting. Small-fruit detection and occlu­
sion remain major challenges for both detection and tracking (Chen and 
Lee, 2014), yet occlusion has received comparatively less attention 
relative to its impact on yield estimation accuracy. Most studies optimize 
for detection mAP rather than orchard-level counting accuracy, and this 
disconnect means reported detection performance rarely translates 
directly into yield prediction error. More critically, the tracking stage, 
where existing tracking algorithms, such as Simple Online and Realtime 
Tracking (SORT) (Bewley et al., 2016) and DeepSORT (Wojke et al., 
2017), often suffer from ID switches, double counting, and unstable 
performance under windy conditions or high fruit density, remains

5.2. Tree-level structural phenotyping
Tree-level structural traits, particularly canopy volume, tree height, 
and crown geometry, are among the most reliable proxies for yield po­
tential at the orchard scale, and their accurate extraction is therefore a 
critical prerequisite for scalable yield prediction. UAV and satellite 
imagery enable orchard-wide monitoring, while LiDAR and ultrasonic 
systems provide high-accuracy 3D reconstructions that closely match 
manual canopy volume measurements (Liu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 
2026b). Deep learning detectors and point cloud processing algorithms 
such as dynamic slicing (Li et al., 2024) and clustering (Liu et al., 2021) 
now recover tree count and crown geometry reliably from these plat­
forms (Trentin and Ampatzidis, 2024).
However, several structural limitations directly constrain yield pre­
diction accuracy. Overlapping canopies and irregular tree spacing 
introduce systematic errors in crown delineation that propagate into 
canopy volume estimates and downstream yield models. LiDAR offers

5.3. Nutrition and health modeling
Nutritional status and disease severity are increasingly recognized as 
important inputs for yield prediction, particularly in HLB-affected re­
gions where disease progression directly drives fruit drop and yield loss. 
UAV multispectral (Costa et al., 2022) and hyperspectral imaging 
(Abdulridha et al., 2019) enable non-destructive estimation of leaf ni­
trogen content and early detection of citrus greening (HLB), black spot, 
and canker (Momeny et al., 2022), providing disease severity indicators 
that can be incorporated as upstream inputs into yield prediction 
frameworks. Satellite-based approaches extend this capability to 
regional scales (Avioz et al., 2025), though at the cost of spatial 
resolution.
However, the integration of nutritional and disease indicators into 
yield prediction models remains limited in practice. Ground-truth 
collection for nutrient content is destructive and labor-intensive, and 
disease datasets are typically crop- or region-specific, severely limiting 
model transferability across orchards and environments. UAV hyper­
spectral imaging, while highly informative, requires complex calibration 
and is costly to deploy at scale. Critically, most studies treat disease 
detection and nutrition estimation as standalone tasks rather than as 
inputs to yield prediction pipelines, leaving their actual contribution to 
forecast accuracy largely unquantified.
5.4. Annotation cost and ground-truth limitations
Across all three feature extraction categories, the cost and scalability 
of ground-truth data collection represent a fundamental bottleneck that 
is rarely discussed explicitly but consistently constrains model devel­
opment. Fruit-level annotation, which is manually counting, localizing, 
and labeling fruits across diverse canopy conditions, is particularly 
labor-intensive, and the cost scales poorly with orchard size, fruit den­
sity, and occlusion severity. Canopy volume and structural trait mea­
surement requires either destructive sampling or time-consuming 
LiDAR-based validation, limiting the number of trees and seasons that 
can be reliably labeled. Disease severity and nutrient content assessment 
typically requires laboratory analysis of leaf or fruit samples, making 
large-scale labeled datasets for nutrition and health modeling prohibi­
tively expensive to assemble. Emerging solutions include weakly 
supervised learning, which trains models on coarse or incomplete labels; 
semi-automated annotation pipelines that use model-assisted pre-la­
beling followed by human verification; and synthetic data generation 
via GANs, which can augment real datasets under rare or difficult con­
ditions (Lu et al., 2022). However, these approaches remain underutil­
ized in citrus yield prediction relative to their adoption in other 
agricultural computer vision domains, and their impact on downstream 
yield estimation accuracy has rarely been quantified. Representative 
publicly available datasets for citrus fruit detection and counting are 
summarized in Table 6, compared by sensing modality, annotation, 
scale, geographic and temporal coverage, and accessibility. As shown, 
they remain concentrated in a few regions (Brazil, China, Florida), are 
largely single-season, and are dispersed across several unconnected re­
positories, underscoring the absence of a shared, multi-region bench­
mark. Addressing annotation bottlenecks systematically, rather than 
treating labeled data as a fixed constraint, is essential for building pre­
diction systems that can scale beyond single-orchard research settings.
6. Yield prediction methods
Building on the input features and extraction methods reviewed in 
Sections 4 and 5, this section examines how different modeling ap­
proaches leverage these inputs to generate yield predictions. Studies are 
organized by prediction horizon: short-, mid-, and long-term, reflecting 
fundamental differences in data requirements, model architecture, and 
operational utility. The choice of modeling approach and validation 
strategy directly determines whether a prediction system can generalize 
beyond its training conditions to support real-world orchard manage­
ment decisions.

However, most studies rely on only one to three discrete data collection 
dates before harvest, producing essentially static pre-harvest predictions 
that fail to fully capture crop growth dynamics. Mid-term prediction, 
typically two to twelve months before harvest, remains the least 
explored horizon. At this stage, fruit is not yet visually prominent, 
limiting RGB-based detection approaches; yet the temporal distance 
from harvest makes climate- and management-based long-term inputs 
insufficiently predictive. Mid-term models must therefore bridge two 
fundamentally different data regimes, requiring both imaging infra­
structure and multi-year management records. This is unfortunate, as 
mid-term forecasts are arguably the most operationally valuable: 
informing irrigation, fertilization, and labor allocation decisions months 
before harvest. In contrast, long-term prediction incorporates not only 
structural or spectral features but also environmental and management 
factors, including climatic variables, such as average temperature, 
rainfall, and relative humidity (Almady et al., 2024), soil composition 
(Mann et al., 2008), and orchard practices, including pruning, fertil­
ization, and pest control (Moussaid et al., 2022). These models are often 
based on historical yield records spanning five to ten years, combined 
with environmental data from local weather stations or in-orchard 
sensor networks. This broader dataset reflects the need to account for 
multi-year growth cycles and management effects in long-term yield 
prediction.
Recently, there has been a growing interest in integrating short-term 
and long-term datasets, combining high-resolution imagery with his­
torical climate and management records to improve both prediction 
accuracy and generalizability. Despite this, systematic time-series 
modeling approaches that leverage continuous or dense temporal data 
remain rare. A deeper issue is that dataset design across all three hori­
zons reflects a fundamental mismatch with operational needs. Short- 
term studies prioritize detection accuracy over counting reliability, 
mid-term studies rarely validate across more than one season, and long- 
term studies depend on historical records that are seldom publicly 
available or standardized across regions. This fragmentation means that 
even well-performing models within their training domain offer limited 
guidance on what data collection strategies matter most for improving 
yield forecast reliability in practice.
Beyond horizon-specific constraints, a cross-cutting characterization 
of datasets reveals structural patterns that affect all three prediction 
windows. In terms of scale, most studies collect data from a single or­
chard or a small number of trees within one season, with few studies 
reporting datasets exceeding 100 trees or spanning more than two 
consecutive years. Sensing modalities are dominated by RGB cameras 
and multispectral sensors, with hyperspectral and LiDAR datasets 
remaining rare due to equipment cost and processing complexity. 
Geographically, the reviewed literature is heavily concentrated in a 
small number of citrus-producing regions, with limited representation of 
tropical and subtropical growing environments outside established 
research hubs. Labeling strategies vary widely: some studies rely on 
manual fruit counts as ground truth, others use harvested weight, and a 
smaller subset employs canopy volume or spectral indices as yield 
proxies, making cross-study comparison inherently difficult. Critically, 
most datasets are proprietary and unpublished. This fragmentation 
means that reported model performance cannot be meaningfully 
compared across studies, and that new research groups face a prohibi­
tive data collection burden before any modeling can begin.
6.2. Prediction algorithms and performance assessment
The reviewed studies demonstrate a broad spectrum of modeling 
approaches for citrus yield prediction, ranging from classical statistical 
methods to advanced hybrid and AI-based frameworks, as summarized 
in Table 8. Statistical and expert-rule-based models have long domi­
nated the field, offering high interpretability with modest data re­
quirements, but their capacity to capture complex non-linear 
relationships is limited (Ye et al., 2008b; Ye et al., 2010; Wang et al., 
2022; Akbar et al., 2009). With the increasing availability of high- 
dimensional sensor data, machine learning and deep learning models 
have become widely adopted, enabling flexible modeling of complex 
feature-yield relationships at the cost of reduced interpretability and 
higher data demands. Hybrid frameworks illustrate a growing trend 
toward combining interpretability with predictive accuracy, while in­
tegrated platforms such as Agroview (Ampatzidis et al., 2024; Ampat­
zidis et al., 2020; Trentin and Ampatzidis, 2024) demonstrate the 
potential of merging UAV imagery, ground-based fruit detection, and 
machine learning regression into cloud-based pipelines for operational 
yield monitoring.
Beyond single-modality approaches, a growing subset of studies 
explicitly adopts multimodal data fusion to improve prediction robust­
ness. Three broad strategies can be identified. Early fusion combines 
inputs from multiple modalities before model training, allowing the 
model to learn cross-modal interactions directly. For example, Swanson 
et al. (2010) fused visual, thermal, and ranging sensor data at the feature 
level for citrus yield prediction, demonstrating that complementary 
modalities can compensate for individual sensor limitations. Late fusion 
trains separate models on each modality and combines outputs at the 
prediction stage, which is more robust to missing modalities but sacri­
fices inter-modal feature learning. Hybrid fusion integrates both stra­
tegies using modality-specific encoders followed by a joint prediction 
head, as demonstrated by Albaaji et al. (2025), whose multi-modal 
neural framework combined visual and structural features for simulta­
neous fruit grading and yield estimation. Across these strategies, spec­
tral and structural modalities consistently contribute most to short- and 
mid-term prediction accuracy, while climate and management variables 
dominate long-term models. Systematic comparisons of fusion strategies 
under controlled conditions remain rare, however, and the trade-offs 
between fusion complexity, data requirements, and cross-orchard

The performance of these approaches has been evaluated using

comparison of performance across these task types is therefore inap­
propriate, as the metrics reflect fundamentally different objectives. 
Validation strategies include k-fold cross-validation, leave-one-year-out 
testing, Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis, and independent dataset 
validation. Across the reviewed studies, a clear performance hierarchy 
emerges. Deep learning and hybrid frameworks consistently outperform 
classical statistical models on high-dimensional sensor data, while 
traditional regression models retain competitive accuracy when input 
features are limited to a small number of well-calibrated variables such 
as canopy volume or NDVI. A more fundamental concern is metric se­
lection: R2 and MAPE are reported inconsistently across prediction ho­
rizons and output scales, and few studies report confidence intervals or 
uncertainty estimates alongside point predictions. This matters for 
operational use: for example, a model reporting R2 = 0.90 on a single- 
season single-orchard dataset tells growers very little about expected 
forecast reliability in their own conditions. Standardized reporting 
protocols that distinguish within-domain from cross-domain perfor­
mance would help, but the deeper challenge is model transferability 
across orchards, cultivars, and regions. Most reviewed studies train and 
test within the same orchard or growing season, and the few that 
attempt cross-orchard or cross-region evaluation consistently report 
substantial performance degradation, suggesting that models are 
capturing site-specific patterns rather than generalizable yield 
dynamics.
Transfer learning and domain adaptation offer the most promising 
technical pathway to address this: pre-training on larger, more diverse 
datasets and fine-tuning on target-domain data has shown success in 
related agricultural computer vision tasks, though direct evidence in 
citrus yield prediction remains limited. Input normalization strategies, 
such as standardizing vegetation indices across sensor types or cali­
brating canopy volume measurements across platforms, represent a 
more immediately actionable approach, as they reduce the domain gap 
without requiring architectural changes. Establishing a shared bench­
mark dataset spanning multiple citrus-growing regions, cultivars, and 
seasons, together with standardized train–validation–test splits, cross-

6.3. Functional taxonomy of citrus yield prediction systems
The paradigms compared in Table 8 are organized by algorithmic 
class. A complementary, higher-level lens instead organizes prediction 
approaches by what fundamentally drives the prediction, yielding five 
functional archetypes with distinct assumptions, inputs, temporal scales, 
and deployment implications.
Biological-driven models, including crop growth, phenology, and 
degree-day formulations (Almady et al., 2024), assume that yield 
emerges from physiological processes, responding to climate and man­
agement inputs over seasonal to multi-year scales. They are causally 
interpretable and extrapolate to novel conditions, but require extensive 
parameter calibration, operate at coarse spatial resolution, and are weak 
at the individual-tree level, giving them moderate deployment 
readiness.
Sensor-driven models (RGB fruit counting, UAV and LiDAR struc­
tural phenotyping, spectral indices) treat yield as directly observable 
from current-season proxies such as fruit count, canopy volume, and 
NDVI, using imagery as the primary input over a short, within-season 
horizon (Trentin and Ampatzidis, 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). They pro­
vide high spatial detail at the tree level but capture only a single-season 
snapshot, remain blind to multi-year dynamics, and are constrained by 
annotation cost and occlusion.
Data-driven AI models, such as statistical, machine-learning, and 
deep-learning mappings (Redmon et al., 2016; Ren et al., 2016), assume 
yield is a learnable function of observed features within the training 
distribution, requiring large, labeled datasets over short- to mid-term 
horizons. They model complex non-linear relationships with high in- 
domain accuracy but are data-hungry, weakly interpretable, transfer 
poorly across orchards, and disregard biological causality.
Multimodal fusion models, such as early, late, and hybrid fusion 
(Swanson et al., 2010; Albaaji et al., 2025), assume that complementary 
modalities jointly reduce uncertainty beyond any single source, 
requiring synchronized multi-sensor and agronomic data over short- to 
mid-term horizons. Redundancy improves robustness and captures more 
yield drivers, but acquisition, annotation, and computational costs are 
high, cross-modal alignment is complex, and few validated pipelines 
exist.
Hybrid digital-twin-inspired systems including mechanistic–mac 
hine-learning couplings, orchard digital twins, and emerging agentic 
frameworks (Escrib`a-Gelonch et al., 2024) posit a continuously updated 
virtual representation coupling biological process models with sensed 
data and learning, integrating real-time sensing, biological models, and 
management history across multiple temporal scales. This archetype 
unifies causal and data-driven modeling and supports continuous fore­
casting and decision support, but remains largely nascent, imposes a 
high integration burden, and depends on the multi-year benchmark 
datasets the field currently lacks.

7. Technology readiness and deployment analysis
While the preceding sections assessed sensing and modeling ap­
proaches primarily by predictive accuracy, commercial deployment 
depends on a broader set of practical constraints. To make this explicit, 
we evaluate the major sensing and modeling paradigms using a Tech­
nology Readiness Level (TRL) framework (Mankins, 1995), assessing 
each along five deployment-relevant dimensions: acquisition cost, 
annotation burden, scalability, robustness and transferability, and 
grower adoption potential (Table 9). This analysis distinguishes tech­
nologies that are scientifically promising from those that are presently 
operationally viable, which is a distinction rarely made in accuracy- 
focused reviews but essential for growers and industry stakeholders 
(Table 9).
Hyperspectral sensing, multimodal fusion, and hybrid mechani­
stic–AI systems are rich in information but have low current readiness, 
constrained by acquisition cost, computational demand, and limited 
grower accessibility. Conversely, RGB imaging and climate-based fore­
casting, though individually coarser, offer the highest near-term 
deployability owing to low cost and high scalability. This inverse rela­
tionship between information richness and operational readiness sug­
gests that the most viable near-term path is not the most sophisticated 
sensor, but rather low-cost, scalable acquisition, for example, RGB and 
climate data collected continuously through IoT-enabled ground-based 
and tractor-mounted platforms (Liu et al., 2026c), coupled with 
modeling advances that extract more value from these accessible 
streams. The more information-rich modalities, meanwhile, remain 
important research frontiers whose deployment barriers must fall before 
commercial adoption becomes realistic.
8. Discussion
Despite notable progress in citrus yield prediction, several critical 
gaps remain. Beyond the methodological limitations examined in 
Section 6, a second and more fundamental challenge is the mismatch 
between the assumptions embedded in data-driven models and the 
biological reality of citrus production. Most AI-based yield prediction 
models implicitly assume that yield is a stationary function of observ­
able inputs within a single season, an assumption that conflicts with 
several well-documented biological phenomena. Biennial bearing, in 
which alternate-year fluctuations in fruit load are driven by carbohy­
drate competition between vegetative growth and fruit development, 
introduces multi-year dependencies that single-season models cannot 
capture. Disease progression, particularly HLB, is non-linear and cu­
mulative: early infection may not manifest in detectable spectral or 
structural signals until yield losses are already irreversible, meaning that 
models trained on visible symptoms systematically underestimate the 
impact of latent infection. Fruit drop dynamics represent an abrupt state 
change that static pre-harvest models are structurally incapable of 
anticipating.
Beyond these, several additional processes also govern citrus yield, 
such as carbohydrate partitioning among roots, shoots, and fruit, root­
stock–scion interactions (Wang et al., 2025), flowering intensity and 
timing (Agustí et al., 2022), and transient responses to water, heat, and 
nutrient stress. These processes are only partially observable, or not 
observable at all, through the sensing modalities reviewed here. The 
recurring pattern is that current models are optimized against observ­
able proxies (canopy vigor, spectral indices, fruit counts) while the un­
derlying causal drivers remain partly or wholly unmeasured. To make 
this divergence explicit, Table 10 maps each major biological process 
against whether current sensing can observe it directly, indirectly, or not 
at all, and the resulting implications for prediction accuracy and un­
certainty. Across the table, the processes that can be observed directly 
are those with an external physical manifestation (flowers, fruit, visible 
lesions), whereas those flagged as indirect or unobservable are internal 
physiological states (carbohydrate partitioning), belowground or geno­
typic factors (rootstock–scion), or dynamics that emerge only across 
time (biennial bearing, fruit drop, latent disease). Critically, the least 
observable processes, such as carbohydrate partitioning, root­
stock–scion interactions, and latent disease, are precisely those that 
introduce the largest and least reducible prediction uncertainty. It fol­
lows that further gains in statistical fit alone are unlikely to resolve these 
errors; instead, they motivate hybrid approaches in which mechanistic 
crop-physiological models represent the unobservable drivers structur­
ally, while machine learning handles the observable, data-rich compo­
nents. Finally, these methodological, biological, and data-related 
challenges are consolidated in Table 11, which synthesizes the key 
research gaps identified across this review.
9. Future research directions
9.1. Expanding the input feature space
Expanding the input feature space beyond the current reliance on 
fruit counts, canopy size, and vegetation indices remains a priority. 
Incorporating physiological indicators, soil-plant-atmosphere in­
teractions, and quantified management variables will be essential for 
building more generalizable models. Multi-modal data fusion combining 
spectral, structural, climatic, and biochemical features offers significant 
potential, but requires careful feature engineering to avoid redundancy 
and overfitting (Javidan et al., 2024). Identifying which combinations of 
spectral, structural, and biochemical inputs provide the greatest 
robustness under HLB pressure remains an open and practically 
important research question.
9.2. Scalable and low-cost data acquisition
Scalable and affordable data collection must be prioritized. UAV and 
hyperspectral imaging remain costly at large scales; future systems

9.3. Explainability, uncertainty, and biological integration
Methodological advances should also focus on model trustworthi­
ness. The prediction models reviewed above generate the yield estimate 
itself, whereas explainability and uncertainty quantification (UQ) act on 
those predictions to clarify why a value was produced and how confi­
dent it is. Both are well established in crop-yield prediction more 
broadly but rare in citrus. In general crop-yield modeling, post-hoc 
explainability methods such as SHAP and LIME attribute predictions 
to interpretable drivers (Joshi et al., 2025), and UQ techniques such as 
Bayesian neural networks, Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles, 
quantile regression, and conformal prediction provide calibrated confi­
dence alongside point forecasts (Mahmood et al., 2026). In citrus, 
interpretability is so far treated only as a qualitative model property 
(Table 8), and confidence intervals are seldom reported (Section 6.2). 
Adapting these techniques would let citrus models expose which can­
opy, spectral, or biological features drive a forecast and report calibrated 
uncertainty, capabilities that matter when predictions inform irrigation, 
harvesting, or logistics decisions. Future work should also prioritize

Super-resolution approaches based on GANs, transformers, and 
diffusion models also hold considerable promise for enhancing image 
quality, reducing occlusion effects, and bridging the resolution gap be­
tween satellite, UAV, and ground-based sensing platforms (Lacerda 
et al., 2025), as illustrated in Fig. 6. However, generative enhance­
ment carries inherent risks: GANs and diffusion models may introduce 
false textures, alter fruit boundaries, or hallucinate occluded regions in 
ways that inflate detection confidence without improving counting ac­
curacy. Future work should therefore prioritize explicit validation of 
enhanced image authenticity and quantify the actual contribution of 
super-resolution to downstream yield prediction error reduction, rather 
than treating image quality improvement as a proxy for prediction 
performance. Transfer learning and multi-task frameworks capable of 
simultaneously estimating yield, detecting disease, and monitoring 
nutritional status represent a particularly promising direction, and 
identifying which architectures generalize best across heterogeneous 
citrus environments would substantially accelerate their practical 
deployment.
9.4. Operational decision-support systems
Research must transition from isolated experimental studies toward 
operational decision-support systems (Ampatzidis et al., 2025). In this 
view, yield prediction is not an isolated modeling task but one compo­
nent of an integrated orchard decision-support ecosystem, as presented 
in Fig. 4. Integrating yield prediction with pest and disease manage­
ment, precision irrigation and fertilization scheduling, fruit thinning, 
harvest logistics and labor allocation, and market planning, and linking 
these functions with economic and sustainability metrics, will be 
essential to ensure that technological advances translate into real-world 
impact for growers and the broader citrus industry. These directions can 
be organized as a research roadmap of short-, mid-, and long-term 
research priorities spanning roughly one to ten years, as summarized 
in Fig. 7. Defining the accuracy and reliability thresholds that yield 
prediction systems must meet before growers adopt them at scale, and 
linking these thresholds to measurable economic outcomes, would help 
bridge the gap between research performance and operational impact.
9.5. Foundation models and agentic forecasting
Foundation models are large architectures pre-trained on broad data 
and subsequently adapted to downstream tasks, offering a fundamen­
tally different starting point from the supervised, per-orchard mapping 
that characterizes the studies reviewed above. Foundation vision 
models, typically built on Vision Transformer backbones and trained 
through self-supervised pretraining, could enable automated feature 
discovery directly from heterogeneous, partially labeled citrus imagery, 
reducing both the annotation burden discussed in Section 5.4 and the 
site-specific overfitting that currently limits cross-orchard transfer in 
Section 6.2 (da Silva et al., 2025). Multimodal large language models 
(MLLMs), which jointly process imagery and text, could further inte­
grate UAV and ground-based imagery with the unstructured agronomic 
records, such as management logs, scouting notes, and climate sum­
maries, directly addressing the multimodal fusion gap identified in 
Table 11 (Zhu et al., 2025).
Beyond representation learning, agentic architectures introduce 
explicit reasoning over records. Such systems could reconcile irrigation, 
fertilization, and HLB-treatment histories with sensed canopy state to 
contextualize an anomalous prediction; perform autonomous data- 
quality assessment by flagging miscalibrated indices, sensor drift, or 
mislabeled frames that presently require manual curation; and provide 
explainable decision support that returns not a bare figure but a trace­
able rationale a grower can act upon (Swati et al., 2026). Taken 
together, these capabilities point toward an evolution from yield pre­
diction models to yield prediction agents, providing systems that 
continuously sense, reason over heterogeneous data streams, quantify 
their own uncertainty, and generate management recommendations 
rather than static point estimates.
This prospect warrants caution rather than uncritical optimism. 
Foundation and agentic approaches remain largely unvalidated for 
perennial-crop yield prediction, and several barriers are specific to the 
citrus setting: a substantial domain gap between general pretraining 
corpora and citrus canopies under HLB pressure, the hallucination risks 
already noted for generative image enhancement, and computational 
and deployment costs that conflict with the low-cost, operational 
monitoring goals emphasized above. Most critically, these data-hungry 
models presuppose the very multi-year, multi-orchard benchmark 
datasets whose absence this review identifies as a central bottleneck 
(Section 8). In the near term, their value is therefore likely to lie in 
narrow, well-scoped roles, such as annotation, data-quality assurance, 
and feature extraction, rather than end-to-end autonomous forecasting, 
with the fully agentic vision remaining a longer-horizon target contin­
gent on the maturation of shared benchmarks.
10. Conclusion
This review synthesized the current state of citrus yield prediction 
across four dimensions: input features, data acquisition and feature 
extraction, prediction methods, and complementary yield-related ap­
proaches. The key finding is that while the field has advanced sub­
stantially, from simple regression models to multimodal deep learning 
frameworks, most studies remain anchored to a narrow set of easily 
measurable indicators, primarily fruit counts, canopy size, and vegeta­
tion indices, and rely on static, single-season datasets that limit gener­
alizability across orchards, cultivars, and regions. Three structural 
bottlenecks constrain further progress. First, genuine time-series 
modeling remains rare despite its potential to capture growth dy­
namics and fruit drop events across the season. Second, biotic stresses 
such as HLB fruit drop, whose yield impact has been well documented, 
remain under-integrated into prediction frameworks. Third, the lack of 
open, multi-year benchmark datasets prevents robust cross-study
