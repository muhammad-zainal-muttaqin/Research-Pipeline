ABSTRACT. Recent years have seen an increase in demand for strawberries, which necessitates automation in the relevant 
agricultural processes. Multiple object detection models have been proposed previously in order to automate agricultural 
processes, through applications such as fruit and disease detection. However, in integrating these improvements, it is 
essential that the deployment costs and weight are not increased significantly. This paper presents an analysis of the 
performance and accuracy of YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-tiny on real-time strawberry detection when inference is carried out 
on an embedded GPU device such as an NVIDIA Jetson Nano. Three frameworks (Darknet, TensorRT, and TensorFlow Lite) 
and three different resolutions (416×416 pixels, 480×480 pixels, and 640×640 pixels) were compared to obtain an optimal 
mAP and inference speed trade-off. The proposed setup is cost-effective and lightweight, which is good for small, unmanned 
ground vehicles and/or drones, and can achieve an accuracy of 91.95% at an FPS rate of 14.6, which makes it a viable 
option for deployment in strawberry fields. 
Keywords.  Image processing, Object detection, Real-time, YOLO

Introduction 
Cultivated strawberries, which are a member of the Rosaceae family, have been grown in North America since 1835. In 
Canada, day-neutral strawberries (which can produce for more than one season) are seeing an increase in acreage year to 
year. In 2019, strawberries achieved the fifth highest farm gate value in Canadian fruit, at 123.7 million dollars. Moreover, 
fresh strawberry exports have seen a steady increase from $2.3 million in 2016 to $10.8 million in 2019. (Canada & Centre, 
2019) 
The steady increase in demand for strawberries necessitates the integration of modern resources with agricultural 
processes. The productivity of advanced agriculture could be significantly improved by reducing the use of manual labor 
and increasing yield. The most extensive application of computer vision so far has been in fruit detection, that is, to detect, 
discriminate and localize individual fruits from the surroundings (Kapach & Barnea, 2012). Such detection allows the 
interaction of the relevant object and robotic manipulators. 
Advances in deep learning have brought about networks and systems which automatically preprocess data and help 
generalize distributions. For example, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) version 4 network utilizes data augmentation 
techniques such as photometric distortion to increase variability in images (Bochkovskiy, Wang, & Liao, 2020). The 
inclusion of these properties in the network simplifies the training process. 
Various techniques have been utilized for fruit detection in the literature. In Bargoti and Underwood (2017), a Faster 
Regional Convoluted Neural Network (Faster-RCNN) was utilized with non-maximum suppression as well as transfer 
learning and a 16-layer Visual Geometry Group (VGG16) network. F1-scores of around 0.9 were obtained with VGG16. 
The work in Sa, Ge, and Dayoub (2016) presented a real time fruit detection system termed DeepFruits which is also based 
on Faster-RCNN, utilizing multi-spectral imaging. Multiple techniques were used to combine Red Green Blue (RGB) and 
Near-Infrared (NIR) imagery, achieving an F1-score of 0.883 at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.4. In Lawal 
(2021), a modified version of Yolov3 (YOLO-Tomato), which is similar to YOLOv4, was utilized to detect tomatoes. 
Training data is assembled using the “Label what you see” technique. The model performed best with a combination of Mish 
activation function and Spatial Pyramid Pooling (SPP) with an F1-score of 98%. Mature tomato detection is also 
implemented in Liu, Mao, and Kim (2019), using Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptors which were utilized 
to train a Support Vector Machine along with False Colour Removal (FCR) followed by coarse to fine scanning method for 
detection. An F1-score of 92% was reported in the work; however this method did not perform well for overlapping and 
blocked tomatoes.  The advantage with Deep Neural Networks as described in Bargoti and Underwood (2017), Sa et al. 
(2016), and Lawal (2021) is that these methods automatically learn feature representations and avoid the need for manual 
extraction of features. Going further, multiple works run a comparison of some of these individual networks. The work in 
Wan & Goudos (2019) proposed an improved RCNN for fruit detection which shows a higher accuracy than Faster-RCNN 
and YOLOv3. However, this network is much slower than YOLOv3 and requires a much larger dataset to train (a mean 
Average Precision or mAP of 86% was achieved with 4000 training images). A comparison of the Faster-RCNN, YOLOv4 
and MobileNet1 SingleShot multibox Detection (SSD) models was observed in Kim, Sung, and Park (2020) in the context 
of real-time vehicle type recognition. The three networks were trained with the same data. The results showed that while 
RCNN achieved the highest accuracy and SSD MobileNet is the fastest, YOLO achieved a suitable balance of speed and 
accuracy, with the highest F1-score. The chief difference between YOLO and RCNN, as described in Yang and Jiachun 
(2018), is that YOLO is a single stage detector, and it approaches object detection as a regression problem. While the 
accuracy is comparable, the speed achieved by YOLOv4 is certainly higher than RCNN, which makes it suitable to smaller 
devices. 
Furthermore, in all of the mentioned works, inference is carried out on desktop or laptop devices with high memory 
specifications. The inherent advantage of YOLOv4 is its support for edge-based devices with the introduction of scaled 
versions (Wang, Bochkovskiy, & Liao, 2021). For example, YOLOv4-tiny was designed for low-end GPU devices and was 
demonstrated to have a higher framerate than other state-of-the-art tiny models. Previous edge-optimized models include 
ResNet-10, ResNet-18, and SSD. The analysis in Langis, Fulton, and Sattar (2020) which ran inferences on various deep 
object detectors including the SSD MobileNet versions showed that YOLOv4-tiny achieved a comparable accuracy along 
with the best efficiency (inference time). Similarly, the work in Magalhães et al. (2021) sought to compare YOLOv4-tiny 
with SSD MobileNet, Res-Net, and a few other models. However, they utilized an integer quantized (INT8) model for 
YOLOv4-tiny which compromised the accuracy. According to the results, the quantized version still achieved a comparable 
F1-score of 85% at a balanced confidence threshold of 49% and also the lowest inference time (4.87 milliseconds) among 
all the models. While Langis, Fulton, and Sattar (2020) and Magalhães et al. (2021) can attest to the efficiency and accuracy 
of YOLOv4-tiny in comparison to other lightweight networks, the results in Fernandes, Rodrigues, and Barros (2021), which 
benchmarked various models (namely the MobileNet versions and YOLOv4-tiny) on a dataset of over 4000 images, showed 
that YOLOv4-tiny had the highest average precision (AP) when detecting smaller objects. This would be beneficial in 
applications such as fruit detection given that the objects can be small and sometimes hidden. The framerate, however, was 
relatively lower in this case as the selected hardware utilized a Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) accelerator whereas the support 

3 
 
for Darknet primarily lies with GPU hardware. Based on this, YOLOv4-tiny seems to be the most suitable network for fruit 
detection on edge devices. 
The article in Shi, Pallis, and Xhu (2019) described edge computing as a system in which devices are placed in close 
proximity to sensors, mobile devices, and end-users. One such edge device is the NVIDIA® Jetson Nano ™ Developer Kit 
(NVIDIA, n.d.) which will be described in the next section. It is a compact and cost-effective option to deploy in an IoT 
system. 
The focus of this paper is to observe the performance of YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-tiny on Jetson Nano in detecting three 
classes in images and videos of strawberry fields. The three classes are flowers, unripe strawberries, and ripe strawberries. 
The two networks will be compared on the basis of metrics such as precision, recall, mAP and frame per second (FPS) rate. 
Further, the performance of YOLOv4-tiny will be observed on three frameworks (Darknet, TensorRT (FP16), and 
TensorFlow Lite) in order to deduce the most suitable one for edge-based computing 
 
Materials and Methodology 
The dataset consists of images and videos which were collected during the summer of 2018 and 2019 from Balamore 
Farm Ltd. (45°24'35.4"N, 63°34'26.3"W) and Millen Farms Ltd. (45°23'57.6"N, 63°33'31.1"W). 
The inference was carried out on NVIDIA® Jetson™ Nano (NVIDIA, n.d.) with a 128-Core Maxwell GPU, Quad-core 
ARM Processor and a 2GB 64-bit LPDDR4 RAM all operating on Ubuntu 18.4. It uses a 5V/3-A power supply through 
USB-C. The GPU features CUDA-enabled parallel computing. For the purpose of testing real-time detection, a Raspberry 
Pi v2 (Pi, n.d.) Camera Serial Interface (CSI) camera was connected to the Jetson Nano via a Mobile Industry Processor 
Interface (MIPI) connector (Figure 1). 
Both YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-tiny were trained for the detection of three classes as mentioned above. Both models can 
be optimized with NVIDIA CUDA® (B, n.d.) and are suitable for inference on the Jetson Nano.

YOLO 
The YOLO models were originally proposed by Redmon et al. (2016), Redmon and Farhad (2018), and Redmon and 
Farhad (2018) and are classified as single-stage detectors. The model, which is based on Darknet (Redmon & Farhadi, 
Darknet: Open Source Neural Networks in C, n.d.), predicts across all bounding boxes and classes on an image 
simultaneously. Improvements came along with YOLOv2 and YOLOv3, where anchor boxes were used instead of fully 
connected layers on top of the convolutional feature extractors, to predict bounding boxes. 
A large improvement came forth in 2020 when (Bochkovskiy, Wang, & Liao, 2020) proposed YOLOv4 with a number 
of new features such as Cross-Stage-Partial-connections (CSP), Cross mini-Batch Normalization (CmBN), Self-adversarial-
training (SAT) and Mish activation. A modern object detection network (Figure 2) consists of a backbone which is pre-
trained on another network, and a head which predicts the classes and bounding boxes. Additional layers, which make up 
the neck, are added between the backbone and head in order to collect feature maps.  

4 
 
 
Figure 2. One-stage Object Detector 
In case of YOLOv4, CSPDarknet53 was selected as the backbone due to its large number of convolutional layers. 
Additionally, the backbone includes a Bag of Freebies (BoF) and Bag of Specials (BoS). BoF consists of methods such as 
Data Augmentation, DropBlock regularization and Class label smoothing to improve accuracy while training without 
affecting the inference cost. Data Augmentation utilizes methods such as photometric and geometric distortion to make the 
model robust to images obtained from new environments, whereas focal loss helps the model deal with distribution bias in 
the dataset. BoS is a bundle of methods which seeks to increase accuracy with a slight increase in inference cost. This 
includes the Mish activation function, CSP and Multi-input weighted residual connections (MiWRC). 
For the neck, an SPP block is added over the CSPDarknet backbone as it significantly increases receptive field. The SPP 
block returns a fixed size of feature map regardless of the image size. This is integrated with a Path Aggregation Network 
(PAN) for parameter aggregation. For the head, YOLOv3 was selected, which includes Spatial Attention Module (SAM) 
and Non-maximum Suppression (NMS) for filtering badly predicted bounding boxes. 
The Scaled YOLOv4 models were introduced in 2021 by Wang, Bochkovskiy, and Liao (2021) in order to fit applications 
utilizing both low-end and high-end GPUs. This is done by changing the number of layers (YOLOv4-tiny has 38 layers 
while YOLOv4 has 162 layers) or applying other networks. The YOLOv4-tiny architecture is similar to that of YOLOv4, 
but with changes that bring about computational efficiency. The model integrates One-Shot Aggregation (OSANet) which 
is an energy-efficient backbone with smaller computational complexity, as described by (Lee, Hwang, Lee, Bae, & Park, 
2019). Gradient truncation is utilized for the best speed trade-off. For the neck, the design of YOLOv3-tiny is followed. 
Additionally, the tiny model uses only two YOLO layers as opposed to three. The above-described scaling down of the 
original model to YOLOv4-tiny makes it suitable for real-time object detection. 
 
Training 
In order to train both YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-tiny, around 200 images were obtained from the dataset as mentioned, 
including snapshots from videos of the strawberry fields. These images were then annotated using LabelImg (tzutalin, n.d.) 
which is graphical annotation tool (Figure 3). The annotations were of three classes: flower, unripe strawberry and ripe 
strawberry. The training images had on an average 10-20 annotations.

Different methods to calculate mAP have been defined, as in the Pascal Visual Objects Challenge (VOC2007) (PASCAL 
Visual Objects Challenge 2007, n.d.) and Microsoft Common Objects in Context (MS-COCO) (Common Objects in Context 
Evaluation, n.d.). The concept of Average Precision or “AP” was first introduced with VOC2007. In contrast to VOC2007 
which utilizes a single IoU threshold to calculate mAP, MS-COCO mAP is averaged over a range of IoU thresholds, which 
has motivated better object localization. Recently a multitude of other methods have been proposed as well, however, MS-
COCO and VOC continue to dominate as popular metrics, as described by Zou, Shi, Guo, and Ye (n.d.). In this study, we 
have utilized the MS-COCO convention to evaluate mAP. 
Using these metrics, the trained YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-tiny models are compared in the next section. 
 
Other Frameworks 
The benchmark inference speed of YOLOv4-tiny on NVIDIA Jetson Nano has been found to be 16 (in case of images). 
In the recent years, various frameworks have been introduced to speed up deep learning inference. One such framework is 
NVIDIA® TensorRT (NVIDIA, TensorRT, n.d.) which has been shown to deliver low latency and high throughput. The work 
in Jeong, Kim, Tan, Lee, and Ha (2021) presents a parallelization technique which shows that TensorRT can provide a 41% 
improvement in throughput while saving 20% of the energy consumption, which is particularly favorable for edge devices. 
TensorRT provides quantization optimizations of INT8 and FP16 for deployment as well. Another framework which 
allows for such optimization is TensorFlow Lite (TensorFlow, n.d.) which has been built for on-device inference. It is slated 
for inference on mobile GPUs.  
In this study, the YOLOv4-tiny model which was trained to detect flowers, unripe strawberries and ripe strawberries is 
integrated with TensorRT and TensorFlow Lite separately and the consequent change in FPS is observed. The best 
combination is thus chosen to obtain an optimal mAP and inference speed trade-off.

Conclusion 
In this study we have utilized YOLOv4-tiny in combination with an embedded GPU to implement real-time detection in 
strawberry fields. The results presented above indicate that YOLOv4-tiny is most ideal for real-time applications with an 
FPS of 14 obtained on the smallest Jetson™ device. A higher inference speed can be obtained by integrating it with TensorRT. 
From the literature it can be inferred that YOLOv4-tiny is comparable to SSD MobileNet and other lightweight networks in 
terms of accuracy. These factors make it a highly valuable object-detection model. Further, the real-time object detection 
system consisting of NVIDIA® Jetson™ Nano and Raspberry Pi Camera v2 is a highly cost-effective option for deployment 
in strawberry fields. 
The visible drawbacks include smaller objects being missed during detection, which could reduce accuracy while 
counting or tracking fruits. Improvements can be made by training on a larger and more diverse dataset. A global-shutter 
camera can be integrated for more powerful real-time detection. Further applications could include real-time disease 
detection and real-time pest detection.
