OrangeFruitNet: A Dataset and On-device Real Time YOLO-based Framework 
for Orange Fruit Detection and Yield Estimation in Orchard Environments 
 
Mohan Khedkar1,2*          Vijay Sambhe1          Vaibhav Dhore1 
Shrinivas Khedkar1          Nikhil Khandare1 
 
1Department of Computer Engineering, Veermata Jijabai Technological Institute, Mumbai, India 
2Department of Information Technology, Government Polytechnic, Maharashtra, India 
* Corresponding author’s Email: mskhedkar_p22@ce.vjti.ac.in 
 
 
Abstract: Accurate fruit detection and yield estimation are critical for precision agriculture and automated orchard 
management. Orange orchards remain underexplored, with limited large-scale annotated datasets. This paper 
introduces OrangeFruitNet, comprising 7,000 images capturing oranges under varying illumination, occlusion, and 
tree density conditions, annotated with precise bounding boxes and validated against expert counts. We benchmark 
YOLOv4-Darknet53, YOLOv5-s, YOLOv8-n, Mask Rs-CNN, and Faster R-CNN for detection and yield estimation, 
evaluating accuracy, speed, and embedded deployment feasibility. YOLOv8-n achieved the highest mean average 
precision (mAP) of 91.2%, F1-score of 0.92, and R² of 0.93 for yield prediction. On high performance GPU, YOLOv5-
s and YOLOv8-n sustained 30 to 32 FPS, while YOLOv4-Darknet53, Mask R-CNN, and Faster R-CNN achieved 
lower throughput, confirming real-time suitability for UAV assisted monitoring. On edge devices, YOLOv5-s 
maintained 8 FPS and YOLOv8-n 7 FPS on Jetson Nano, while YOLOv4 achieved 6 FPS; on Raspberry Pi 4, 
YOLOv8-n reached 2 FPS, YOLOv5-s 2.5 FPS, and YOLOv4-Darknet53 3 FPS, demonstrating feasibility under low 
power hardware constraints. Data augmentation enhanced robustness to lighting variations, occlusion, and fruit 
clustering. OrangeFruitNet provides a benchmark dataset and framework that combines large scale annotations, state-
of-the-art models, and real-time deployment, advancing precision agriculture through intelligent orchard management 
and yield prediction. 
Keywords: Precision agriculture, Computer vision, Deep learning, Fruit detection, Yield estimation, YOLO (You 
Only Look Once), Orange Fruits, Agricultural expert validation, Dataset limitations, OrangeFruitNet dataset. 
 
 
1. Introduction 
Accurate fruit detection and yield estimation 
remain core challenges in precision agriculture. 
Manual counting is labour intensive, error-prone, and 
unsuitable for large-scale orchards [1, 2]. Advances 
in computer vision and deep learning have enabled 
automated fruit recognition [4, 5], yet occlusion, 
overlapping fruits, inconsistent illumination, and 
background clutter continue to limit accuracy [6, 7]. 
Most existing models also struggle to deliver real-
time performance in the field [8, 9]. One-stage 
detectors 
such 
as 
the 
YOLO 
family 
have 
demonstrated strong potential for fruit detection [3, 
14]. 
Applications 
to 
apples, 
mangoes, 
and 
strawberries have shown encouraging results [10-12, 
31]. However, orange and citrus fruits remain 
relatively underexplored due to the lack of large, 
well-annotated datasets [16, 31]. In this paper, we 
address the challenges of detecting orange fruits in 
orchard environments by leveraging state-of-the-art 
object detection models and a systematic dataset 
preparation strategy. A key contribution is the 
introduction of OrangeFruitNet (ours), a dedicated 
benchmark dataset of annotated orange images 
designed for fine-tuning and evaluation in real world 
orchard conditions. To the best of our knowledge, 
OrangeFruitNet is the first reproducible dataset 
focused exclusively on oranges, complementing 
existing generic fruit datasets.

Unlike existing citrus datasets such as CitDet [36] 
and Green Citrus [16], which primarily contain 
images captured under controlled illumination or 
limited geographic coverage, OrangeFruitNet(Ours) 
was designed to reflect real-world orchard variability. 
It integrates multi-season and multi-location data 
from distinct orchards, collected using heterogeneous 
capture devices (DSLR, UAV, and smartphone). 
Each image includes expert-verified annotations on 
occlusion severity and illumination tier (bright, 
diffuse, 
shaded), 
enabling 
more 
fine-grained 
benchmarking of detection robustness. Furthermore, 
OrangeFruitNet(Ours)  introduces a domain-specific 
augmentation pipeline that emulates environmental 
variability (canopy density, sunlight angle, haze) 
rather than generic geometric or color perturbations. 
These 
attributes 
make 
OrangeFruitNet(Ours) 
fundamentally distinct from previous datasets, 
providing a reproducible benchmark aligned with 
practical agricultural sensing conditions. The main 
contributions of our paper are as follows: 
1). OrangeFruitNet (ours): We develop a new 
high quality orange fruit image dataset 
consisting of 1,750 manually annotated 
images expanded to 7,000 via targeted offline 
augmentations. The dataset captures orchard 
specific challenges such as occlusion, 
clustering, 
and 
illumination 
variability, 
providing the first reproducible benchmark 
dedicated to oranges. 
2). YOLO-based detection framework: We 
design and implement a citrus fruit detection 
framework trained on OrangeFruitNet and 
integrated with benchmark datasets such as 
COCO [32] and ImageNet [33], enhancing 
model robustness and generalizability. 
3). Comprehensive 
benchmarking: 
We 
evaluate multiple state-of-the-art detectors 
(YOLOv4, YOLOv5, YOLOv8 vs. Faster R-
CNN and Mask R-CNN) under standard 
metrics including precision, recall, F1-score, 
mAP, FPS, and power usage, establishing 
OrangeFruitNet as a valuable resource for the 
research community. 
4). Yield estimation framework: We propose 
an integrated framework that aggregates 
image level predictions into tree and orchard 
level yield estimates, validated against expert 
provided ground truth counts. 
5). Real-time embedded deployment: We 
demonstrate lightweight YOLO variants on 
low power devices such as the NVIDIA 
Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi 4, achieving 
low latency, energy efficient operation 
suitable for UAV assisted and mobile orchard 
monitoring. 
The remainder of our paper is organized as 
follows. Section 2 reviews related work on fruit 
detection and yield estimation. Section 3 presents the

(OrangeFruitNet (Ours)), augmentation, annotation, 
model 
selection, 
training 
and 
inference 
configurations, and deployment on embedded

Section 5 concludes the paper and highlights

2. Related work    
2.1 Fruit detection and yield estimation in 
agriculture 
Fruit detection has long been a challenge in 
precision agriculture. Early methods using image 
processing and segmentation for apples [1, 6] were 
sensitive to background and illumination. Deep 
learning, with models such as YOLO [3], Faster R-
CNN [13], and Mask R-CNN [15], has advanced 
robustness. Surveys in [2, 5, 8, 9], and [17] provide 
comprehensive reviews. In addition, the progressive 
integration of multispectral and UAV-based imagery 
with deep models has been shown to improve 
detection under heterogeneous field conditions [11, 
25]. However, many of these studies emphasize 
algorithmic accuracy but devote limited attention to 
model validation using real orchard scenarios or 
expert assessments, which remains a critical 
requirement for operational deployment. Benchmark 
datasets like COCO [32], ImageNet [33], and Open 
Images [34] enabled transfer learning but lacked 
agricultural specificity. Agriculture-focused datasets 
such as MinneApple [7, 23], Fruit-360 [35], 
PhenoSet 
[18], 
and 
CitDet 
[36], 
improved 
performance but mostly target apples, berries, or 
generic fruits, leaving citrus fruits underrepresented. 
Furthermore, 
while 
these 
datasets 
provide 
standardized annotations and large-scale benchmarks, 
their environmental conditions (lighting, occlusion, 
canopy density) often differ from real orange 
orchards, thus limiting their applicability for yield 
estimation tasks in natural field settings [28, 38].

2.2 YOLO-based models for real-time fruit 
detection 
YOLO models are widely adopted in agricultural 
computer vision for their speed–accuracy balance. 
Early applications in apple [4, 10] and mango 
orchards [20] were promising. Later work explored 
lightweight YOLO variants for embedded systems 
[26]. Recent studies benchmarked YOLOv4 [14], 
YOLOv5 [16, 21], and YOLOv8 [22] in orchard 
environments, confirming real-time feasibility, but 
orange focused applications remain limited. Notably, 
model compression and pruning techniques have 
been introduced to deploy YOLO variants on low-
power devices without significant performance loss 
[24, 26]. Such advances indicate the potential for 
integrating YOLO-based frameworks with edge 
computing platforms for in-field decision support. 
Moreover, adaptive training strategies such as class-
balanced 
sampling 
and 
domain-specific 
augmentation have been proposed to address 
imbalanced fruit distributions and canopy variability 
[16, 20]. These approaches, while effective for apples 
and strawberries, remain largely unexplored for citrus 
detection where fruit density and color similarity to 
foliage present additional challenges. 
2.3 Orange and citrus specific works 
Orange and Citrus yield estimation has received 
less attention than apples. Wang et al. [27] developed 
citrus detection methods under complex backgrounds. 
Fu et al. [16] evaluated YOLOv5 for green citrus 
detection. Silva et al. [38] benchmarked deep 
learning models for citrus detection, noting 
challenges 
with 
occlusion 
and 
illumination. 
Rahnemoonfar and Sheppard [19] used deep 
simulated learning for fruit counting, and Stein et al. 
[28] specifically targeted automated orange yield 
estimation. UAV-based monitoring has also been 
explored [11, 25, 29], but practical deployment for 
orange yield estimation remains limited. Additionally, 
while these studies provide valuable insights into 
model behavior under orchard conditions, their 
reliance on relatively small or custom datasets 
restricts reproducibility and cross-study comparison 
[36, 38]. Incorporating cross-seasonal data and multi-
sensor fusion, as demonstrated in related apple 
studies [23], could significantly enhance the 
robustness of citrus yield estimation frameworks. 
Furthermore, only a few works explicitly quantify the 
relationship 
between 
detection 
accuracy 
and 
downstream yield estimation metrics [19, 28]. 
Establishing such quantitative linkages is essential 
for validating models as decision-support tools for 
growers and agricultural planners. 
2.4 Limitations of prior research 
Although notable progress has been achieved, 
significant gaps remain. Citrus fruits remain 
underrepresented, with most detection frameworks 
focusing on apples or general fruit categories. 
Benchmark datasets such as MinneApple [7], CitDet 
[36], and Fruit-360 [35] are not optimized for orange 
detection under natural orchard conditions, limiting 
generalizability. Furthermore, few studies validate 
detection results against agricultural expert judgment, 
which is essential for reliable yield estimation.

2.5 Research gap 
As summarized in Table 1, prior research has 
focused predominantly on apples, mangoes, and 
strawberries, while orange-specific detection and 
yield 
estimation 
remain 
comparatively 
underexplored. Despite the economic significance of 
citrus fruits, few works address challenges unique to 
oranges, such as clustered fruit arrangements, canopy 
variability, and heterogeneous orchard conditions [1, 
12, 20, 27, 28]. Progress is further constrained by 
limitations in available datasets. General-purpose 
benchmarks such as COCO [32], ImageNet [33], and 
Open Images [34] provide minimal representation of 
citrus fruits, while domain-specific collections like 
MinneApple [7], CitDet [36], and PhenoSet [18] are 
not tailored to orange orchards. Models trained on 
these datasets often underperform in the presence of 
occlusion, variable illumination, and overlapping 
fruit [30, 31]. To address this gap, the present study 
develops a dedicated, annotated orange dataset with 
augmentation 
strategies 
designed 
to 
improve 
robustness under orchard-specific conditions. Finally, 
although YOLO-based detectors have demonstrated 
strong accuracy [16, 21, 22], their real-time 
deployment on resource-constrained devices and 
validation against agricultural expert counts remain 
limited [2, 9]. Most prior works emphasize accuracy 
alone without ensuring practical adaptability in 
orchard environments. 
In this paper we propose a dataset driven YOLO 
framework for orange fruit detection and yield 
estimation, integrating robust model training, real-
time embedded deployment, and validation against 
expert judgment. By addressing dataset limitations, 
orchard 
specific 
challenges, 
and 
practical 
deployment constraints, the framework contributes a 
field ready solution for precision orange fruits 
agriculture. 
3. Methodology    
This section details the proposed framework for 
orange fruit detection and yield estimation. As shown 
in Fig. 1, the framework begins with image 
acquisition from orchard environments and proceeds 
through dataset preparation (cleaning, annotation, 
and augmentation), model training and evaluation, 
embedded deployment on low-power devices, and 
final yield estimation validated against expert counts. 
The modular design ensures that each stage can be 
independently improved or replaced as new datasets 
and models become available. This structured 
workflow 
also 
enables 
reproducibility 
and 
straightforward benchmarking of the proposed 
system, providing a clear foundation for the detailed 
procedures described in next Subsections 3.1 to 3.5. 
3.1 Dataset preparation 
An effective citrus fruit detection model was 
developed using a two-stage data strategy: (1) 
initialization on a generic object detection dataset and 
(2) fine-tuning on citrus- and orange-specific datasets, 
with a strong emphasis on our newly proposed 
OrangeFruitNet (ours) benchmark. For pretraining, 
the Microsoft COCO dataset [32] was employed, 
providing object-level annotations across 80 classes. 
ImageNet [33] was not reused directly, as modern 
YOLO backbones are already initialized on 
ImageNet. OrangeFruitNet, introduced in this work, 
complements existing citrus datasets by offering a 
dedicated, reproducible benchmark of annotated 
orange images for fine-tuning and evaluation.

3.1.1. Pre-processing and annotation 
Prior to training, all images underwent standard 
preprocessing steps to ensure dataset integrity and 
consistency. Duplicate and low-quality samples were 
removed. Images were resized to (640 x 640) pixels 
to match YOLO input requirements, and pixel values 
were normalized to the [0, 1] range for stable 
optimization. Annotations were reformatted into 
YOLO format (c, xc, yc, w, h) where c represents the 
class label and (xc, yc, w, h) are normalized with 
respect 
to 
image 
width 
and 
height. 
This 
harmonization minimized bias across heterogeneous 
datasets. 
3.1.2. Data augmentation 
To overcome dataset scarcity and better reflect 
orchard variability, offline augmentation strategies 
were applied to citrus-oriented datasets. Building on 
CitDet [36], Green Citrus [16], and UAV-based citrus 
imagery [29], we introduce OrangeFruitNet (ours) as 
a dedicated benchmark dataset. OrangeFruitNet 
consists of 1,750 RGB images of oranges, collected 
via web scraping and manually annotated using 
Roboflow. Representative samples are shown in Fig. 
2, 
capturing 
real-world 
challenges 
such 
as 
illumination variability, clustering, occlusion, and 
fruit density. To enhance robustness, offline 
augmentations including rotation, horizontal flipping, 
and brightness adjustment expanded OrangeFruitNet 
to 7,000 images. The name OrangeFruitNet was 
chosen in line with established conventions (e.g., 
ImageNet [33]) to emphasize both its orange fruit 
specific focus and its reproducibility as an internet-
sourced 
benchmark 
dataset.

3.1.4. Dataset licensing and redistribution 
To ensure transparency and reproducibility, all 
images included in OrangeFruitNet (ours) have been 
verified for their source, license type, and 
redistribution eligibility. The dataset comprises two 
categories of imagery: a) Publicly available, open-
licensed content collected from research and 
educational repositories such as CitDet [36], and 
Green Citrus [16], each permitting academic reuse 
under open licenses or explicit citation terms. b) 
Field-acquired and web-sourced orchard images 
manually verified to originate from non-commercial, 
publicly accessible sources authorizing academic 
research use. All retained images include metadata 
specifying origin, license type, and contributor 
acknowledgement.

3.2 Model selection 
Considering 
the 
requirement 
of 
real-time

3.3 Training procedure 
All models were trained on an NVIDIA GeForce 
RTX 4070 GPU SUPER (12 GB), 12th Gen Intel 
Core i7-12700 2.10 GHz processor, and 64 GB RAM, 
running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS, CUDA 12.1, cuDNN 8.9, 
and PyTorch 2.2. Following the dataset preparation 
in sub-section 3.1, training leveraged both pre-trained 
weights from Microsoft COCO [32] for initialisation, 
followed by fine-tuning on citrus-specific datasets 
(CitDet 
[36] 
and 
Green 
Citrus 
[16]) 
and 
OrangeFruitNet (Ours). UAV-based imagery [29] 
was excluded from training and used exclusively as 
an external evaluation set for cross-dataset testing (no 
UAV images were included in any training split). 
Auxiliary 
datasets 
(MinneApple 
[7], 
AppleGrowthVision [23], RaspberrySet [18]) were 
selectively used to enhance generalization under 
challenging conditions. The complete training 
process, including dataset splits, augmentation, and 
fine-tuning, is summarized in Algorithm 2. The 
offline augmentations, viz rotation, flipping, and 
brightness adjustment, enhanced training robustness 
by simulating variability in lighting, fruit density, 
occlusion, and clustering. These augmentations 
allowed the models to learn more generalized 
features, 
reducing 
overfitting 
and 
improving 
performance under real orchard conditions.   
For all citrus datasets, images were split into 
training, validation, and test sets, typically in an 
80:10:10 ratio without cross-contamination across 
sources, ensuring sufficient data for model learning 
while reserving representative samples for unbiased 
evaluation and hyperparameter tuning. Images were 
resized to 640 × 640 pixels, normalized to [0, 1], and 
augmented through rotation (+15°), horizontal 
flipping, and brightness scaling (±15 %) to simulate 
orchard variability. For YOLOv4, the default 
Darknet-53 backbone and anchors from the original 
configuration [14] were used. For YOLOv5 (small s 
and medium m variants) [21] and YOLOv8 (nano n 
and small s variants) [22], we adopted the official 
Ultralytics 
implementations 
with 
auto-anchor

using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate = 
0.001, β₁ = 0.937, β₂ = 0.999) with a cosine annealing 
scheduler and a batch size of 16 for 200 epochs 
balancing convergence and overfitting control [16, 
27]. The input pipeline employed random mosaic 
augmentation and adaptive image caching for GPU 
efficiency. For reproducibility, the random seed was 
fixed to 42 across all experiments.

YOLOv8) used the standard multi-part loss function 
comprising: 
first 
classification

ensuring

3.4 Evaluation metrics 
Model performance was assessed using metrics 
directly relevant to orange fruit detection in orchard 
environments. Precision quantifies the proportion of 
correctly detected fruits, indicating robustness 
against false positives such as foliage or branches. 
Recall measures the fraction of actual fruits 
successfully detected, reflecting sensitivity under 
occlusion and clustering. The F1-score, the harmonic 
mean of precision and recall, provides a balanced 
indicator of overall detection reliability. Detection 
accuracy was further evaluated using Mean Average 
Precision (mAP) at two Intersection-over-Union 
(IoU) thresholds, mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95, to 
capture both coarse and fine localization quality. 
Inference speed (FPS) on an NVIDIA RTX GPU was 
recorded to assess real-time feasibility for UAV- or 
ground-based deployment. 
All metrics were computed on the reserved 
validation and test sets comprising images unseen 
during 
training. 
Each 
dataset’s 
split 
and 
augmentation counts exactly match Table 2, ensuring 
numerical reproducibility across all experiments 
(Tables 4 to 9). Each experiment was repeated three 
times with different random seeds (42, 84, and 126); 
reported results represent the mean ± standard 
deviation (SD) or 95 % confidence interval (CI) 
across these runs. This protocol ensures statistical 
reliability and supports reproducibility of the 
presented results.

4. Results and discussion    
4.1 Detection accuracy 
The evaluation confirmed that YOLO-based 
models consistently outperformed region-based 
detectors in orange fruit detection tasks. As 
summarized in Table 4, the dataset distribution used 
for training, validation, and testing follows an 
80:10:10 split across multiple citrus datasets, 
ensuring balanced evaluation and minimizing bias. 
YOLOv5-s and YOLOv8-n achieved mean Average 
Precision (mAP) values exceeding 93% on orange 
specific datasets, demonstrating clear superiority 
over Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN, which, 
despite their strong accuracy in controlled conditions, 
suffered from lower efficiency and slower inference 
[13, 15]. YOLOv4 performed moderately, offering a 
balanced trade-off between accuracy and speed, but 
was less competitive compared to the more recent 
YOLO variants [14, 21, 22]. Table 5 presents the 
comparative performance of all evaluated models 
(ours) across precision, recall, F1-score, mAP, and 
inference 
speed, 
providing 
a 
comprehensive 
benchmark for orange fruit detection, while Table 6 
summarizes state-of-the-art results directly cited 
from prior works [7, 16, 29, 36]. These findings 
reinforce the suitability of YOLO models for high-
accuracy detection in real orchard environments 
where computational efficiency is critical.  
All reported training results exclude UAV-Citrus 
images to prevent data leakage. UAV-Citrus was 
reserved exclusively for cross-dataset validation to 
demonstrate transfer robustness. 
4.2 Robustness to orchard challenges 
Field-level conditions such as occlusion, dense 
fruit clustering, and variable illumination posed 
significant challenges to detection accuracy. To 
address this, the augmentation strategies applied 
during dataset preparation, such as rotation, flipping, 
and brightness adjustments, exposed the models to a 
broader range of visual conditions, thereby 
improving resilience to orchard-specific variability. 
As a result, lightweight YOLO variants retained 
reliable detection performance under shadows, 
partial occlusion, and canopy clutter, maintaining 
stable precision and recall values even in the presence 
of severe environmental disturbances. This outcome 
supports prior claims that carefully designed 
augmentations strengthen model generalizability in 
agricultural contexts [16, 27, 38]. Table 7 quantifies 
the impact of augmentation on model performance 
across all datasets, and Fig. 3 illustrates sample 
detection visualizations under bright light, occlusion, 
and clustered fruit scenarios. 
4.3 Dataset-wise, cross-dataset and public dataset 
evaluation 
To assess generalization across different citrus 
datasets, we performed both in-dataset (train/test 
within the same dataset) and cross-dataset (train on 
one dataset, test on another without fine-tuning) 
evaluations using CitDet [36], Green Citrus [16], 
UAV-Citrus [29], and the proposed OrangeFruitNet 
(Ours). For all experiments we used the training 
procedure described in Section 3.3 (pretrained COCO 
initialization, 200 epochs, Adam optimizer with 
lr=0.001, batch size 16, seed = 42). Images were 
resized to 640×640 and augmented consistently with 
the pipeline in Section 3.2. Reported metrics are 
mAP@0.5 (mean ± standard deviation) computed 
over n = 3 independent runs for each cell. Cross-
dataset tests evaluate a model trained on the full 
training split of the source dataset and tested on the 
official test split of the target dataset with no 
additional fine-tuning. 
The UAV-Citrus dataset was excluded from 
training to preserve independence for external 
generalization testing. It was used solely for cross-
dataset evaluation. 
As shown in Table 8, the in-dataset baselines (top 
four rows) confirm high accuracy across all datasets. 
Cross-dataset evaluation reveals that models trained 
on CitDet or Green Citrus exhibit a substantial drop 
(≈ 6–10 %) when tested on OrangeFruitNet, 
indicating dataset bias driven by domain differences 
such as illumination, view angle, and canopy 
variability. 
In 
contrast, 
models 
trained 
on 
OrangeFruitNet show only minor performance drops 
when evaluated on CitDet, Green Citrus, and UAV-
Citrus (≈ 1–3 % relative to in-dataset performance), 
demonstrating that OrangeFruitNet provides broader 
domain coverage and yields models with superior 
cross-domain robustness.

4.5 Yield estimation results 
As shown in Table 12, the integration of detection 
outputs into the yield estimation framework produced 
block 
level 
counts 
closely 
matching 
expert 
assessments. Across three orchard blocks (1,200 -
1,500 fruits each), the automated estimates deviated 
by only 1.5 - 3.2% from manual counts, yielding an 
overall R² of 0.93 with specialist annotations ( [28, 
31]). Fig. 5 visualizes this relationship and shows that 
predicted counts scale linearly with expert counts, 
demonstrating the framework’s reliability for 
orchard-scale yield prediction under real-world 
conditions. These findings indicate that the proposed 
system not only detects fruits with high precision but 
also translates these detections into accurate yield 
estimates, thereby reducing labor-intensive manual 
counting and improving 
decision-making for 
precision agriculture. The consistency between 
predicted and observed counts across multiple 
orchard blocks confirms the robustness of the 
proposed framework for operational deployment.  
As summarized in Table 12, the baseline canopy-
scaling model provides close agreement with expert 
counts (overall R² = 0.93) across all orchard blocks. 
However, to further enhance interpretability and 
account for spatial variations in canopy density, a 
regression-based extension was implemented as 
described in Subsection 3.5. This model jointly 
considers canopy area, mean fruit diameter, and fruit 
density as predictors of yield, producing statistically 
more stable results across heterogeneous tree 
structures. Table 13 compares the two methods using 
the same dataset with expert verified tree level yields.  
The regression model improves correlation with 
expert measurements from 0.89 to 0.94 while 
reducing RMSE by ≈ 29 %. It shows particularly 
strong performance for trees with dense or uneven 
canopies, where simple area scaling tends to under- 
or over-estimate yield. These results confirm that 
incorporating structural and spatial features enhances 
the robustness of the yield prediction while 
maintaining computational efficiency suitable for 
embedded deployment. A scatter plot illustrating 
predicted versus expert yield correlations for both 
models is shown in Fig. 6. 
5. Conclusion and future work  
Our work developed and validated a YOLO-
based framework for orange fruit detection and yield 
estimation under natural orchard conditions. The 
proposed system achieved high detection accuracy, 
robust performance in the presence of occlusion and 
lighting variability, and close agreement with 
agricultural expert counts, thereby addressing the 
limitations identified in Section 2. A key contribution 
of 
this 
work 
is 
the 
introduction 
of 
OrangeFruitNet(ours), a new and integrated orange-
specific dataset that, together with existing citrus 
datasets, substantially enhances model robustness 
and benchmarking for future research. These results 
demonstrate the feasibility of deploying lightweight 
YOLO models for operational precision agriculture 
in citrus orchards. 
Future work can explore the integration of IoT-
based orchard monitoring systems to enable 
continuous and automated yield tracking [37]. 
Leveraging multispectral and UAV imagery may 
further improve detection performance under 
challenging conditions such as occlusion and variable 
lighting [29]. Expanding large-scale, annotated 
orange datasets [36, 38], including extending 
OrangeFruitNet, will enhance model generalizability 
and robustness. Finally, hybrid approaches that 
combine object detection with 3-D reconstruction 
techniques 
could provide 
more 
precise 
and 
comprehensive yield estimation, offering greater 
value for precision agriculture applications.
