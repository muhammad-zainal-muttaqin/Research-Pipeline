A B S T R A C T  
Fruits counting is important in management of orchard and plantation since better decision for labor and logistic 
can be made based on complete and accurate counting of fruits. Computer vision-based fruits counting has been 
research focus as it’s an automatic way for recognition of dense fruit on the branch. However, complete fruits 
counting of a whole tree hasn’t hardly been studied. And there is a lack of robust and accurate fruits counting 
method in complex orchard scenarios, like covering, shadow, clustering in image. In this paper, a panoramic 
method for fruit complete yield counting based on deep learning object detection is proposed, and was validated 
on a holly tree with dense fruits. Firstly, images were taken surrounding the fruit trees using UAV, and SIFT based 
images matching were performed to form a complete panoramic unfolding map of the fruit tree surface. Then, a 
YOLOX object detection network was built and trained with novel samples augmentation and composition 
strategies. Finally, fruits counting YOLOX was performed on the panorama to count the whole plant fruits 
number. The accuracy and effectiveness of this method were tested at different scales and scenarios. The results 
show that: (1) high-quality panoramic images can be built for an accurate fruit number counting. (2) The Sta­
tistical Rate (SR) between detected number and actual number is as high as SR > 96% when the ring shot 
parameter of Holly tree is R ≤1.2 m, SR > 95% when R ≤1.6 m. The Detection Rate between detected number 
and captured number in the panorama image is over 99% when R ≤1.2 m and over 97% when R ≤2.0 m. The 
result is superior to previous researches. (3) it has good robustness against shading, covering, incomplete con­
tour. Comparisons between the proposed method and other methods has been done and the result show the 
proposed method is the most effective in fruits counting. Moreover, we proposed and verified the positive effects 
of Gaussian convolution kernel and γ-component control on fruit detection rate. The YOLOX-based fruit counting 
method can be extended to a wide range of fruits, like apples, lychee and so. Moreover, YOLOX has excellent 
inferencing efficiency which makes it a good potential for real-time application in orchard and plantation 
management.   
1. Introduction 
Nowadays, in the background of agriculture development towards 
intelligent agriculture (Yan-e, 2011) and precision farming (Auern­
hammer, 2001), accurate production information provides better deci­
sion support. Accurate yield statistics are important especially for the 
fruits and vegetables yield count which have become hot topics. Accu­
rate and complete yield counting is essential to these tasks, so for fruit 
yield counting, it must include both visible fruits and those hidden ones 
under leaves, in shadows, and in the distance (Gongal et al., 2016). In 
addition, for clustered growing fruits, contours sticking to each other is 
also a problem must to be solved (Courtois et al., 2010). The successful 
application of computer vision for yield counting has greatly improved 
productivity, and avoided repetitive labor inputs which dependent on 
extensive manpower and rough empirical estimates. Previous re­
searchers have explored and practiced various computer vision ap­
proaches for more efficient and robust yield counting solutions. 
Several mathematical morphology methods and Color Space seg­
mentation methods are used to extract fruits such as, color threshold- 
based segmentation (Payne et al., 2013), Watershed (Bargoti and Un­
derwood, 2017), Circular Hough Transform (Lu et al., 2018), Corrosion 
and Expansion (Lv et al., 2019). Morphological methods are usually 
combined with color segmentation or other methods for better seg­
mentation of clustered growing fruits under natural conditions such as

(Dorj et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2021, Li et al., 2016). However, 
morphological approaches rely on the obvious contrasts between fruits 
and backgrounds. For example, in (Lin et al., 2020), the leaves and fruits 
are very similar, which caused the leaves to be identified as fruits. 
Moreover, color segmentation is difficult to avoid the pixel loss of tar­
gets, such as fruits in shadows (Wu et al., 2020). 
Many feature extraction and processing methods, such as in 
(Muhammad, 2015), Local binary pattern (Ojala et al., 2002) and Weber 
local descriptor (Chen et al., 2009) for textures extraction and Principal 
Component Analysis (Wold et al., 1987) for feature dimensionality 
reduction, are used for fruit detection, but usually need to be used in 
combination with Machine Learning algorithms. Some traditional ma­
chine learning methods are widely used in fruit detection, such as 
Support Vector Machines (Guo et al., 2019), Random Forests (Pereira 
et al., 2018), Clustering (Fachrurrozi et al., 2017), Bayesian classifier 
(Amatya et al., 2016), and Neural Networks (Azarmdel et al., 2020), 
these are the critical steps for the fruits to be classified or identified. 
However, these approaches can’t be directly used for high-dimensional 
features learning, and often require features space downscaling or used 
finite type features (Gongal et al., 2015, Naik and Patel, 2017). Such as 
(Zhang et al., 2014), the authors used color, texture, and shape to 
compose the feature space, then used principal component analysis to 
reduce the dimensionality of the feature space, and finally used a hybrid 
model of fitness-scaled chaotic artificial bee colony algorithm and 
feedforward neural network for classification. In (Zhang et al., 2016) the 
authors also performed principal component analysis on the constructed 
feature space to reduce the dimensionality. These methods depend on 
the conspicuous features types of objects, hence the portability and 
generality are not strong (for example, red fruits can be extracted based 
on color, but this may not be valid for green fruits.), and also lack the 
robustness for the inconspicuous features in complex scenes. 
Object detection is a major application of deep neural network as it 
greatly improved vision-based detection accuracy. High-dimensional 
features are processed by many layers of convolution so that they 
have strong robustness and resistance against noises and deficiency. 
Deep convolution neural network has been used in many agricultural 
applications. In (Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018), it demonstrated 
that deep learning methods provide higher detection precision than 
previous image processing methods. A classical object detection network 
for target processing includes classification task and localization task. 
The first one is two-stage approach, such as Faster RCNN series (Ren 
et al., 2015, Girshick et al., 2014, Girshick, 2015), in which classification 
and localization are divided separately and finally transformed into 
classification and regression. The second is a one-step approach, which 
does classification and localization simultaneously. The representative 
network of this kind are the SSD (Liu et al., 2016) and YOLO series 
(Redmon et al., 2016, Redmon and Farhadi, 2018a, Bochkovskiy et al., 
2020). In the study of (Mirhaji et al., 2021a), the authors used the YOLO- 
v4 network to identify oranges under different light conditions and 
obtained a detection rate over 90%. In the review of (Koirala et al., 
2019), it showed that among the studies using the RCNN series, the 
YOLO series for fruit detection obtained detection rates over 80%. The 
Faster RCNN and YOLO series has been used for fruit detection or as a 
baseline modified to suit different requirements. For example, in the 
study of (Zhang et al., 2022), the authors improved the YOLOV4-tiny 
network and deployed it on Nvidia Jetson Nano for real-time infield 
strawberry detection. YOLOX is a latest proposed YOLO series network. 
It’s an anchor-free version of YOLO, with a simpler design but better 
performance. It aims to bridge the gap between research and industrial 
communities. Its potential in yield counting hasn’t been verified yet. 
The systematic solution to achieve complete fruit counting of a tree is 
insufficiently studied. Besides the detection methods, it is essential to 
capture the entire tree fruits without duplication and omission for 
complete and accurate counting of all fruits. Unlike post-harvest or 
flowering period yield statistics when trees have few leaves and are less 
susceptible to background interference, infield non-contact on-tree 
fruits counting are facing more complex scenarios (Cubero et al., 2016). 
In (Song et al., 2014), it counts fruits in sequential multiple images, 
using a statistical model to eliminate duplicate counts. However, such 
methods are complicated to operate and have a probability of misclas­
sification. The panorama (Jiang et al., 2021) provides a novel and 
feasible approach. In the study of (Chen et al., 2019), the researchers 
used Faster RCNN for fruit detection on stitched orthophotos of straw­
berry fields. In the study of (Mekhalfi et al., 2020), the authors designed 
an agricultural machine that can walk around the field, take pictures, 
and stitch the photos to form a whole panorama of the kiwifruit orchard, 
then the Viola-Jones detection algorithm was used for yield estimation. 
However, these researches use photos from one direction or form 
orthophotos. The encircled panorama in a 360◦view without fruit 
omission has seldomly been studied. 
Therefore, in this study, a new approach for complete yield counting 
was proposed, which mainly consists of (1) using multi-view images of 
fruit trees stitched together to form a unfold panorama; and (2) using the 
latest single step deep convolution neural network YOLOX for fruit 
counting. The main contributions of this study include, (1) using a 
sample augmentation method specific to real scenarios and validating 
the network’s generalization ability in complex scenarios. (2) The per­
formance of other fruit detection methods on panoramas were 
compared. (3) Methods to improve detection rates based on image 
processing were explored.

2. Material and method 
2.1. Experimental subject 
Holly trees with fruits was taken as an experiment subject in this 
research. Holly fruits are berry-like drupes, usually spherical, red at 
maturity, with a membranous or firm papery exocarp. Their Fruits 
usually grow in aggregates or adhesions, with obvious color contrast to 
the leaves, and the fruit shape is close to the regular geometric circle. 
Since holly trees are similar to bush-like economic fruit trees such as 
cherries and mulberries in terms of height and profile, and the fruit 
distribution and color are widely representative of economic fruit trees, 
the research results based on holly fruits are expected to be extended to 
other kinds of fruits. 
2.2. Image acquisition 
The images were acquired by a DJI Mavic 2pro drone equipped with 
a 4000 × 3000 high-resolution RGB digital camera. It integrates high- 
resolution sensing module and intelligent (automatic) light field/focus 
adjustment program. The UAV can be flexibly used to photograph trees 
at different heights so that it can be used for big fruit trees like apple, 
grapefruit as well. In order to make the canopy as complete as possible 
and avoid stitching errors due to capturing too much background (e.g., 
buildings behind trees, roads, etc.), it is necessary to design shooting 
methods according to the size of the holly trees. 
The details of the photographing method are shown in Fig. 1c. The 
drone was to move in the parallel plane of the canopy tangent plane at a 
fixed distance to the center of the canopy. Images were taken around the 
holly tree at 30◦elevation angle. Three parameters were calculated to 
determine the flight of the UAV, camera height “H”, surrounding radius 
“R”, lens tilt angle “α”. The longitudinal field of view (rad) of the camera 
in the test was “φ”, the lateral field of view was “ψ”. 3 parameters, the 
canopy height h, the canopy maximum radius w, and bottom trunk 
height l, need to be measured or estimated in advance. R, H, α are 
calculated as follows:

2.3. Tree panorama building 
After capturing the photos of fruit trees, these photos were stitched to 
form a panorama and panorama will be used in detection and fruit 
counting. First, feature points were extracted to match the images. The 
algorithms such as SIFT, SURF, and FAST are commonly used to detect 
and extract feature points. Given the crown texture and color were 
similar over a large region and there were differences in brightness 
between images taken under backlighting and light ward, the SIFT 
(Lowe, 2004) was selected in this experiment since it can maintain 
robustness to changes in brightness, rotation, and scale scaling during 
shooting process, and also maintain a certain degree of stability to 
perspective changes, affine transformations, and noises. After the 
feature points were extracted, images were stitched to form a panorama, 
which includes the following main steps (Brown and Lowe, 2007):  
(1) Cluster images in the same scene. The K-nearest neighbors is used 
to search the m (m = 6) candidates with the most features 
matching each image. And then RANSAC is performed to 
compute the homography, and a probabilistic model is invoked to 
verify the image match based on the number of inliers.  
(2) Correct spatial consistency of multiple images. Using bundle 
adjustment to calculate parameters of all cameras, and then im­
ages were added to the bundle adjuster one by one, with the best 
matching image being added at each step.  
(3) Perform straightening, gain compensation, and multi-band 
blending on the stitched panorama. The stitched panorama was 
shown in Fig. 2.

in lighting conditions, camera shooting angles and spatial scales 
(determined by the target), the objects are covered, shadowed,

The Mosaic and MixUp generated samples are not sufficiently realistic. 
In the experiment, besides the Mosaic and MixUp samples enhancement,

2.4.1. Simulation of tiny and blurred samples 
Obviously, fruits at the edges of the field of view are tiny and blurred 
on the image. These tiny and blurred fruits are likely to be unrecog­

taken at a long distance (surround radius (m), R ∈[2, 2.5]); (2) Gaussian 
(Gs) convolution kernels and down sampling were used to construct 
triple-scale spatial samples to model fruits in different distance as the 
Fig. 3(a) shows. 
2.4.2. Enhancement of shaded, blurred samples 
Under natural illumination, there are many fruits in the shadows, 
these samples are required to be enhanced. In the test, (1) a 2D Gaussian 
matrix was used to convolve a few normal samples to make them blur­
red; (2) then, γ component adjustment was done on the blurred samples 
to change the ratio of light and dark parts to enhance the dark parts as 
the Fig. 3(b) shows.

2.5. Object detection model 
YOLOX (Ge et al., 2021) takes YOLOv3-SPP (Howard et al., 2017) 
and Darknet53 (Redmon and Farhadi, 2018a) as the baseline, and in­
tegrated anchor-free detectors, SimOTA label assignment strategy, and 
decoupled head. Its structure is illustrated in Fig. 4. It takes CSPDar­
knet53 as the backbone feature extraction network. Focusnet (Gao et al., 
2019) is used in backbone to allow the network to reduce the width and 
height of the input image and stack the information into the channels, 
improving the efficiency of information abstraction. The PAnet structure 
(Liu et al., 2018) is used in strengthened feature extraction network in 
YOLOX, which can not only up-sample the features, but also down- 
sample the features again to achieve feature fusion. Three feature 
output layers are obtained from the strengthened feature extraction 
network, each of them will obtain three predictions in the classifier and 
regressor (YOLO Head) to predict whether there is an object or not at 
each feature point in the feature layer, the class of the object, and the 
location parameters of the object. 
The YOLOX training is based on the Pytorch framework (version 
1.11). The hardware for training was a desktop computer running with 
36 GB of RAM, an AMD R5 5600X CPU (3.7 GHz), and an Nvidia RTX (8 
GB) graphics card. The training input was the holly fruit samples set 
(image size: 640 × 640), and the prediction input was the panorama 
(image size is not fixed). The panorama cannot be directly used for 
prediction due to its size. So, an input processing module for panorama 
which includes segmentation functions was added before the prediction 
and the prediction results were projected on the panorama. The seg­
mentation parameters can be adjusted to avoid the fruit being 
segmented (Image size: 640 × 640, can be slightly adjusted).

3.3. Comparisons with other methods 
3.3.1. Color-morphology method 
As mentioned in section 1, Color Space and Morphological methods 
are used as traditional method. In this experiment, an comparative 
experiment was carried out to test the color-morphology method. First 
the panorama was segmented from the complex backgrounds using 
color space, then morphologically processed and finally counted using 
Laplace operators. The circular Hough Transform wasn’t in consider­
ation due to the possible irregularity of the covered fruit contours. 
Comparison was made between the Color-Morphology method and the 
deep learning network on the holly tree surface panorama. The results 
are in Table 4 below: 
In Fig. 10, a comparison of YOLOX and Color-Morphology method 
counting performance are shown. As seen from the figure, the Color- 
Morphology method has more omissions. In addition, the contours of 
the fruits by Color-Morphology method are not precise enough. From 
the perspective of feature types, the Color-Morphology method recog­
nizes fruits mainly based on color, and contours. The variety of features 
that YOLOX can extract is much richer than Color-Morphology, which is

4. Discussion 
4.1. Analysis of counting loss 
As shown in Fig. 9 in Section 3.2.2, although the model has a high 
detection rate for fruits in extreme scenarios, there is still a few missing 
at the current confidence(confidence = 0.5) threshold. As seen in Fig. 10, 
these missed fruits are very difficult to identify even by the human eye. 
The reasons for the missing fruits can generally be summarized as 
incomplete fruit contours, fake pixel values, insufficient pixels, and 
mutual interference between highly similar targets. 
As shown in Fig. 14, both covered and mutilated fruits are considered 
to be incomplete in contour. This is detrimental to the prediction of 
object detection networks which are trained from mostly standard fruit 
samples. In the Fig. 14a1 the two identified fruits are very close in color 
and illumination, but the #2 fruit has a more complete contour than the 
#1 fruit and therefore gets a higher confidence score. In Fig. 14b, a 
similar phenomenon is observed. In Fig. 14a, the incomplete contour of 
the fruit may not be the main factor of unrecognized, as it can be 
observed that the integrity of the contour exceeds that of some of the 
identified fruits at the edges in Fig. 14b. However, owing to the rela­
tively severe shadow covering and insufficient effective pixels, the 
extracted features contain overly shadow information so that fruit in­
formation is overwhelmed. 
Both shadows and blurs can be considered as a loss of the true color 
of the object. The effect of shadows and blurs can be reduced by opti­
mizing the sample composition, but can’t absolutely eliminated. Obvi­
ously, the most significant difference between the numbered #1 and #2 
fruit and the unidentified fruit in Fig. 14c1 is the inconsistency in the 
degree of shadow. The unidentified fruit in Fig. 14c1 is also affected by 
the size, which can be found by comparing it with the fruit in Fig. 14c2,

Area of fruits that far from the camera is small. If the area is too 
small, the convolution calculation and pooling calculation can’t extract 
enough object information in the local area, and the background will 
dominate that area, thus reducing the probability of the fruit being 
recognized. This makes it impossible to be identified as a “fruit” under 
high confidence. As shown in Fig. 14e, the unidentified fruits are tiny 
and surrounded by a large number of leaves, and the fruit is 
overwhelmed. 
Aggregate adherent fruit with various contours seems to have less 
effect on detection and positioning. In fact, the identification of the fruit 
by the network are based on multiple information, such as color, contour 
shape, and texture. Despite the fruit contour adhere with each other, the 
localization method of the neural network with the help of the difference 
in color of the adherent area can properly discriminant and accurately 
count the clustered fruits. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 14f, the vast 
majority can be identified with a reasonable sample composition. 
4.2. Confidence adjustment and prediction intervention. 
Due to the filtering mechanism by the parameter Confidence, the 
network does not recognize object with Confidence < 0.5 as a fruit. 
However, for a single class of objects with distinct characteristics, pre­
dictions with lower confidence may be feasible. For example, in this 
experiment, the holly berries are spherical and red, the shape and the 
color are distinguishable from the surroundings. More objects can be 
identified as fruits if the confidence threshold is lower as shown in the 
Fig. 15b. 
Another feasible way to improve the detection rate is to intervene the 
prediction process of the network by parametrically adjusting the pre­
diction matrix. The basic control strategies include:  
(1) the signal characteristic of the input matrix is Y = (X + e) γ. A 
correction matrix is calculated based on the signal characteristics 
of the original matrix to compensate for the γ component and 
adjust the signal ratio of the high-frequency signal to the low- 
frequency.  
(2) Using a 2D Gaussian kernel to convolve with the input matrix, 
each element of the matrix is weighted and averaged with the 
surrounding elements to spread the information of each element. 
The parameter optimization matrix is set by equal gradients, and the 
prediction matrix is optimized in 10 iterations. The most identified one 
from the multiple prediction results is selected as the final result, and the 
prediction results are mapped to the original prediction samples. The 
effect of various strategies on the detection rate is illustrated in Fig. 15. 
γ-component compensation and Gaussian kernel convolution have a 
positive effect on shaded and blurred samples. 
5. Conclusion 
In this paper, we proposed a convolutional neural network based 
panoramic fruits counting method and was validated on holly fruit. 
First, a DJI Mavic 2pro UAV was used to take images of tree surface by 
circular route, and SIFT feature points were extracted in these images 
and used for stitching to form an unfolded panorama of tree surface. 
Then, a YOLOX object detection network was built, and a sample 
augmentation and composition strategy specific to the actual scenes was 
designed to prepare dataset for the training of the network. Finally, the 
fruits were predicted and counted on the panorama. The counting

efficiency at different distances and scenarios were tested. Results sug­

gies is an effective method for dataset preparation as it enable the 
network to achieve high and consistent detection rate with distance 
increase, and maintain robustness of the model to scenes such as shad­
owing, contour fragmentation, adhesion, covering, blurring, and long 
distance. (3) We tested the performance of other methods on the 
panorama, and found that both methods did not achieve higher detec­
tion rates than YOLOX. The reasons for the loss of counts in extreme 
scenes are discussed. (4) 2 approaches to improve detection rate were 
discussed: ① proper Confidence reduction may improve the detection 
rate and the results are reliable due to its singularity and high differ­
entiation from the background. ② Gaussian convolution and γ-compo­
nent compensation have positive effects on the detection rate 
improvement. 
In summary, the proposed method is effective and the result is 
impressive. It can enable complete and accurate fruits counting of trees 
and has strong implications for other species fruits as well. Moreover, as 
a single-stage method, YOLOX has better inferencing efficiency and has 
good potential for real-time application in orchard and plantation 
management.
