Abstract: Time to maturity and yield are important traits for highbush blueberry (Vaccinium corym-
bosum) breeding. Proper determination of the time to maturity of blueberry varieties and breeding
lines informs the harvest window, ensuring that the fruits are harvested at optimum maturity and
quality. On the other hand, high-yielding crops bring in high profits per acre of planting. Harvesting
and quantifying the yield for each blueberry breeding accession are labor-intensive and impractical.
Instead, visual ratings as an estimation of yield are often used as a faster way to quantify the yield,
which is categorical and subjective. In this study, we developed and shared a high-throughput
phenotyping method using neural networks to predict blueberry time to maturity and to provide
a proxy for yield, overcoming the labor constraints of obtaining high-frequency data. We aim to
facilitate further research in computer vision and precision agriculture by publishing the labeled
image dataset and the trained model. In this research, true-color images of blueberry bushes were
collected, annotated, and used to train a deep neural network object detection model [You Only Look
Once (YOLOv11)] to detect mature and immature berries. Different versions of YOLOv11 were used,
including nano, small, and medium, which had similar performance, while the medium version
had slightly higher metrics. The YOLOv11m model shows strong performance for the mature berry
class, with a precision of 0.90 and an F1 score of 0.90. The precision and recall for detecting immature
berries were 0.81 and 0.79. The model was tested on 10 blueberry bushes by hand harvesting and
weighing blueberries. The results showed that the model detects approximately 25% of the berries on
the bushes, and the correlation coefficients between model-detected and hand-harvested traits were
0.66, 0.86, and 0.72 for mature fruit count, immature fruit count, and mature ratio, respectively. The
model applied to 91 blueberry advance selections and categorized them into groups with diverse
levels of maturity and productivity using principal component analysis (PCA). These results inform
the harvest window and yield of these breeding lines with precision and objectivity through berry
classification and quantification. This model will be helpful for blueberry breeders, enabling more
efficient selection, and for growers, helping them accurately estimate optimal harvest windows. This
open-source tool can potentially enhance research capabilities and agricultural productivity.

1. Introduction
Blueberry (V. corymbosum sect. Cyanococcus) is a high-value fruit crop, contributing
approximately USD 4.7 billion to the U.S. economy in 2020 [1]. The increasing consumer
appreciation for blueberries—due to their flavor, versatility, and health benefits—has
driven a 97% rise in per capita blueberry consumption over the past decade [2]. This
growing demand continues to boost blueberry production across the country and elsewhere.
The primary cultivated blueberry types in the U.S. include northern highbush (NHB,
2n = 4x = 48), southern highbush (SHB, 2n = 4x = 48), lowbush (LB, 2n = 4x = 48), and
rabbiteye (RE, 2n = 6x = 72). The NHB and LB, both native to North America, require high
chilling hours and thrive in the cooler climates of the northwest and northeast regions.
The SHB, on the other hand, was developed by introgressing the low chill requirement
from several diploid and hexaploid species, including V. darrowii, V. fuscatum, V. elliottii,
V. pallidum, V. virgatum (formerly V. ashei), into the NHB varieties [3,4]. These cultivated
blueberries exhibit significant diversity in yield, phenological development, adaptability to
soil and weather, fruit quality, and plant morphology.
One of the primary goals of the blueberry breeding programs at the University of
Georgia and North Carolina State University is to develop SHB varieties that are both high-
yielding and have staggering harvest windows. Early maturing varieties provide growers
with a valuable market advantage by allowing them to tap into premium pricing. However,
the high volume of imported blueberries during the U.S. southeastern harvest season incurs
market price competition. Between April and May 2024, USD 200 M of fresh blueberries was
imported into the U.S. [5]. Developing SHB blueberries with staggering harvest windows
will mitigate the risk of price crashes due to the high volume of production conflicting
with the imported blueberries. Therefore, extending the production season by developing
varieties with staggering maturity times is critical for sustaining the competitiveness
of the U.S. blueberry industry. This is particularly beneficial for growers in Georgia,
North Carolina, and Florida by offering greater resilience to rising temperatures and
unpredictable weather patterns [6] (Ashrafi Pers. Comm.). However, as with many breeding
programs, blueberry phenotyping faces challenges due to labor and time constraints.
Efficient and high-throughput phenotyping methods are critically needed to assist the
proper selection of breeding progenies to advance them to the next stage. Various high-
throughput phenotyping techniques have been employed to enhance data quality and
consistency in blueberry breeding to characterize multiple traits. For example, the canopy
architecture traits of blueberry bushes were analyzed using Unmanned Aerial Systems
(UASs) and point cloud construction [7]. The yield estimation of lowbush blueberries was
achieved through the blue pixel index, which was extracted from RGB images captured
by cameras mounted on a motorized vehicle [8]. Additionally, an instant segmentation
algorithm based on Mask R-CNN [a type of Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)] was
developed to accurately detect blueberry clusters from images taken with mobile devices.
Lobos et al. (2019) [9] explored the use of leaf-level reflectance to assess physiological
traits in five Vaccinium cultivars under different stress conditions, and partial least square
modeling provided good trait estimates and demonstrated the potential of reflectance-
based tools for assessing physiological traits under environmental stresses in blueberry
breeding programs. Furthermore, hyperspectral reflectance imaging was utilized for the
non-destructive detection of internal bruising in harvested berries [10]. Collectively, these
high-throughput systems facilitate the quantification of phenotypic traits in blueberries,
significantly accelerating the process of breeding line selection. However, there are limited
or no reports on high-throughput yield and fruit maturing prediction tools.
Blueberry time to maturity and yield are important traits for both growers and breed-
ers. Developing high-yielding cultivars with targeted maturing time suitable to the local
growing environment is a priority for breeding programs. Growers need to harvest within
the optimal time window to ensure the fruit’s highest quality and desirable maturity. Har-
vesting too early can lead to excessive removal of green fruits, whereas harvesting too late
results in overripened fruits that compromise the overall fruit quality [11]. Cultivars with

concentrated ripening time are desirable for growers who adopted mechanical harvesting,
as they can enhance the profitability of the harvesting process. However, estimating both
yield and time to maturity in blueberries presents challenges, as berries on the same bush
do not ripen simultaneously during the harvest season. Blueberry time to maturity can be
approximated by the ratio of purple to green fruits, with a high ratio indicating a greater
level of maturity. Previously, true-color images, also referred to as RGB (red, green, and
blue) images, were used to classify blueberry fruit stages. Among the four tested classifiers,
the color component analysis-based detection was identified as the most effective method
for distinguishing blueberry developmental stages [12]. Recently, advancements in artificial
intelligence have enhanced object detection capabilities [13]. Deep neural networks have
been trained to estimate berry ripeness and yield [14–19]. Previous research has primarily
focused on developing novel model architectures [17,19,20] or exploring their potential
applications in production systems [15,16]. Although research has proved that deep neural
networks can do an excellent job counting objects [21], the model for counting blueberries
with the goal of yield prediction and determining time to maturity is unavailable for small
breeding programs.
Advancements in computing resources and the availability of foundational models
have greatly empowered practitioners in object counting and detection tasks [22]. High-
performance hardware, such as GPUs and cloud-based platforms, enable efficient training
and deployment of complex models. Additionally, pre-trained models, such as those based
on YOLO, Mask R-CNN, and other state-of-the-art architectures, provide a strong starting
point for customization. These foundational models allow practitioners to leverage transfer
learning [23], significantly reducing the time and effort required to train their models for
specific applications, in our case, counting blueberries. With the increasing availability
of resources for object detection and counting, human labeling has become a significant
component of projects like ours [21,24]. To streamline this process and reduce redundancy,
we aim to publish our labeled dataset, making it accessible to researchers and practitioners.
By sharing these data, we hope to facilitate collaboration, enhance model training efficiency,
and enable others to build on our work without the need for extensive manual labeling.
This will accelerate research and contribute to the broader understanding of blueberry
phenotyping and yield estimation.
In this study, we adapted the YOLOv11 model architecture for blueberry phenotyping,
focusing on predicting yield and estimating time to maturity within two breeding programs.
We developed a high-throughput phenotyping pipeline to support these objectives. Our
goals are threefold: (1) to create and publish a labeled image dataset for integration
into computer vision and machine learning projects; (2) to develop a robust pipeline
for yield prediction and time-to-maturity estimation; and (3) to make the model publicly
available for breeders, particularly those with small-sized budgets. This model will enable
blueberry breeders to optimize the selection of high-yielding, early maturing varieties,
and growers can use it to estimate optimal harvest windows, improving decision making
and productivity.
2. Materials and Methods
Study Locations and Description of the Blueberry Bushes. Images were collected
initially at the UGA Alapaha research farm, Alapaha, GA (31.3852◦N, 83.2229◦W, Figure 1).
The blueberry breeding population at this location consists of 91 SHB breeding lines from
27 independent crossing families, selected based on the adaptability of the local growing
environment and fruit quality. The advanced selections were clonally propagated by
softwood cuttings and transplanted in 2018 and 2019. To generalize the model, another
set of images was collected at Horticultural Crop Research Station Ideal Field located in
Castle Hayne, NC (34.3648◦N, 77.8387◦W). A mechanical harvest trial of advanced SHB
breeding selections was planted in 10–11 plant plots 1 m apart with 3 m row centers in
October–November of 2019 and 2020.

Image Collection, Curation, and Annotation. In Alapaha, true-color images of the
population were collected on May 12 and 23 of 2023 using a handheld digital camera
(Panasonic, Lumix DMC-LX10) with a resolution of 5472 × 3648. In Castle Hayne, true-
color images were collected on the yield trial on 19 May 2024, using a digital camera
(Canon EOS Rebel T7) with a resolution of 6000 × 4000. The operator stood approximately
1 m away from the target bush and held the camera facing the bush with the field of
vision centered on the middle of the canopy of the bush. All images were taken from one
side of the bushes. The weather conditions during image collection varied between clear
and overcast in both locations. Approximately 80 raw images from both locations were
split 5 × 3 into 15 cropped images for preparing the image dataset for training. Some
pictures were cropped, in a way that the dimensions after the cut were set equal to or
smaller than 1200 × 1200 pixels (Figure 2). Object detection models such as YOLO and
Mask R-CNN do not work well out of the box to detect small objects in high-resolution
images. One common way to address this problem is to slice the image into smaller tiles
before running inference [25]. The cropped images (~466) were then exported to Roboflow
(https://roboflow.com/, accessed on 8 December 2024) for annotation with two classes
(Figure 3): mature fruit (ripe) and immature fruit (green and pink). In Roboflow, Auto-
Orient and Resize (640 × 640 pixels) were applied to the images in preprocessing. The
following augmentations were then applied in Roboflow: flip horizontally, crop 0–20%,
rotation –15◦to = 15◦, saturation –25% to +25%, and blur up to 2.5 pixels. A total of
2686 images were exported for model training.
Model Training. Following augmentation, the dataset containing 2686 images was
split into training (80%), validation (14%), and test (6%) sets. The dataset was exported
and used to train three versions of YOLOv11 models: nano, small, and medium (using
Ultralytics 8.3.13 [26] in PyTorch 2.4.0 [27]). The training was conducted on a workstation
computer equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti GPU. The YOLOv11 trainer
ran for 30 epochs with a batch size 8 and an image size of 680 (Figure S1). A confidence
threshold of 0.4 was chosen after examining the relationship between precision and recall as
the confidence threshold changes (Figure S2). To evaluate the performance of three models
(nano, small, and medium) on the validation and test datasets, metrics including precision,
recall, and F1 score were calculated using Equations (1)–(3) [28].

Model Application. To evaluate the efficacy of the model for phenotyping blueberry
breeding accessions, 455 images of individual bushes from 91 blueberry breeding genotypes
were taken on 12 May 2023, with each accession having five bushes. The object detection
model was applied to detect mature and immature berries from these images. Mature fruit
ratio was computed by dividing the number of mature fruits by total fruits counts. For
each image, a visual rating of mature fruit ratio was given by a trained technician on a scale
of 1 to 6, with 1 being 0% mature fruit, 3 being 40% mature fruit, and 6 being 100% mature
fruit. This scale is used in the UGA blueberry breeding program to rate the maturity ratio.
Visual ratings from two images per line were averaged. The correlation was determined
between model-based and visually rated mature fruit ratios using Pearson correlation in R.
Principal component analysis was conducted on fruit counts and mature ratios from both
model prediction and visual ratings using package FactoMineR [30], and the associated
graphs were made using package factoextra [31] in R.
Availability of the Labeled Images and Model. Labeled images are published in
Zenodo [32], an open access repository supported by the European Organization for
Nuclear Research (CERN). The model is available on GitHub [33]. To optimize the model’s
effectiveness, it is suggested that images be captured on overcast days or using polarized
lenses to minimize glare and maintain a consistent distance from the bushes. Depending
on the camera specification, images are suggested to be cropped into 700×700 pixels for
inference. A Graphical User Interface (GUI) is being developed for running the model on
counting blueberries for a single image and for a batch of images. Once developed, it will
be published on GitHub.
3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Model Performance on the Validation and Test Datasets
Metrics, including precision, recall, and F1 score, indicated similar performance from
three models, with YOLO11m having slightly better performance (Table 1). The model
shows strong performance for the mature berry class, correctly identifying 91% of ripe

7 of 14
blueberries, with precision ranging from 0.85 to 0.90 and F1 score ranging from 0.88 to
0.90. The model correctly identifies 72–79% of the green berries, with precision ranging
from 0.78 to 0.84 and F1 score ranging from 0.76 to 0.80. Our precision in detecting mature
berries is comparable to another study, in which similar images were used to train an
improved YOLOv8 model to achieve 0.86 precision in detecting ripe blueberries [34].
This demonstrated that the model performs well in identifying mature berries with high
precision and minimal misclassification, demonstrating its potential for practical application
in detecting ripe blueberries. While there are some challenges in distinguishing green
berries from the background, the model still performs reasonably well in those categories.
The model can be even more effective with further refinement, particularly in enhancing the
distinction between green berry and background. Although other methods, such as color
component analysis [12] and HOG-SVM classifiers [35], may report higher precision, YOLO-
based models offer key advantages in speed, efficiency, and automatic feature extraction.

3.2. Model Testing
The results in Table 2 highlight the model’s ability to detect blueberries across
10 different breeding lines. On average, the model detected 25% mature berries, rang-
ing from 16% to 34%, and 26% immature berries, with a range of 12% to 44%. The model’s
predictions correlated well with hand-harvested data, showing an r value of 0.66 for mature
berries and 0.86 for immature (green) berries. Several factors may have impacted the
model’s performance, including images capturing only one side of the bush and some
berries being obscured by leaves. Variations in detection across the breeding lines could be
attributed to genotypic differences in growth stage, canopy architecture, and foliage density.
Occlusion by other objects in blueberry fruit detection has been a common issue reported
previously [36]. The average mature/immature ratio detected by the model (0.84) was
closely aligned with the hand-harvested data (0.84). Additionally, a significant correlation
(r = 0.72) between model-detected and hand-harvested mature ratios highlights the model’s
potential for accurately estimating fruit maturity, particularly in production systems with
limited genotypic variation.
Since only one image was taken for each bush, the berry detection by the model
was limited to the area covered by the image, whereas the hand-harvested berry count
included all berries produced by the bush. As a result, only about 25% of the berries were
captured through image analysis. This percentage is important information and can serve
as a reference and baseline for further improvements in yield prediction. Despite this
limitation, the strong correlation for both mature and immature berries indicates that 2D
images taken with handheld cameras can reliably estimate blueberry yield and differentiate
the productivity of various blueberry breeding lines. In this study, it took about 2 h for
three people to hand-harvest 30,815 berries from the ten bushes, equating to an average of
12 min for one person to harvest 1000 berries. One of the greatest challenges in blueberry

Horticulturae 2024, 10, 1332
8 of 14
breeding programs is the limited availability of labor during the fruiting season. The high
labor demand required for manual berry harvesting and yield evaluation makes collecting
data for all breeding lines in the program impractical. Therefore, breeders often rely on a
visual scale of 1 to 10, with 1 being the least desirable and 10 being the most desirable, to
assess fruit load on the bushes [37]. The main advantage of using single-view images in
blueberry phenotyping is that it significantly reduces the time and resources needed for
image collection and processing while still providing reasonably accurate results. Although
multi-view imaging improves the detection accuracy by 5.2% to 15.7% [36], single-view
images can still achieve reliable maturity and yield estimates, especially in environments
with consistent canopy architecture and minimal occlusion. This approach is particularly
instrumental when rapid assessments are needed in large-scale production or breeding
programs. Imaging analysis provides a high-throughput, objective alternative to determine
crop load, accelerating the selection process in breeding programs.

3.3. Model Application for Mature Fruit Ratio and Harvest Window in Breeding Lines
We further applied fruit detection on 91 breeding advanced selections to detect differ-
ential fruit yield and maturation. A large variation in the fruit yield across the breeding
genotypes was detected, which was influenced by yield potential and the phenological
development of each advanced selection (Figure 4). The histograms present insights into
the distribution of fruit counts and maturity ratios detected by the model and visual
ratings across different blueberry bushes (Figure 4). The distribution of immature fruit
counts is right-skewed (Figure 4A), with most bushes having fewer than 100 immature
fruits. However, a few outliers show higher counts. In contrast, the mature fruit count
is more symmetrically distributed (Figure 4B), with most bushes producing between 100
and 400 mature fruits. The mature ratio shows that most bushes have a high proportion
of mature fruits (Figure 4C), with ratios clustering between 0.6 and 0.9, indicating favor-
able maturity levels. The visual ratings are discrete data (Figure 4D), which may not be
adequate to capture the full spectrum of variations among the breeding lines. In contrast,
quantitative data derived from measurements such as fruit counts and maturity ratios
offer a more comprehensive understanding of blueberry phenotyping, allowing breeders
to make informed decisions based on precise metrics rather than subjective assessments.

Significant correlations among several variables related to fruit counts, maturity ratios,
and visual ratings were found (Figure 5). A strong positive and significant correlation
between total fruit count and both mature fruit count (r = 0.90, p < 0.05) and immature
fruit count (r = 0.84, p < 0.05) was observed. The correlation between visual ratings and the
mature ratio was also strong (r = 0.84, p < 0.05). Conversely, there were strong negative
correlations between visual ratings and both immature fruit count (r = −0.80, p < 0.05) and
total fruit count (r = −0.61, p < 0.05). Using PCA based on these traits, we investigated
the contributions of each trait, and the new variable 1, which explains 71% of the variance,
is largely influenced by mature, immature, and total fruit count (Figure 6A). Both visual
ratings and model-predicted mature ratios are negatively associated with the new variable
2, which explains 23.2% of the variance. We clustered genotypes based on the PCA results
into four groups (Figure 6B) and computed the averages of the traits for each group (Table 3).
Approximately 28% of the genotypes were grouped into Cluster 1, characterized by a high
mature ratio but a low fruit count. These lines, categorized as early maturing, likely had
most of their ripened fruits drop to the ground and go undetected, with the image analysis
capturing only the remaining ripened fruits on the bushes. Imaging these genotypes earlier
could provide a more apparent characterization of their productivity. In contrast, Cluster 3
(24% of the genotypes) had a lower mature ratio (0.63) but a high fruit count, indicating
that these genotypes are entering their optimal harvest window and exhibiting strong
productivity. Clusters 2 and 4 showed medium fruit counts but differed in mature ratio.
Genotypes in Cluster 4 are likely late maturing, with a lower mature ratio (0.55), further

seasons will provide insights into the environmental impacts on the blueberry yield and

3.4. Limitations of the Model and Future Direction for Improvement
The model has some limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting
the results. First, the training images were collected exclusively from southern highbush
blueberry plants, approximately 4 to 5 years old, and taken in May. This may limit
the model’s performance when applied to different blueberry types, such as rabbiteye

Horticulturae 2024, 10, 1332
12 of 14
or northern highbush varieties, particularly at various growth stages. Additionally, the
model’s detection capabilities only capture a fraction of the total yield (~25%), making it
useful in production systems where canopy architectures and growth stages are uniform.
While images taken in direct sunlight were included in the training set, the model struggled
to identify berries affected by sun glare. Using a polarized filter may help reduce this
glare and improve detection accuracy. A few image collection considerations could be
implemented to optimize the model’s effectiveness, such as capturing images on overcast
days, using polarized lenses to minimize glare, and maintaining a consistent distance from
the bushes.
Nevertheless, this open access model offers blueberry breeders an image analysis tool
that can classify and count immature and mature blueberries in a high-throughput and
objective manner. For breeding programs hosting hundreds or even thousands of breeding
lines, implementing this new model will replace conventional categorical visual ratings of
yield and berry developmental stages by actual berry counts. This valuable information on
productivity and maturity will improve the precision of selecting high-yielding and early
maturing varieties. In addition, the growers will be able to properly gauge the harvest
windows of their fields using this image analysis tool. Future improvements will focus on
expanding the model’s adaptability to diverse blueberry types and environmental condi-
tions, enhancing its utility across a broader range of applications. In addition, developing
an automated image acquisition system would streamline the entire phenotyping workflow,
significantly improving the efficiency of data collection.
4. Conclusions
In this study, we developed and shared a high-throughput phenotyping method using
neural networks to predict blueberry time to maturity and to provide a proxy for yield.
By publishing both the labeled image dataset and the trained model, we aim to facilitate
further research in computer vision and precision agriculture. The dataset provided can be
easily integrated into other machine learning models, broadening its utility across related
fields. Moreover, our model offers practical applications for blueberry breeders, enabling
more efficient selection and for growers, helping them accurately estimate optimal harvest
windows. This open-source resource can potentially enhance research capabilities and
agricultural productivity. However, the generalizability of the model is limited by the
diversity of the training dataset, which may not fully capture the variability in different
regions or conditions. Efforts should prioritize dataset expansion and diversity, ensuring
broader representation of environments and cultivars to improve robustness. Collabora-
tion with breeders and growers is also essential to ensure the model addresses practical
challenges and integrates seamlessly into existing agricultural workflows. Additionally,
optimizing the model for edge computing and deployment can enable real-time, on-farm
decision making.
