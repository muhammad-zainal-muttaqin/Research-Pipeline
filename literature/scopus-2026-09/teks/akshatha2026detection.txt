From detection to identity preservation: A
pipeline–scenario–protocol–readiness review of datasets for visual
multi-person tracking and re-identiﬁcation
Shavantrevva Bilakeri1 · Manisha2 · K. R. Akshatha1
Received: 27 April 2026 / Accepted: 8 August 2026 / Published online: 27 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026, modiﬁed publication 2026
Abstract
Multi-person tracking (MPT) requires visual systems to localize people in video frames and preserve their identities over
time. Although public datasets have advanced person detection, person re-identiﬁcation (Re-ID), person search, and tracking,
the benchmark ecosystem remains fragmented across task deﬁnitions, annotation formats, visual scenarios, and evaluation
protocols. Existing reviews mainly summarize algorithms or benchmark performance, but provide limited guidance on how
different dataset families support the complete tracking-by-detection pipeline. This review analyzes 37 public datasets intro-
duced or used between January 2005 and April 2026, with a ﬁnal veriﬁcation update conducted on 9 June 2026. The datasets
include 8 detection-support datasets, 9 person Re-ID/person-search datasets, and 20 core tracking benchmarks spanning
single-camera, multi-camera, crowded-scene, driving, sports, long-term, and synthetic tracking settings. To move beyond
catalogue-style listing, we introduce a Pipeline–Scenario–Protocol–Readiness (PSPR) framework that characterizes datasets
according to pipeline role, scenario complexity, evaluation protocol, deployment readiness, and benchmark limitations. The
review shows that dataset choice strongly shapes what a tracking system can and cannot evaluate: detection datasets mainly
test localization, Re-ID datasets support appearance-based identity modelling, and tracking benchmarks differ in whether
they stress association ambiguity, crowd density, camera motion, cross-camera re-entry, or long-term identity preservation.
By linking dataset families to tracking failure modes, evaluation protocols, and deployment scenarios, PSPR provides a
practical guide for benchmark selection and interpretation. The curated dataset metadata, search strings, screening table,
PSPR annotations, and supplementary benchmark resources are publicly available at https://github.com/ssbilakeri/pspr-mpt-
dataset-review and archived on Zenodo at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20823270.

This fragmented dataset ecosystem creates several chal-

useful for detector pretraining, some for identity represen-

Second, their visual scenarios vary substantially, ranging

footage, sports videos, dense crowds, synthetic scenes, and
long-tail open-world videos [2–4, 28, 30–32]. Third, their
annotation richness and evaluation protocols are inconsis-
tent. Detection datasets typically report average precision or
miss rate, re-identiﬁcation datasets use Rank-1 accuracy and
mean average precision, while tracking benchmarks report
metrics such as MOTA, IDF1, HOTA, DetA, AssA, iden-
tity switches, and fragmentation. These differences make it
difﬁcult to compare datasets directly or interpret benchmark
scores fairly.
Existing reviews and dataset summaries often organize
resources by task type or application domain. While use-
ful, such organization does not fully explain how datasets
contribute to the complete MPT pipeline. For example, a
detection dataset may improve localization accuracy but can-
not evaluate long-term identity preservation [9, 14, 15]. A
re-identiﬁcation dataset may improve appearance embed-
dings but may not represent online tracking failures caused
by missed detections, occlusion, and track fragmentation [19,
22, 23]. Similarly, a tracking benchmark may test crowd
density, nonlinear motion, cross-camera association, or long-
term re-entry depending on its design [2, 27–29]. Therefore, a
review of MPT datasets should not only list dataset statistics,
but also analyze what each dataset can and cannot evaluate.
To address this need, this paper presents a uniﬁed tax-
onomy and benchmark-oriented review of public datasets
relevant to visual multi-person tracking and re-identiﬁcation.
Unlike catalogue-style dataset surveys, the review orga-
nizes datasets from a tracking-by-detection perspective
and analyzes their role in detector training, appearance
modelling, data association, trajectory construction, and
benchmark evaluation. The review further introduces a
Pipeline–Scenario–Protocol–Readiness (PSPR) framework
to interpret datasets according to their pipeline role, visual
scenario complexity, evaluation protocol, deployment rele-
vance, and benchmark limitations. This framework is then
used to compare representative detection-support datasets,
re-identiﬁcation datasets, UAV and aerial datasets, and core
tracking benchmarks.
Dataset design for identity-preserving multi-person track-
ing is also a broader visual computing problem, not only a
benchmark-selection issue in computer vision. The useful-
ness of a dataset depends on how visual data are acquired,
annotated, rendered or simulated, interpreted, and eval-
uated in relation to real deployment settings. This con-
nects MPT datasets to wider visual computing concerns,
including controllable synthetic data generation, neural and
renderedscenerepresentations, multimodal sensing, privacy-
preserving visual analytics, human-centered monitoring, and
responsible deployment in public or semi-public environ-
ments. From this perspective, dataset design shapes not only
algorithmic performance, but also how visual systems rep-
resent people, support interaction, handle uncertainty, and
generalize across real-world conditions.
The main contributions of this review are as follows:
• This review proposes a uniﬁed taxonomy for public
datasets used in visual multi-person tracking, organiz-
ing them into core tracking benchmarks, auxiliary person
detection datasets, and person re-identiﬁcation or person-
search datasets.
• It introduces a Pipeline–Scenario–Protocol–Readiness
framework for analyzing datasets according to their func-
tional role in the tracking pipeline, dominant visual
challenges, evaluation protocol, deployment relevance,
and limitations.
• It provides an analytical comparison of representative
datasets, focusing on their strengths, weaknesses, suit-
ability for tracking-by-detection pipelines, and limita-
tions for evaluating real-world identity persistence.
• It presents a protocol-aware discussion of benchmark
interpretation, highlighting how detector quality, re-
identiﬁcationsupervision,associationdesign,crowdden-
sity, camera motion, and metric choice inﬂuence reported
tracking performance.
• It identiﬁes key gaps in the current dataset ecosystem,
including limited long-term identity evaluation, frag-
mented metric protocols, insufﬁcient adverse-condition
coverage, limited multimodal annotations, and weak sup-
port for open-world and deployment-ready tracking.

1.1 Application domains and pipeline components
Multi-person tracking lies at the intersection of three closely
related visual computing tasks as shown in Fig.1: object
detection, person re-identiﬁcation, and temporal tracking.
Understanding these components is important because each
dataset family reviewed in this paper contributes differently
to the overall tracking pipeline.
1.1.1 Object detection
Object detection provides the frame-level localization
required by most tracking-by-detection systems. A detec-
tor identiﬁes persons or other objects in each video frame
and outputs bounding boxes, masks, or keypoints that serve
as inputs for downstream association. Detection quality
strongly affects tracking performance because missed detec-
tions can fragment trajectories, false positives can create spu-
rious tracks, and imprecise boxes can reduce the reliability of
appearance features [33–35]. Generic detection datasets such
as PASCAL VOC [8], MS COCO [9], ImageNet [10], Open
Images [11], Objects365 [12], and LVIS [13] are useful for
broad detector pretraining, while pedestrian-focused datasets
such as CrowdHuman [14], WiderPerson [15], CityPer-
sons [16], Caltech Pedestrian [17], and TinyPerson [18]
are more directly relevant to person tracking because they
emphasize occlusion, crowd density, scale variation, and
small pedestrian localization.
1.1.2 Person re-identiﬁcation
Person re-identiﬁcation aims to match the same individ-
ual across different camera views, time intervals, or video
segments. In MPT systems, re-identiﬁcation features are
commonly used to associate detections, recover identi-
ties after occlusion, and reduce identity switches [19, 22,
23]. Re-identiﬁcation datasets such as Market-1501 [19],
DukeMTMC-reID [20], CUHK03 [21], MSMT17 [22],
MARS [23], Occluded-DukeMTMC [24], and LS-VID [25]
have been widely used to train and evaluate appearance
embeddings. These datasets are important for identity mod-
elling, but they typically evaluate query-gallery retrieval
rather than complete online tracking. Therefore, strong
performance on a re-identiﬁcation benchmark does not nec-
essarily guarantee robust tracking when detections are noisy,
targets are heavily occluded, or trajectories are fragmented.
Recent advances in unsupervised and contrastive person
Re-ID further show that identity-discriminative appearance
representations can be improved without relying entirely on
exhaustive identity supervision. Such methods are relevant
to multi-person tracking because stronger Re-ID embed-
dings may improve association and identity recovery after
occlusion, although retrieval performance alone still does not
guarantee robust online tracking [36].
1.1.3 Multi-person tracking
Multi-person tracking combines detection, appearance mod-
elling, motion prediction, and data association to generate
continuous trajectories for multiple individuals. Tracking
benchmarks such as MOT15 [26], MOT16 and MOT17 [26],
MOT20 [2], DanceTrack [27], SportsMOT [4], BDD100K
[3],TAO[28],CityFlow[29],WildTrack[30],VisDrone[31],
and MOTSynth [32] provide video-level evaluation of tra-
jectory quality. However, these benchmarks differ in their
dominant challenges. MOT17 provides a general pedestrian
tracking baseline [26], MOT20 stresses dense crowd occlu-
sion[2],DanceTrackemphasizesassociationundernonlinear
motion and similar appearance [27], SportsMOT focuses on
sportsscenarioswithfastmovementandsimilaruniforms[4],
BDD100K reﬂects moving-camera driving scenes [3], and
TAO supports long-term and open-world tracking evalua-
tion [28].
2 Related works
Several surveys have reviewed multi-person tracking from
algorithmic, application-speciﬁc, or deep-learning perspec-
tives. These works provide valuable summaries of tracking
formulations, detector-association pipelines, deep feature
learning, motion modelling, and benchmark performance.
However, most existing surveys primarily organize the liter-
ature around tracking methods rather than around the dataset
ecosystem that supports multi-person tracking. As a result,
detection datasets, person re-identiﬁcation datasets, and core
tracking benchmarks are often discussed only as evaluation
resources or experimental settings, rather than as functionally
connected components of the tracking-by-detection pipeline.

public datasets used across the visual multi-person tracking

3.1 Scope and search strategy
The review focuses on publicly available datasets relevant
to multi-person tracking, with the goal of analyzing the
dataset ecosystem that supports person-centric tracking-by-
detection pipelines. To maintain a clear scope, the litera-
ture search was organized around three dataset groups: (i)
core multi-person tracking benchmarks, (ii) auxiliary person
detection datasets, and (iii) auxiliary person re-identiﬁcation
(Re-ID) or person-search datasets used for appearance mod-
eling and data association. This grouping reﬂects the struc-
tureofmoderntracking-by-detectionpipelines,whereperson
localization, identity representation, and trajectory associa-
tion are jointly responsible for tracking performance [5, 6,
19, 26].
Searches were conducted using combinations of key-
words such as multi-person tracking dataset, pedestrian
tracking benchmark, person detection dataset, pedestrian
detection benchmark, person re-identiﬁcation dataset, video
person Re-ID, and person search benchmark. These searches
were performed across major scholarly and benchmark
sources, including Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM
Digital Library, SpringerLink, CVF Open Access, arXiv,
and ofﬁcial dataset webpages when necessary to verify
release information, annotation protocols, and benchmark
splits.
The screening process was performed in two stages. First,
candidate papers were ﬁltered using titles and abstracts.
Second, full-text review was conducted on the benchmark
paper or ofﬁcial dataset source to determine whether the
resource satisﬁed the review scope. When multiple papers
described successive versions of the same benchmark fam-
ily, the canonical release paper or the most relevant ofﬁcial
benchmark paper was retained as the primary source. The
ﬁnal search update was conducted in June 2026, and the
ﬁnal review included 37 public datasets: 8 detection-support
datasets, 9 person re-identiﬁcation/person-search datasets,
and 20 core tracking benchmarks.

3.4 Data extraction and synthesis
After screening, the selected datasets were analyzed using a
structured extraction framework. Separate comparison tables
were created for core tracking datasets, auxiliary person
detection datasets, and auxiliary person Re-ID datasets,
so that each benchmark family could be compared using
attributes relevant to its functional role in the tracking
pipeline [44, 45].
For each core tracking dataset, the following details were
collected: task type, whether the benchmark is single-camera
or multi-camera, scene type, camera motion, temporal dura-
tion or continuity, number of identities, annotation type,
occlusion or re-entry difﬁculty, evaluation metrics supported,
and domain (e.g., surveillance, street, sports, campus, or
synthetic). These attributes are important because tracking
benchmarks differ substantially in the type of difﬁculty they
emphasize, such as crowd density in MOT20, association
ambiguity in DanceTrack, long-term tracking in TAO, and
synthetic domain variation in MOTSynth [2, 27, 28, 32].
For each auxiliary person detection dataset, the extracted
ﬁelds included whether the dataset is person-only or multi-
class, the type of box or mask annotations, crowd density,
scale variation, day/night/weather diversity, and the typi-
cal role of the dataset in tracking studies. These attributes
were selected because detector quality strongly affects down-
stream tracking performance in tracking-by-detection sys-
tems [9, 14–16].
For each auxiliary person Re-ID dataset, the extracted
ﬁelds included whether the dataset is image-based or video-
based, the number of identities, the number of cameras,
query-gallery or tracklet structure, occlusion or cross-camera
difﬁculty, and the typical role of the dataset in tracking
pipelines. These ﬁelds reﬂect the role of Re-ID datasets
in learning identity-discriminative appearance features for
association and identity recovery [19, 21–23].
The extracted information was then synthesized in two
steps. First, datasets were grouped into the proposed tax-
onomy to clarify their pipeline-level role in multi-person
tracking. Second, the resulting tables were used to support
the comparative analysis and gap discussion. This allowed
the review to go beyond simple dataset listing and instead
evaluate how existing resources differ in annotation design,
tracking difﬁculty, scene diversity, and practical suitability
for detector training, identity modeling, and ﬁnal trajectory
evaluation.
3.5 Proposed PSPR framework
Although existing reviews often classify visual datasets
according to task type, camera setting, or application domain,
such classiﬁcations do not fully explain how a dataset con-
tributes to the complete tracking-by-detection pipeline [44,
45]. The PSPR framework is introduced to convert these
dataset-level differences into a reusable analytical structure
for benchmark interpretation and dataset selection. There-
fore, a dataset-level review of multi-person tracking should
not only describe the basic statistics of datasets, but also clar-
ify where each dataset ﬁts within the broader visual tracking
pipeline, what type of tracking challenge it exposes, and how
suitable it is for practical algorithm evaluation.
To address this gap, this review introduces a Pipeline–
Scenario–Protocol–Readiness (PSPR) framework for ana-
lyzing public datasets used in multi-person tracking research,
as illustrated in Fig.3. The framework evaluates each dataset
using ﬁve complementary dimensions: pipeline role, visual
scenario complexity, evaluation protocol, deployment readi-
ness, and benchmark limitation. These dimensions allow
datasets from object detection, person re-identiﬁcation, per-
son search, and tracking benchmarks to be compared under
a single analytical structure. Instead of treating detection, re-
identiﬁcation, and tracking datasets as separate benchmark
families, the PSPR framework interprets them as functionally
connected resources that jointly inﬂuence the development
and evaluation of multi-person tracking systems.
The ﬁrst dimension, pipeline role, identiﬁes whether a
dataset mainly supports detector training, appearance mod-
elling, person search, trajectory association, or full tracking
evaluation. This is important because modern tracking sys-
tems commonly follow a tracking-by-detection paradigm,
where detection quality, re-identiﬁcation representation,
motion modelling, and association strategy jointly affect
ﬁnal tracking performance [5–7]. The second dimension,
visual scenario complexity, captures the practical difﬁculty
introduced by crowd density, occlusion, viewpoint varia-
tion, camera motion, scale variation, lighting changes, and
long-term identity re-entry, as observed across benchmarks
such as MOT20, DanceTrack, BDD100K, TAO, and Vis-
Drone [2, 3, 27, 28, 31]. The third dimension, evaluation
protocol, describes whether the dataset supports public-
detection evaluation, private-detection evaluation, single-
camera tracking, multi-camera tracking, identity retrieval, or
open-world tracking. These protocol differences are impor-
tant because public-detection benchmarks better isolate
association robustness, whereas private-detection settings
reﬂect the combined effect of detector quality, appear-
ance modelling, and data association [2, 26]. The fourth
dimension, deployment readiness, assesses how closely the
dataset resembles real-world tracking conditions such as
surveillance, autonomous driving, aerial monitoring, sports

4.1 Hierarchical dataset taxonomy and boundary
definitions
To improve internal consistency, the reviewed datasets are
organized into a hierarchical taxonomy based on their pri-
mary role in the visual multi-person tracking pipeline. This
taxonomy separates auxiliary datasets used for detector or
appearance-model training from benchmarks that directly
evaluate trajectory generation and identity preservation. The
grouping also distinguishes between tracking benchmarks
that stress different forms of difﬁculty, such as single-camera
association, cross-view identity continuity, UAV or moving-
camera perception, motion-heavy scenarios, synthetic data
generation, and long-term/open-world tracking.
The taxonomy is based on the primary function of each
dataset rather than on dataset popularity alone. Some datasets
may support more than one role. For example, WILD-
TRACK can support multi-camera pedestrian localization
as well as cross-view tracking analysis, while BDD100K
can be used for moving-camera trafﬁc-scene perception and
broader tracking evaluation. In such cases, the dataset is
assigned to the group that best reﬂects its dominant bench-
mark use in the present review. This prevents detection
datasets, Re-ID datasets, person-search datasets, and full
tracking benchmarks from being treated as interchangeable
resources. Table S2 shown in supplementary summarizes
the hierarchical taxonomy used to separate detection-support
datasets, Re-ID/person-search datasets, single-camera track-
ing benchmarks, multi-camera benchmarks, moving-camera
benchmarks, motion-heavy benchmarks, synthetic datasets,
and long-term/open-world tracking resources.
This hierarchy clariﬁes that each dataset family answers
a different evaluation question. Detection-support datasets
mainly test whether people can be localized reliably, but
they cannot evaluate identity preservation. Re-ID and person-

4.2 Practical dataset selection guide
The PSPR framework can be used as a practical guide
for selecting datasets according to the dominant research
objective or failure mode. Instead of choosing datasets only
by popularity, researchers should ﬁrst identify whether the
study targets detector failure, dense-crowd tracking, asso-
ciation ambiguity, Re-ID feature learning, person search,
cross-camera identity continuity, moving-camera tracking,
long-term/open-world tracking, or synthetic-to-real transfer.
Figure 4 summarizes this decision process. For example,
detector-focused studies may use CrowdHuman, CityPer-
sons, WiderPerson, EuroCity Persons, or MS COCO; dense-
crowd tracking is better represented by MOT20; associ-
ation ambiguity is better evaluated using DanceTrack or
SportsMOT; cross-camera identity continuity can be studied
using WildTrack, DukeMTMC-type resources, or CityFlow-
type settings; and long-term/open-world tracking is better
aligned with TAO or PathTrack-style resources. MOTSynth
is useful when the goal is synthetic training or controlled
annotation.
The decision tree should be read as a suitability guide
rather than a dataset ranking. Each branch links a research
problem to datasets whose PSPR proﬁle best matches the
required pipeline role, scenario difﬁculty, evaluation proto-
col, deployment context, and benchmark limitation.

4.3 Practical implications for dataset selection
The PSPR framework can be used as a practical guide for
selecting datasets according to the target failure mode or
research objective. If the main goal is to improve person
localization before tracking, detection-support datasets such
as CrowdHuman, CityPersons, WiderPerson, EuroCity Per-
sons, and MS COCO are appropriate. If the goal is to improve
identity representation, Re-ID and person-search datasets
such as Market-1501, MSMT17, MARS, PRW, and CUHK-
SYSU are more suitable.
For
standard
single-camera
pedestrian
tracking,
MOTChallenge datasets such as MOT15, MOT16, and
MOT17 remain useful baseline benchmarks. MOT20 is more
appropriate when the objective is to stress-test tracking
under dense crowds and severe occlusion. DanceTrack and
SportsMOT are better suited for evaluating association under
similar appearance, nonlinear motion, and complex human
interactions. WildTrack, DukeMTMC-type resources, and
CityFlow-type settings are more relevant for multi-camera
identity preservation and cross-view association. BDD100K
and KITTI Tracking are useful when the focus is moving-
camera or trafﬁc-scene tracking, while MOTSynth supports
synthetic training and controlled failure-mode analysis. For
long-term identity persistence, disappearance, re-entry, or
open-world tracking, TAO and PersonPath-style datasets are
more appropriate.
Thus, dataset selection should be treated as a benchmark-
design decision rather than a matter of popularity. The
most suitable dataset depends on whether the study aims
to test localization robustness, appearance modelling, asso-
ciation reliability, cross-camera continuity, moving-camera
robustness, synthetic-to-real transfer, or long-term identity
preservation.

4.5 Analysis of person re-identification datasets
Person re-identiﬁcation datasets support the appearance-
modelling component of multi-person tracking by providing
features for cross-frame association and identity recovery
after occlusion [51, 52]. However, most Re-ID benchmarks
follow query-gallery retrieval protocols rather than online
frame-by-frame tracking, so Re-ID accuracy should not be
interpreted as a direct measure of tracking robustness.
The second major auxiliary group consists of person
Re-ID datasets, which support the learning of appearance
embeddings used for identity association in multi-person

For clarity, Re-ID datasets may be divided into image-
based benchmarks, video-based benchmarks, and bridge
benchmarks that couple identity learning with full-scene
detection [19, 21–23, 49, 53–56].
Among image-based Re-ID benchmarks, Market-1501
remains one of the most widely used foundations for appear-

because it provided one of the ﬁrst large-scale Re-ID settings
suitable for deep learning [21]. DukeMTMC-reID extends
image-based identity learning into a large multi-camera
benchmark derived from DukeMTMC [49], and MSMT17
is widely used because it introduces a larger and more chal-
lenging multi-camera Re-ID setting with substantial scene
and lighting variation [22]. These datasets are most appro-
priate when the goal is to pretrain or ﬁne-tune appearance
embeddings for association modules.
For video-based Re-ID, MARS is a prominent benchmark
because it provides identity-labeled tracklets and is naturally
compatible with temporal appearance learning [23]. iLIDS-
VID and PRID2011 remain important legacy video-based
benchmarks, especially for evaluating sequence-level iden-
tity matching across camera views [53, 54]. These datasets
are relevant to data association because they expose temporal
appearance variation more directly than cropped still-image
datasets, even though they are not tracking benchmarks in
the strict sense.
Finally, two datasets deserve special treatment as bridge
benchmarks between detection and Re-ID: PRW and CUHK-
SYSU. Unlike conventional cropped Re-ID datasets, both
operate on full-scene images and therefore connect local-
ization quality with identity retrieval more directly [55, 56].
In practice, these datasets can be discussed either in a short
bridge subsection or at the end of the Re-ID section, depend-
ing on how strictly the review wants to separate person search
from appearance modelling. For a multi-person tracking sur-
vey, their main value lies in showing that person association
is not learned in isolation from detection noise, scene clutter,
and gallery construction. Supplementary Table S3 summa-
rizes the practical relevance of representative Re-ID and
person-search datasets for multi-person tracking.
Recent unsupervised and contrastive person Re-ID meth-
ods further support the role of Re-ID datasets as appearance-
modelling resources for tracking-by-detection pipelines. For
example, Xiong et al. [36] proposed an adaptive clustering
and weighted regularization contrastive learning framework
for unsupervised person Re-ID, showing how identity-
discriminative representations can be improved without rely-
ing entirely on fully supervised identity labels. Such meth-
ods are relevant to multi-person tracking because stronger
appearance embeddings can improve cross-frame associa-
tion and identity recovery after occlusion. However, they
also reinforce an important limitation of Re-ID benchmarks:
even when identity features are learned more robustly, query-
gallery or tracklet retrieval performance does not by itself
evaluate missed detections, online association errors, tra-
jectory fragmentation, or long-term identity persistence.
Therefore, Re-ID datasets should be interpreted as support-
ing appearance modelling and association modules, rather
than as complete substitutes for tracking benchmarks.
4.6 Analysis of core multi-person tracking
benchmarks
Core tracking benchmarks are the most direct datasets for
evaluating multi-person tracking because they include video
sequences, frame-wise detections or annotations, identity
labels, and evaluation metrics for trajectory quality. How-
ever, these benchmarks differ substantially in what they
actually test. Some mainly stress detection quality, some
stress association, some stress crowd density, and others test
moving-cameraorlong-termtracking.Therefore,benchmark
scores should not be compared without considering dataset
design and protocol differences.
Core tracking datasets form the central focus of this review
because they are the benchmarks used to evaluate the ﬁnal
behavior of MPT systems: localization quality, temporal con-
tinuity, identity preservation, and resistance to fragmentation
or identity switches. Rather than treating all benchmarks as
a ﬂat list, it is more informative to organize them by tracking
scenario,becausetheprimarychallengeofabenchmarkoften
determines which model components matter most. Some
datasets emphasize conventional single-camera pedestrian
MOT, others emphasize cross-view identity continuity, while
others deliberately stress crowd density, human articulation,
or long-range temporal persistence [2, 4, 20, 26, 27, 30, 32,
57–59].
4.6.1 Single-camera pedestrian MOT
The most established benchmark family in this category
is MOTChallenge, particularly the MOT15, MOT16, and
MOT17 releases, which remain canonical reference points
for single-camera pedestrian tracking [26]. These datasets
are especially important because they provide the standard
evaluation setting against which many tracking-by-detection
methods have been compared. Alongside MOTChallenge,
PathTrack is useful as a large-scale person trajectory dataset
built using path supervision, and it is valuable not only for
benchmarking but also for training person matching and
association modules [57]. Together, these datasets deﬁne the
most conventional setting of pedestrian MOT: single-camera
tracking with identity preservation over time, but without the
added complexity of multi-camera handoff.

4.6.2 Multi-camera person tracking
For multi-camera person tracking, the dataset emphasis
shifts from frame-to-frame continuity within a single view

here, especially for dense multi-camera pedestrian scenes
with precise calibration and synchronized viewpoints [30].
These datasets are best treated separately from ordinary
single-camera MOT, because they stress challenges such as
camera handoff, spatial calibration, view-dependent appear-
ance change, and long-range identity consistency across
disjoint ﬁelds of view.
4.6.3 Crowded-scene tracking
Crowded-scene tracking deserves its own subgroup because
crowd density changes the failure mode of a tracker. In
these settings, missed detections, severe overlap, and iden-
tity switches become much more frequent than in ordinary
pedestrian videos. MOT20 is the clearest benchmark for this
category, having been designed speciﬁcally for very crowded
pedestrian scenes [2]. HiEve further expands the notion of
human-centriccomplexitybyintroducingdenseandcomplex
event settings with large numbers of trajectories and difﬁcult
crowd interactions [58]. For a person-centric review, these
datasets are essential because they directly expose the lim-
its of association strategies under heavy occlusion and dense
human interaction.
4.6.4 Sports, dance, and motion-heavy tracking
In many benchmarks, appearance cues are sufﬁciently infor-
mative that a tracker can rely heavily on Re-ID features.
However, this assumption weakens in motion-heavy scenes
where people look similar and move dynamically. Dance-
Track was introduced speciﬁcally to address this issue by
stressing uniform appearance, diverse motion, and extreme
articulation [27]. SportsMOT provides a related but domain-
speciﬁc setting in which players move rapidly, interact
frequently, and are often visually similar within the same
team [4]. These benchmarks are especially valuable because
they reveal whether a tracker can move beyond simple
appearance matching and reason more effectively about
motion, articulation, and trajectory dynamics. Recent graph-
matching and differentiable assignment approaches have
also emphasized that occlusion and varying object appear-
ances remain central challenges in modern MOT, further
supporting the need to analyze benchmarks according to
association difﬁculty rather than detection accuracy alone
[60].
4.6.5 Long-term, re-entry, and synthetic settings
Long-term person tracking is still comparatively under-
represented in the literature, but it is important for real
deployments because people often leave and re-enter the
ﬁeld of view. PersonPath22 is one of the clearest recent
benchmarks for this setting, offering longer and more varied
real-world person tracks than earlier MOT datasets [59]. In
parallel, MOTSynth represents a different but increasingly
important direction: synthetic large-scale person tracking
data that can support training for detection, Re-ID, segmen-
tation, and tracking at once [32]. While PersonPath22 is best
viewed as a long-term real-world benchmark, MOTSynth
is best positioned as the principal synthetic person track-
ing benchmark. Their inclusion broadens the review beyond
short surveillance clips and helps capture current efforts to
address data scale and annotation limitations in person track-
ing. As provided in Supplementary Table S2 summarizes
representative tracking benchmarks from an analytical per-
spective.
The PSPR-based classiﬁcation in Tables S2, S3, and
S4 from supplementary data reveals that no single dataset
fully captures all requirements of real-world multi-person
tracking. Detection datasets improve localization but do
not evaluate identity persistence. Re-ID datasets improve
appearance representation but often ignore detector fail-
ure, occlusion-driven fragmentation, and online association
constraints. Core tracking benchmarks provide trajectory-
level evaluation, but they differ substantially in what they
emphasize. MOT17 provides a general pedestrian tracking
baseline [26], MOT20 stresses crowd density [2], Dance-

moving-camera trafﬁc scenes [3], and TAO provides long-
term and open-world diversity [28].

5 Dataset limitations and future benchmark
requirements
The PSPR-based analysis reveals that current datasets used
in multi-person tracking research remain limited by sev-
eral cross-cutting issues related to data collection, annotation
design, scene diversity, and evaluation protocol. These lim-
itations affect auxiliary object-detection datasets, person
re-identiﬁcation datasets, and core tracking benchmarks in
different ways. Therefore, rather than viewing these limita-
tions as isolated dataset-speciﬁc weaknesses, they should be
interpreted as structural gaps in the current dataset ecosystem
supporting tracking-by-detection pipelines.
5.1 Limitations of object detection datasets
Auxiliary object-detection datasets are essential for training
and pretraining detectors used in multi-person tracking sys-
tems. However, many widely used detection bench

A major limitation of these datasets is their bias toward
daytime, ground-level, RGB imagery. This restricts the
generalization of detection models to more challenging con-
ditions such as aerial viewpoints, night-time scenes, thermal
imagery, adverse weather, and low-light surveillance. In
addition, most generic detection datasets are image-level
resources and do not provide video sequences suitable for
evaluating temporal consistency or spatio-temporal reason-
ing. This is particularly important for tracking-by-detection
systems, where missed detections in individual frames can
fragment trajectories and reduce identity consistency.
Annotation richness is another limitation. Most datasets
primarily provide two-dimensional bounding boxes, while
richer annotations such as instance masks, keypoints, 3D
bounding boxes, depth information, camera calibration,
time-of-day information, and weather metadata are less con-
sistently available. Although datasets such as LVIS and Open
Images improve instance-level and large-scale annotation
coverage, they are still not optimized for evaluating person
tracking over time. Extreme object scales also remain chal-
lenging: tiny pedestrians, distant aerial targets, and heavily
occluded people are often underrepresented or inconsistently
annotated. Furthermore, large-scale datasets may contain
label noise and missing small-object annotations, which can
negatively affect detector training and downstream tracking
performance.
Future detection-support datasets for multi-person track-
ing should therefore include more diverse environments,
including day and night conditions, varied viewpoints,
adverse weather, aerial scenes, and indoor settings. They
should also incorporate temporal sequences, richer anno-
tations such as masks, keypoints, and 3D labels, and mul-
timodal inputs such as RGB, thermal, depth, and LiDAR.
Improved quality control for small-object annotations and
more balanced representation of rare object scales and
underrepresented categories would further improve detector
robustness in real-world tracking applications.
5.2 Limitations of person re-identification datasets
Person re-identiﬁcation datasets are important for learning
appearance representations that support identity association
in multi-person tracking. However, most Re-ID benchmarks
evaluate query-gallery retrieval rather than online frame-
by-frame tracking. Therefore, strong performance on Re-ID
datasetsdoesnotnecessarilyimplyrobustperformanceinfull
tracking scenarios, where identity preservation is affected by
missed detections, occlusion, motion ambiguity, track frag-
mentation, and long-term disappearance.
Many Re-ID datasets are collected in relatively con-
strained surveillance environments such as campuses, malls,
and street scenes. These datasets are often biased toward
daytime RGB imagery and provide limited coverage of
night-time scenes, adverse weather, thermal imaging, depth
sensing, or other non-RGB modalities. As a result, Re-ID
models trained on these benchmarks may not generalize well
to real-world monitoring systems that operate across diverse
lighting, weather, and sensor conditions.
Identity distribution is another important limitation. Sev-
eral Re-ID datasets contain long-tail identity distributions,
where some individuals appear frequently while others have
only a few samples. This imbalance can affect embedding
learning, few-shot generalization, and fair evaluation across
identities. Video-based Re-ID datasets partially address tem-
poral appearance variation by providing tracklets, but many
tracklets are automatically generated and may include detec-
tion or tracking noise. Moreover, these datasets rarely capture
true long-term disappearance, re-entry after extended occlu-
sion, or wide-area identity transitions across large camera
networks.
Another challenge is the lack of consistency in evaluation
protocols. Re-ID datasets vary in query-gallery splits, camera
conﬁgurations, attribute annotations, and reported metrics
such as Rank-1 accuracy and mean average precision. These
inconsistencies make cross-dataset comparison difﬁcult and
limit the direct transfer of Re-ID ﬁndings to multi-person
tracking evaluation.
Future Re-ID datasets should include more diverse light-
ing and weather conditions, broader camera networks, mul-
timodal sensing, and more balanced identity distributions.
They should also capture extended occlusion, long-term
reappearance, and cross-camera re-entry events that more
closely resemble real tracking deployments. Standardized
evaluation protocols and richer identity-level annotations
would make Re-ID datasets more useful for identity asso-
ciation within multi-person tracking pipelines.
5.3 Limitations of core multi-person tracking
benchmarks
Core tracking benchmarks provide the most direct evalu-
ation of multi-person tracking because they include video
sequences, identity labels, and trajectory-level metrics. How-
ever, current tracking datasets also have important limita-
tions. Many benchmarks consist of relatively short video
clips, which limits their ability to evaluate long-term iden-
tity persistence, delayed re-entry, and identity recovery after
extended occlusion. This is a major gap because real-world
surveillance, trafﬁc monitoring, sports analytics, and aerial

Scene and sensor diversity also remain limited. Many
tracking datasets are based on static RGB cameras recorded
under relatively favorable conditions. Night-time tracking,
adverse weather, indoor robotics, underwater environments,
thermal imaging, depth sensing, and LiDAR-based track-
ing are still underrepresented. Although datasets such as
BDD100K, VisDrone, TAO, and MOTSynth expand the
diversity of tracking scenarios, no single benchmark fully
captures the range of deployment conditions required for
robust real-world tracking.
Annotation design is another limitation. Most tracking
datasets provide 2D bounding boxes and identity labels,
but richer annotations such as part-level tracks, synchro-
nized 3D trajectories, semantic event labels, occlusion state,
interaction labels, and camera calibration metadata are less
common. Such annotations would be valuable for modern
tracking systems that need to reason about group merging,
object handoff, partial visibility, multi-view geometry, and
scene-level context.
Finally, evaluation protocols remain fragmented across
datasets. Some benchmarks emphasize detection quality,
others emphasize association quality, and different datasets
report different combinations of MOTA, IDF1, HOTA, DetA,
AssA, identity switches, and fragmentation. Since these met-
rics capture different aspects of tracking performance, results
should not be interpreted without considering the dominant
difﬁculty and evaluation protocol of each dataset. For exam-
ple, high MOTA may reﬂect strong detection performance
but may not necessarily indicate strong identity preservation
if IDF1 or HOTA remains low.
5.4 Future benchmark agenda for robust
multi-person tracking
The next generation of multi-person tracking benchmarks
should move beyond short, controlled video clips and sup-
port more realistic evaluation of identity preservation under
deployment-like conditions. First, future datasets should
include longer continuous videos with target disappearance,
re-entry, prolonged occlusion, and scene transitions, so that
long-termidentitypersistencecanbeevaluatedmoredirectly.
Second, benchmark coverage should be expanded to adverse
weather, night-time scenes, low-light surveillance, aerial
viewpoints,movingcameras,anddensepublicenvironments,
because many current datasets remain biased toward rela-
tively clear RGB imagery.
Futurebenchmarkdevelopmentshouldalsoreﬂectbroader
advances in spatiotemporal modelling, visual representa-
tion learning, and multimodal visual computing. Recent
tracking studies show that temporal dynamics, spatial prox-
imity, topological relationships, and complementary sensing
cues can inﬂuence target association and identity con-
tinuity. For example, satellite-video tracking guided by
spatial-temporal proximity and topological relationships
highlights the importance of target overlap, local spatial
conﬁguration, and topology-aware association, which is con-
ceptually relevant to dense pedestrian tracking scenarios
involving occlusion and group motion. Similarly, video-SAR
tracking with foreground-background motion feature com-
pensation shows that background interference and clutter
can affect tracking continuity, which is relevant to UAV,
aerial, and surveillance datasets. These observations sup-
port the need for future person-tracking benchmarks with
richer RGB, thermal, depth, LiDAR, multi-view, and clutter-
aware annotations [61–64]. Similarly, dataset distillation
and synthetic/implicit scene representation methods suggest
promising directions for efﬁcient benchmark construction,
benchmark compression, controllable synthetic data genera-
tion, and viewpoint-diverse evaluation [65].
Privacy and governance should also be treated as dataset-
design requirements. Identity labels should be released
with clear usage terms, access controls where needed,
and privacy-preserving procedures such as face blurring,
restricted redistribution, or anonymized identity coding.
Benchmarks should also support open-set and unknown-
identity evaluation, because real deployments often involve
people who are not part of a ﬁxed closed-world identity
set.
Finally, future benchmarks should standardize reporting
of detection quality and association quality separately. In
addition to aggregate metrics, benchmark reports should
include detection-sensitive measures, identity-sensitive mea-
sures, association-sensitive measures, and clear documen-
tation of public/private detection settings, detector training
data, Re-ID supervision, post-processing, and leaderboard
access dates. Cross-domain evaluation should also be encour-
aged, where methods trained on one dataset family are tested
on another to assess generalization. Together, these recom-
mendations would make future benchmarks more useful for
evaluating robust, fair, and deployment-ready multi-person
tracking systems.

computing directions. Controllable simulation and syn-
thetic data can help generate rare crowd, lighting, weather,
and camera-motion conditions, but they should be paired
with real-world validation to address domain shift. Multi-
modal sensing, neural scene representations, and privacy-
preserving visual analytics may further support responsible
tracking in human-centered environments. Such datasets

5.5 Ethical, privacy, and governance considerations
Ethical and governance issues are important in visual multi-
person tracking and person re-identiﬁcation because many
datasets contain public-space video, surveillance footage,
identity labels, cross-camera associations, and other infor-
mation that may make individuals traceable across time or
location. Therefore, dataset suitability should not be assessed
only by scale, annotation richness, or benchmark perfor-
mance, but also by privacy risk, licensing conditions, consent
context, and responsible-use restrictions.
Researchers should verify the current access policy and
usage terms of each dataset, especially for benchmarks
that have been withdrawn, access-restricted, or redistributed
through unofﬁcial sources. Public-space recording does not
automatically remove privacy concerns, particularly when
identity labels, trajectories, or Re-ID annotations are pro-
vided. Future datasets should therefore include clearer docu-
mentation of collection context, consent or ethical approval
where applicable, anonymization procedures, redistribution
rules, and intended or prohibited uses.
Fairness and representativeness are also important. Track-
ing and Re-ID models may perform unevenly across demo-
graphic groups, clothing styles, lighting conditions, camera
viewpoints, geographic regions, and environmental settings.
Dataset documentation should therefore report collection
conditions and known biases where possible. In this review,
deploymentreadinessisinterpretednotonlyastechnicalreal-
ism, but also as responsible-use readiness, including clear
licensing,privacy-awareannotations,accessgovernance,and
sufﬁcient diversity for robust and fair evaluation.
Multi-person tracking and Re-ID datasets require
careful governance because they often contain information
that can make individuals traceable across time and space.
In particular, identity labels, trajectory annotations, re-entry
events,andcross-cameraassociationscancreateprivacyrisks
beyond ordinary object detection. Therefore, a dataset being
recorded in a public or semi-public space should not be
interpreted as being ethically risk-free. Responsible dataset
documentation should report the recording context, con-
sent or notice conditions where applicable, anonymization
steps, face or body blurring policies, license restrictions, pro-
hibited uses, geographic and demographic coverage, access
limitations, and any subsequent withdrawal or restriction of
the dataset. These details are important because benchmark
usefulness depends not only on annotation richness and eval-
uation protocol, but also on whether the dataset can be used
responsibly in human-centered deployment settings.
As summarized in Table 8, privacy risk generally increases
as datasets move from detection-only annotations to identity-
labelled Re-ID, trajectory-level tracking, and multi-camera
association. This does not mean that detection datasets
are risk-free, but rather that identity persistence and cross-
camera linkage introduce additional governance require-
ments. Future benchmark releases should therefore include
datasheets or equivalent documentation, clear licensing,
access conditions, anonymization policies, prohibited-use
statements, and metadata describing geographic and demo-
graphic coverage where such information can be responsibly
reported.

The proposed Pipeline–Scenario–Protocol–Readiness
(PSPR) framework provides a structured way to interpret
datasets according to their pipeline role, scenario complexity,
evaluation protocol, deployment relevance, and benchmark
limitations. The main value of PSPR is that it reframes dataset
selection as a benchmark-design decision: the appropriate
dataset depends on the target failure mode, the evaluation
protocol, and the intended deployment setting.
The review further highlights that benchmark results must
be interpreted in relation to evaluation protocol and met-
ric choice. Public- and private-detection settings measure
different aspects of the tracking pipeline, while MOTA,
IDF1, HOTA, DetA, and AssA emphasize different perfor-
mance dimensions. Future datasets should therefore priori-
tize longer continuous videos, adverse-condition coverage,
multimodal sensing, richer identity-level annotations, and
standardized protocols that separately quantify detection
quality and association performance.
Overall, this review provides a structured reference for
dataset selection, fair benchmark interpretation, and future
benchmark design for robust real-world multi-person track-
ing systems.
