A B S T R A C T
This study presents a review of the impacts of Industrial Revolution 4 (IR4) technologies on the oil palm industry, 
focusing on their applications in enhancing productivity, sustainability, and socio-economic outcomes. Key 
technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain, Internet of Things (IoT), big data analytics, and 
remote sensing were examined for their roles in various applications. Findings indicate that these technologies 
have significantly improved palm oil production processes, offering innovative solutions to enhance efficiency 
while reducing environmental impact. They also contribute to addressing socio-economic challenges such as 
labour rights issues and transparency in supply chain management. Among 98 of the reviewed studies in this 
literature review, IR4 technologies were applied the most for oil palm plantation mapping (33.1 %), disease 
detection (24.8 %) and palm fruit grading (10.8 %). However, challenges like high implementation costs and 
workforce skill requirements remain as barriers to its widespread adoption. Future research should focus on 
developing scalable, cost-effective solutions to ensure equitable access across different segments of the industry. 
Lastly, this review also discussed the potential of Industrial Revolution 5 (IR5) concept to revolutionise the oil 
palm sector and guide it toward a more human-centric and sustainable future.
1. Introduction
The oil palm industry represents a significant sector in the global 
agricultural market as a major commodity, supplying about 40 % of all 
vegetable oil [1]. It is highly cost-efficient in terms of yield per hectare, 
producing 4.3 million tonnes per million hectare (Mt/Mha) of oil 
compared to other vegetable oil sources like a combination of soybean 
or rapeseed producing 0.52 Mt/Mha of oil [2]. The cultivation of oil 
palms is concentrated in tropical regions, with Indonesia and Malaysia 
both accounting for over 80 % of global production. Other significant 
producers include Thailand, Colombia, and Nigeria [3]. The vegetable 
oil from palm oil and palm kernel oil are versatile and widely used in a 
vast array of food products, cosmetics, and biofuels [4]. Palm oil plays a 
vital role in providing employment for producing countries, especially in 
rural areas where job opportunities are scarce, contributing to their 
economic development and poverty reduction [5]. This industry pro­
vides jobs across its entire supply chain, from plantation workers to 
processing plant employees and those involved in logistics and export, 
providing a source of income to millions of workers. However, the 
profitability and growth of this industry to meet global demand have led 
to significant environmental concerns and social impacts.
Rapid expansion of oil palm plantations is often linked to defores­
tation of vast areas of tropical rainforests into monoculture plantations, 
resulting in loss of habitats for many endangered species [6]. A dramatic 
reduction in species richness was found when compared to natural for­
ests, with the composition of species in plantations being dominated by 
more generalist and invasive species such as crazy ants, rats, blood py­
thons, and barn owls [7]. Moreover, the clearing of tropical forests and

for workers are ongoing challenges that require attention from both the 
industry and regulatory bodies. To address the environmental and social 
challenges, various initiatives and certification schemes have been

impacts such as labour rights, gender parity, child labour, forced labour, 
and Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) of landowners and affected 
communities including customary rights over land of indigenous people 
[1].
Since the 1750s, we have experienced three industrial revolutions 
where innovation that drove them completely revolutionised how pro­
duction was done as shown in Fig. 1. The First Industrial Revolution 
marks a transition from hand production methods to machines through 
the use of steam power and water power [12]. About a century later, the 
Second Industrial Revolution introduced early factory electrification 
and the mass assembly line [13]. The Third Industrial Revolution saw 
the early development of factory automations and robotics when com­
puters hit the scene around the middle of the 20th century [14]. The 
Fourth Industrial Revolution, or IR4, marks a significant shift in the 
industrial landscape, propelled by the seamless integration of several 
systems, tools, and digital technology innovations into manufacturing 
and production processes. In agricultural sector, central to this revolu­
tion are key technologies such as internet of things (IoT), artificial in­
telligence (AI), blockchain, big data analytics, and remote sensing [15]. 
Each of these plays a pivotal role in enhancing operational efficiency, 
driving innovation, and fostering sustainable practices across various 
sectors, notably in agriculture. The IoT serves as the backbone of IR4, 
facilitating the interconnectivity of devices, sensors, and machines. In 
the agricultural sector, IoT technologies enable precise monitoring and 
control over farming conditions, significantly optimising resource use. 
For instance, sensors deployed throughout farmlands can monitor soil 
moisture and nutrient levels, providing data-driven insights for irriga­
tion and fertilisation, thereby enhancing crop yield while minimizing 
waste [16]. AI further amplifies the capabilities of IoT through intelli­
gent data analysis, pattern recognition, and predictive modelling. Ap­
plications of AI in agriculture range from crop health monitoring to 
predictive analytics for yield optimisation [17]. By processing vast 
datasets, AI algorithms can predict potential pest infestations or dis­
eases, enabling preventive measures to safeguard crops. Additionally, 
AI-powered tools assist in automating complex decision-making pro­
cesses, thereby reducing the reliance on manual labour and subjective 
judgment [18]. Remote sensing in agriculture usually involve to the use 
of satellite or aerial imagery to monitor and assess various aspects of 
agricultural practices and environmental conditions. It provides valu­
able data for managing crops, detecting changes in land use, and 
improving agricultural productivity [19]. Big data analytics stands at 
the forefront of transforming agricultural practices into data-driven 
science. By analysing data from diverse sources, including satellite im­
agery, weather stations, and on-ground sensors, farmers can gain in­
sights into optimal planting times, crop rotation strategies, and pest 
management. This holistic view enables a more informed approach to 
farming, significantly increasing productivity and sustainability [20]. 
Blockchain technology offers a revolutionary approach to ensuring 
transparency and traceability within the supply chain. In the agricul­
tural sector, blockchain can be used to document the journey of products 
from farm to table, providing consumers with verifiable information 
regarding the origin, treatment, and quality of their food. Apart from 
building consumer trust, this practice also empowers farmers to achieve 
fairer pricing and recognition for sustainable practices [21].
The objective of this literature review is to i) evaluate the state of IR4 
development in palm oil production processes, ii) explore the impact of 
IR4 on productivity, sustainability, environmental, supply chain man­
agement, social, and economic factors, iii) analyse the gap and limita­
tion of IR4 implementation in the oil palm industry. As part of the gap 
analysis, this study aims to address challenges faced by the oil palm 
industry in adopting smart technologies and transitioning towards In­
dustrial Revolution 5.0 (IR5), a more human-centric approach. In the 
context of this work, IR4 and IR5 are defined are as follows:
“IR4 can be defined as the integration of intelligent digital technologies 
into manufacturing and industrial processes. It encompasses a set of tech­
nologies that include industrial IoT networks, AI, big data, robotics, and 
automation. IR4 allows for smart manufacturing and the creation of intelli­
gent factories. It aims to enhance productivity, efficiency, and flexibility while

2. Background
Due to the rapid development of IR4, the following sections focus on 
several main IR4 technologies which are relevant to oil palm industry, 
including AI, IoT, big data, blockchain, and remote sensing. AI enables 
computers to perform complex tasks such as image recognition and 
predictive analytics. IoT uses networked sensors to enhance agricultural 
productivity. Big data analytics extracts actionable insights from large 
datasets to improve decision-making. Blockchain technology provides 
secure, transparent record-keeping across decentralised networks. 
Remote sensing collects environmental data via technologies like sat­
ellites and Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV), supporting various plan­
ning and monitoring applications. Together, these technologies drive 
automation, optimise data-driven strategies, and enhance operational 
efficiencies.
2.1. Artificial intelligence (AI)
AI refers to any technique that enables computers to mimic human 
intelligence such as learning and problem-solving. Machine learning 
(ML) is a subset of AI that focuses on developing ability of algorithms to 
learn autonomously and improve their performance over time as they 
are exposed to more data [24]. Fig. 2 shows several learning methods 
and examples of ML algorithms. Supervised training is one learning 
method of ML which involves training a model on a labelled dataset 
where the correct output is known, allowing the model to learn over 
time to yield the desired output. Common tasks for this type of ML al­
gorithm include classification to assign datapoints into categories, for 
example, to classify emails either as spam or not spam. Another type of 
task is regression where the goal is to predict a continuous value based 
on input features. Common supervised ML algorithms include decision 
tree, naïve bayes, support vector machine, and linear regression [25]. 
Another learning type of ML is unsupervised learning where the data 
used to train the model is not labelled, so the algorithm tries to learn 
patterns and structure from the data. Unsupervised learning is optimal 
for tasks such as clustering, by grouping data points into clusters based 
on their similarities and feature reduction which help to simplify models 
by removing irrelevant or redundant features. Examples of unsupervised 
learning algorithms are K-means clustering, hierarchical clustering and 
principal component analysis [26]. Semi-supervised learning is a hybrid 
approach that involves training on a small amount of labelled data and a 
large amount of unlabelled data. This is useful when getting labelled 
data is expensive or time-consuming. An example of semi-supervised 
training is an iterative process called self-training where a model is 
initially trained on labelled data. The model then labels unlabelled data, 
and the most confident predictions are added to the labelled dataset. 
After that, the model is retrained on the augmented labelled dataset 
[27]. Reinforcement learning involves training an agent to make de­
cisions by interacting with an environment. The agent receives rewards 
or penalties based on the action it takes, with the aim of maximising the 
cumulative rewards [28]. Transfer learning is a learning method where 
knowledge learned from a previous task is re-used (transferred) to boost 
performance on a related task and improve learning efficiency [29].
A subset of ML is Deep learning (DL), built on neural network that 
consist of multiple layers of interconnected neurons used to model and 
understand complex patterns in large datasets. Fig. 2 shows some ex­
amples of DL architectures. Artificial neural network (ANN) is the 
foundational architecture which many DL models are built upon. ANN 
consists of multiple layers of neurons where each layer receives input 
from previous layer, processes it, and passes the output to subsequent 
layers. The complexity of the model increases with the number of layers 
[30]. Multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is a type of feedforwards ANN that 
consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output 
layer. Activation functions are applied to determine the output of each 
neuron. Backpropagation is the most extensively used algorithm 
employed to train MLP which refers to the process of distributing the 
calculated error at the output of a neural network back through its layers 
[31]. Convolutional neural network (CNN) is a DL architecture that 
learns directly form input data without the need for manual feature 
extraction. CNN improves the design of traditional ANN like MLP where 
each layer considers optimum parameters for meaningful output and 
reduces model complexity while technique such as dropout help deal 
with overfitting. Several variants of CNN include VGG (Visual Geometry 
Group), AlexNet, Inception, Xception, and ResNet (Residual Network) 
[31]. Another DL architecture is Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) 
which is designed to handle sequential or time-series data by feeding the 
output from previous step as input for current stage. RNN possess a form 
of memory that enables them to use information from previous inputs to 
influence the current input and output. The output of RNN is reliant on

Variants of RNN include LSTM (long short-term memory) and GRU

Computer vision is a field of application in AI that trains computers 
to interpret and understand visual data using cameras and DL models. 
CNN architectures like LeNet-5, AlexNet, VGGNet, and ResNet can be 
used for image classification tasks such as identifying and classifying 
images while advanced object detection models such as YOLO (you only 
look once) and Faster-RCNN (region convolutional neural network) are 
employed for locating and identifying objects within images or videos 
[35]. The initial step in computer vision is image acquisition and con­
verting them to numerical format that can be processed. The next step is 
image processing through techniques such as edge detection to identify 
boundaries within images to distinguish objects, feature extraction to 
extract important attributes from images for analysis, and segmentation 
algorithms to partition images into regions of interest [36]. Natural 
language processing (NLP) focuses on enabling computers to under­
stand, interpret, and generate human languages in a meaningful and 
useful way [37]. DL models based on RNN, LSTM, and more recently, 
transformers, have revolutionised NLP by achieving state-of-the-art re­
sults in many tasks for understanding and generating text. Transformers 
have led to the development of powerful models like bidirectional 
encoder representations transformers (BERT) for classification tasks, 
semantic search, and retrieval task, and generative pre-trained trans­
formers (GPT) for text generation and translation [38].

2.3. Big data analytics
Big data analytics refers to a set of advanced data analytics tech­
niques and tools that handle and analyse massive datasets to provide 
actionable insights and support decision making process. Big data is 
characterised by five “V's” [44], i.e. 
• Volume: the massive amount of data generated
• Velocity: the speed at which data is generated and processed
• Variety: the different types of data
• Veracity: the quality and accuracy of data, and
• Value: the usefulness derived from the data
Fig. 4 summarises the types of data source, data processing, data 
storage, and data analysis related to big data. Several types of big data 
include structured, unstructured, and semi-structured data.Structured 
data are highly organised information that is easily searchable stored in 
spreadsheets or relational databases. Most data generated such as text, 
images, audio, video, and sensor data are unstructured which lacks a 
pre-defined data model making it more difficult to collect, process, and 
analyse. Semi-structured data occupies the middle ground between 
structured and unstructured data that does not follow strict standards. 
An example is the extensible markup language (XML) documents that 
contains user-defined data-tags which allows them to be read by com­
puters [45]. The collected data can be processed in real-time or in batch 
to clean data by removing errors, inconsistencies, and duplicates, or to 
transform data into suitable format for analysis. The real-time process­
ing model analyses data in continuous form as soon as possible to derive 
results while in batch processing model, data is first stored before being 
analysed [46]. There are several types of data storage methods such as 
distributed data, data lakes, and data warehouse [47]. In distributed 
data system, data is divided into smaller blocks which are replicated and 
stored across multiple storage nodes providing improved fault tolerance. 
Data lakes is a centralised repository designed to store large amount of 
raw structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data while data 
warehouse is a structured repository that integrates data for analysis and 
reporting purposes. There are several types of data analysis methods 
such as descriptive analytics which examines data to describe its fea­
tures in a historical context to understand what has happened [48]. 
Diagnostic analysis involves looking at the data to determine why 
something happened. Predictive analysis uses statistical models and 
forecast techniques that relies on historical data to identify trends and 
determine how likely specific outcomes are in the future. Prescriptive 
analysis goes one step further by recommending actions you can take 
based on the insights gained from predictive analysis. It involves using 
optimisation and simulation algorithms and is particularly useful in 
decision-making processes where you need to consider multiple vari­
ables and scenarios.
2.4. Blockchain
Blockchain is a method of recording information that makes it almost 
impossible to be changed, hacked, or manipulated [49]. A blockchain is 
a distributed ledger that duplicates and distributes transactions across 
the network of computers participating in the blockchain. Each block in 
a blockchain contains a set of transactions that are encoded into a 
cryptographic hash. The hash of each block depends on the previous 
block, creating a secure chain from the genesis block to the current 
block. Unlike traditional ledgers or databases that are controlled by a 
single entity, blockchain is distributed across a network of computers 
referred to as nodes. This decentralisation ensures that no single party 
can control or manipulate the data. Every transaction on the blockchain 
is visible to every participant and cannot be changed once recorded. This

transparency helps to build trust among participants. Fig. 5 summarises 
the evolution of blockchain. The first generation of blockchain, known 
as Blockchain 1.0, focussed on the concept of distributed ledger (DLT) to 
support decentralised, peer-to-peer digital currency transactions [50]. 
Blockchain 2.0 expanded its application beyond currency to include 
smart contracts; the latter are self-executing contracts with the terms of 
the agreement directly written into lines of code. They automatically 
execute actions under specific conditions, which are recorded on the 
blockchain, making the process efficient and transparent [51]. Block­
chain 3.0 introduced decentralised applications called dApps that run on 
a network of computers, promoting inter-chain transactions through 
techniques like sharding. The Proof of Stake and Proof of Authority 
consensus are introduced to reduce computational load and energy 
consumption, enabling faster and more scalable transaction processing. 
Consensus mechanism refer to the approach used to achieve agreement, 
trust, and security across a decentralised computer network [52]. 
Blockchain uses various consensus mechanisms to agree on the validity 
of transactions. There are various types of consensus mechanisms such 
as proof of work (PoW), proof of stake (PoS), delegated proof of stake 
(DPoS), proof of capacity (PoC), proof of importance (PoI), and so on. 
These protocols can be defined into either proof-based or voting-based 
protocols [53]. PoW is a proof-based protocol that involve solving 
complex mathematical puzzles, which requires intensive computational 
power. The first node to solve the puzzle gets the right to add the new 
blocks to the blockchain and receives a reward. The other nodes in the 
network can accept its validity or contest it when necessary. PoS is a 
voting-based protocol that selects the creator of a new block based on 
their economic stake in the network. Validators must own a stake in the 
system to mine or validate transactions. For example, if a participant 
owns 10 % of the total stake, they have a 10 % chance of mining the next 
block. A pseudorandom mechanism selects a block proposer, and a 
committee of nodes, chosen based on their stake, decides on the validity 
of the block. Votes can be cast by committee members to approve or 
reject the proposed block which is added to the blockchain if two-thirds 
of the committee approve. The latest generation, Blockchain 4.0 seeks to 
integrate with other technologies such as AI, IoT, and Big Data to create 
seamless and efficient business solutions [50].
2.5. Remote sensing
Remote sensing plays an important role by using advanced data 
collection methods and technologies like optical sensors, radar sensors, 
and light detection and ranging (LiDAR), to enhance operational 
efficiency and decision-making (Fig. 6). Remote sensing satellites are 
equipped with multispectral, hyperspectral, and radar sensors to capture 
high-resolution images of the surface of the Earth. UAVs provide flexible 
and precise data collection capabilities, especially in hard-to-reach 
areas. Ground-Based Sensors can monitor in real-time environmental 
conditions such as temperature, humidity and moisture [54]. Optical 
remote sensing utilises multispectral and hyperspectral imaging to 
capture detailed images across various wavelengths including visible 
light, near infrared, and short-wave infrared, to provide rich information 
about vegetation health, soil conditions, and land use [55]. Multispec­
tral sensors capture light across broad range of spectral bands while and 
hyperspectral sensors captures narrow spectral bands over continuous 
spectral range. Radar remote sensing, particularly synthetic aperture 
radar (SAR), uses radio waves to penetrate clouds and vegetation, 
enabling all-weather, day-and-night data collection for applications 
such as topographic mapping and disaster management [56]. LiDAR, 
employs laser pulses to generate high-resolution maps which are crucial 
for precision agriculture, forestry, and urban planning [57]. Spatial 
resolution refers to the smallest object or detail that can be detected or 
distinguished in an image captured by a remote sensing system. 
Different applications require different spatial resolutions; higher reso­
lutions of up to 30 m per pixel allows for detailed analysis of urban 
infrastructure or individual tree counting while lower resolution of 1 km 
per pixel is useful for region-scale land cover mapping [58]. This com­
bination of technologies allows for comprehensive monitoring and 
analysis, supporting the automation, optimisation, and sustainability 
goals of IR4.
3. Review methodology
The purpose of this literature review is to gather application of IR4 
technologies in the oil palm industry. The Scopus search engine was 
implemented to perform automated search for articles. The search string 
used was as follows: “oil palm” AND “industry 4.0″ OR “machine 
learning” OR “internet of things” OR “big data” OR “blockchain” OR 
“artificial intelligence” OR “automation” OR “cloud computing”. This 
search string was used for a more flexible search to avoid missing rele­
vant studies. The date range was set to 2014 – 2024 and the document 
type filter was set limit to Article. The initial search was performed 
based on the title, abstract, and keywords however, the full text was 
considered for final selection, categorisation, and information extrac­
tion. Several inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined to set 
boundaries for the review to filter search results. Inclusion criteria

In this section, the impact of IR4 technology on the oil palm industry

4.1. Application of IR4 technologies in the oil palm industry
In this section, the latest developments and applications of IR4 
technologies in the oil palm industry are discussed to understand the 
state of development for the gap analysis. Fig. 7 summaries the review 
outcome on the state of IR4 technologies usage in the respective oil palm 
industry applications, as well as example data collection and execution 
methods. Detailed review and discussion of each application are

4.1.1. Oil palm breeding
Recent studies have emphasised the enhancement of oil palm 
breeding methods by integrating modern biotechnological approaches 
and analytical techniques. These advancements incorporate genomic 
data, sophisticated phenotyping methodologies, and the application of 
ML technologies. Such integrations aim to streamline the oil palm 
breeding process, enabling shorter breeding cycles, refined genetic 
predictions, and the selection of superior breeding materials. Genetic 
markers are pivotal in describing genetic variations and their impacts on 
phenotypic traits. These markers are crucial for enhancing the accuracy 
of selection processes in breeding programs. For instance, one study 
employed genotyping-by-sequence (GBS) to analyse interspecific hy­
brids (F1) and backcrosses (BC2) of oil palm [60]. This research utilised 
ML algorithms, specifically Random Forest and Gradient Boosting, to 
predict phenotypic outcomes. The study identified 47 significant 
markers associated with composition of oleic acid in F1 hybrids, and 12 
markers connected to both oil composition and leaf width traits in BC2 
hybrids. The prediction from the ML models showed similar accuracy for 
different traits, with prediction errors less than 20 %. Another study 
assessed various marker systems and machine learning models for 
genomic selection to boost breeding efficiency and oil yield [64]. This 
study compared two genetic marker systems, simple sequence repeats 
(SSRs) and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), in predicting key 
traits of oil palm. The RF model had the highest accuracy for 
mesocarp-to-fruit (M/F) at 37 % and oil-to-dry mesocarp (O/DM) at 47 
%. Comparatively, SVM achieved the highest accuracy for shell-to-fruit 
(S/F) at 47 % and kernel-to-fruit (K/F) at 28 %. Overall, machine 
learning models performed slightly better overall, with average accu­
racies around 32 % with SNPs compared to 31 % for traditional statis­
tical methods. Additionally, a novel framework applying ML was 
developed to predict heritability in oil palm breeding, focusing on 
phenotypic data such as average bunch weight and oil content [65]. This 
approach promises to increase the precision and efficiency of breeding 
programs by concentrating on observable traits instead of relying on 
genetic data. Overall, these advancements collectively signify a shift 
towards more efficient and precise oil palm breeding strategies, utilising 
cutting-edge technologies to obtain significant improvements in genetic 
selection and breeding outcomes. These studies highlight the integration 
of genomic-wide association studies, ML, and genomic selection for

4.1.2. Autonomous vehicle
The adoption autonomous vehicle in oil palm plantation can reduce 
the reliance on manual labour for hazardous or physically demanding 
tasks, potentially improving worker safety and health. Robotic systems 
can also lead to more consistent and fair working conditions by mini­
mizing the potential for labour exploitation. Several studies explored the 
potential of autonomous vehicles and automated steering system for oil 
palm operations. A control system was developed based on enhanced 
self organizing incremental neural network (ESOINN) to control a 4- 
wheel skid steer mobile robot for navigating in an oil palm plantation 
[66]. The robot collects new data as it operates in the field, which are 
then used to further train the ESOINN model. The root mean square 
error of the robot path decreased significantly after three incremental 
learning stages, showing a reduction of about 46 %. The ESOINN model 
also had significant improvement with a 98.83 % reduction in learning 
time from initial to third incremental stage. Overall, ESOINN demon­
strated great learning time efficiency and consistent error reduction, 
making it suitable for real-time, adaptive applications in robot trajectory 
control. Another study designed a cost-effective unmanned ground 
vehicle (UGV) equipped with necessary sensors for autonomous navi­
gation to aid in-field operations [67]. Extensive testing was conducted to 
validate the performance of the UGV which included evaluations of the 
drive-by-wire system, steering response, throttle response, and 
path-tracking capabilities. The UGV can achieve a maximum speed of 45 
km/h which shows the potential for rapid transit across the plantation, 
that can be valuable for tasks requiring fast navigation, such as trans­
porting goods or monitoring large areas. The steering response has a low 
dead time of 20 milliseconds which is essential for quick adjustments, 
especially when avoiding obstacles or navigating tight spaces. The UGV 
also achieved dead time in throttle control of 133 ms at 0.5 m/s and 80 
ms at 1.0 m/s. Dead time in throttle control represents the delay be­
tween increasing the throttle and the acceleration of the robot. Lower 
dead times at higher speeds indicate better responsiveness, meaning the 
UGV can adjust its speed more quickly in response to commands, which 
is crucial for adapting to different field conditions. The capabilities of 
the robot in speed, range, and control make it suitable for various 
in-field operations, with some adjustments needed for consistent trac­
tion and braking on steep slopes and challenging surfaces. Additionally, 
another study evaluated the effectiveness of manual versus 
autopilot-automated steering systems in tractor operations for soil 
electrical conductivity mapping in an oil palm plantation [68]. The 
automated steering system significantly reduced the operator energy 
expenditure by 70.67 % compared to manual steering, reducing fatigue 
during field operations. The field capacity of tractor operations also 
improved by 21.63 % with the automated steering system achieving 
9.06 hectares per hour compared to 7.10 hectares per hour with manual 
steering. This improvement is attributed to better positioning accuracy 
and reduced overlapping of passes in the field. Overall, the integration of 
autonomous vehicles enables more efficient oil palm plantation 
operations.
4.1.3. Irrigation and pesticide application
Precise irrigation ensures adequate hydration of crops, reduces water 
consumption, and prevent waterlogging and nutrient leaching while 
efficient pesticide application protects crops from pests and diseases, 
minimizing reliance on chemical use. The potential of adopting smart 
irrigation systems is explored which consists of a combination of mois­
ture and temperature sensors, controllers, and servers to optimise water 
distribution, achieving optimal moisture conditions and increasing crop 
yield [69]. The study highlights the return on investment (ROI) is 
influenced by the size of the plantation and the yield improvement from 
using the smart irrigation system. The smart irrigation systems can be a 
positive investment for plantations larger than 1.5 hectares when using a 
local server setup and 1.75 hectares with a hybrid server setup. For 
plantations with sizes greater than 9.5 hectares, the ROI can exceed 400 
% over a 5-year period. In another study, the sustainability of three 
pesticide application methods in oil palm plantations was evaluated, 
focusing on economic, environmental, and social impacts [70]. Drone 
powered by either grid electricity, gasoline generator, and solar panel, 
were compared to tractors and knapsack based on ROI, carbon footprint, 
and exposure to pesticides. The use of drones offered great ROI between 
17 % to 40 %, and significantly reduced pesticide exposure times to 720 
h per year compared to 91,440 h per year with knapsack sprayer, 
minimizing health risk to operators. Although solar-powered drone had 
the lowest carbon footprint it achieved overall sustainability index of 
81.3 %. The gasoline-powered drone exhibited the highest overall sus­
tainability index of 83.4 %, offering a balance between sustainability 
and costs.
4.1.4. Soil moisture monitoring
A balanced and sufficient soil moisture is important to sustain a high 
yield in oil palm plantations. Therefore, having accurate soil condition 
data can assist in making informed irrigation decision. The effectiveness 
of the water cloud model (WCM) was evaluated in retrieving soil 
moisture in oil palm plantations using satellite radar data from PALSAR- 
2 [71]. The study found that HV polarisation combined with the leaf 
area index as a vegetation indicator could retrieve soil moisture with 
about 80 % accuracy. Building upon this, the field-based leaf area index 
was compared to SAR-derived radar vegetation index (RVI) to estimate 
soil moisture [72]. The model using field-based LAI under HV polar­
isation achieved root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.033 m³/m³ with an 
R² of 0.949, indicating high accuracy. Using RVI under HV polarisation 
produced similar results, with an RMSE of 0.031 m³/m³ and R² values as 
high as 0.960. These results suggest that radar-based indicators are 
reliable substitutes for field-based measurements, offering accurate soil 
moisture readings while effectively reducing the need for extensive 
field-based data collection. A novel approach was introduced to enhance 
soil monitoring in oil palm plantations using IoT technology [73]. The 
system collects pH and moisture levels using sensors and transmits them 
to a receiver via LoRa, an energy-efficient wireless technology with a 
long range which is suitable for plantations where internet connection is 
typically unavailable. The collected data is uploaded to an online plat­
form that workers can access via smartphones or computers. This allows 
them to view real-time soil conditions and make informed decisions 
without needing to physically check each site. The results demonstrate 
the capability of the system to monitor essential soil metrics where the 
LoRa signal maintained connectivity up to 500 m under tested condi­
tions. Another study used wireless sensor network (WSN) for gathering 
site-specific agrometeorological including soil moisture and pressure 
deficit [74]. Simulations were done to generate irrigation decisions from 
the collected agrometeorological data and compare oil palm yield with 
and without the proposed irrigation method. Results showed that when 
using the irrigation method, production of fresh fruit bunches increased 
by 27 %, proving its potential for enhancing production efficiency.
4.1.5. Palm fruit grading and ripeness assessment
Palm fruit grading and ripeness assessment are crucial in optimising 
oil yield and quality in palm oil production. Fruits at their optimal 
ripeness contain the highest quality oils, possessing ideal fatty acid 
compositions and enriched vitamin contents. IR4 advancements have 
led to the adoption of rapid, non-invasive technologies for accurate fruit 
grading and ripeness assessment, which are beginning to replace tradi­
tional manual methods. These innovations enhance productivity and 
ensure consistency across the oil palm sector. One notable development 
is the use of computer vision systems which provide quick, reliable, and 
non-destructive assessment for ripeness, oil content, and free fatty acid 
levels in oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) [75]. The system utilises 
multilayer perceptron artificial neural networks (MLP-ANN) and for­
ward stepwise multiple linear regression (FS-MLR), to precisely predict 
the quality parameters at a high throughput of 12 tons of FFB per hour.

The MLP-ANN proved optimal for ripeness and oil content predictions, 
achieving accuracy rates of 93.5 % and 96.41 %, while FS-MLR was most

segmentation for computer vision system, to improve the detection and 
grading of oil palm fruit ripeness. A novel contour-based approach was 
employed with Canny edge detection combined with morphological 
operations to handle diverse shapes and colours of the fruits [76]. The 
proposed method achieved an average segmentation accuracy of 90.13 
%, with a false positive error of 2.92 % and a false negative error of 5.20 
%. Another system involves the use of Microsoft Kinect cameras for 
automatic classification of FFB, capturing RGB, near-infrared, and depth 
images of the palm bunches [77]. Simple volume integration scheme 
(SVIS) was used to determine the relative volume of each FFB to esti­
mate its weight. Another algorithm uses colour and infrared data to 
classify the FFB into three grades based on estimated oil content. The 
Kinect-based system achieves an 83 % accuracy rate and can process one 
sample in about six seconds, making it a potentially efficient tool. 
Another automated system was developed to accurately determine the 
ripeness of oil palm fruits in real-time [78]. The system uses images of oil 
palm fruits taken by CCD cameras, where these images are processed to 
extract specific features such as colour and texture, using several tech­
niques including Gabor wavelets and statistical methods. The ANN 
showed high accuracy of 93 % and processing time of 0.44 s per image 
when combined with basic grey level halo matrix (BGLAM) extraction 
techniques, making it a suitable classifier for real-time maturity grading 
in oil palm fruit. Additionally, a compact model was developed which 
uses one-dimensional hue histograms instead of full-colour RGB images, 
reducing data size and speeding up processing [79]. The model applies 
dual-tree complex wavelet transforms (DT-CWT) to help remove noise 
from images, and long short-term memory (LSTM) network to classify 
images based on their hue characteristics. The model was trained on 800 
images of palm bunches and achieved 91.67 % accuracy even in noisy 
conditions.
One interesting study investigates the use of deep learning to classify 
the ripeness of oil palm FFB on mobile devices, which are more acces­
sible to farmers but limited in processing power [80]. The application of 
lightweight convolutional neural networks (CNN) on mobile devices 
showcases the adaptation of advanced models to platforms with limited 
computational resources, utilizing transfer learning and data augmen­
tation to improve classification accuracy. This optimised model reached 
89.3 % accuracy and classified images in about 96 milliseconds, making 
it a feasible tool for real-time ripeness classification using a mobile de­
vice. Another research investigates the application of CNN, specifically a 
pre-trained CNN model, AlexNet, for recognizing the ripeness of oil palm 
FFB using image analysis [81]. AlexNet achieved higher accuracy of 100 
% but required more processing time between 50.61 to 84.47 s, 
compared to the basic CNN model which performed best at 92 % ac­
curacy with processing time of 3.4 s.
Furthermore, the potential of Raman spectroscopy has also been 
explored for its capability to non-invasively determine the ripeness of oil 
palm fruit. One study utilised machine learning algorithm to analyse the 
components of organic compounds such as β-carotene and amino acid to 
distinguish the ripeness of fruits [82]. Results from this study showed 
that the medium, weighted KNN, and tri layered neural network clas­
sifier achieved an accuracy of 90.9 % in classifying the ripeness level of 
oil palm fruitlets. Another study used Raman spectroscopy to analyse 
carotene compounds such as β-carotene, lycopene, lutein, and neo­
xanthin in the fruit skin [83]. A total of 19 different machine learning 
models were tested to classify fruit samples based on these carotene 
indicators, where the best model, a KNN classifier, achieved 100 % ac­
curacy. Both studies suggests that Raman spectroscopy can reliably and 
quickly determine oil palm ripeness by analysing carotene levels, of­
fering a promising tool for improving harvest timing and oil quality.
Lastly, a technology was developed to monitor the quality of palm oil 
during processing by detecting chlorophyll levels in palm oil through 
light-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (LICF) [84]. The system also uses 
IoT connectivity to send data to the cloud, making it accessible remotely. 
The LICF system recorded data continuously with 500 ms integration 
time, averaging 10 readings, and a 1-minute interval between re­
cordings, resulting in approximately 1320 measurements per day. This 
innovation helps mill operators quickly assess fruit ripeness and oil 
quality, potentially improving efficiency and reducing the need for 
manual quality checks
4.1.6. Oil palm plantation site detection and mapping
Integration of ML techniques with remote sensing technologies has 
significantly enhanced the detection and classification of oil palm 
plantations. Several studies on oil palm tree detection from UAV images 
utilised the histogram of oriented gradients (HOG) feature extractor to 
identify distinctive patterns of palm trees. One study demonstrated the 
effectiveness of the XGBoost classifier, combined with HOG features, in 
accurately identifying tree species from UAV images, achieving an 
overall accuracy of 97.94 % [85]. Another study used HOG features to 
train an SVM classifier, which achieved an overall accuracy rate of 99.21 
% at the training site and ranged between 94.63 % and 99.90 % at four 
validation sites [86]. Additionally, a comparison of the performance of 
SVM and kNN classifiers in identifying tree species using HOG features 
revealed that the SVM classifier achieved an overall accuracy of 93.33 
%, while the kNN classifier achieved 85.71 % [87]. Furthermore, a study 
evaluated the performance of enhanced faster region-Based convolu­
tional neural network (EFRCNN) for detecting and counting oil palm 
trees in large-scale plantations from high-resolution drone images. Re­
sults from this study showed the EFRCNN model achieved the highest 
overall accuracy of 96.8 %, demonstrating higher accuracy than the 
other methods including the original FRCNN, SVM, and template 
matching [88].
The adaptation of CNNs to high-resolution satellite imagery has 
further advanced the field, enabling age classification of oil palm plan­
tations with high precision. A study classified oil palm plantations based 
on the ages of the trees using high-resolution Ikonos panchromatic im­
agery employing with CNNs such as AlexNet and VGG for multi-class 
classification task. The study achieved high classification accuracy 
with the VGG19 classifier which achieved an accuracy of 75.57 % - 
94.74 % [89]. The DeepLabv3+ CNN model was trained to differentiate 
between smallholder and industrial oil palm plantations based on can­
opy characteristics using Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 satellite images. The 
study successfully produced a global map of oil palm plantations and 
achieved an overall accuracy of 98.52 % [90]. Another study developed 
a method for detecting oil palm trees over large geographical areas using 
QuickBird satellite imagery [91]. A two-stage CNN approach was uti­
lised to accurately identify oil palm trees where one CNN was used for 
land cover classification and another one CNN for object (oil palm tree) 
classification. This approach achieved a classification accuracy of 95.47 
% for land-cover types and 91.53 % for object types on the validation 
samples. Furthermore, a study developed a DL-based approach, specif­
ically the Residual Channel Attention Network (RCANet), for mapping 
oil palm plantations from Google Earth high spatial-resolution satellite 
images [92]. The method was effectively applied for large-scale oil palm 
plantation mapping and achieved an overall accuracy of 96.88 %. 
Similarly, another study also used Google Earth satellite imagery to 
develop an efficient method for detecting oil palm plantations [93]. This 
was achieved by employing GoogLeNet which is a type of CNN to 
identify whether an image contains oil palm plantations. Once an image 
is confirmed to contain oil palm plantations, the images had their 
plantation delineated at higher accuracy. The Gabor texture feature with 
SVM method achieved an overall accuracy of 92.29 % which out­
performed other combinations of texture features and ML methods used 
in this study.

different satellite platforms. Another cloud computing platform is 
Remote Ecosystem Monitoring Assessment Pipeline (REMAP), that hosts 
an open source stacked Landsat data that allows land cover classification 
to be implemented using a built-in RF algorithm [103]. Classifications 
were performed to discriminate the land covers in Peninsular Malaysia: 
oil palm, built-up, bare soil, water, forest, other vegetation and paddy. 
The study achieved an overall accuracy of 80.34 % for the period 
1999–2003 (Period 1) and 79.53 % for the period 2014–2017 (Period 2). 
It was also found that the oil palm area in Peninsular Malaysia increased 
by 23.59 % from Period 1 to Period 2.

while Indonesia had about 1.23 billion oil palms covering 15.29 million 
hectares. Another study demonstrates that using data fusion and ML 
algorithms significantly improves the accuracy of land use or land cover 
classification, particularly for oil palm plantations in Ranong Province, 
Thailand [105]. Data fusion integrates multiple sources data including

Normalised Difference Water Index, Normalised Difference Vegetation 
Index, and Land Surface Temperature. In this study, the RF model ach­

auxiliary variables. Next, a study evaluated the effectiveness of using 
single date and multi-temporal Landsat 8 imagery to improve the ac­

large-scale oil palm plantations, smallholdings, and peat swamp forest 
using several classifiers. In this study, the Random Tree classifier using 
Landsat multi-temporal dataset achieved an overall accuracy of 95.54 
%, and the Kappa coefficient was 0.933, outperforming other classifiers 
like Maximum Likelihood and SVM. Moreover, another study compared 
SVM and MLC approaches for classification of oil palm plantations with 
Landsat ETM+ satellite images [107]. In this study, the SVM method 
achieved an overall accuracy of 78.3 % and a kappa statistic of 0.73, 
outperforming the MLC which had an overall accuracy of 71.9 % and 
scored 0.65 for the kappa statistic. Despite the success of the SVM al­
gorithm, the study acknowledges that the classification accuracy is still 
insufficient for large-scale implementation, where misclassification 
occurred mainly between young oil palm and shrubs or other crops. In 
addition, a method was developed for counting oil palm trees and esti­
mating their age using WorldView-3 satellite imagery and LiDAR data 
[108]. An SVM algorithm for object-based image analysis was employed 
to count oil palm trees achieved an overall accuracy of 98.80 %. Sub­
sequently, height models extracted from LiDAR data were integrated 
with multi regression methods to estimate the age of the trees which 
showed an overall accuracy of 84.91 %. Furthermore, one study com­
pares the coverage of oil palm plantation detection using visual inter­
pretation and automated classification methods across different regions 
of Borneo[109]. It was found that visual mapping detected 8.0 Mha of 
industrial oil palm plantation area, whereas the automated method 
detected only 3.8 Mha of closed canopy oil palm within these areas. 
Lastly, a study was done to generate accurate land cover maps of oil 
palm plantations in three districts located in Selangor, Malaysia using 
ML and Landsat 8 satellite imagery [110]. Performance of SVM, ANN, 
and MLC classification algorithms was assessed to determine which al­
gorithm provides the highest accuracy for oil palm mapping. Results 
from this study showed overall accuracy of SVM, ANN, and MLC were 
98.96 %, 99.39 %, and 15.30 % respectively.

ML to identify nutrient deficiencies in oil palm leaves [111]. The SVM 
with radial basis function (SVM-RBF), RBF, and ANN classifiers were 
compared in identification between healthy or magnesium, potassium, 
and nitrogen deficient oil palm leaves. The study found that the

A study evaluated the use of leaf spectral measurements to classify 
nitrogen (N) sufficiency levels in oil palm leaves, comparing the per­

simulated from SPOT-6 satellite data, the study found SVM to be 
particularly effective for N status classification, achieving accuracies

lower than RF. The study also highlighted that classification accuracy 
tends to decline with frond age, emphasizing the importance of selecting 
the appropriate frond for assessment.

chlorophyll status with r2 = 0.503, 0.718, 0.496, 0.328, 0.752 respec­

and boron (B) levels with r2 = 0.655, 0.491 respectively. Furthermore, a 
study highlighted the feasibility of nutrient levels specifically, N, P, K, 
Mg, and Ca, classification of oil palm trees using Landsat-8 satellite 
images combined with SVM, MLP, and RF ML algorithms [116]. SVM 
and RF models showed the best potential for classifying nutrient levels in 
oil palm, particularly for nitrogen and potassium, with accuracy around 
75–80 % for N and K, but P, Mg, and Ca levels were less accurately 
classified due to dataset limitations. Lastly, metabolomics was applied to 
analyse the impact of varying K levels on oil palm saplings. The 
orthogonal projection to latent structures ML model was employed to 
analyse metabolic data and predict K requirements [117]. Results 
showed that only 6 % of the samples were misclassified, typically be­

showing the strong classification capability of the model for K suffi­
ciency. This study demonstrated that metabolomic profiles could pro­
vide direct insights into the potassium status of plants and their growth 
potential, highlighting the effectiveness of metabolomics as a diagnostic 
tool capable of reducing environmental impact from excessive fertiliser 
use.
4.1.8. Disease detection and classification
The traditional method of oil palm tree disease detection is done by 
experienced personnel via visual inspection which can be time 
consuming. Recent advancements in remote sensing and ML have been 
vital to improve time efficiency and automate the detection process. 
Several studies have utilised unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) equipped 
with hyperspectral or multispectral camera and ML algorithms for 
detection and classification of BSR disease in oil palm trees. The 
multilayer perceptron (MLP) model was used to learn spectral features 
from different infection stages [118]. In this study, hyperspectral images 
undergo pre-processing step followed by disease detection by the MLP 
model achieving overall accuracy of 87 %. Another study automates BSR 
detection at the seedling stage using DL model and hyperspectral images 
[119]. Segmented images of oil palm seedlings were generated using 
mask region-based convolutional neural network (Mask RCNN) model 
to investigate if background images affect the performance of the 
detection. A 16-layer deep CNN (VGG16) model was then trained on 
both original and segmented images. The VGG16 model trained on 
original images performed better which revealed that BSR detection can 
be done without having to manually extract image features. The random 
forest (RF) ML algorithm was used in the classification of oil palm trees 
into four BSR infection categories; healthy, early-stage, late-stage, and 
dead [49]. This study identified spectral features related to the different 
stages and highlighted the effectiveness of using few spectral bands in 
the red-edge region for classifying the infection categories. UAV multi­
spectral data was used to compare RF, Support Vector Machine (SVM), 
and Maximum Likelihood (MLC) classifiers for classification of BSR 
disease [120]. The result from this study showed that the overall accu­
racy of RF (81 %) followed by SVM (68 %) and MLC (53 %).
Satellite imagery and was also combined for BSR detection and 
classification. Eight bands of reflectance values from multispectral 
WorldView-3 satellite imagery was examined where the yellow band 
(585 – 625 nm) was found to be the most significant band for separating 
between classes of BSR severity levels. Supervised ML algorithms, De­
cision Tree (DT), RF, and SVM, were used to classify the severity of BSR 
disease in an oil palm plantation although in this study, the overall ac­
curacy achieved was low at approximately 53 % - 54 % [121]. ALOS 
PALSAR-2 satellite imagery was used to identify potential backscatter 
variables and examine the performance of MLP and RF model in clas­
sifying oil palm trees that are non-infected and infected with BSR [122]. 
The MLP classifier had a higher success rate in correctly classifying 
non-infected (100 %) and infected (91.30 %) while the RF model also 
performed well with a success rate of 94.11 % for non-infected and 
91.30 % for infected trees using HV polarisation. This shows that SAR 
data, particularly using the HV polarisation, could be a reliable 
approach for detecting BSR disease. The QuickBird satellite imagery was 
used to evaluate three ML models: SVM, RF, and classification and 
regression tree (CART) for predicting, classifying, and mapping BSR 
disease in oil palm plantations [123]. The red band of QuickBird im­
agery was noted as the most important variable for classifying healthy 
palms while the NIR band was the most important for unhealthy palms. 
The RF achieved an overall accuracy of 91 % followed by CART and SVM 
at 80 % and 77 % respectively.
Thermal imaging also possesses potential for early detection of basal 
stem rot (BSR) in oil palm seedlings. A study analyses thermal properties 
such as maximum, minimum, mean, and standard deviation of pixel 
intensity values to identify significant differences between healthy and 
infected seedlings [124]. Various ML models, including SVM and 
k-nearest neighbour (k-NN), were applied, achieving a high accuracy 
with the SVM model. Another study employed ML classifiers, Naïve 
Bayes (NB), MLP, and RF, along with imbalance data approaches to 
classify between healthy and infected oil palm trees [125]. The 
maximum temperature, Tmax was the most indicative feature for classi­
fying tree health using the random oversampling (ROS) approach. 
Dielectric spectroscopy was combined with ML, specifically the linear 
discriminant analysis (LDA) algorithm to detect BSR in oil palm [126]. 
By employing a genetic algorithm for feature selection, the study effi­
ciently reduces data complexity while retaining crucial information for 
disease classification. Terrestrial laser scanning data was utilised

Ganoderma boninense (G. boninense), a causative agent of BSR, has 
been the subject of various studies focusing on early detection. Near- 
infrared (NIR) spectroscopy was coupled with ML models for classifi­

higher spectral reflectance data compared to healthy samples. The de­

precision and sensitivity with efficient performance time.
Oil palm leaf diseases are caused by various pathogens including 
fungi, bacteria, and viruses. A study developed a new method for 
detecting oil palm leaf disease by applying colour feature extraction 
colour spaces [132]. K-means clustering was used for segmenting the 
leaf areas and PCA was employed to reduce feature dimensions and 
select the most important features. An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) 
was used to classify the leaves based on selected features, achieving high 
performance in differentiating between healthy and infected leaves. 
Another study enhanced the detection of oil palm leaf diseases using 
convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly focusing on AlexNet 
and the combination of AlexNet with SVM [133]. This study demon­
strated that AlexNet CNN outperformed a hybrid AlexNet-SVM in terms 
of disease detection accuracy and found that DL models can automati­
cally handle feature extraction and classification more efficiently than 
traditional ML methods.
Metisa plana, also known as bagworm, is a pest that can cause severe 
defoliation of oil palm trees from feeding activity of the larvae. It has 
rapid reproduction rate and brief life cycle, potentially spreading to 
epidemic proportions significantly reduce oil palm yield. A study uti­
lised UAV to collect multispectral data for key vegetative indices 
calculation [134]. The KNN ML model classifies severity of infestations 
based on the most relevant indices as classification features. Hyper­
spectral imaging was also combined with ML to distinguish between 
instar stages of Metisa plana larvae (Mohd Johari et al., 2022). Hyper­

differentiate between instar stages. The Weighted K-Nearest Neighbour 
classifier effectively categorised the larvae and identified significant 
wavelengths and morphological features of the larvae with high preci­
sion and accuracy. Green wavelengths were found to be particularly 
effective due to their correlation with chlorophyll content from the 
leaves fed upon by the larvae. Prediction of Metisa plana population in 
oil palm plantations was done by examining satellite-measured relative 
humidity [135]. Regression analyses and artificial neural network 
(ANN) were applied to develop predictive models, where ANN models 
were more adept at capturing the non-linear relationships between 
relative humidity and Metisa plana populations. In another study, ANN 
model with feature selection techniques like Genetic Algorithm, Back­
ward Stepwise, and Forward Stepwise was used to analyse the rela­
tionship between weather parameters and the presence of bagworm 
larvae at different stages [136]. Red palm weevil is another destructive 
pest that target oil palm trees. The larvae feed on the soft fibres and 
terminal buds of trunks, eventually hollowing out the tree from the in­
side. This feeding can go undetected initially, making early detection 
difficult through visual inspection. A study aims to improve early 
detection of the Red Palm Weevil using optical fibre distributed acoustic 
sensing (DAS) enhanced by convolutional neural networks (CNNs) 
[137]. The optical fibre DAS captures acoustic signals from trees while 
CNNs was employed to analyse the collected acoustic data and distin­
guish between infested and healthy signals with high accuracy.
These studies focus on different diseases and pests, employing 
technologies such as UAV hyperspectral imaging, multispectral imaging, 
thermal imaging, simulation modelling, and so on, highlighting the 
effectiveness of various ML and statistical models in improving the 
detection and classification accuracy.

help track and reduce these emissions [138]. The supply chain phases 
were analysed for carbon emissions, where key pollutants, such as car­

integrated with the carbon credit system where entities that reduce their

GIS maps that visualise the distribution of above-ground carbon stocks 
across vast forest areas. The diverse ecosystems of Peru were found to 
harbour 6.928 petagrams (Pg) of carbon, with only 2.9 Pg located in 
protected areas. The study also estimated net carbon emissions of 0.096 
Pg and a carbon sink of 0.08 Pg across 1.7 million hectares and 1.5 
million hectares, respectively between the year 2012 and 2017. Overall, 
this study demonstrates that satellite technology can help tropical

4.1.10. Palm oil yield prediction
The IR4 application for oil palm yield prediction includes a variety of 
methods featuring ML techniques, big data analytics, and satellite and 
remote sensing technologies to enhance yield prediction accuracy and 
efficiency in the management of oil palm plantations. Several studies 
focused on applying ML techniques to predict oil palm yields. A study 
explores the use of Extreme Learning Machines (ELM) which is a type of 
feedforward neural network to predict palm oil production [144]. 
Several parameters were considered including planting age, land area, 
number of trees, and yields where the mean average percentage error 
(MAPE) was used to evaluate the performance of the predictive model. 
The ELM model demonstrated moderately accurate prediction for 
monthly palm oil production with an average MAPE of 20.1 %. Another 
study evaluated the ML model, Extra Tree Regressor and AdaBoost Re­
gressor, based on six statistical evaluation metrics [145]. The models 
identified several key features that significantly impacted oil palm yield 
prediction including rainfall, cloud amount, number of rain days, wind 
speed, and root zone soil wetness. The Extra Tree Regressor and Ada­
Boost Regressor achieved mean R2 values of 0.61 and 0.63 respectively, 
indicating a good fit for the data.
Apart from that, satellite remote sensing technologies offer a valu­
able data source for oil palm yield prediction. The utilisation of NDVI 
and other vegetation indices derived from satellite imagery provide 
crucial insights into the physiological status of crops, which directly 
correlate with yield potentials. One study presents a new method to 
predict the yield of oil palm using machine learning and time-series data 
from Landsat satellite imagery, focusing on a vegetation index called 
NDVI, which indicates plant health [146]. The researchers used algo­
rithms, including RF and AdaBoost, to classify land as oil palm or non-oil 
palm and predict future yields based on chronological changes of 
maximum NDVI values of oil palm between the ages of 8 and 18 years. 
The RF model achieved a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 0.384, 
Mean Square Error (MSE) of 0.148, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 
0.147. As comparison, the AdaBoost model had a slightly higher error 
with an RMSE of 0.410, MSE of 0.168, and MAE of 0.176, indicating less 
accuracy than the RF model. In addition, yield prediction models were 
developed using various ML and DL algorithms including multiple linear 
regression, XGBoost, support vector regression, random forest, and a 
deep neural network [146]. Multi-source data were integrated, such as 
satellite-derived vegetation indices, satellite-derived climatic variables 
such as land surface temperature and rainfall precipitation, weather 
station data, and field surveys. Among the models, deep neural network 
achieved the best performance particularly with selected predictors 
achieving R2 = 0.91, RMSE = 2.92 tons/ha, MAE = 2.56 tons/ha, MAPE 
= 0.09 tons/ha. Another study predicted oil palm yield using vegetation 
and humidity indices derived from Landsat satellite images and various 
ML techniques [147]. Moisture-related indices, such as NDMI and MSI 
which are related to water content and stress in the plants, showed the 
highest correlations with yield. In this study, the neural network model 
showed the best performance for multi-species palm data with R2 =
0.8139, RMSE = 0.3437 t/ha, and MAE = 0.2605 t/ha. On the other 
hand, the RF model performed best for the AVROS variety with R2 =
0.8214, RMSE = 0.3452 t/ha, and MAE = 0.2669 t/ha.
The integration of big data analytics in oil palm yield prediction is 
another important area. A study focuses on using a big data approach to 
improve operations in the oil palm plantation industry. A weight esti­
mation algorithm was introduced that helps plantation managers esti­
mate the weight of harvested fruit more accurately and in real-time 
[148]. This algorithm is implemented in a portable device that workers 
can carry around the plantation. By centralizing data from different 
sources such as field sensors, plantation management systems, and 
weighbridges, the system provides plantation managers with up-to-date 
data to make quicker, more informed decisions. This helps reduce waste 
such as fruit left behind in the fields and minimises errors from manual 
data entry at weighbridges. The system achieved a ± 5 % tolerance level 
between the estimated and actual weights measured at the weighbridge, 
demonstrating reliability in the weight prediction model. A real-world 
example of implementing big data analytics in oil palm plantations is 
the Plantation Micro Macro Program (PMMP) utilized by a large estate 
in Labis, Johor, Malaysia [149]. By digitizing all estate operations, 
PMMP provided a mobile system that recorded activities with photo 
evidence and GPS location stamps, ensuring data accuracy and trans­
parency. This digital transformation from 2015 to 2020 led to significant 
improvements where mill ripeness increased from an average of 90.20 % 
to 97.70 %, oil extraction rate increased from 19.20 % to 19.80 %, yield 
per hectare rose from 19.20 tons to 24.15 tons, and harvesting rounds 
enhanced from 2.30 rounds to 3.21 rounds. Within 12 months, the 
plantation also realised a remarkable ROI of 728 %. This significant ROI 
highlights the value of digital transformation in enhancing the opera­
tional efficiency and profitability of oil palm plantations. Overall, there 
are multiple approaches to oil palm yield prediction, employing 
advanced ML techniques, robust big data frameworks, and the strategic 
use of satellite and remote sensing data. These methods contribute to 
understanding yield dynamics and offer substantial improvements in the 
accuracy and reliability of predictions.

4.1.12. Crude palm oil price prediction
The evolution of forecasting methodologies concerning the crude 
palm oil (CPO) market, has seen significant advancements with the 
integration of IR4 technologies. There is a trend toward employing ML 
models with past production and environmental data to enhance the 
predictive accuracy of CPO prices. One study used a large dataset 
covering CPO prices from 2010 to 2020 to train and test the k-NN model 
for forecasting CPO prices [156]. The goal was to improve forecasting 
accuracy by measuring how sensitive predictions were to errors using 
Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), combined with a metric called 
the detection rate. The k-NN model achieved a MAPE of 0.013526 % 
when forecasting CPO prices, indicating a very low error rate in pre­
dicting future prices based on past data. When combined with the 
detection rate, the sensitivity of the model improved further, resulting in 
a MAPE sensitivity value of 0.000361 %. This very low percentage 
shows that combining MAPE with the detection rate can greatly enhance 
accuracy in forecasting. Another study explored the impact of the Simple 
Evolving Connectionist System (SECoS), an evolution-based artificial 
neural network, on CPO price forecasting to improve business intelli­
gence capabilities of companies involved in palm oil production and 
trading [157]. The research focuses on predicting crude palm oil prices 
for the upcoming two weeks by analysing previous data and trends. The 
study showed minimal error rate, where the forecasting model achieved 
a MAPE value of 0.035 %, which improves decision-making for busi­
nesses. Additionally, incorporating this forecasting into an e-commerce 
and business intelligence model reduced operational costs by 33.3 % in 
trading activities by facilitating direct transactions between CPO sellers 
and buyers. Furthermore, a time series-based forecasting model using 
Long Short-Term Memory (LTSM) was developed to forecast CPO prices 
[158]. Past CPO prices and weather variables data such as temperature 
and rainfall are used as input factors to predict the future price 
movements. The LSTM model demonstrated an average accuracy of 90 
% in predicting monthly CPO price fluctuations which suggests that 
LSTM-based forecasting models are highly effective in predicting CPO 
prices when weather elements are considered as predictors. The inte­
gration of these advanced methodologies aligns with the broader trends 
of IR4, where data-driven decision-making and automation are impor­
tant. The use of ML models not only enhances predictive accuracy but 
also supports more informed and timely decision-making processes, 
essential for managing the unpredictable nature of the CPO market.
4.1.13. Environmental conservation
Advanced predictive models using ML and big data analytics are 
effective tools for managing and preventing deforestation related to oil 
palm plantation expansion. The utility of these models lies in their 
ability to analyse vast amounts of data, identify patterns, and make 
accurate predictions about deforestation activities, public perception, 
and future impacts on biodiversity. One study explores the random 
forest model to predict deforestation specifically due to oil palm plan­
tations [159]. The authors developed a framework that uses satellite 
images and various criteria such as proximity to existing plantations, 
mills, roads, and water sources to identify and predict deforestation 
patterns related to oil palm. The model was tested in the Aceh province 
of Indonesia, an area with high deforestation and dense oil palm plan­
tations. By examining criteria like distance to plantations and clustering 
deforested areas, the study aimed to accurately predict areas at risk of oil 
palm-related deforestation. The model achieved a reasonable accuracy 
of 82 %, supporting its potential to guide decision-makers in planning 
sustainable palm oil production while minimizing deforestation impacts 
. Another study developed a predictive model using Structural Equation 
Modelling to analyse the relationships between identified themes in 
public opinions regarding palm oil and its environmental impacts [160]. 
SAS Text Analytics was used to process the text data from 4260 online 
social media posts related to palm oil, which involved text parsing, 
filtering, clustering, and topic modelling to identify the key themes. 
Overall, the study highlights the role of social media in shaping public 
discourse on palm oil, suggesting that both governments and corpora­
tions need to address environmental concerns more effectively. The 
researchers also propose a predictive model to further understand how 
these online discussions might influence sustainability practices. Lastly, 
a PREDICTS (Projecting Responses of Ecological Diversity in Changing 
Terrestrial Systems) database which compiles diversity data across four 
continents was used to assess biodiversity measures [161]. The study 
employs linear mixed-effects models to predict how further expansion of 
oil palm plantations and other land-use changes might impact biodi­
versity in the future. This insight helps conservationists prioritise actions 
in regions where biodiversity is most threatened by land-use changes, 
such as Asia, where oil palm plantations have a particularly severe 
impact. To summarise, advanced predictive models offer powerful tools 
for managing and preventing deforestation and understanding its 
broader impacts. These models can provide high-accuracy predictions, 
uncover underlying trends, and inform targeted conservation strategies. 
Predictive models can significantly contribute to environmental con­
servation efforts and the sustainable management of tropical forests.

particularly with respect to environmental, social, and economic im­
pacts as summarised in Fig. 8. Identifying these impacts is important for 
greater transparency, which is crucial for engaging with stakeholders, 
including investors, customers, and local communities. Transparent 
operations build trust and can lead to more collaborative and supportive 
relationships with stakeholders. Understanding the impacts also helps in

4.2.1. Environmental impact
The various application of IR4 technologies in the oil palm industry 
focuses on reducing negative impacts towards the environment and 
promoting sustainability. ML models and remote sensing methods play a 
crucial role in the management of diseases and pests.
Recently, Malaysia has launched an AI-driven smart palm oil mill, 
which is capable in boosting efficiency and sustainability in the palm oil 
industry. This innovation uses artificial intelligence and advanced sen­
sors to optimise processes, reduce waste, and enhance energy efficiency. 
The system supports eco-friendly practices in palm oil production, 
addressing global demands for sustainability while improving produc­
tivity [162]. In oil palm plantations, drones are being used effectively to 
boost pest management, specifically for pests like the Rhinoceros Beetle, 
which can damage young palms [163]. A single drone team can cover 
15–20 hectares daily, significantly more than productivity through 
manual labour. This ensures that all plants are treated promptly, which 
is crucial for maintaining crop health during critical growth phases. 
Furthermore, as of June 2024, agricultural drones have already treated 
more than 500 million hectares of farmland around the world [164]. 
This has led to significant savings, including a cumulative reduction of 
210 million metric tons of water and 47,000 t of pesticides. Therefore, 
timely and targeted treatment offered by drones reduces the need for 
widespread chemical treatments, preventing detrimental effects to the 
environment while promoting healthier crop. Apart from that, the 
deployment of IoT devices facilitates real-time soil monitoring providing 
critical data that help optimise irrigation and fertilisation practices 
through drones or smart irrigation systems [69]. This ensures water and 
fertilisers are used efficiently, minimising resource wastage and pre­
venting over-irrigation which could lead to soil erosion and nutrient 
leaching.
Blockchain technology has been introduced to enhance traceability 
and accountability of greenhouse gas emissions throughout the palm oil 
supply chain. For example, a company has developed a protocol for 
tokenizing live, certified carbon credits from nature-based projects on 
the blockchain [165]. The approach involves tokenizing carbon credits 
from various projects that reduce or remove CO₂ from the atmosphere. 
Each token represents one metric ton of CO₂ equivalent emissions 
reduced or removed. Additionally, remote sensing technologies enable 
precise mapping of plantation boundaries which is crucial for moni­
toring deforestation activities and land-use changes. Global Forest 
Watch is an innovative online platform developed by the World Re­
sources Institute that provides real-time data on global forest cover, 
deforestation, and land-use change [166]. GFW uses satellite imagery to 
monitor changes in forest cover globally, allowing users to track 
deforestation activities and land-cover changes in near real-time.
Several gaps have been identified based on other studies related to 
the environmental impact of IR4 technology application in the agricul­
tural sector. One of them is the indirect effects towards local wildlife 
from increased technological activities. For example, the frequent flights 
of UAVs throughout plantations could potentially cause significant dis­
turbances to wild animals because of their noise [86]. These activities 
could disrupt animal behaviours or contribute to habitat fragmentation. 
There are also potential difficulties in integrating new technologies with 
existing agricultural infrastructure which could delay technology 
adoption. One primary example is the lack of suitable infrastructure in 
rural areas for network availability which are crucial to transfer data 
efficiently between sensors and other devices via the internet [167]. 
Furthermore, IoT devices and sensors are usually low-power but can 
become energy-intensive when deployed in large numbers. They often 
rely on batteries or solar power, which require periodic maintenance 
and optimal environmental conditions to function efficiently [168]. 
Integration of smart farming practices with better renewable energy 
systems can effectively address the challenge related to energy con­
sumption [169]. Addressing these issues will ensure that technological 
advancements contribute positively to environmental sustainability.
4.2.2. Social impact
The social benefits of applying IR4 technologies in the oil palm in­
dustry offer a transition that allows more automated and technologically 
advanced practices, apart from providing improvements in job quality 
and social upliftment. Deployment of technologies like blockchain for 
traceability in the supply chain not only enhances transparency but also 
supports fair trade practices. This can lead to better income distribution 
and reduced exploitation, ensuring that the economic benefits of palm 
oil production are more equally shared among local communities [150]. 
The introduction of advanced technologies reduces the reliance on 
manual labour for repetitive tasks like palm fruit grading and ripeness 
assessment [84]. While this can lead to job displacement in certain 
areas, it also opens opportunities for local workforce training in new

4.2.3. Economic impact
The economic impact of IR4 on the oil palm industry offers sub­
stantial improvements in operational efficiency, cost reduction, and 
product quality. These factors drive economic growth in the oil palm 
sector, aligning it with future market demands. For example, the inte­
gration of AI, remote sensing, and advanced ML algorithms into palm oil 
production significantly enhances the efficiency of operations. Disease 
detection through hyperspectral imaging and thermal imaging allows 
for early intervention, reducing crop losses and enhancing yield [120]. 
The use of predictive analytics and simulation models in assessing the 
impacts of climate change on disease progression in oil palm plantations 
allows producers to anticipate and mitigate risks. This proactive 
approach in managing potential future scenarios helps in ensuring 
steady production levels, which is crucial for economic stability [8]. AI 
models have been extensively used to predict palm oil yield to optimise 
production process. These models analyse large datasets such as past 
yield data, weather patterns, and soil conditions to forecast future yields 
[147]. Advanced analytics has also been used to integrate various data 
sources and process large datasets to identify trends and make informed 
decisions about future yields [148]. Forecasting yields allows producers 
to plan their finances better and ensure they can meet production targets 
and financial obligations. DL models were used to predict crude palm oil 
prices. Producers can plan production schedules and optimise inventory 
management based on the expected price trends. Artificial neural 
Network (ANN) models were used to predict future crude palm oil prices 
based on datasets of historical prices [156]. Long short-term memory 
(LSTM) model excels at predicting future values in time series data, 
making them useful for crude palm oil price prediction based on his­
torical price data along with weather variables [112]. Integration of 
machine vision systems, DL models, and IoT provide more accurate and 
efficient method of palm fruit grading and ripeness assessment [84]. 
These technologies provide consistent grading to ensure the palm fruits 
meet quality standards which is important for customer satisfaction and 
minimising waste. Proper ripeness assessment ensures fruits are picked 
at the peak ripeness levels which can fetch better prices in the market. 
Lastly, the adoption of genomic data and ML in oil palm breeding ac­
celerates the development of high-yield, disease-resistant oil palm va­
rieties. Shorter breeding cycles and refined genetic predictions reduce 
the time and cost associated with developing new varieties, thereby 
enhancing the economic returns on research and development in­
vestments [65].
One barrier to the adoption of IR4 technologies is the high initial cost 
of purchasing the technology and other associated expenses [174]. 
Small and medium enterprises, find it challenging to afford these high 
initial costs due to limited financial resources. Therefore, a detailed 
cost-benefit analysis of IR4 technologies implementation done in a few 
studies can help justify the initial costs and visualise the expected eco­
nomic returns over time to help stakeholders make informed investment 
decisions [175]. For example, Chen [176] presented an assessment on 
the potential economic costs involved in three different insecticide 
spraying processes for a 180 ha oil palm plantation. There is a significant 
initial equipment costs, including two drones for about $21,427 and 32 
batteries for $19,695 as shown in Table 3. These initial costs will become 
more prominent, especially for larger sized plantations. Therefore, 
smallholder plantation owners could benefit from further exploration 
into information regarding financing or subsidy options available to 
support adoption of these technologies [177]. Furthermore, the effects 
of technology adoption on local job markets also need to be thoroughly 
examined to understand how the introduction of IR4 technology in the 
oil palm industry affects employment levels and wage structures [178]. 
Addressing these gaps could provide a more robust understanding of the 
economic implications of integrating IR4 technologies into the oil palm 
industry, ensuring that economic benefits are maximised and equally 
shared across the industry.
5. Limitations and future directions towards industrial 
revolution 5.0
IR4 marks a technological revolution, characterised by the integra­
tion of technologies like IoT, AI, big data analytics, blockchain and 
remote sensing. However, this rapid digital transformation often leads to 
an overemphasis on technology at the expense of other crucial aspects 
like human factors, sustainability, and adaptability. The main priority of 
IR4 is to automate processes, focusing on improving mass production by 
digitalisation and AI-driven technologies, to increase efficiency and to 
reduce human intervention in manufacturing processes. IR5 places more 
emphasis on people at the centre of production processes, so to achieve 
social goals alongside industry growth; the latter is done by focusing on 
human factor, environmental sustainability, supply chain resilience, and 
the integration of new technologies [179].

IR4 primarily focuses on implementing cutting-edge technologies 
such as AI, remote sensing and IoT devices to handle labour-intensive 
tasks. While this can maximise efficiency and reduce cost, it can also

reduce the direct involvement of workers in the processes. The knowl­

identification are overshadowed by automated systems [180]. This loss 
of craftsmanship can affect the quality of the work and reduce the sense 
of pride and fulfilment among workers. Furthermore, heavy reliance on 
automated systems can lead to worker alienation and decreased job 
satisfaction, where employees may feel disconnected from the work 
process as their roles become more about monitoring machines rather 
than engaging in hands-on tasks. Continuous interaction with machines 
and a lack of engaging tasks might also lead to mental health issues, such 
as stress and depression, as workers feel their roles are diminished in 
significance [181]. In the oil palm industry, particularly in rural com­
munities, the traditional ways of working are integral to the social 
fabric. Traditional farming practices often involve collaborative work 
that fosters social bonds within the community. The shift from manual 
labour to automated systems reduces the need for such collaboration, 
disrupting cultural practices and social structures that have been in 
place for generations, potentially weakening these social ties [182]. As a 
result, communities may resist the adoption of new technologies, fearing 
the loss of their traditional ways of life. This resistance can hinder the 
implementation of IR4 technologies and create friction between workers 
and management. The rapid adoption of new technologies in IR4 creates 
a demand for new skills in digital literacy, machinery operations, and 
data analysis [183]. However, this rapid technological change can 
outpace the ability of the existing workforce to adapt. The skill gap can 
slow down the adoption of new technologies, as companies may struggle 
to find or train workers with the necessary skills to operate and maintain 
advanced systems. Lower-skilled workers are at the highest risk of job 
displacement as their roles are automated. Without support for educa­
tion and skills development, these workers may lack access to resources 
necessary for retraining causing them to struggle to transition into new 
roles within the industry.
To address these limitations, IR5 emerges as a complementary 
paradigm that re-integrates the human touch into technological envi­
ronments. It emphasises collaboration between humans and machines, 
where technology enhances human capabilities without replacing them 
to achieve higher productivity, creativity, and worker well-being [184]. 
Collaborative robots (cobots) can potentially assist workers in physically 
demanding tasks such as FFB harvesting, reducing physical strain on 
workers while keeping them actively involved in the process. This 
collaborative approach ensures that workers do not feel displaced or 
redundant. For example, researchers at the Universitat Polit`ecnica de 
Catalunya were collaborating with European partners on the CANOPIES 
project to develop robots that work alongside humans to harvest and 
prune table grape vines [185]. The project focuses on developing robots 
for human-robot collaboration, which can identify ripe grapes and 
determining optimal pruning points. Significant investments in training 
and education would be needed for workforce upskilling which is 
important for workers to handle cobots and intelligent systems, along­
side ergonomic design improvements for machinery [186]. Recently, 
virtual and augmented reality (XR) technologies are being used to 
effectively train and upskill farmers [187]. VR simulations can create 
immersive learning experiences where multiple trainees and educators 
can interact in real-time, regardless of their physical location. This al­
lows for collaborative learning and knowledge sharing, even across vast 
distances. These human-centric approach ensures that technological 
progress benefits all stakeholders and leads to a more inclusive and 
sustainable industrial future. By integrating human skills and creativity 
into the work process, IR5 aims to enhance job satisfaction where 
workers can engage in more meaningful tasks that require 
problem-solving, decision-making, and creativity, thereby fostering a 
sense of purpose and connection to their works.
5.2. Sustainability
There are also limitations concerning sustainability associated with 
IR4 technologies. In the oil palm industry, the integration IoT devices 
and automated systems for monitoring and optimising farming practices 
significantly increases energy demands. These technologies often run 
continuously, necessitating robust power supplies, primarily by non- 
renewable energy sources such as fossil fuel for electricity generation 
[188]. This increased energy consumption not only raises production 
costs but also contributes to a higher carbon footprint, which is coun­
terproductive to the environmental sustainability goals of the industry. 
The rapid adoption of new technologies in the oil palm industry, such as 
upgraded sensors, drones, and data processing equipment, results in a 
quicker turnover of electronic devices. As these devices become obsolete 
faster due to technological advancements, they contribute to growing 
volumes of e-waste [189]. Proper disposal and recycling mechanisms are 
crucial, as mishandling of this waste can lead to severe environmental 
contamination and health risks, especially in countries where regulatory 
frameworks for e-waste management are insufficient or inadequately 
enforced. Furthermore, the benefits of IR4 technologies in the oil palm 
sector are not uniformly distributed. Advanced technologies tend to be 
more accessible in regions with better infrastructure and more sub­
stantial investments [190]. This access disparity can worsen the digital 
divide, where smaller producers or those in less developed areas might 
not gain the same advantages from technological advancements. This 
divide can lead to unequal growth opportunities and deepen 
socio-economic disparities within the industry.
IR5 encourages the use of smart grids and renewable energy sources 
to power operations. By integrating solar, wind, or bioenergy solutions 
specifically tailored to the needs and locations of palm oil production 
facilities, the industry can reduce its reliance on fossil fuels [191]. There 
is also an emphasis on energy efficiency in every layer of technology 
deployment, from the design of IoT devices and sensors to the operation 
of data centres. Energy-efficient automation systems that adapt their 
energy use based on real-time data can also reduce overall energy con­
sumption significantly [192]. IR5 also promotes recycling, reuse, and 
reduction of waste. One way to achieve this is through the development 
of more durable devices made using advanced manufacturing tech­
niques such as 3D printing and modular design [193]. This can extend 
the life of electronic devices used in palm oil production, reducing the 
volume of e-waste generated. By focusing on a circular economy, IR5 
also encourages the recycling and repurposing of electronic compo­
nents. New business models could be developed around the refurbish­
ment of old equipment or the harvesting of valuable materials from 
outdated technology, which would also create new job opportunities 
within the industry. Additionally, IR5 encourages the development of 
scalable and modular technologies that can grow with the business. 
Smaller palm oil producers can start with basic functionalities and 
gradually add more advanced features as needed, spreading the in­
vestment over time [194]. This reduces the financial burden and en­
courages broader participation in digital transformation.
5.3. Resilient supply chain
Supply chain management revolutionised through automation, data 
analytics, and interconnected systems of IR4 technologies brings several 
limitations, particularly in terms of building resilient supply chains. 
Reliance on digital infrastructure including IoT devices, cloud 
computing, and data analytics platforms, to streamline supply chain 
operations makes it more vulnerable to cyber-attacks, data breaches, 
and other security threats. A successful cyber-attack can disrupt opera­
tions, leading to significant financial losses and reputational damage 
[195]. Dependence on digital infrastructure also means that any tech­
nical failures, such as server outages or software bugs can halt supply 
chain activities. To keep the digital infrastructure running smoothly, 
continuous investment is required for maintenance and upgrades, which 
can be costly and resource intensive. In addition, the Malaysian Sus­
tainable Palm Oil (MSPO) certification scheme is a national certification 
standard in Malaysia aimed at ensuring sustainable palm oil production

that is environmentally friendly, socially responsible, and economically 
viable [196]. A similar initiative by Indonesia is the Indonesia Sustain­
able Palm Oil (ISPO) certification [197]. Despite these initiatives, 
ensuring sustainability and traceability throughout the supply chain 
through implementation of advanced technology remains complex and 
challenging [198]. An integrated data system is required to manage and 
verify large volumes of data from each stage of the supply chain such as 
the source of the FFBs, transportation details, processing methods, and 
product quantities. Furthermore, different technological solutions may 
not always be compatible, causing issues with data integration and 
communication. This can hinder real-time decision-making and 
responsiveness in the supply chain which is critical for maintaining the 
quality and efficiency of palm oil production.
IR5 aims to integrate humans and intelligent machines closely. 
However, this shift may increase cybersecurity risks, with threats arising 
from the expanded network of connected devices, sensitive data security 
concerns, and complex supply chains vulnerabilities [199]. To address 
these concerns, AI-based threat detection could be used to predict and 
mitigate cyber threats in real-time, reducing the risk of cyber-attacks 
and data breaches [200]. Regular security audits and updating cyber­
security protocols can also help identify vulnerabilities to ensure 
ongoing protection to safeguard digital infrastructure [201]. Further­
more, security risks from the interconnected nature of IR5 could be 
addressed through edge computing, which decentralises data processing 
by using multiple local devices or edge nodes, reducing reliance on a 
central system. This way, if one node fails, other nodes can continue 
operating independently. Additionally, edge computing enhances the 
security of supply chain operations by keeping sensitive data close to its 
origin [202]. Processing data at the network edge lowers the risk of data 
breaches and other security threats. In addition, operations can be 
simplified by developing integrated platforms that are compatible with 
different technological solutions to streamline data flow and commu­
nication. For example, the MSPO Trace is a traceability system associ­
ated with the MSPO certification scheme that involves the tracking and 
documentation of palm oil and its derivatives throughout the supply 
chain, from plantations to end consumers [203]. Lastly, the use of open 
standards and interoperable systems makes it easier to manage and 
integrate various technologies within the supply chain [204]. Designing 
intuitive user interfaces for managing IoT devices and AI systems can 
also reduce operational complexity.
Transitioning to IR5 in the oil palm industry is feasible but requires 
strategic planning and investment. By enhancing human roles, achieving 
sustainability, and strengthening resilience, IR5 can significantly 
improve productivity in the sector. However, overcoming challenges 
like workforce development, regulatory hurdles, and technological costs 
will be crucial for success.
6. Conclusion
In this review, various impacts of IR4 technologies on the oil palm 
industry were explored, focusing on innovations such as AI, IoT, 
blockchain, big data analytics, and remote sensing. These technologies 
have significantly advanced palm oil production processes, offering new 
ways to optimise efficiency and reduce environmental impact. Addi­
tionally, these technologies have shown promise in addressing labour 
rights issues and improving supply chain transparency, thereby pro­
moting more ethical practices within the industry. However, despite 
these advancements, the integration of IR4 technologies presents chal­
lenges, including high implementation costs and the need for skilled 
workforce, which may hinder widespread adoption, especially in 
developing regions. Future researches should, therefore, aim to lower 
these barriers and explore scalable, cost-effective solutions that can be 
deployed across different segments of the oil palm industry. While IR4 
technologies have the potential to revolutionise the oil palm sector, their 
success will depend on a balanced approach that considers both tech­
nological innovation and its broader socio-economic consequences. By 
fostering inclusivity among stakeholders and continuing to invest in 
research and development, the oil palm industry can address the com­
plexities of modern agriculture and move toward a more sustainable 
future.
