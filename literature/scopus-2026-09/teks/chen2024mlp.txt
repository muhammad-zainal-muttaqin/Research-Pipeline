A B S T R A C T  
Accurate and efficient tomato detection is essential for the practical deployment of robotic picking in practical 
agricultural applications, but it still remains significantly challenging to detect tomatoes in complex scenarios 
with fluctuating light, overlapping fruits, and occlusion from branches and leaves when solely using RGB images. 
The recent development of RGB-D sensors has brought one promising opportunity to adopt multimodal fusion for 
implementing high-quality fruit detection. However, the feasibility of the existing multimodal fusion and feature 
extraction architectures for lightweight tomato detection tasks, especially in complex agricultural scenarios, 
raises questions that need to be explored. As a remedy, we proposed a multimodal fusion encoder that leveraged 
depth and near-infrared modalities to assist RGB images in making full use of multimodal data. Moreover, the 
encoder contained a plug-and-play structure capable of being implemented as MLP-based (Multi-Layer Percep­
tron), ViT-based (Vision Transformer), or CNN-based (Convolutional Neural Networks) architectures. Further­
more, we developed a lightweight experimental detection framework based on YOLOv7-tiny by means of 
integrating the multimodal fusion encoder, and YOLO-DNA (Depth and Near-infrared Assisted) was put forward 
based on the MLP-based architecture after conducting comprehensive analysis of the aforementioned three ar­
chitectures. In addition, a tomato multimodal dataset containing visible, depth, and near-infrared images was 
established. Experimental results demonstrated that YOLO-DNA achieved mAP0.5 of 98.13% and mAP0.5:0.95 of 
74.0%, an average increase of 5.01% in mAP0.5 and 14.55% in mAP0.5:0.95 over mainstream lightweight detection 
models, with a detection speed of 37.12 FPS, meeting the demand of real-time tomato detection. This finding has 
the potential to advance research on fruit detection in the field of intelligent agricultural harvesting.   
1. Introduction 
Tomatoes are a widely cultivated and consumed vegetable crop with 
massive international production rates (Wang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 
2022). Due to their diverse culinary uses and potential health benefits, 
tomatoes are a versatile and nutrient-dense food that plays a significant 
role in global food systems and serves as a substantial source of income 
for many farmers. As the area for tomato farming continues to expand, it 
is becoming increasingly vital to understand and address the impact of 
human labor and storage duration on product quality. The traditional 
practice of hand-selecting tomatoes entails individuals visually exam­
ining each tomato and determining its maturity level based on their 
personal perceptions. However, human perception is subjective and may 
not be consistent, resulting in inconsistent tomato selection and poten­
tially compromising the overall product quality (Wang et al., 2022). The 
utilization of robotic tomato picking technology can effectively reduce 
labor expenses, optimize production efficiency, and categorize tomatoes 
according to standardized maturity assessment, all of which can 
improve the overall quality of the products. 
Tomato picking methods have evolved significantly in recent years, 
with deep learning techniques gaining considerable popularity. Current 
research predominantly focuses on real-time tomato maturity detection 
in RGB images as the upstream task, and various studies have reported 
promising results (Chen et al., 2021; Mutha et al., 2021; Su et al., 2022;

Wan et al., 2018). However, it is worth noting that the mAP value can 
only denote the model’s detection performance within the confines of 
the current dataset and annotation, and the deployment and application 
of the model in a practical environment continue to remain issues. 
Despite exhibiting higher mAP values, the model’s deployment suit­
ability in practical environments is closely related to its inherent 
generalization ability, which is the ability to accommodate novel and 
varied input data, encompassing diverse scenarios such as emerging 
fruit overlap patterns, complex occlusions, unseen lighting variations, 
and differences among camera devices (Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 
2022). However, these aforementioned diverse scenarios are difficult to 
fully cover in artificially constructed datasets (Muzammul and Li, 2021). 
Consequently, the model’s practical performance may diverge from the 
apparent excellence signaled by its mAP performance metrics. Hence, it 
becomes apparent that except for mAP, these metrics, e.g., robustness, 
efficiency, and adaptability, are more critical for achieving optimal 
detection results. In particular, considering the demands of industrial 
deployments, the development of lightweight models becomes crucial 
for the purposes of cost savings and real-time detection. This brings us to 
another question: how can we strike a good balance between lightweight 
models and excellent performance? 
The adoption of cutting-edge techniques could offer a viable solu­
tion. By leveraging high-performance models, it becomes feasible to 
improve the quality of tomato picking while simultaneously reducing 
production and maintenance expenses. However, there are still great 
challenges that need to be overcome in practical deployment. More 
specifically, tomato picking robots must ensure stable and efficient 
operation in various scenarios, including challenging conditions, such as 
rainy days, nighttime, and backlight situations that are commonly 
encountered in practical environments. It is well known that illumina­
tion variations interfere with vision-based downstream tasks and are 
detrimental to the model’s ability to extract the semantic information 
that is of real interest, and existing models usually perform well under 
trained illumination conditions but poorly under unknown conditions 
due to their limited generalization abilities (Yao et al., 2023). While the 
RGB detection model has been widely studied, relying solely on a single 
RGB sensor may not provide sufficient features to address all complex­
ities. The issues of stable detection performance in cases of overexposed 
or noisy RGB images remain pressing concerns that require attention 
and resolution. Finding effective solutions to these problems is essential 
for ensuring the success and reliability of tomato-picking robots in 
practical deployments. 
Researchers have proposed multimodal fusion techniques to better 
address the challenges of tomato harvesting in the field. As crops can be 
represented differently in various modalities, the integration of multi­
modal data can provide complementary and consistent information, 
effectively compensating for the limitations of relying solely on a single 
modality. Previous studies have shown that multimodal data have the 
potential to improve the performance of detection models (Gan et al., 
2018; Garillos-Manliguez and Chiang, 2021). Among these, depth and 
near-infrared modalities are widely used in mainstream agricultural 
research due to their easy accessibility, low equipment cost, and the fact 
that the devices have been widely deployed in harvesting equipment 
(Fan et al., 2022; Kasampalis et al., 2021; Li et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023; 
Rong et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2022). In prior research, the combination of 
RGB-D or RGB-NIR modalities via multimodal fusion techniques ach­
ieved promising performance. Nonetheless, this raises questions: what 
specific advantages can the model gain from the fusion of depth or near- 
infrared modalities, and is it feasible to effectively fuse features from 
both these modalities to further improve the performance? 
In addition to the choice of modality, so far, there have been diverse 
approaches to implementing multimodal fusion, where decision-level 
and feature-level fusion techniques have emerged as two main cate­
gories of approaches. Decision-level fusion techniques usually attempt 
to combine detection results from several modalities directly to optimize 
the bounding box afterwards (Pang et al., 2020). Regrettably, such 
methods might not be able to make full use of the rich semantic infor­
mation that each modality inherently offers, particularly in fruit detec­
tion tasks. Many detection results based on depth data were inaccurate, 
and although the decision-level fusion achieved higher precision at the 
cost of recall decrease, its overall performance, including mAP, recall, 
F1-score, and FPS (Frame Per Second), was still lower than other fusion 
methods (Sun et al., 2022). In contrast, feature-level fusion techniques 
collect features from multimodal data individually and perform feature 
fusion at subsequent stages, which can reduce computing effort without 
losing too much information (Chen et al., 2017). Moreover, simply 
modifying the number of input channels in the model can be one of the 
easiest ways of achieving multimodal fusion. 
Inappropriate fusion methods can amplify the noise present in 
multimodal data, leading to a significant reduction in the model’s per­
formance. Therefore, the feature extraction architecture that imple­
ments these fusion approaches is more critical in determining the 
performance of the model, and a thorough understanding of how to 
accurately extract and fuse multimodal features is critical for improving 
detection results. Specifically, in terms of multimodal feature extraction, 
the convolutional neural network (CNN) has been commonly used as the 
fundamental architecture in most pioneering works. However, the lim­
itations of the convolution operation have motivated researchers to 
explore alternative architectures with enhanced global receptive field, e. 
g., Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and the Vision Transformer (ViT). The 
pioneering work of Dosovitskiy et al. (2021) brought ViT into the field of 
computer vision, but the computing methods of the ViT structure require 
a large number of parameters and training data to achieve excellent 
performance (Zhao et al., 2021). Lightweight models are important for 
industrial deployment, and given the superior performance of ViT, many 
researchers have turned to research on lightweight ViT designs (Li et al., 
2022; Maaz et al., 2023; Mehta and Rastegari, 2022; Huang et al., 2022). 
On the other hand, in recent years, the MLP architecture has become 
a hit, starting with the work by Tolstikhin et al. (2021), who revealed 
that MLP models can achieve comparable performance to convolutional 
or attentional models. Subsequently, Ding et al. (2022) combined CNN 
and MLP by adding local prior information to a fully connected layer 
using a reparameterization approach, Touvron et al. (2023) added a 
residual architecture to further improve model performance, and Liu 
et al. (2021) introduced a gating mechanism to further enhance the 
performance of the MLP-Mixer. Guo et al. (2023) demonstrated that 
MLP-like models are better at acquiring global structural information in 
images with stronger generalization ability. However, the MLP archi­
tecture also has difficulty in achieving lightweight deployment due to 
the large number of parameters, similar to the ViT architecture. In terms 
of perception ability, the MLP or ViT architecture has a global receptive 
field compared to the CNN architecture (Peng et al., 2021), and 
considering structural complexity, the MLP architecture is simpler and 
more friendly for industrial deployment compared to the ViT architec­
ture (Zhang et al., 2022). There are significant differences among ben­
efits when these aforementioned three architectures deal with different 
tasks, and a more detailed comparative analysis of the three architec­
tures in the field of agricultural fruit detection is still urgently needed. 
In order to thoroughly understand the pros and cons of these DNN 
(Deep Neural Network) structures, Zhao et al. (2021) conducted a 
comprehensive comparison of the MLP, ViT, and CNN architectures in 
the context of image classification tasks on the ImageNet-1K dataset. 
Their findings substantiated the fact that the MLP architecture out­
performs others among lightweight models. However, the question 
below remains: Can these image classification conclusions be extrapo­
lated to the realm of lightweight object detection tasks, especially in the 
complex scenarios of agriculture? Specifically, how do these three ar­
chitectures fare in regard to detecting tomatoes in densely planted en­
vironments? Moreover, within the intricate field of multimodal fusion, 
how does the fusion operation of these architectures contribute to 
detection performance? Inspired by these points, to improve the per­
formance of tomato detection, we proposed a multimodal fusion encoder

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Tomato multimodal dataset 
2.1.1. Dataset construction 
There are several high-quality datasets available for agricultural and 
plant-related computer vision tasks, however, these datasets are limited 
to single modalities (typically RGB images) with plants in less chal­
lenging environments, making it impractical to meet our needs of 
multimodal tomato detection. Thus, we constructed a multimodal image 
dataset utilizing the Azure Kinect depth camera at the Shucheng County 
Institute of Agricultural Science in Lu’an City, Anhui Province, China. 
The dataset was collected in June 2022 and April 2023 and contains 
RGB, depth, and near-infrared images of tomatoes of different maturity 
levels, covering the common concerns in the construction of agricultural 
datasets such as occlusion, illumination, aberration, and field of view 
conditions (Lu et al., 2023; Shang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Since 
data were obtained from different sensors and for the purpose of 
reducing spatial deviation, the three modalities were aligned using the 
camera manufacturer’s API and finally saved at a resolution of 
1280x720. The depth camera was positioned approximately 0.3–2.0 m 
away from the tomatoes and 1.0–2.0 m above the ground to mimic the 
frame from the perspective of an eye-in-hand robotic arm, with a frame 
rate of 30 FPS, as depicted in Fig. 1. 
The dataset consisted of 1,342 samples, on average with twenty to­
matoes in each sample, at various stages of maturity, captured under 
diverse illumination and cluttered background conditions, which can be 
used to represent typical difficulties and evaluate model performance in 
practical situations. Fig. 2 presents typical example images in the tomato 
multimodal dataset, such as normal illumination and occlusion (a), 
overexposure and motion blur (b), overlapping and closer FOV (Field Of 
View) (c), dense environment and farther FOV (d), poor illumination 
and low characterization (e), and sodium light and low saliency (f). In 
comparison to existing public datasets, our tomato multimodal dataset is 
distinguished by its focus on these complex, practical agricultural

According to the specific requirements of harvesting and storage, 
orchard management operations often require distinguishing among 
fruits with noticeable color disparities, e.g., differentiating between 
mature and immature fruits, as well as distinguishing between fruits

for categorizing maturity levels were as follows: tomatoes with a com­
plete red hue were categorized as mature tomatoes, those with a full 
green or white coloration were categorized as immature tomatoes, and 
the others were categorized as half-ripe tomatoes. We manually labeled 
the tomato segmentation information on the RGB image using LabelMe 
software (Russell et al., 2008) and generated object detection boxes by 
extending the external rectangle of the segmentation polygon by fifteen 
pixels up and ten pixels down, on the left and right, respectively, as 
shown in Fig. 3. This approach generates bounding boxes with stan­
dardized location criteria relative to the tomato objects, which helps to 
improve the standardization of the training data, paving the way for 
downstream tasks, e.g., picking point detection. Notably, the uniqueness 
under sodium light complicates the process of segmentation annotation. 
Consequently, data collected in sodium light environments were only 
used for evaluating the model’s generalization ability and were excluded 
from both the training and validation datasets.

demonstrated stability and superiority in complex scenarios. However, 
it is still a key challenge to extract and fuse features and semantic in­
formation correlations among different modalities to overcome the 
multimodal feature conflict phenomenon, which urgently needs to be 
solved to break through the performance bottleneck of the vision system 
of tomato picking robots. 
2.2. Proposed method 
2.2.1. Overview of the experimental framework 
To fairly compare the MLP-based, ViT-based, and CNN-based ar­
chitectures, we need a unified framework to exclude other factors that 
may affect tomato detection performance. In addition, considering the 
stability of the model itself, as well as the demand for a lightweight 
model for practical deployments, we developed a lightweight experi­
mental detection framework based on YOLOv7-tiny and integrated the 
multimodal fusion encoder into the beginning part of the backbone. 
Fig. 5 illustrates the overview of our experimental framework, with red 
highlights marking the multimodal fusion encoder, which is the core of 
this paper. The only variable component is the plug-and-play structure 
within the multimodal fusion encoder. It should also be pointed out that 
our proposed YOLO-DNA model is the implementation of the MLP-based 
architecture of this framework. 
As shown in Fig. 5, the framework we designed is simple. First, the 
multimodal fusion encoder was used as the main architecture of the 
YOLOv7-tiny backbone network for performing multimodal feature 
extraction and fusion. The processed features from the multimodal

fusion encoder served as the initial feature output of the backbone’s FPN 
(Feature Pyramid Networks) architecture. Second, two additional co­
ordinate attentions were incorporated behind each of the other two 
layers of the FPN architecture. In detail, coordinate attention has been 
shown to effectively enhance the performance and prevent overfitting of 
deep learning models when conducting object detection tasks and can be 
used to improve the performance of a model with very little computa­
tional overhead (Hou et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2022). The application of 
coordinate attention enabled the model to better inhibit the negative 
effects of multimodal data and played an important role in suppressing 
the potential overfitting phenomenon. 
In Fig. 5, the CBS (Conv2D-BatchNorm-SiLU) and ELAN (Efficient 
Layer Aggregation Networks) modules basically form the backbone ar­
chitecture. The CBS module employed a convolutional layer, batch 
normalization layer, and SiLU activation function to address common 
issues, such as gradient explosion and vanishing. On the other hand, the 
ELAN module comprised several CBS modules that allow for the shortest 
longest gradient path, which helped the model learn and converge in a 
faster way. Coordinate attention was good at capturing cross-channel 
information as well as orientation-aware and position-sensitive infor­
mation, which helps the model accurately locate and identify objects of 
interest. Specifically, to preserve long-range interactions for channel 
attention, coordinate attention performed global pooling in the hori­
zontal and vertical directions. Afterwards, the two feature maps were 
concatenated and then convoluted to interact with the information in 
both directions. After applying batch normalization and nonlinear 
activation functions, the feature maps were convoluted again in the 
horizontal and vertical directions, and the sigmoid activation function 
was applied. Finally, the resulting two feature maps were multiplied by 
the original feature map. 
2.2.2. Multimodal fusion encoder 
As shown in Fig. 5, to further explore the differences in multimodal 
feature extraction ability among MLP-based, ViT-based, and CNN-based 
architectures, we proposed a multimodal fusion encoder that embraced 
a two-stage fusion approach, commencing with the fusion of depth and 
near-infrared data while concurrently extracting features from the RGB 
images with another architecture. With the relatively richer feature in­
formation thus acquired, a subsequent stage using another CNN-based 
architecture further fused three modalities together. 
Specifically, for RGB images, the architecture of the original detec­
tion models has proven its strength in feature extraction. Consequently, 
this original architecture from the original backbone architecture was 
retained, and the FPN (Feature Pyramid Networks) architecture was 
removed. Different from RGB images, depth and near-infrared data 
typically encompassed fewer local features, making it challenging to 
extract their features using convolutional operations. As a result, an 
alternate fusion network was employed to perform the integration and 
interaction between these two modalities. Since there is no relatively 
well-established feature extraction architecture as in the case of the RGB

we designed a plug-and-play structure that is adaptable for imple­
mentation as MLP-based, ViT-based, or CNN-based architectures for 
performing or exploring their fusion process. 
Upon laying the groundwork with these two feature extraction ar­
chitectures, salient attributes, for example, edges and textures, became 
richer. At this stage, the feature information from all three modalities 
was further fused. Thus, we employed a CNN architecture for subse­
quent extraction and fusion, including eight cascaded CBS modules. This 
process operated through a stepwise input process, with the resultant 
eight features undergoing further concatenation and using another CBS 
module for subsequent feature extraction and fusion. To further amplify 
fusion effectiveness and inhibit negative impacts from multimodal data, 
a coordinate attention module was incorporated. 
The two-stage feature fusion process enhanced the ability of the 
multimodal fusion encoder to integrate information appropriately and 
efficiently from all three modalities. The multimodal fusion encoder 
offered one unified framework to evaluate the efficiency of MLP-based, 
ViT-based, or CNN-based architectures in extracting and fusing multi­
modal feature information. The entire tomato detection model served as 
the foundational component of the backbone network. The encoder 
directly accepted and processed raw data from all three modalities. This 
design ensured data integrity before performing the feature fusion 
process with different architectures. 
2.2.3. MLP-based, ViT-based, and CNN-based fusion architectures 
There are numerous established architectures to implement feature 
extraction. In addition to the CNN-based architecture represented by 
ResNet, the MLP-based architecture represented by MLP-Mixer and the 
ViT-based architecture represented by Vision-Transformer have gained 
great attention due to their superior global receptive field. These three 
different architectures exhibit different computational processes during 
feature extraction, which can be succinctly encapsulated through the 
following formulation: 
F MLP = PatchEmbed(MLPLayer(X) )
(1)  
F ViT = PatchEmbed(TransformerEncoder(X) )
(2)  
F CNN = ConvolutionalLayer(X)
(3) 
In terms of feature extraction, all three architectures could extract 
abstract and high-level feature representations from the input data. 
However, the fundamental underpinning of the three architectures 
resided in their different methodologies for feature extraction. Within 
the CNN-based architecture, feature extraction acted via a process of 
sliding and computation across the input image utilizing a convolutional 
kernel. On the other hand, the MLP-based and ViT-based architectures 
seemed similar but different in their methods, they initially segmented 
the input image into different sequences through a PatchEmbedding 
layer. Subsequently, the MLP employed a fully connected block, whereas 
the ViT employed a self-attention block to extract features. The input 
image here was denoted as X ∈RC×H×W, where the parameter C signified 
the input channels, specifically representing depth and near-infrared 
data, amounting to a total of two channels, while H and W denoted 
the spatial dimensions. It is noteworthy that the dimension of the output 
tensor yielded by the MLP-based or ViT-based architecture differs from 
that yielded by the CNN-based architecture. Specifically, the former 
feature map was denoted as O1 ∈RH⋅W×C, while the latter was 
O2 ∈RC×H×W. The features from the MLP-based or ViT-based architec­
ture can be considered to have undergone a more complex convolutional 
operation and contain more dimensions and higher-level features. It is 
important to emphasize that this difference does not impede the inter­
facing with the subsequent CNN-based architectures. Obviously, the 
reshape operation preserved the meaning of the parameters in an 
appropriate way while only changing their spatial representation. With 
these differences, the effectiveness of these three architectures in 
influencing multimodal fusion of depth and near-infrared data, as well 
as their subsequent value in helping models detect tomatoes in complex 
scenarios, warrants comprehensive exploration.

F MLP, F ViT and F CNN have different properties, and Table 1 shows 
the major comparable properties of the three different architectures 
(Han et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021). Weight sharing means that there 
are connections that have the same and shared weights. Convolution- 
like architectures shared connection weights between spatial posi­
tions, while attention-like architectures shared weights between chan­
nels or within each group of channels. Therefore, F MLP and F CNN had 
weight sharing properties across channels and across positions, respec­
tively, while F ViT, combining convolutional-like and attention-like ar­
chitectures, had two weight sharing properties. The high computational 
efficiency of the convolutional architecture was due to its sparse con­
nectivity, and both F CNN and F ViT that used the convolutional archi­
tecture had this property. Dynamic weight is a unique property of the 
ViT architecture, which uses dot-product operations in multi-head self- 
attention. Finally, it is well known that the MLP-based and ViT-based 
architectures have a global receptive field. In summary, the different 
properties of the three architectures motivate us to explore their per­
formance in multimodal fusion tasks and further analyze their effects on 
the tomato detection model.

3. Task-specific analysis of multimodal fusion architectures on 
tomato detection 
In this section, we thoroughly investigate the impact of MLP-based, 
ViT-based, and CNN-based multimodal fusion architectures on the ef­
fect of lightweight object detection models in the agricultural field. Our 
investigation encompasses a comprehensive examination of their effects 
on various aspects, including evaluation metrics, task-specific perfor­
mance, feature map analysis, and model robustness. Within our exper­
imental framework, we developed three models based on the 
implementation of the three architectures mentioned in Section 2.2.3: 
the Exp-MLP, Exp-ViT, and Exp-CNN models. The Exp-3C and Exp-5C 
models, where the multimodal fusion encoder was not integrated but 
directly modified the input channel instead, were used as comparison 
baseline models for comparing the differences between single-modal 
and multimodal and between input-level fusion and feature-level 
fusion. In addition, Exp-3C, Exp-5C, and Exp-CNN were pure CNN 
models, giving us insights about the differences among no-fusion, input- 
level fusion, and feature-level fusion methods. 
3.1. Settings 
Our computer consisted of a GeForce RTX 2080Ti graphics card with 
12 GB of RAM running CentOS 7.9. Our proposed models were imple­
mented using PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019) based on CUDA 11.2. For 
training, the batch size was 16, the epochs were 200, the Adam opti­
mizer was used with a momentum of 0.937, the learning rate ranged 
from 0.001 to 0.00001, and the cosine annealing function was used to 
adjust the learning rate. We also used several training techniques to 
accelerate convergence and prevent overfitting during the training 
process, including CUDNN optimization, mixed precision training, and 
label smoothing (Zhang et al., 2021) with a weight of 0.005. For the 
training parts, we randomly selected the training and validation datasets 
from the tomato multimodal dataset at a ratio of 9:1 and used a number

Based on numerous robustness experiments, we have summarized 
the general results of the different models in complex scenarios. Spe­
cifically, we employed a blinded assessment method to ensure that the 
statistics presented in Table 4 are as objective and reliable as possible. In 
this method, each evaluation session presented the assessor with three 
images representing the detection results from the three different 
models under investigation. Importantly, the correspondence between 
the models and their respective results was anonymized for the evalu­
ators to prevent any potential bias. Evaluators were asked to rank the 
results for each set of three images based on the assessed term. Following 
the completion of all evaluation sessions, the scores assigned by all 
evaluators to each image were aggregated and statistically analyzed to 
derive the data presented in Table 4, where more “+” symbols signify 
better performance. The term “tomatoes with low color saliency” as­
sesses the model’s ability to detect tomatoes that are similar in color to 
the leaves or in shadow. Meanwhile, “occluded or small tomatoes” as­
sesses the model’s ability to effectively detect occluded or small to­
matoes. The term “precise and focused ROI” assesses the precision of the 
region of interest (ROI) within the heatmap, considering its accuracy in 
covering the tomato and the level of prediction confidence. The term 
“detection accuracy” assesses the model’s ability to both detect a greater 
number of tomatoes and classify them accurately. Finally, “stability” 
assesses the model’s consistent performance across numerous 
experiments, such as in two sequential frames where the model is able to 
detect the tomato in the first frame but fails to detect any tomatoes in the 
second frame. 
The CNN-based architecture notably enhances the model’s robust­
ness in normal illumination scenarios. However, its performance di­
minishes under scenarios of poor illumination or sodium light, 
indicating a strong reliance on RGB images within the CNN-based ar­
chitecture, with its robustness linked to the quality of the RGB image. 
Conversely, the ViT-based architecture exhibits relatively ordinary 
performance. Its strong feature extraction ability allows it to detect 
occluded or small tomatoes. However, the ViT-based architecture poses 
significant computational demands, and within the field of agricultural 
applications, it is difficult to construct a comprehensive and large 
dataset to accommodate different farm harvesting environments. Last, 
the MLP-based architecture is not as powerful as the ViT-based archi­
tecture in feature extraction ability, but it also has a global receptive 
field, which provides a more robust performance under poor illumina­
tion and sodium light scenes where RGB images show defects. However, 
it does not perform as well as the CNN-based architecture under normal 
illumination scenes.

4.3. Contribution of the depth and near-infrared data 
To explore the advantages of incorporating depth or near-infrared 
data and to analyze the different contributions of various modalities to 
the detection performance, we conducted comparative experiments 
presented in Table 7. These experiments employed the YOLO-DNA 
model, with the variables being the input modalities into the MLP 
fusion architecture. For fairness, all models were not loaded with pre­
training weights. 
Clearly, the utilization of RGB-D-NIR data outperformed the usage of 
either RGB-D or RGB-NIR data alone. The near-infrared modality 
contributed to valuable features related to edges and texture, resulting 
in a more significant enhancement in AP and P metrics compared to the 
depth modality. The depth modality, on the other hand, provided 
essential features related to spatial relationships, resulting in a more 
significant increase in R metrics. The F1 metric, serving as one balance 
between precision and recall, prevented scenarios where the model 
excelled in one aspect but lagged in another. The utilization of RGB-D- 
NIR consistently improved the model’s F1 metric, indicating its ability 
to accurately detect tomatoes with a lower chance of missed or false 
detections. In addition, the inclusion of both depth and near-infrared 
data substantially boosted the model’s performance, as reflected in 
mAP00.95, where RGB-D-NIR significantly augmented the model’s pre­
diction confidence in classifying individual tomatoes. In summary, 
depth data contributed to the improvement of the recall metric, while 
near-infrared data improved precision, and the combination of depth 
and near-infrared data could improve the overall detection performance 
of the model. 
5. Conclusion 
This paper investigated the task-specific contribution of MLP-based, 
ViT-based, and CNN-based architectures in the context of multimodal 
fusion and lightweight object detection in the field of fruit detection. 
Specifically, we proposed a novel multimodal fusion encoder designed 
to utilize complementary information from depth and near-infrared 
modalities, improving the abilities of RGB detection models. The 
multimodal fusion encoder was designed using two-stage feature fusion 
architecture. First, the encoder employed a plug-and-play structure that 
can be implemented as MLP-based, ViT-based, or CNN-based architec­
tures, extracting features from the depth and near-infrared modalities. 
Subsequently, these extracted features are fused with more features 
extracted from RGB images. The encoder was integrated into an exper­
imental framework based on YOLOv7-tiny, allowing us to conduct 
comprehensive experiments and analyses. 
Our robustness experiments revealed different characteristics among 
the CNN-based, ViT-based, and MLP-based architectures. The CNN- 
based architecture demonstrated notable efficiency in extracting edge 
and detail features. However, its performance exhibited a high depen­
dence on the quality of the RGB image, rendering it particularly effective 
solely in normal illumination scenarios. In contrast, the ViT-based ar­
chitecture had strong feature extraction ability, but its inherent prop­
erties posed challenges for lightweight integration and required high 
inference cost, making practical deployment difficult. Furthermore, 
both the CNN-based and ViT-based architectures exhibited unstable 
performance, often suffering from such a phenomenon between suc­
cessful detection in the current frame and failure in the next one. 
Conversely, the MLP-based architecture achieved a good trade-off in 
performance, offering stable robustness across a wide range of complex 
scenarios. Given these insights, we implemented the MLP-based archi­
tecture within our experimental framework and introduced the YOLO- 
DNA model. The YOLO-DNA achieved mAP0.5 of 98.13% and 
mAP0.5:0.95 of 74.0%, an average increase of 5.01% in mAP0.5 and 
14.55% in mAP0.5:0.95 over mainstream lightweight detection models, 
and achieved 37.12 FPS, meeting the demand of real-time tomato 
detection. 
Moreover, those strategies, such as incorporating adaptive pre­
processing techniques that enhance image quality under varying illu­
mination conditions or integrating multimodal data sources to reduce 
reliance on RGB images alone, could address the limitation within CNN- 
based architecture. Optimizing the ViT-based architecture, such as 
combining monarch mixer architecture for efficiency or utilizing 
knowledge distillation techniques, may alleviate the computation cost. 
While MLP-based architecture shows good promise, advanced tech­
niques to prevent overfitting need to be further explored for large MLP 
parameters and small training sets. Finally, to enhance stability, inte­
grating sequential data processing techniques could help maintain 
detection reliability across frames.

complex scenarios and densely planted environments, promoting the
