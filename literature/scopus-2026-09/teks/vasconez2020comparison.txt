A B S T R A C T
Knowing beforehand the amount of fruit to be harvested leads to better logistics and decisions making in the
agricultural industry. In the last years, several sensors –mainly artiﬁcial vision systems– and sensing techniques
have been proposed to address the fruit counting problem with sundry results. Convolutional neural networks
(CNN) arise as the current trend in processing imagery information, due to their adaptability and eﬃciency in
object detection. However, there is still missing an insightful analysis of the usability of such technique in fruit
counting problems in groves, since the learning process is sensitive to the training input data, the sensor (aﬀected
by environmental conditions) and the architecture chosen to process the imagery set. Therefore, in this work we
test two of the most common architectures: Faster R-CNN with Inception V2 and Single Shot Multibox Detector
(SSD) with MobileNet. These detection architectures were trained and tested on three fruits: Hass avocado and
lemon (both from Chile), and apples (from California - USA), under diﬀerent ﬁeld conditions. To address the
problem of video-based fruit counting, we use multi-object tracking based on Gaussian estimation. Our system
achieves fruit counting performances up to 93% (overall for all fruits) using Faster-RCNN with Inception V2, and
90% (overall for all fruits) using SSD with MobileNet. Such results can lead to further improve the decision
making process in agricultural practices.
1. Introduction
Currently, agriculture requires new technologies to improve pro-
duction and crop management practices. The later has become a need in
many countries due to the growing food demand, which is markedly
visible in urban areas (FAO, 2009; Vasconez et al., 2019). The use of
technology, such as artiﬁcial vision systems, can contribute to ﬁnd so-
lutions and to add value into several farming problems, thus promoting
productivity, better quality control processes, and to achieve ﬂexible
farm management, among others (Vasconez et al., 2019). Strategies
that allow data acquisition and analysis from agricultural environments
can help to optimize current practices, such as crop counting, yield
estimation, pathogen and diseases detection and crop maturity classi-
ﬁcation. In particular, yield estimation can be greatly beneﬁted by ar-
tiﬁcial vision technologies. Although these approaches depend on the
amount of fruits that can be detected by the sensors, the yield estima-
tion can be signiﬁcantly improved by applying a correction factor
provided by the agronomist, or by using systems that blow air to the
leaves and branches to reduce occlusion (Edan et al., 2000; Hed and
Centinari, 2018). However, despite the advances that have been made
to solve the fruit counting and yield estimation problems, they still are
open issues (Jarvinen et al., 2018; van Dael et al., 2017; Munera et al.,
2017; Vasconez et al., 2019; Vasconez et al., 2018).
Computer vision approaches have been studied for diﬀerent crop
detection and classiﬁcation problems. Detection approaches use color-
based techniques –such as color thresholding (Gongal et al., 2015;
Payne et al., 2013)–, shape-based techniques –such as Circular Hough
Transform (CHT) (Bargoti and Underwood, 2017)– and local texture
features (Sengupta and Lee, 2014). On the other hand, classiﬁcation
approaches commonly use template matching, neural networks, and
supporting vector machine (Wu et al., 2013; Kurtulmuş and Kavdir,
2014). Although these schemes can reach fast computing performances,
they suﬀer from lack of accuracy under challenging conditions such as
crop variability, multi-crop detection, illumination changes, occlusion
problems, among others (Kurtulmuş and Kavdir, 2014; Xu et al., 2013).

sion methods (LeCun et al., 2015). Particularly, approaches such as
Faster R-CNN, region-based fully convolutional networks (R-FCN),
Single Shot Multi Box Detector (SSD) and You Only Look Once (YOLO)
have shown that is possible to detect not only one, but several diﬀerent
classes for challenging on-line object detection applications (Solberg,

Despite the increasing trend in using CNNs, there are still very few
attempts in issuing them for solving the fruit counting problem. For
example, in Jarvinen et al. (2018) it is implemented a Faster R-CNN
detection method with optical ﬂow for fruit tracking on an apple
orchard, trained with 15k iterations. The work used image sequences to
better detect occluded apples. A diﬀerent approach for fruit counting
using detection techniques is based on image segmentation, presented
in Bargoti and Underwood (2017), where a multi-scale multilayered
perceptron was implemented including two feature learning ap-
proaches and a convolutional neural network. The fruit pixel segmen-
tation results are binary masks that constitute the input of a watershed
segmentation stage (WS) and a circular Hough transform algorithm
(CHT), to thus detecting and counting apples. In Rahnemoonfar and
Sheppard (2017), a deep neural network is trained using features on
multiple scales based on an Inception-ResNet architecture. Such net-
work was trained with synthetic images and tested on real images of
tomato plants, reaching 91% of accuracy. However, such network was
tested using 128 × 128 pixel images, which may not take into account
important features from the fruit due to the low resolution. There are
other works focused only on fruit detection, such as (Sa et al., 2016),
which uses Faster R-CNN combined with depth information from near-
infrared images (NIR). Such method is compared to the conditional
random ﬁeld that obtains color and textures features from RGB –red,
green and blue– and NIR –near infra-red– images, which showed to be
much slower than their proposed Faster R-CNN, when tested on a data
set composed of rock-melons, mangos, oranges, among others. Other
work that uses depth information is (Gené-Mola et al., 2019), where it is
studied the beneﬁts of fusing RGB-D and radiometric information ob-
tained with a Kinect v2 (from Microsoft) for apple detection. In such a
work, it is adapted the Faster R-CNN model to use it with ﬁve-channel
input images: RGB, depth (D) and range-corrected intensity signal. The
results showed an improvement of 4.46% using the F1-score metric
when adding depth and range-corrected intensity channels. A diﬀerent
approach is used to study the maturity classiﬁcation of passion fruits
using a detector-based architecture (Tu et al., 2018), where it is pre-
sented an algorithm based on RGB-D images from the ﬁeld using a
Kinect sensor. The detection algorithm was trained for ﬁve diﬀerent
maturity categories; then two Faster R-CNN detection models were used
to process both RGB and depth information. The fruit features were
extracted using dense scale-invariant features transform and locality-
constrained linear coding. Finally, a classiﬁcation stage based on sup-
port vector machine allowed for ﬁnally obtaining the fruit maturity
level. Another fruit detection application for maturity level classiﬁca-
tion can be found in Habaragamuwa et al. (2018), where the im-
plemented system detects two classes of strawberries (mature and im-
mature) using a deep convolutional neural network based on a region
proposal, ﬁne-tuning feature extraction, classiﬁcation, and duplicate
removal stages.
Despite the attempts shown above related to the detection stage,
there are still important considerations to be taken into account, such
as: (i) the number of iterations required by the training procedure; (ii)
some of the previously mentioned approaches only consider one type of
fruit (Jarvinen et al., 2018; Bargoti and Underwood, 2017), that be-
longs to a particular canopy structure and trees’ distribution along the
ﬁeld, which might be important characteristics to analyze; (iii) other
works do consider more than one type of fruit, but without considering
occlusion problems (Sa et al., 2016). In addition, several works still
require additional analysis justifying the use Faster R-CNN against
other approaches (Jarvinen et al., 2018; Sa et al., 2016). As can be seen,
there still remains open an insightful trade-oﬀamong the diﬀerent CNN
architectures to evaluate their performance in agricultural applications;
speciﬁcally, for fruit detection and counting (Jarvinen et al., 2018;
Bargoti and Underwood, 2017; Sa et al., 2016; Habaragamuwa et al.,
2018).
Finally, in addition to the detection procedure that is carried out for
each image, it is required a method to count fruits throughout the
image sequences obtained from the camera in motion. At this stage,
over-counting arises as one of the main issues that aﬀect the fruit
counting process. Such problem is related to false negative detections
and fruit occlusion presented during sequential images analysis. Several
methods have been proposed to tackle over-counting problems. In
Wang et al. (2013), a stereo vision approach was used to count fruits in
an apple orchard. It ﬁrst applies color segmentation and morphological
operations to convert pixel regions into distinct individual apples. Block
matching method was also used to triangulate the 3D positions of all
fruits detected. Then, over-counting was reduced by calculating the
distance between every two apples and merging them using a threshold
criterion. Fruits that were detected only once during sequential images
processing were also discarded during the counting procedure. In Stein
et al. (2016), mango fruits were detected and tracked to avoid over-
counting. To this end, a navigation system provided the camera with
pose information that was used to calculate the epipolar geometry be-
tween subsequent image pairs, which allowed for pair-wise association
of the corresponding fruits. Tracking was also carried out by linking the
pair-wise associations that allowed the unique count and triangulation
of the fruit. Unlike the previous methods, in our work we use a single
RGB camera and a Gaussian approach to overcome the over counting
problem.
Despite the eﬀorts and advances made on applying convolutional
neural networks and artiﬁcial vision systems in agriculture, there is still
missing a comprehensive analysis of the usability of such approaches
under real ﬁeld conditions and using diﬀerent architectures. This work
is focused on tackle such problem.
This work is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present the
general architecture of this work: the data set acquisition, the CNN
approaches under evaluation, the fruit counting and tracking system
and data management. Results for fruit detection on images, and multi-
object tracking and counting for frame sequences are presented in
Section 3. Finally, the discussion is presented in Section 4, and the
Summary and Conclusions in Section 5.

Several image sequences were acquired from the ﬁelds for the three
fruit classes using a commercial RGB camera calibrated with 360 × 640

training, validation and test sets. In all fruit image sets, data augmen-

duce over-ﬁtting in supervised learning algorithms (Krizhevsky et al.,
2012; Jarvinen et al., 2018). To this end, 20% of the images were
selected randomly to apply two data augmentation methods on them
and thus to enlarge the training set. The ﬁrst method consisted of ro-

of using the fruit images with diﬀerent pixel resolutions. The number of
images considered for the training set –which includes the augmented
data–, validation and test sets were determined following the guidelines
from (Krizhevsky et al., 2012; Jarvinen et al., 2018), and they are
shown in Table 1. The validation dataset is usually used to perform an
unbiased assessment of the detection architecture ﬁt on the training

hand, the test set is used to perform an unbiased assessment of the ﬁnal
model weights (resulting from best results from validation assessment)

during training or cross-validation evaluation, therefore it is also known
as hold-out set. The fruit classes bounding boxes ground truth in the
training and test image sets were labeled using LabelImg software
(Tzutalin, 2015). For the labeling process, small and immature avo-
cados were discarded, as well as apples that were not, at least, 30%
visible, and lemons in a decomposition stage.

2.2.1. Faster R-CNN
This integrated convolutional object detection method consists
mainly of two stages. The ﬁrst stage is a deep fully convolutional net-
work called region proposal network (RPN) that is responsible for
proposing rectangular regions that might contain objects. The second
stage uses a region classiﬁer such as Fast R-CNN (Girshick, 2015) to
classify the regions proposed for the RPN (Ren et al., 2017). This meta-
architecture is illustrated in Fig. 3.
At the ﬁrst step of the Faster R-CNN architecture, an RGB image is
processed through a pre-trained CNN (feature extractor) obtaining a
convolutional feature map that is used for the RPN to generate a group
of proposals on the image. To this aim, a set of anchors, which are ﬁxed-
sized reference bounding boxes, are created for the convolutional fea-
ture map at each window location. Then, a small neural network slides
over the convolutional feature map and takes as input the sliding
window (Ren et al., 2017; Fuentes et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2015). The
RPN uses all the anchors to generate a pre-selected number of bounding
box proposals (anchors represented in the original image) that can
contain objects. To do so, the RPN makes two diﬀerent types of pre-
dictions: an objectness classiﬁcation (object or background) and a re-
gression for the bounding box coordinates. The anchors generally
overlap on the same object, for which it is necessary to use a post-
processing algorithmic approach called Non-Maximum Suppression.
The NMS discards the proposals that have an Intersection over Union
(IoU) between the box proposals and the ground-truth larger than a
predeﬁned threshold value (Ren et al., 2017; Fuentes et al., 2017; Ren
et al., 2015; Agarwal et al., 2018). In the second stage of the Faster R-
CNN architecture (box classiﬁer), the box proposals previously obtained
are processed to extract crop feature maps from the same feature map
used for the RPN. The cropped features obtained are processed for the
remaining feature extractor layers such as region of interest (ROI)
pooling layer and fully connected networks (all part of the Fast R-CNN)
to infer the class probability and bounding box coordinates for each
class. A key idea of this approach is that the RPN and the box classiﬁer
stage are concatenated as one uniﬁed network that can be trained and
tested as an end-to-end process (Fuentes et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2017;
Ren et al., 2015).
2.2.2. Single shot multibox detector (SSD)
Unlike the previous method, single SSD does not require of an RPN
to obtain the region proposals, and also omits the feature re-sampling
stages that Faster R-CNN uses (Liu et al., 2016). Instead, SSD computes
both the location and class scores using convolutional ﬁlters, as illu-
strated in Fig. 4.
One of the main features of SSD is that it uses convolutional layers
after the main network to help to detect objects at several scales, since
layers have diﬀerent sizes. Each extra feature layer can predict an ob-
ject score category or a shape oﬀset related to a default box position,
relative to the location on each feature map. For a default box, SSD
infers scores for each object category and produces adjustments in the
box to better match the object shape. This approach combines predic-
tions from multiple feature maps with diﬀerent resolutions to facilitate
detections of several sizes objects (Liu et al., 2016). Once the bounding
boxes and the object class scores are obtained, non-maximum sup-
pression is used to obtain the ﬁnal detection results. The default boxes
in SSD are the equivalent of the anchor boxes used in Faster R-CNN (see
(Ren et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2016; Agarwal et al., 2018)) but applied to
several feature maps for diﬀerent resolutions.

2.2.4. Training parameters and considerations
Both Faster R-CNN with Inception V2 and SSD with MobileNet were
trained using the data set that includes all diﬀerent fruit classes as in-
dicated in Table 1. During training, the images from the training set
were resized for each detection algorithm to ﬁt into their feature ex-
tractor stages (Huang et al., 2017).
In addition, transfer learning technique was performed to train each
architecture by importing weights from previous training on COCO data
set, which contains 91 objects types with 2.5 million labeled instances
in 328k images (Lin et al., 2014). Usually, training satisfactorily a CNN
based detector need several hundred thousand or even millions of
images. However, using a pre-trained model from a previous object
detection task reduces the training to just ﬁne-tuning the weights,
which can drastically increase the accuracy of the method, as well as to
reduce the amount of required training images needed to obtain
satisfactory results (Jarvinen et al., 2018; Rajpura et al., 2018; Huang
et al., 2017; Howard et al., 2017). Each algorithm was trained using a
computer equipped with Nvidia Quadro video card processor and 16 Gb
RAM, using asynchronous gradient updates, following the guidelines
from (Huang et al., 2017).
Object detection methods aim to minimize two types of losses: the
ﬁrst is anchor box category loss (multiway classiﬁcation), and the
second loss is positive anchor box oﬀset loss (box regression). Both
Faster R-CNN and SSD models minimize classiﬁcation and regression
during the training process. However, since it is diﬃcult to know be-
forehand how many iterations are required to obtain satisfactory de-
tection results (Fuentes et al., 2017; Jarvinen et al., 2018), we eval-
uated each detection algorithm using the validation set at every 10,000
steps, since early stops during training is a form of regularization used
to avoid overﬁtting which might help to achieve better results (Agrawal
et al., 2014; Kang et al., 2016; Howard et al., 2017; Girosi et al., 1995).
The loss functions used for Faster R-CNN with Inception V2 and SSD
with MobileNet for 80,000 steps are presented in Fig. 5, which shows
how the total loss function is reduced.

computed the precision-recall (PR) curve for each approach. The PR
curve is an eﬃcient evaluation method to study and compare the per-
formance of an object detector and is the metric used for the PASCAL
VOC object detection challenge (Everingham et al., 2010). It provides
more information than other methods such as Receiver Operator
Characteristic (ROC) curves and F1 score (Davis and Goadrich, 2006).
An object detector of a particular class is considered of high perfor-
mance if its precision (related to low false positive rate) stays high as
the recall (related to low false negative rate) increases. Additionally, we
use the average precision (AP) and the mean average precision (mAP)
as metrics of performance comparison (Davis and Goadrich, 2006).
The object detection results on the validation set for the proposed
approaches are depicted in Fig. 6, which is an example of the execution
of the algorithm. It is to be noted that for the three fruit cases, occlusion
was present and several fruits were not detected. The PR curves for
avocado, apple, and lemon fruit categories using Faster R-CNN with
Inception V2 and SSD with MobileNet methods are presented in Fig. 7
and Fig. 8 respectively. As can be seen, such ﬁgures show the behavior
of CNNs approaches performance at a diﬀerent number of training
iterations. In overall, for the Faster R-CNN with Inception V2, the best
performance for the three fruits is observed at 70k, whereas for SDD
with MobileNet, at 40k. However, the Faster R-CNN with Inception V2
needed approximately 220 ms of computing time per frame, whilst the
SSD with MobileNet require approximately 60 ms. The previous best
model results tested on the validation set for each architecture are then
evaluated on the test sets as illustrated in Fig. 9. It can be observed that
the detection results are similar for the validation and test set, which
means that the selected models have acceptable generalization abilities
for the present data set. Such results also suggests that overﬁtting
problems does not aﬀect signiﬁcantly the performance of the detection
approaches. The object training and evaluation results are summarized
in Table 2.
3.2. Fruit counting results
Six diﬀerent tests were carried out to evaluate the fruit counting
performance, each consisting of 5, 15, 30, 60 and 100 image sequences
for avocado, apple and lemon categories, shown in Table 3. For each
test, we used both Faster R-CNN with Inception V2, at 70k, and SSD
with MobileNet, at 40k (see Section 3.1), detection methods along with
the proposed multi-object tracking algorithm (see Section 2.3). The
sequences were taken under several lighting conditions and diﬀerent
levels of fruit density (see Section 2). As ground truth, fruit counting
was previously manually recorded. A sample of the on-line multi-object
tracking results is illustrated in Fig. 10. The bounding boxes with thick
edges without color ﬁll represent the detected fruits, whilst the
bounding boxes that have been ﬁlled with color represent the result of
the multi-object tracking procedure. Each box color represents a label
of a particular fruit, and the number at the top represents the fruit count
predicted by the data association stage. The multi-object tracking al-
gorithm helps preventing a fruit from being counted several times due
to false negatives characteristic of the detection algorithm and by the
occlusion of the fruit by leaves or branches.

4. Discussion
During the development of this work, a number of lessons were
learned:
• We tested the CNNs approaches on three groves. However, fruits
hang diﬀerently for each tree. Although data was recorded in a
similar fashion for the three cases –i.e., sensor in hand and recording
the sides of the corridors– it is to be studied how to enhance the data
acquisition procedure. For example, in (Sanz-Cortiella et al., 2011)
it is presented that LiDAR measurements are diﬀerent according to
the height of the sensor with respect to the canopy, and therefore,
diﬀerent conclusions can be obtained from the data. A similar si-
tuation can be expected in our case, in which each grove is likely to
have its own directives to optimize the acquisition of data.
• During data acquisition, fruits,especially apples and lemons, were
susceptible to be partially or totally occluded by leaves or branches
(or depending on the grove, they could have been inside the canopy,
unreachable by the sensor capabilities). The CNNs approaches are
highly dependent on the training set. Since we trained only with
images obtained from side views of the corridor, then only fruits
visible from the sensor location were detected. We were able to
detect only fruits with a minimum of 30% of visibility (see Section
2.1) and they were considered for the fruit counting error evaluation
shown in Table 3 and Fig. 11. It would be expected a better ap-
proximation to the real fruit harvesting amount if some correction
factor were included, given by the agronomist expertise. In our case,
we compared the fruit detected and counting against manually
counting.
• Since the methodology followed in this work consisted on recording
video images from the groves and then process them for fruit
counting, it was not observed changes of the results according to the
illumination variation registered during the ﬁeld data acquisition. In
fact, it was not considered data acquisition during dawn, direct
sunlight, sunset or night, since the process of acquiring video images
recording is quick and easy to handle by, e.g., an operator.
• The detection results obtained showed that the use of a greater
number of iterations does not necessarily mean for a better perfor-
mance of the proposed architectures. This could be due to the fact
that early stopping during training can be used as a regularization
technique, useful to reduce over-ﬁtting (Prechelt, 2012).
• From an implementation perspective, the system based on Faster R-
CNN with Inception V2 was able to reach fruit counting perfor-
mances from 44% to 93% (see Fig. 11), whereas for SSD with Mo-
bileNet, performance ranged from 24% to 90% (see Fig. 11).
• The results in Fig. 11 shows that Faster R-CNN with Inception V2
provided the best results for avocado, apple lemon in almost all tests
for the diﬀerent frames considered. The SSD approach with

• Overall, the results obtained are encouraging: CNNs approaches can
be used to estimate fruit production. For avocados, Faster R-CNN
with Inception V2 for 2 and 15 frames, showed the minimum re-
lative error of 7%; For apples, the SSD with MobileNet reach the
minimum relative error of 10% (slightly better than Faster R-CNN
with Inception and 30 frames with minimum relative error of 13%);
Finally, for lemons, the Faster R-CNN with Inception for 15 and 30
frames reach the minimum relative error of 20%. This means that in
general, the Faster R-CNN with Inception V2 method is more eﬃ-
cient in terms of precision. However, the Faster R-CNN with
Inception V2 need approximately 220 ms of computing time per
frame, whilst the SSD with MobileNet require only around 60 ms.
This means that the SSD with MobileNet method can tackle pro-
blems with high-speed requirements. Finally, it is worth mention
that multi-object tracking processing time takes only 10 ms per
frame for the fruit counting procedure.
• It can be observed that both Faster R-CNN with Inception V2 and
SSD with MobileNet usually reach better fruit counting results for 15
frames. This behaviour might be due to a trade-oﬀamong the oc-
cluded fruits that became visible and the over-counting problems
related to false negative detections. For example, it can be observed
that for 2 frames, several occluded fruits were not detected, but
became visible at frame 15 for most of the cases. That causes the
fruit count error to decrease during the ﬁrst frames. On the other
hand, the false negatives related to detection causes the multi-object
tracking algorithm to count more than once the same fruit. It per-
manently increments the counting error as the number of processed
frames increases.
• It can be seen that some precision-recall curves appear to be slightly
discontinuous for low recall regions. This can be improved by in-
creasing the amount of data samples used in the validation and test
data sets and it will lead the authors’ future work.
• We also tested the detection architectures with image resolution of
800 × 600 pixels, and we observed that the computing time in-
creases up to 0.45 s for Faster R-CNN with Inception V2, and up to
0.19 s for SSD with MobileNet. Since one of the objectives of this
work is to obtain a fruit count on-line, the resolution of 360 × 640
was ﬁnally chosen for our experiments. In addition, since MobileNet
and Inception V2 have been extensively tested to obtain high per-
formances, we did not change the number of convolutional layers of
the feature extractor stages. However, more feature extractors will
be tested in future works of the authors.
5. Summary and conclusions
In this work, we implemented and evaluated the performance of two
CNNs approaches for solving the fruit counting problem. In particular,
Faster R-CNN with Inception V2 and SSD with MobileNet were im-
plemented for fruit detection from video recordings; and a multi-object
tracking method was used for tracking and data association of the la-
beled fruits, which allowed to count them. The equipment consisted of a
commercial camera manually handled by an operator as he traversed
through corridors in the grove. Video images were acquired from the
side of the corridors and later processed by the proposed approaches.
Three groves were chosen: Hass avocado and lemon groves (from
Valparaiso region in Chile) and apple grove (from California).
The implemented architectures showed a minimum relative error of
7% when counting avocados; 13% when counting apples and 20% for
lemons, using Faster R-CNN with Inception V2; with SSD with
MobileNet, minimum relative errors were 18%, 10% and 25% for
avocados, apples, and lemons, respectively. Nevertheless, the Faster R-
CNN with Inception V2 require approximately 220 ms of computing
time per frame, whilst the SSD with MobileNet require only around
60 ms.
Despite the results, the CNN architectures are highly dependent on
the training set and its quality (for example, their possible blurriness),
and the results obtained herein might not be concluding for other
groves with diﬀerent fruits. However, results have shown to be en-
couraging.
