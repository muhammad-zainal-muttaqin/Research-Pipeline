A B S T R A C T  
Robots must first detect the number of banana bunches when making judgements on sterile bud removal and 
estimating weight for harvest in the field environment. Banana bunches are complex in shape, arranged in a 
nonlinear helical curve along the stalk, and have different growth states in different periods, with bunches widely 
spaced in the early period and densely arranged in the harvest period. Deep learning nor classical image- 
processing algorithms alone can detect and count bunches in both periods. Therefore, these algorithms were 
combined to calculate the number of bunches in the two periods. For counting bunches in the debudding period, 
the convolutional neural network Deeplab V3 + model and classic image-processing algorithm were combined to 
finely segment bunches and calculate bunch numbers, providing intelligent decision-making for judgment on the 
timing for debudding. To count bunches during harvest, based on deep learning to identify the overall banana 
fruit cluster, the edge detection algorithm was employed to extract the centroid points of fruit fingers, and the 
clustering algorithm was used to determine the optimal number of bunches on the visual detection surface. An 
estimation model for the total number of bunches, including hidden ones, was created based on their helical 
curve arrangement. The results indicated a target segmentation MIoU of 0.878 during the debudding period, a 
mean pixel precision of 0.936, and a final bunch detection accuracy rate of 86%. Bunch detection was highly 
challenging during the harvest period, with a detection accuracy rate of 76% and a final overall bunch counting 
accuracy rate of 93.2%. Software was designed to estimate banana fruit weight during the harvest period. This 
research method provided a theoretical basis and experimental data support for automatic sterile bud removal 
and weight estimation for bananas.   
1. Introduction 
The trend of using agricultural robots to aid in the intelligent man­
agement of orchards has grown due to labor shortages. The ability for 
banana robots to perform automatic sterile bud removal (SBR) and es­
timate weight during harvest is a significant milestone in the develop­
ment of smart banana farms. The visual detection system plays a crucial 
role in enabling robotic operations. Machine vision is used to detect the 
number of banana bunches, allowing for informed decisions to be made 
regarding the timing of SBR. According to agronomic research, it is 
recommended to maintain 6–9 bunches per cluster after SBR to maxi­
mize production and income. At harvest, the weight of the entire fruit 
cluster can be estimated by detecting the number of bunches. 
Autonomous banana bunch counting poses several technical chal­
lenges, including the complex banana garden environment, which is 
easily impacted by noises such as wind and changing illumination. The 
green color of banana fruit is also similar to that of branches and the 
background, adding to the difficulty. Additionally, the irregular shape 
and dense arrangement of bunches, spirally arranged in three di­
mensions along the stem, make counting even harder for the human eye 
and only very experienced banana farmers are able to perform the task 
accurately. As a result, the use of computer vision to detect and count 
banana bunches is a highly challenging task that has yet to be success­
fully addressed in current research.

RGB images have been analyzed with classical image-processing al­
gorithms to extract multiple features (Tang et al., 2023), including color 
(Luo et al., 2018), texture (Apte & Patavardhan, 2021), shape (Lu et al., 
2018). This traditional method of analyzing RGB images has been 
widely used for target detection and segmentation of various fruits, 
including but not limited to apples (Silwal et al., 2017), tomatoes (Wu 
et al., 2019), citrus fruits (Lin et al., 2020), kiwis (Fu et al., 2019), cu­
cumbers (Fernandez et al., 2018), and lychees (Li et al., 2020). The 
conventional segmentation algorithms have the advantage of providing 
a relatively fast processing speed while extracting relevant information 
from an image to a certain extent (Wang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019). 
However, they have some limitations, including the requirement of 
significant differentiation of one or multiple combined features of the 
target object from the background in the image. This can result in a 
relatively high intensity of subsequent processing and limit its applica­
bility in complex agricultural scenarios (Li et al., 2022). 
With the advancements in parallel computing hardware in recent 
years, deep learning algorithms, particularly convolutional neural net­
works, have gained increasing popularity in the field of picking robots 
(Zhou et al., 2022a; Zhou et al., 2022b). At present, deep neural network 
algorithms have been applied in the field of image processing for a va­
riety of purposes, including classification (Boogaard et al., 2020), 
detection (Koirala et al., 2019), and segmentation (Kang & Chen, 2019). 
Among these, segmentation algorithms can be further divided into two 
categories: semantic segmentation networks and instance segmentation 
networks. The semantic segmentation method classifies each pixel in an 
image and provides basic information, such as the location and contours 
of various target objects in the image (Chen et al., 2014). This approach 
has been widely used in the processing of fruit and vegetable images, 
and several classic semantic segmentation models have been developed. 
For instance, Majeed et al. used a SegNet network to segment apples in 
RGB images, including stems, branches, and gridlines (Majeed et al., 
2020). Barth et al. created a large-scale semantic segmentation dataset 
for sweet peppers and used Deeplab v2 to segment the fruits, stems, and 
leaves of the sweet pepper (Barth et al., 2019). Additionally, Peng et al. 
used Deeplab V3 + models to fine-tune the segmentation of multiple 
bunches of lychee fruit and fruiting branches in a large field of view 
(Peng et al., 2020). 
Intelligent detection in banana orchards has started late. Scholars 
have employed classical and deep learning algorithms to detect bananas 
(Fu et al., 2022), count banana plants on a farm (Neupane et al., 2019), 
draw land use map of banana orchards (Clark & McKechnie, 2020), 
segment banana stalks (Chen et al., 2021), locate points for SBR from the 
inflorescence axis (Wu et al., 2022), and obtained three-dimensional 
information of banana fruit stem ends (Chen et al., 2020). This study 
aimed to address the technical challenge of detecting and counting ba­
nana bunches in an effort to provide support for SBR and estimate 
harvest yield using banana robots. 
In light of the limitations of single image processing algorithms and 
deep learning in achieving accurate banana bunch counting, we propose 
a novel fusion algorithm to achieve automatic bunch counting during 
the SBR and harvest periods. 
The main contributions of this paper include:  
(1) The integration of Deeplab V3 + and classical image processing 
algorithms to achieve fine segmentation of banana bunches 
during SBR, calculate the number of bunches, and provide 
intelligent decision-making for SBR timing.  
(2) Identification of the banana fruit cluster using YOLOv5-Banana 
deep learning, with subsequent use of classical image process­
ing algorithms to detect the contours of each banana fruit finger 
and extract the centroid points. Clustering optimization and the 
silhouette coefficient method are used to determine the optimal 
number of fruit bunches on the vision detection surface.  
(3) Establishment of an estimation model based on the growth and 
arrangement patterns of banana bunches, which enables the 
counting of the overall bunch number, including hidden bunches. 
The structure of the paper is as follows: The second section presents 
the materials and data, the third section details the structure and 
implementation of the algorithm, the fourth section provides the 
experimental results and analysis, and the fifth section provides a 
summary and conclusion. 
2. Materials and data collection 
2.1. Testing equipment and software 
The experimental setup comprised of both hardware and software 
components, as detailed in Table 1. The sample database was obtained 
through a mobile and visual system integrated into the banana debud­
ding robot platform. A smartphone with a high resolution of 2316 ×
1080 and 48 million pixels was utilized for data acquisition. The visual 
system employed a Kinect 2.0 depth camera with a resolution of 1920 ×
1080. All samples were collected in a static state using both methods. 
Subsequently, after training the algorithm, the program code, and the 
complete sample database were incorporated into the robot’s IPC, 
enabling the robot to execute the operation. 
2.2. Image and data collection 
Samples were collected in two batches. In December 2021, samples 
were collected the banana farm of Guangdong Academy of Agricultural 
Sciences. In March 2022, samples were collected in Caojiang Town. The 
sample collection was carried out under natural lighting conditions 
without any artificial lighting sources. A total of 1500 original images 
were acquired, comprising 575 samples from the SBR period and 925 
samples from the harvest period. The sample size was later increased to 
2000 and 1500 through data augmentation techniques. The collected 
samples revealed that during the SBR period, banana bunches were 
widely spaced (Fig. 1a), whereas during the harvest period, the bunches 
were densely arranged (Fig. 1b). 
3. Algorithms and description 
In this study, different methods were used to detect and count ba­
nana bunches according to the different growth and arrangement 
characteristics of bunches in the SBR and harvest periods. In the SBR 
period, the convolutional neural network Deeplab V3 + model was 
utilized for rough segmentation of bunches, followed by the application 
of classical image processing algorithms for fine segmentation and 
calculation of the bunch count. In the harvest period, the YOLOv5- 
Banana algorithm in a previous study (Wu et al., 2022) was used for 
the identification and detection of clusters amongst the near-color 
background, with an edge detection algorithm employed to extract the 
centroid points of each banana fruit finger. The K-means clustering al­
gorithm and silhouette coefficient (SC) method were integrated to 
determine the optimal number of fruit hands on the detection surface, 
and finally, an estimation model was established based on the helical

3.1. Recognition of banana fruit cluster structure and visual detection 
objects 
The focus was on detecting and determining the total count of 
bunches within a banana fruit cluster, including any bunches that may 
be obscured on the side or back. The shape of a banana fruit cluster is 
complex and composed of multiple, densely arranged levels of fruit 
bunches (Fig. 3). Its structural components include the stalk, bunch 
stem, fruit fingers, and banana bunches, the latter of which is made up of 
two rows of fruit hands. During the SBR period, the visual detection 
object was identified as “banana bunches”, while during the harvest 
period, it was identified as the “fruit hands”, or half a bunch. To achieve 
the goal of detecting and counting the bunches, a combination of deep 
learning and classical image processing algorithms was employed in this 
study, specifically tailored to the growth and arrangement of banana 
bunches during the SBR and harvest periods, respectively. 
3.2. Counting of banana bunches in the SBR period 
3.2.1. Rough segmentation of banana bunches 
In the SBR period, the large distance between the banana bunches 
enabled nearly all of the bunches on the stalk to be detected simulta­
neously. However, the complex background, scattered and irregular 
distribution of segmentation targets in the image, as well as the need for 
high segmentation accuracy, needed to be taken into consideration. To 
address these challenges, the Deeplab V3 + segmentation network was

employed to perform pixel-level semantic segmentation of the bunches, 
which provided the contour of the target fruit bunch in the image, 
including basic information such as its location. 
The DeeplabV3 + is a prominent architecture for semantic segmen­
tation, incorporating modifications to the original DeepLabV3 design. 
The additions include a decoder module, consisting of both an encoder 
and decoder component. The encoder leverages the Xception backbone 
network and an Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) module 
featuring dilated convolutions. The Xception model employs depthwise 
separable convolutions (Chollet, 2017). The original image was down­
sampled by Xception to generate a high-level semantic feature map. The 
ASPP module is comprised of a 1 × 1 convolution and three dilated 3 × 3 
convolutions with dilation ratios of 6, 12, and 18. The decoder employs 
bilinear interpolation to upscale the encoder’s feature map output by a 
factor of four, and this result is concatenated with low-level features 
obtained via a 1 × 1 convolution. The resulting feature map is then 
subjected to a 3 × 3 convolution, followed by further upscaling by a 
factor of four. The final output is a segmentation prediction map with 
the same resolution as the input image (Fig. 4). 
3.2.2. Fine segmentation of banana bunches 
The predicted segmentation map after semantic segmentation might 
have the problem of banana area adhesion between different bunches, 
compromising the clarity of the bunch boundary. To enhance the seg­
mentation results and distinctly define the boundary, classical image 
processing algorithms were utilized as a post-processing step. 
The segmentation prediction map was read in and transformed from 
RGB to grayscale format. Subsequently, threshold segmentation was 
performed on the grayscale image to generate a single-channel binary 
image of the prediction area, which underwent morphological process­
ing. The closing operation was applied to fill in small holes in the binary 
image, followed by the opening operation to separate the different 
bunch areas. This step also eliminated any isolated white pixels in the 
image. To smooth the edges of the binary image, median filtering was 
applied. The final binary image obtained had smooth edges, clearly 
separating different bunches, and was used as a mask to perform oper­
ations on the original predicted segmented image. The resulting region 
image marked the position of the binary image on the original image and 
represented the desired banana segmentation between different 
bunches. 
3.3. Calculation of banana bunches in the harvesting period 
Banana bunches are closely arranged in the harvesting period and 
the whole bunch segmentation method in the SBR period cannot be used 
as the bunch stem is not visible at the bottom. The complex shape of each 
fruit finger and the two-dimensional nature of the visual detection sur­
face present a challenge in detecting the number of hidden bunches that 
are occluded on the sides and back of the banana fruit cluster. To address 
this issue, this study innovatively applied classical image segmentation 
algorithms to extract the centroid of each banana fruit finger. The 
number of fruit hands on the visual detection surface was determined 
through a combination of clustering optimization and the silhouette 
coefficient method. Furthermore, based on the characteristic helical 
curve arrangement of the bunches, a calculation model was developed to 
estimate the overall number of bunches in a cluster.

3.3.1. Segmentation of banana fingers and centroid points extraction 
To improve the detection accuracy of the number of bunches, the 
initial step involved the identification and extraction of the entire ba­
nana fruit cluster. In the preliminary study, the authors conducted multi- 
object recognition research on banana fruit, rachis and buds, improved 
the backbone network and loss function in the YOLOv5 network struc­
ture, and constructed the YOLOv5-Banana model to provide support for 
accurate and fast recognition of multiple features in complex scenes in 
the banana garden. Due to the challenge of detecting bunches in the 
harvest period, this study first used this model to recognize the banana 
fruit cluster, determine the area of interest, and then performed bunch 
detection. 
The YOLOv5-Banana model optimized the backbone network by 
incorporating an Involution Bottleneck sub-module in place of the 
Bottleneck module in the C3 module of the YOLOv5 network. This 
modification reduced the number of parameters and computation for 
recognizing multiple banana targets, thus improving accuracy. More­
over, the loss function was altered from GIOU to EIOU, which expedited 
convergence by reducing the discrepancy between the width and height 
of the target and anchor boxes. The results of the banana cluster 
detection are illustrated in Fig. 5. 
The RGB images of the banana cluster regions were preprocessed 
(Fig. 6a) and converted into the HSV color space (Fig. 6b) to obtain the 
V-component images, which underwent histogram equalization to 
enhance brightness (Fig. 6c). These processed V-component images 
were then merged into HSV restoration maps (Fig. 6d). The re-fused HSV 
restored images after V-component brightness stretching were employed 
for further experiments to determine the number of banana hands, 
reducing the impact of edge contour adhesion among adjacent banana 
fingers on recognition results. The processed cluster images were 
binarized to extract the banana edges (Fig. 7a). The binary images had 
mainly background noise blocks and internal noise holes. The binarized 
images were processed and analyzed using morphological closing and 
opening operations, which removed most of the background environ­
mental objects and cleared the contour between fingers, resulting in 
better banana sample image data (Fig. 7b). Subsequently, Canny edge 
detection was applied to obtain the edge contours of each finger 
(Fig. 7c), and the centroid coordinate points were extracted by 
computing the centroid coordinates of the edge contour (Fig. 7d). 
3.3.2. K-means Clustering and SC method to determine the optimal number 
of fruit hands 
In the K-means clustering algorithm, the set of coordinate points of 
the corresponding contour was obtained by calculating the input edge 
and contour information of the fruit finger. The sample points were 
gathered into one class according to the similar characteristics between 
the centroid points in the centroid coordinate point set. Then, the N 
centroid points were divided into k clusters and the standard value k that 
satisfies the requirement with the smallest variance; that is, the number 
of clusters. The data during sampling of the orchard image showed that a 
banana fruit cluster generally had 5 to 20 hands. Thus, the algorithm set 
the banana fruit hands at 5 ≤k ≤20. Here, the number of bunches was 
determined by identifying the fruit hands, as one bunch has two hands. 
To enhance the clarity of the clustering results, the algorithm divided the 
centroid points into different colors to indicate the cluster class that each 
centroid point belongs to and display the distribution of centroid points 
among the different cluster classes. 
However, the final output of the K-means clustering may not always 
meet the requirements of this experiment, as it could result in centroid 
points belonging to different hands being classified into the same clus­
ter. Hence, it was essential to evaluate the clustering effect for different 
values of k before the K-means clustering algorithm converged. To this 
end, the SC method was utilized to determine the optimal clustering 
solution through assessment and evaluation, producing the best number 
of banana fruit hands for each centroid point set map of the banana 
bunch region images. 
The SC method was used to evaluate the clustering effect of the K- 
means algorithm by combining the degree of interior cohesion and 
exterior seperation. For each cluster, there was a corresponding average 
SC value to evaluate its clustering effect. The average SC was denoted as 
SC ∈[-1,1] in the SC method. A larger average SC indicated a closer 
sample distance of the centroid points in the cluster, greater distance 
between clusters, and better clustering effect corresponding to the 
number of banana fruit hands. The clustering value k was thus closer to 
the actual number of banana hands.

4.1. Tests and results of bunch segmentation of bananas in the SBR period 
The dataset was split into a training set and a test set with an 8:2 
ratio. The DeepLabV3 + semantic segmentation network was trained 
using the stochastic gradient descent method with a batch size of 8, 
sample crop size of 512 × 512, weight decay coefficient of 0.0001, and 
500 training iterations. 
The evaluation of the DeepLabV3 + network was performed using 
two commonly used semantic segmentation metrics: mean intersection 
over union (MIoU) and mean pixel precision (MPA). These metrics were 
used to assess the accuracy and quality of the network’s segmentation 
performance. MIoU calculated the degree of coincidence between the 
predicted result and true value in the original image by calculating the 
intersection and union of both sets of values. Assuming that the total

that belong to class j but are predicted to be class i. The value range of 
MIoU was 0 to 1. The higher the value of MIoU, the greater the accuracy 
of the predicted segmentation map.

nanas trees planted in a regular patterns possess fruit bunches in a neat 
and standardized manner, arranged regularly in a nonlinear helical 
curve along the stalk. From top to bottom, the 1st and 2nd bunches are 
basically ~ 180◦symmetrical and starting from the 3rd, 4th, and 5th 
bunches, the bunches are arranged in a counterclockwise ~ 120◦
downward helical, and all remaining bunches arranged in this way.

For a single sample, there will be counting errors, for example, 
sample A has a counting error of −1 and sample B has a counting error 
of + 1, then the total count of banana bunches of the two samples (A +
B) will have a counting error of 0. The method proposed in this study to 
count the total number of banana bunches in all samples had an accu­
racy rate of 93.2% (Table 5). The calculation was based on comparing 
the actual total number of banana bunches (455) to the number counted 
using the proposed method (424). It should be noted that while there 
may be counting errors in individual samples, these errors may offset 
each other when adding up the total number of bunches, resulting in a 
high accuracy rate. The results of the detection showed that the main 
source of error was due to the impact of illumination, causing difficulties 
in extracting the centroid points. Despite this, the final accuracy of the 
total number of banana bunches was found to be acceptable for weight 
estimation purposes, with a 93.2% accuracy. Further optimization of the 
algorithm was carried out to improve the accuracy of bunch counting 
during the harvesting period. 
4.2.2. Weight estimation software 
Not only can the detection and recognition of the number of fruit 
bunches be utilized by robots to determine the need for bud removal, it 
also serves as a valuable tool in fruit weight estimation during the 
harvesting period. Fig. 13 shows the banana weight estimation software 
presented in the paper. The software calculates the total number of 
banana bunches and then determines the overall weight of the fruit by 
using the known average weight of a single bunch (which can be ob­
tained through agricultural experience or manual weighing). The 
average weight of bananas can vary based on the season, as important 
factors influencing this include air and soil temperature and humidity. 
The software also displays these important indicators suggested by 
agronomists to be recorded, in order to provide data for multi-factor 
studies of the entire banana life cycle. Please note that the display of 
these indicators in the paper is for demonstration purposes only and has 
no bearing on the results. In this study, bananas from Gaozhou City were 
selected, with the average weight of each bunch 3 kg, and the net weight 
at 80% of gross weight. 
The method proposed in this study, which is based on recognition of 
the number of fruit bunches, has the potential to provide accurate 
weight estimates for bananas in a given region and time period,

5. Conclusions 
This study innovatively proposed a novel approach to detect and 
count the number of banana bunches in complex orchard environment, 
in both SBR and harvesting periods. The bunches in the SBR period were 
segmented by the convolutional neural network Deeplab V3 + model 
and classical image-processing algorithm, reaching a final detection 
accuracy 86%, supportive for intelligent timing judging for SBR. Bunch 
counting is extremely difficult in harvest, as they are tightly packed. This 
paper integrated deep learning, image processing and clustering algo­
rithm to obtain the bunch number on detection surface, and propose an 
estimation model for counting the total bunch number, with hidden 
ones, using their geometric arrangement pattern. The detection accu­
racy rate for banana bunches was 76% and the final counting accuracy 
rate for the overall tested samples 93.2%. The proposed methods pro­
vided a theoretical basis and experimental data support for automatic 
SBR and crop yield estimation of bananas. The algorithm for detecting 
banana stems in this paper has an acceptable rate of success, however, 
there is still room for improvement regarding its complexity. The seg­
mentation network model is quite large, causing slower processing times 
when segmenting high-resolution images. The recognition results are 
still susceptible to lighting conditions. Future studies should aim to 
improve the visual algorithm’s robustness to lighting variations, 
construct a more efficient and compact segmentation network, and 
enhance overall stability and speed of performance.
