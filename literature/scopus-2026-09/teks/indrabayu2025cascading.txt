A Cascading of YOLOv8 and Random Forest Regression in Oil Palm 
Fresh Fruit Bunch Mass Estimation System using Unmanned Aerial 
Vehicle Imagery 
Indrabayu a,*, Muhammad Ijlal Nurhadi a, Sofyan Tandungan a, Muhammad Abdillah Rahmat a 
a Department of Informatics, Hasanuddin University, Makassar, Indonesia 
Corresponding author: *indrabayu@unhas.ac.id 
Abstract—Efficient management of oil palm farms requires accurate pre-harvest planning to maximize productivity. Traditional 
methods for estimating the mass of Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs) typically involve manual sampling and weighing, which are time-
consuming and prone to errors. This study presents a novel system combining unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) photography with 
geometric feature extraction using YOLOv8-Segmentation and machine learning models—Random Forest Regression (RFR)—to 
estimate FFB mass. The system addresses challenges posed by dynamic drone imagery, including environmental variations and frond 
occlusions. Instead of directly integrating YOLOv8 with the regression models, geometric features such as the minor axis, perimeter, 
and eccentricity are extracted from the segmented images and used to train the RFR for mass estimation. The top-performing model, 
using features extracted from YOLOv8-Small-Segmentation with the minor axis and eccentricity, achieved a Root Mean Square Error 
(RMSE) of 3.95 and a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 2.87 for frond-covered FFBs. For frond-uncovered FFBs, the best-performing 
features were the minor axis, perimeter, and area extracted using YOLOv8-Large-Segmentation, resulting in an RMSE of 3.91 and 
MAE of 2.91. These results demonstrate the system's capability to accurately estimate FFB mass based on UAV-captured imagery and 
feature extraction. This approach offers a scalable and efficient solution for pre-harvest planning in oil palm plantations, addressing 
the limitations of traditional methods while improving operational efficiency and accuracy in yield estimation. 
Keywords—FFB mass estimation; unmanned aerial vehicle; YOLOv8-segmentation; random forest regression.

I.
INTRODUCTION
The palm oil industry is a critical component of the 
agricultural sectors of numerous tropical countries, including 
Indonesia, which is one of the world's primary producers of 
palm oil. Palm oil is a vital raw material in a variety of 
products, including biodiesel, cosmetics, and food products 
[1]. The sustainability and efficiency of the palm oil sector 
have profound effects on both the economic growth of 
producing countries and the global economy [2]. 
Pre-harvest planning is a crucial component of palm oil 
plantation management, aimed at enhancing output and 
maximizing revenues [3]. This process typically involves 
estimating yield by manually measuring the mass of Fresh 
Fruit Bunches (FFB) from randomly chosen trees to 
determine the average mass. FFB contains crude palm oil, 
derived from the flesh of the fruit (the mesocarp), and palm 
kernel oil, extracted from the kernel of the fruit [4]. Precise 
yield estimation is essential not only for planning before the 
harvest but also for evaluating plantation performance, 
conducting productivity studies, and making broader 
economic forecasts within the sector [5]. 
Nevertheless, 
traditional 
approaches, 
despite 
their 
extensive application, possess notable limitations. The 
intrinsic heterogeneity in the size and mass of FFBs among 
various trees frequently results in sampling errors, which 
affect the precision of yield estimations. Furthermore, manual 
weighing is both time-consuming and labor-intensive, making 
it inefficient for large-scale plantations [6]. 
Given these limitations, there is a pressing need for an 
innovative approach that leverages modern technology to 
provide effective and efficient yield estimations. Accurately 
determining the mass of FFBs is crucial for sustainability 
monitoring, productivity analysis, and optimizing pre-harvest 
production planning within the palm oil industry. Industry 
stakeholders can improve operational efficiency, minimize 
environmental impact, and develop economically sustainable

production strategies by conducting precise productivity 
analyses and pre-harvest production planning [7]. 
In response to these needs, computer vision and regression 
approaches have been suggested as potential solutions to 
overcome the constraints of traditional FFB mass estimation 
[8]. Computer vision can identify and quantify FFBs from data 
captured by Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV). UAVs, 
commonly referred to as drones or aircraft with no pilot on 
board, are equipped with cameras, sensors, and communication 
devices, among other payload systems [9]. UAVs can be 
remotely controlled and provide highly precise images along 
with essential geographical data for identifying and measuring 
FFBs [10]. The utilization of UAVs in agriculture has seen 
significant growth, providing novel opportunities for precision 
farming, as demonstrated by research on crops such as grapes 
[11], apples [12], and wheat [13]. 
Regression analysis can be used to create a mathematical 
model that predicts the mass of FFBs by using variables 
obtained from these images [14]. The combination of 
computer vision, UAV data, and regression analysis provides 
a methodical technique for accurately calculating the mass of 
FFBs. This establishes a dependable basis for pre-harvest 
planning. Recent progress in machine learning, specifically in 
the application of algorithms such as Random Forest and 
Support Vector Machines, has demonstrated encouraging 
outcomes in predicting agricultural yield. Further highlighting 
the potential of these technologies in enhancing accuracy and 
efficiency in mass estimation [15]. 
A study by [16] proposed a deep learning system for 
estimating the single and overall weights of melons using 
UAV 
images, 
employing

and

algorithms. The model achieved a detection precision of 0.92 
and a MAPE of 16%, demonstrating its effectiveness in 
accurately predicting both individual and total melon weights. 
Similarly, [17] utilized Random Forest to predict biomass and 
yield in a tomato phenotyping experiment. This model, using 
plant area and vegetation indices extracted from UAV 
multispectral images, achieved an accuracy of 87.95% for 
fresh shoot mass and 66.51% for yield mass. 
In

promise.

approach [19] applied boosting algorithms such as XGBoost to 
estimate food weight based on features extracted from images, 
achieving an RMSE of 6.05 grams and proving highly accurate 
in predicting food item weights from a single image. 
The objective of this project is to leverage advancements in 
agricultural yield estimation to develop a system that 
precisely calculates the weight of FFB in oil palm plantations. 
The objective of this system is to provide an effective and 
efficient approach for pre-harvest planning. It achieves this by 
utilizing UAV imagery that has been divided into segments 
using YOLOv8-Segmentation and then applying a Random 
Forest Regression model. The knowledge acquired from this 
research has the potential to give farmers and producers the 
ability to make informed choices in managing plantations and 
processing crops, hence improving production and reducing 
losses caused by incorrect harvesting. 
II. MATERIALS AND METHOD 
A. Data Collection 
Data for this investigation was collected using a DJI Mavic 
Air. The UAV is equipped with a 12-megapixel camera that 
can record videos at a resolution of 1080p with a frame rate 
of 30 frames per second [20]. The drone has a 3-axis gimbal 
to capture consistent video, reducing motion blur and 
ensuring steady imagery. 
The data collection was conducted by positioning the UAV 
approximately 2 meters in front of the oil palm FFB. This 
distance was selected to guarantee optimal visibility and 
clarity of the FFBs while simultaneously ensuring a safe 
operating range for the drone. The UAV remained in a steady 
position to record videos of 200 samples of FFBs in two 
distinct conditions: 100 with fronds covering them and 100 
without fronds. The collected dataset was subsequently 
partitioned following a 70:15:15 ratio for training, validation, 
and testing. 
The DJI Mavic Air utilizes stereo vision technology in its 
front obstacle sensing system to accurately calculate the 
distance ahead. The technology uses a pair of front-facing 
cameras that capture photos from slightly different 
perspectives. The method employs image comparison to 
compute depth information by analyzing the discrepancy 
between the images [21]. The depth (Z) can be determined by

After the videos were taken, the FFBs were harvested and 
measured to get their actual masses. The FFBs were carefully 
detached from the palm tree and measured directly using a 
bench scale with a 150 kg capacity and 500-gram precision. 
The importance of this stage lies in its ability to establish the 
true masses of the FFBs, which are vital for the development 
and evaluation of the mass estimate models. 
B. Image Pre-processing 
The video data underwent processing by extracting frames 
and transforming them into separate images. The pictures 
were initially adjusted to a resolution of 1920×1080 but later 
downsized to 640×360. To preserve the accuracy of feature 
extraction and analysis [22], it is imperative to resize the 
images while maintaining the aspect ratio between the width 
and height, thereby avoiding any distortion. 
The segmentation labelling procedure involved utilizing 
these images to identify the specific parts that correspond to 
the FFBs accurately. The labelling technique in this study was 
meticulously carried out, guaranteeing a precise definition of 
the FFBs by properly delineating their exact contours. The 
contour-based 
annotation 
provides 
a 
more 
accurate 
representation of the FFBs, improving the model’s capacity to 
distinguish FFBs from the surrounding environment and other 
entities depicted in the image [23]. For example, precise 
contour labelling, as shown in Fig. 1, accurately outlines the 
boundaries of the FFB, highlighting its shape and position on 
the palm tree.

C. Segmentation Model 
The YOLOv8-Segmentation algorithm is specifically 
developed to carry out object identification and segmentation 
simultaneously in a unified framework. This capability has 
gained significant importance in many computer vision 
applications in the field of agriculture [24]. YOLOv8 
architecture has essential components such as the backbone, 
neck, and head. These components work together to handle 
incoming images, extract features, and generate final 
predictions [25].  
The training method of YOLOv8-Segmentation comprises 
multiple essential processes that attempt to optimize the 
model for precise object detection and segmentation in 
images. During each iteration of training, a subset of images 
from the FFB dataset is inputted into the model, which 
subsequently executes a forward pass. The images are 
transmitted through the central part of the system to extract 
crucial characteristics, which are subsequently analysed by 
another component to improve their representation at many 
scales [26].  
The iterative process is performed for each batch in the 
dataset, and the training is carried out for a fixed number of 
epochs or until the model achieves satisfactory performance. 
The thorough training process of YOLOv8-Segmentation 
guarantees the correct identification and segmentation of 
FFBs, with a high level of precision in distinguishing them 
from the background. 
To 
evaluate 
the 
performance 
of 
the 
YOLOv8-
Segmentation model, many assessment measures are 
employed: Precision, Recall, and Mean Average Precision 
(mAP) are discussed in reference [27]. Precision is a metric 
that quantifies the level of accuracy of a model in correctly 
recognizing essential items, while avoiding the inclusion of 
false positives. Recall quantifies the model's capacity to 
correctly detect and include all pertinent entities present in the 
dataset. Mean Average Precision (mAP) is computed by 
taking the average of the average precision (AP) values for all 
classes and IoU (Intersection over Union) thresholds.

D. Feature extraction 
The feature extraction process is initiated after the 
YOLOv8-Segmentation model has effectively segmented the 
FFBs in each frame of the video. This process involves 
extracting features, including the major axis, minor axis, area, 
perimeter, and eccentricity, which are essential for accurately 
characterizing the shape and size of the FFBs. The extraction 
process commences by accurately fitting an ellipse into the 
demarcated area. This guarantees that the ellipse precisely 
follows the outline of the segmented mask, thus perfectly 
capturing the shape of the FFB. 
The YOLOv8-Segmentation model initially generates 
segmentation outcomes, which are subsequently converted 
into binary masks. The binary mask is generated by 
overlaying the segmentation output and converting the 
segmented region into a binary image using a predefined 
colour range.

Once the binary mask is generated, as shown in Fig. 2, the 
contours of the segmented area are identified. These contours, 
which outline the boundary of the segmented region, are used 
to form a convex hull. The convex hull is essentially the 
smallest convex polygon that can completely enclose all the 
contour points, providing a simplified yet comprehensive 
boundary for the segmented area [28]. The procedure of 
fitting an ellipse to the convex hull is essential, since it ensures 
that the extracted features accurately represent the shape and 
dimensions of the FFB.

E. Mass Estimation Model 
The mass estimation model is constructed using Random 
Forest Regression (RFR) and XGBoost, which is trained on the 
retrieved features: major axis, minor axis, area, perimeter, and 
eccentricity. The models are built upon a strong basis, as it 
correlate these traits with the measured masses of the FFBs [31]. 
To identify the most influential features for the model, 
Mean Decrease Accuracy (MDA) testing is performed. The 
feature importance is evaluated by the MDA method, which 
quantifies the reduction in model accuracy when the values of 
a feature are permuted [32]. The equation for computing

Random Forest Regression works by constructing multiple

accuracy through gradient descent optimization [34]. 
To assess the effectiveness of the model, two primary

Mean Absolute Error (MAE). RMSE quantifies the average 
magnitude of errors, giving more weight to larger errors 
because of the squaring operation. In contrast, MAE measures 
the average absolute differences between the predicted and

After developing the mass estimation models for oil palm 
FFB, the models are integrated into a system designed to 
estimate the mass of FFBs based on two distinct conditions: 
frond-covered FFB and frond-uncovered FFB. To ensure 
accurate mass estimation under these varying conditions, two 
separate models are developed. The first model is tailored for 
estimating the mass of frond-covered FFB, while the second 
model is designed for frond-uncovered FFB. 
The system utilizes a branching logic to ascertain the 
appropriate model to employ, taking into consideration the 
segmentation findings and the eccentricity value of the FFBs. 
More precisely, after the video of the FFB is entered into the 
system, the FFBs are divided into segments, and pertinent 
characteristics are retrieved. If the segmentation results in a 
single segmented object with an eccentricity greater than 0.7, 
this indicates that the frond-covered FFB, prompting the 
system to use the corresponding model for covered FFBs. 
Conversely, if the segmentation results in more than one 
segmented object or an eccentricity less than 0.7, this suggests 
that the frond-uncovered FFB, and the respective RFR model

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
A. Segmentation Model 
The YOLOv8-Segmentation model underwent training for 
15 epochs, with a batch size of 16. The initial learning rate 
(lr0) was set to 0.01, and other essential parameters, such as 
momentum (0.937), weight decay (0.0005), and the Adam 
optimizer with 'auto' settings, were utilized. Fig. 5 illustrates 
the segmentation models' performance by presenting the 
mean Average Precision (mAP) values for epochs 1 to 15. The 
Nano, Small, and Medium models demonstrated consistent 
improvements, beginning with initial mean average precision 
(mAP) values of 0.621, 0.689, and 0.646, respectively. The 
values increased to 0.817, 0.837, and 0.844, respectively, at 
the end of the training phase. The Large and Xtra models, 
which are the most sturdy, initially achieved mAP values of 
0.717 and 0.727, and steadily outperformed the other models, 
ultimately attaining mAP values of 0.859 and 0.866, 
respectively.

IV. CONCLUSION 
The mass estimation system for oil palm Fresh Fruit 
Bunches (FFBs) was developed by extracting geometric 
features such as area, perimeter, and eccentricity from the 
segmented 
results 
of 
YOLOv8-Segmentation. 
These 
extracted features were then used to train Random Forest 
Regression (RFR) and XGBoost models. For frond-covered 
FFBs, the highest level of accuracy was achieved using the 
YOLOv8-Small-Segmentation model with minor axis and 
eccentricity as key features. The system attained a Root Mean 
Square Error (RMSE) of 3.95 and a Mean Absolute Error 
(MAE) of 2.87 using RFR. For frond-uncovered FFBs, the 
system also performed effectively using minor axis and area 
features, resulting in an RMSE of 3.91 and MAE of 2.91. 
These results demonstrate the system’s capability to provide 
accurate mass predictions by leveraging geometric features 
from the segmented images, making it a reliable tool for pre-
harvest planning and improving operational efficiency in oil 
palm plantations.
