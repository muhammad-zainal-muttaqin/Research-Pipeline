A B S T R A C T
Accurate yield estimation is crucial for optimizing agricultural management and market forecasting. However, 
the variable scale and occlusion of grapes cause their detection to have a negative impact, thereby reducing 
the precision of yield estimation. This study proposes a Grape Detection and Yield Estimation framework 
based on the YOLO architecture (GDYEY) by integrating a grape detection network into a depth estimation 
network. Specifically, a complex and diverse grape detection dataset was first constructed to facilitate verifying 
estimation performance and offer convenience to this research field. Moreover, a novel target detection 
mechanism based on Knowledge Distillation and Enhanced Convolutional Neural Networks (KD-ECNN) is 
designed by employing knowledge distillation to effectively transfer semantic information from the teacher 
to the student network. Generalization capability is enhanced through this approach. Additionally, a new 
yield estimation strategy based on Depth Estimation was presented by embedding the LapDepth Network to 
improve the accuracy of yield estimation. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method 
achieves a mAP of 81.6% in grape detection, surpassing 15 mainstream detection models. Additionally, grape 
yield estimation shows a significant improvement, with accuracy gains of approximately 25% and 10% over 
the counting-only and area-based estimation methods, respectively.
1. Introduction
Accurate grape yield estimation is essential in modern agriculture, 
especially for automated harvesting and precision farm management. 
In grape yield estimation, grape detection is responsible for the target 
location. Therefore, fast and accurate grape detection is crucial for yield 
estimation. In practice, efficiently achieving this goal is still a challeng-
ing research topic under an anfractuous environment, such as grape 
size, light conditions, and occlusion, which complicate the detection 
process. To alleviate this issue, researchers have attempted to carry out 
extensive research. A large number of computer vision-based methods 
have emerged such as covering grape stem detection (Kalampokas 
et al., 2021), stem recognition (Wu et al., 2023), grape inflorescence 
detection (Khokher et al., 2023), pre-bunch detection (Du et al., 2022), 
disease detection (Ji and Wu, 2022), grape segmentation (Jinhai Wang 
et al., 2023), and so on. These methods have made an important 
contribution to the research on grape yield estimation.
For grape detection, to further improve the accuracy of grape de-
tection, Huang and Li (2023) proposed TLKA-YOLOv7 for grape bunch 
detection and segmentation by embedding the Triple Large Kernel

yield estimation method named GDYEY. The main contributions are as 
follows:
(1) A diverse grape detection dataset (GD) is established to en-
hance the model’s generalization ability and adaptability in complex 
environments.
(2) A new object detection network KD-ECNN is designed. The 
network embeds knowledge distillation into augmented convolutional 
neural networks to incorporate advanced modules and optimization 
strategies. This strategy can significantly improve the accuracy and 
robustness of grape detection.
(3) A improved YOLO architecture is proposed. Embedding ECA into 
the ELAN module for robust feature aggregation under varying lighting 
and occlusion conditions. DCN is introduced in the C2f module for 
adaptive receptive field adjustment and occlusion processing. Design an 
SPF module to achieve excellent multi-scale feature fusion for detecting 
clusters of different sizes. Propose an AMMP module to enhance feature 
localization and robustness in complex backgrounds.
(4) A yield estimation strategy based on depth estimation (YEDE) 
is proposed, which improved the accuracy of grape yield estimation 
significantly.
2. Related works
2.1. Grape detection
In the field of precision agriculture, grape testing is crucial and plays 
a core role in grape yield prediction, disease and pest monitoring, and 
maturity assessment. Liu et al. (2015) introduced an image processing 
algorithm that combines color and texture information, and used sup-
port vector machines for grape detection. Wang et al. (2016) proposed 
a vision-based pipeline: region extraction, color-feature extraction, and 
grading for red and black grapes.
Lidar technology has also been applied in grape detection and 
fruit technology. Henry et al. (2017) used the three-dimensional (3D) 
imaging technology of conventional 24 GHz frequency modulated con-
tinuous wave (FMCW) radar for detecting and remotely estimating 
the number of grapes. Eccleston et al. (2018) developed a synthetic 
aperture radar (SAR) system for detecting grape clusters in grapevines 
and distinguishing leaf scattering. In recent years, deep learning meth-
ods have been widely applied in the field of grape detection. Qiu 
et al. (2022) proposed a grape maturity detection and visual pre-
localization algorithm based on improved YOLOv4, which significantly 
improved the accuracy and robustness of fruit detection. Zhang et al. 
(2022) proposed a real-time grape cluster detection method based on 
the YOLOv5s deep learning algorithm. By constructing a new grape 
dataset, a grape detection model with strong robustness, generalization, 
and real-time adaptability was achieved. Wu et al. (2024) proposed a 
lightweight TiGra-YOLOv8 model based on YOLOv8 and integrated an 
attention scale fusion module for grape detection.
2.2. Grape yield estimation
Grape yield estimation, as an important component of agricultural 
production management, has received increasing attention in recent 
years with the development of intelligent agriculture. Anastasiou et al. 
(2018) proposed a new method for estimating the yield and quality 
of fresh grapes based on satellite and near-end sensing. Arab et al. 
(2021) proposed a method for predicting grape yield based on time-
series vegetation indices using satellite remote sensing and machine 
learning. The vegetation indices extracted from satellite images (NDVI, 
LAI, and NDWI) can be used to efficiently a predict fresh grape yield 
at different growth stages. Subsequently, many studies began to use 
image processing and deep learning techniques for grape detection, and 
combined the characteristics of fruit quantity, size, maturity, etc. to 
estimate yield. Barbole and Jadhav (2022) proposed a deep learning 
regression model that combines the basic structure of U-net, VGG-
16, and an attention module to predict the weight of grape clusters 
in images with small errors. Parr et al. (2023) utilized the distorted 
peaks caused by diffuse light scattering within each grape fruit in 
the depth map, and used a smartphone with color and time-of-flight 
depth cameras to automatically estimate the grape yield of Chardonnay 
grapes.
3. Methodologies
This study proposes a novel method for grape detection and yield 
estimation named GDYEY, and the framework diagram of GDYEY is 
illustrated in Fig. 1. As Fig. 1 shows, the proposed framework contains 
two modules, grape detection and yield estimation. In particular, KD-
ECNN is designed for grape detection. Then, the coordinate information 
and size information of the bounding box are extracted and filtered to 
generate the optimal box. For yield estimation, the depth information 
of the corresponding target is extracted from the LapDepth network and 
is associated with the corresponding area size of the target.
3.1. Grape detection based on KD-ECNN
To address the challenges in grape detection caused by lighting, 
color, and occlusion, we propose the KD-ECNN real-time detection net-
work. As depicted in Fig. 2, the KD-ECNN structure primarily includes 
teacher and student networks. In particular, to facilitate more efficient 
training, the teacher network is responsible for learning comprehensive 
feature representations and imparting this knowledge to the student 
network. Both the teacher network and student network share an 
identical structure and act as one-stage object detection models.
The KD-ECNN is composed of ECNN, CC2f_DCN, Efficient Layer Ag-
gregation Networks-Efficient Channel Attention (ELAN-ECA), Average 
and Max Mixture Pooling (AMMP), and Spatial Pyramid of Faster (SPF).
ECNN inherits the main structural concepts of the YOLO network, 
so it has a similar number of parameters as the YOLO network and 
can perform real-time detection. ECNN can directly predict the category 
and location within an image, effectively enhancing feature extraction 
ability.For occlusion issues, the backbone incorporates a CC2f_DCN

3.1.2. CC2f_DCN
The C2f module draws inspiration from the design concepts of the 
C3 module and ELAN. This allows YOLOv8 to maintain a lightweight 
structure while capturing richer gradient flow information. However, 
stacking multiple instances modules may lead to redundant channel 
information, and the standard convolutional kernels might restrict 
the network’s receptive field. These limitations would cause missed 
detections, particularly when dealing with occluded targets.
To address these issues, we introduce deformable convolution (Dai 
et al., 2017) (DCN) to enhance the feature representation. First, an 
offset is generated through a convolutional layer, using the same convo-
lution kernel as a regular convolution layer. The size of the output offset 
matches the input feature map, and the generated channel dimension 
is 2N, corresponding to the original output features and the offset 
features. By learning different optimal convolution kernel structures 
based on target data, DCN enhances feature extraction for objects. 
Therefore, the DCN equips the model with adaptive sampling, enriching 
features and lifting accuracy on occluded berries.
Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the CC2f_DCN 
module, which is integrated into the early layers of ECNN. As shown 
in Fig. 5, this module consists of Conv, Split, Bottleneck, and Concat. 
Bottleneck replaces vanilla 3 × 3 with deformable convolution to 
harvest richer object details.

AMMP (Fig. 7) shrinks feature maps, cuts compute, curbs overfit-
ting, and anchors feature locations. This module consists of average 
pooling and max pooling. Average pooling preserves overall informa-
tion by calculating the average value within each pooling window, but 
it may ignore key details. Max pooling effectively retains prominent 
features by extracting the maximum value from each pooling window, 
though it may lose some subtle but important information. Therefore, 
we integrate two pooling methods by employing max and average 
pooling in conjunction with feature fusion.

3.1.5. Self-knowledge distillation
Knowledge distillation(KD) is now widely applied to various
machine-learning tasks, such as image classification (Zeng et al., 2024), 
object detection (He et al., 2023), and natural language process-
ing (Wan et al., 2024). Distillation transfers the teacher’s detection 
know-how to the student, cutting compute and boosting field real-
time performance. Knowledge distillation enhances the multi-scale 
representation ability. In contrast to traditional knowledge distillation 
methods (Hinton, 2015), this study employs an identical CNN structure 
for both the teacher and student networks. Specifically, ECNN is trained 
first, and the model weights with the best performance are saved. 
During the knowledge distillation phase, the teacher network loads 
these pre-trained weights. For the same batch of input images, teacher 
and student predictions are compared to compute loss; this difference 
guides the next batch update. Simultaneously, the student network’s 
predictions are compared with the true labels to calculate the loss 
and perform backpropagation. For example, Tian et al. (2023) used 
self-knowledge distillation to improve the performance of CNN for the 
license plate recognition task.
In addition, traditional distillation methods only use the network’s 
final prediction results to calculate the loss, which is insufficient to 
optimize the student network’s performance. Therefore, we utilizes the 
intermediate layer predictions of ECNN to calculate the loss and per-
form forward propagation, as shown by Loss3 in Fig. 2. This enhanced 
distillation method leads to significant performance improvements in 
the student network.

typically used to optimize the model. The distillation loss is calculated 
between the teacher network’s predictions (soft labels) and the student 
network’s predictions (soft predictions). The KD formula is given by Eq.

3.2. Grape yield estimation based on depth estimation
Yield estimates alert growers to crop swings and guide labor and 
harvest schedules. With the continuous development of precision agri-
culture, automation in sowing, yield estimation, and harvesting has 
gradually become a reality. However, these technologies are primarily 
applied to crops such as wheat, rice, and corn. For fruits and vegetables 
like grapes, the canopy structure is more complex, often resulting in 
overlapping and occlusion of the fruits. As a result, most vineyards 
still rely on manual sampling to estimate overall yield. This approach 
is time-consuming and cannot ensure accurate estimates. Therefore, 
precise and efficient automated yield estimation techniques are needed 
to improve the management efficiency of grape cultivation.

To accurately estimate grape yield, this study proposes a new 
method, YEDE. KD-ECNN is employed for grape detection, while the 
LapDepth network provides depth information. The grape size is then 
combined with this depth data to estimate the overall grape yield. The 
specific algorithm is given in Algorithm 1.
In the vineyard scene, relying solely on 2D bounding box detec-
tion often leads to duplicate counting and false detections due to the 
overlapping projections of grape clusters at different distances. To 
alleviate this issue, the proposed method introduces a monocular depth 
estimation module into the detection framework. The main function 
of the module is to predict the depth value of the center point for 
each detection box and use dynamic thresholds for spatial stratification. 
The module eliminating far-field candidate boxes with severe overlap, 
thereby reducing the risk of false detections caused by depth projection 
overlap. Unlike traditional NMS, this method introduces spatial con-
straints using depth information, making it more physically reasonable 
in complex three-dimensional environments.
In this method, the target detection network serves as the foun-
dation for detecting grape targets in the images. Feed-forwarding all 
images returns per-image bounding-box coordinates and sizes. The 
center point and boundary box information of the bunch of grapes 
are input into the depth estimation network. The network calculates 
the depth estimation information for each bunch of grapes based on 
the center point coordinates, and then combines the area size of the 
boundary box to conduct precise yield estimation. The depth estimation 
network, which is key to this method, extracts depth information for 
each bounding box. Once the bounding box information for all images 
is input into the network, the depth information of the center point 
of each bounding box, representing the depth of each grape target, 
can be obtained. Combining the number of grapes, the size of each 
grape cluster, and the depth information enables more accurate yield 
estimation. The advantage of this method is that it is not limited 
to a two-dimensional perspective but incorporates depth dimension 
analysis, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the spatial 
distribution of the grapes and enabling more precise yield estimation.

3.2.1. Depth estimation network
In vineyard scenarios, occlusion can be divided into near-field oc-
clusion and far-field occlusion. Near-field occlusion caused by leaves, 
branches, or adjacent grape clusters on the same depth plane. Far-field 
occlusion where grape clusters at different depths overlap in the image 
projection. Near-field occlusion mainly affects target visibility, while 
far-field occlusion often results in blurred target boundaries, reduced 
detection confidence, and duplicate counting.
To achieve accurate yield estimation, this study employs a monocu-
lar depth estimation network based on Laplacian pyramid depth resid-
uals (Song et al., 2021) to extract depth information for the targets. 
In this network, encoded features are processed through stacked con-
volutional blocks, which generate sub-band depth residuals at each 
level of the pyramid. The depth residuals from each level are com-
bined to progressively refine the depth map from coarse to fine scales, 
thereby improving the accuracy of depth boundary predictions. With 
the multi-layer depth residual decoding approach, the network can 
more effectively utilize encoded features to estimate depth information 
in complex scenes.
The structure of this method is illustrated in Fig. 8. The network 
consists of a pre-trained encoder and a decoder for recovering depth 
residuals. The encoder uses ResNext101 compresses the input color 
image into latent features through deeply stacked convolutional blocks. 
The encoder reduce the spatial size to 1/16 of the original resolution 
while embedding the relationship between color and depth values in 
a compact latent space. The decoder is divided into multiple branches 
of the Laplacian pyramid. One branch handles the highest level of the 
pyramid, performing the decoding task to recover the global layout 
of the depth map, while the other branches generate depth residuals. 
These residuals are guided by the Laplacian residuals corresponding to 
the scale of the input color image (from L4 to L1). The depth residuals 
are combined with the intermediate depth map using pointwise addi-
tion from the higher levels of the Laplacian pyramid. In this study, a 
monocular depth estimation module was introduced into the detection 
network. It not only serves to filter out candidate boxes that are too 
far away and severely occluded, but also enables the detection results 
to be stratified by depth, which is helpful in reducing the interference 
caused by distant occlusion during the feature fusion stage. Unlike 
directly eliminating distant targets, the depth estimation strategy in the 
proposed method retains the visible clusters in the near scene while 
reducing false detections and duplicate counting caused by the overlap 
of distant and near targets through dynamic thresholds and spatial 
stratification strategies.
3.2.2. Bounding box information acquisition
After obtaining the detection results from the grape images, the next 
step is to extract the center point coordinates and the corresponding 
bounding box area for each detected target. The data from each image 
is organized into an array, where each array contains multiple lists. 
In each list, the first two entries represent the target’s center point 
coordinates, and the third represents the bounding box area. These 
data are then fed into the depth estimation network, which provides 
depth information for each grape bunch based on the center point 
coordinates. Finally, combining the bounding box size allows for more 
accurate yield estimation, which contributes to improving the accuracy 
of yield prediction by integrating spatial and geometric information.
3.2.3. Bounding box screening and filtering
To prevent duplicate estimation of grape bunch yield, we filter de-
tection results before inputting them into the depth estimation network.

4. Dataset
4.1. Dataset construction
Though Deep learning models have achieved significant success 
in object detection tasks, sufficient labeled data is guaranteed for 
training an effective model. Currently, the number of publicly available 
grape datasets is limited, and the available datasets represent relatively 
uniform scenes, which restricts progress in complex environments. To 
address this, we construct the GD dataset to enhance the model’s 
detection capabilities across various environments. The GD dataset not 
only increased the sample size, but also significantly expanded the scale 
diversity and occlusion complexity. GD introduced special scenarios 
such as night scenes, strong back-lighting, and high-density vineyards, 
which were underrepresented in WGISD. Fig. 10 shows some example 
images from this dataset.
As shown in Table 1, the GD dataset includes various types, scales 
and colors of grapes across multiple environments, covering different 
planting scenarios, lighting conditions. It contains 1900 images, all 
sourced from the Roboflow platform, and is randomly divided into 
training and validation sets at a 9:1 ratio. Additionally, to further 
enhance the diversity of scenes, we select images from the grape_
detection_californianight dataset to ensure that the dataset effectively 
represents a wide range of complex environments, which improves the 
model’s generalization ability and adaptability.
4.2. Pre-processing
In this study, data processing involves transforming raw information 
into a more useful and optimized format for enhancing its informative-
ness and practical value. This study adopts a phased data processing 
approach to ensure the final output aligns with the expected objectives.
In the first phase of data processing, data cleaning involves in-
specting the dataset’s image quality and removing any damaged or 
low-quality images to maintain consistency. Next, the dataset is split 
into training and validation sets at a 9:1 ratio, ensuring the effec-
tiveness of the model in training tasks while reserving a portion for 
validation. Finally, data format conversion is conducted to provide 
VOC and COCO formats, meeting the requirements of different ob-
ject detection models. The data format conversion includes converting 
YOLO-format label files (txt format) into XML and JSON formats. This 
conversion step was critical for ensuring the adaptability and flexibility 
of the dataset. These preprocessing establish a high-quality, properly 
formatted dataset, providing a solid foundation for optimizing model 
performance in subsequent training and validation stages.
4.3. Data annotation
The LabelImg toolbox is used to annotate the images. For example, 
the label ‘‘grapes’’ is assigned to the annotated objects, and the YOLO 
format is used to store the labeled data. The YOLO format follows a 
specific data structure, with each label defined by five parameters: the 
first value represents the class ID, the second and third values represent 
the center coordinates of the bounding box, and the last two values 
represent the width and height of the bounding box. Additionally, 
this study provides the dataset in VOC and COCO formats to increase 
its applicability and enable its adaptation in various object detection 
models.

6. Experimental results and analysis
6.1. Comparison of detection performance on different datasets
Due to the diversity of grape-growing environments and to avoid 
the need for repeatedly retraining models for grape detection, this 
study constructs a more complex grape detection dataset. To verify the 
generalization ability of the models trained on the GD dataset, the pub-
licly available GrappoliV2 (G2d) and Two-type-grape-detection dataset 
(Ttgdd) are used as validation datasets. The models were trained on the 
GD, G2d, and Ttgdd datasets, and validated through comparison on G2d 
and Ttgdd. Since models trained on the two public datasets performed 
poorly when validated on the GD dataset, we did not conduct extensive 
experimental comparisons involving these datasets in this study.
The G2d dataset contains only one target type, whereas the Ttgdd 
dataset includes two target types. To adapt for experimental validation, 
we unify the target types in Ttgdd into a single type, as shown in 
Fig. 11, which lists image examples from both public datasets. It can 
be inferred that these models trained on G2d and validated on Ttgdd 
might perform better than those trained on Ttgdd and validated on G2d.

detection performance.

6.2.4. Performance impact of IoU loss on ECNN

6.5. Yield estimation experiment
The test data for this experiment were collected in Yinchuan, China, 
with 50 grape images selected for yield estimation. Fig. 20 depicts the 
grape samples, where the grapes are in the early fruit stage, about one 
month before maturity.
6.5.1. Grape count
Counting is the foundation of yield estimation and is also essential 
for achieving accurate results. To evaluate the precision of our yield 
estimation, we conducted a comparative analysis of counting perfor-
mance between the proposed detection method and several mainstream 
networks. The comparison results are visualized in Fig. 21, which 
displays the linear regression relationships between the actual counts 
(x-axis) and the estimated counts (y-axis) for each method.
As shown in the figure, the proposed method demonstrates superior 
counting accuracy, with its results most closely aligning with the actual 
values. This is quantitatively confirmed by its linear regression equation 
𝑦= 0.8678𝑥, which is nearest to the ideal 𝑦= 𝑥. While YOLOv5 and 
YOLOv7 also exhibit competent performance, their regression equa-
tions (𝑦= 0.8213𝑥 and 𝑦= 0.7928𝑥, respectively) indicate larger 
deviations from the ideal line, resulting in greater counting errors com-
pared to our method. In contrast, YOLOv8n, YOLOv9, and YOLOv10n 
show unsatisfactory performance with significant discrepancies from 
the ground truth. In conclusion, the proposed method surpasses all 
comparative approaches in counting precision.
6.5.2. Yield estimated
Since the counting results are the primary variable in yield esti-
mation, in this section, we evaluate several different yield estimation 
methods. Two methods of comparison are included. The first method 
involves directly using the counting results, assuming a standard yield 
(or average yield per grape cluster) for each cluster and that all clusters 
produce the same yield. The second method combines the counting 
results with the area of the grape clusters (bounding box area).
Table 13 presents the error between the estimated and actual yields 
for several methods, and p represents per image. The actual yield in 
the table is obtained from the actual weighing, which is visible from 
the table. Our evaluation reveals that relying solely on detection count-
ing for yield estimation results in substantial inaccuracy. Although 
incorporating the bounding box area as a supplementary parameter 
improves the accuracy compared to the pure counting-based method, 
this combined approach still demonstrates considerable estimation er-
ror, indicating the need for further refinement. In contrast, the yield 
estimation method proposed in this study produces the smallest error, 
with estimates closest to the actual yield. As the estimated grape yield 
using the proposed model is compared with the actual yield in Fig. 
22, the regression line for the proposed method is 𝑦= 0.7499𝑥, with 
𝑅2 = 0.4985. That means the proposed method performs well in grape 
yield estimation.
The 𝑅2 value obtained in this article is 0.4985, indicating that there 
is still a certain difference between the estimated results and the true 
values. The scattered distribution of data points can be attributed to 
several factors: natural variability in grape cluster composition, detec-
tion uncertainties caused by occlusion and lighting, and the inherent 
limitations of estimating weight from 2D bounding boxes, which fails 
to account for 3D geometry and fruit density. The fitted line’s deviation 
from the origin results from intentionally retaining the intercept term in 
the regression model, a statistically sound approach that compensates 
for systematic detection bias in low-yield scenarios, albeit creating a 
line offset. Despite these variations, the strong correlation between 
detection results and actual yield remains evident in the overall trend.
As shown in Table 13, although the proposed method improves 
yield estimation accuracy, there remains a margin of error. This is 
primarily due to the complexity of the detection environment, which 
increases the difficulty of detection and counting. The depth estimation 
approach adopted in this study remains insufficiently explored, and 
its limitations in complex scenarios have adversely impacted yield 
estimation accuracy. Given that depth estimation serves as the cor-
nerstone of the proposed method, enhancing the performance of the 
depth estimation model is pivotal to improving overall effectiveness. 
Consequently, further optimization and reinforcement of depth esti-
mation capabilities will be crucial for achieving more accurate yield 
assessments in complex environments, thereby boosting the method’s 
overall reliability and practical utility.
This study employed two simplified assumptions for yield estima-
tion: uniform cluster weights and weight estimation via bounding box 
area. These simplifications reduced modeling complexity and enabled 
rapid deployment in scenarios lacking detailed annotations and 3D 
data. However, they could not fully capture real-world biological varia-
tions, such as differences in fruit density within clusters, morphological 
changes, and observation errors from lighting or sensor noise.
To enhance estimation accuracy, future work should integrate 3D 
geometric features with color-texture information (Nuske et al., 2014) 
to better represent internal cluster structure and fruit distribution. In a 
related approach, Yang et al. (2024) proposed a probability map-based 
framework for grape detection and counting, which has demonstrated 
strong performance in cluster detection, berry counting, and yield 
quantification tasks.

7. Conclusion
To enhance model generalization, we developed the GD dataset 
and validated the method’s adaptability across diverse complex envi-
ronments. The proposed KD-ECNN detection framework incorporates 
multiple high-performance modules and knowledge distillation strate-
gies, achieving a significant improvement in detection accuracy while 
maintaining computational efficiency. Experimental results on the GD 
dataset demonstrate that KD-ECNN attains an mAP of 81.6%, out-
performing other mainstream models. Furthermore, the YEDE yield 
estimation method improves accuracy by approximately 25% and 10% 
compared to counting-only and area-based approaches, respectively.
In summary, grape detection and depth estimation constitute the 
core processes for yield prediction. While KD-ECNN has exhibited 
strong performance in detection accuracy and real-time inference, and 
YEDE has enhanced the robustness of yield estimation, limitations 
remain in depth estimation under challenging conditions such as in-
sufficient nighttime lighting or small grape clusters. In addition, due to 
the lack of a production dataset, it is impossible to compare and verify 
the predicted production with the actual production. Future research 
will focus on refining the depth estimation module by integrating addi-
tional scene features and multimodal information, building production 
dataset, thereby advancing the accuracy and reliability of detection and 
yield prediction across a wider spectrum of application scenarios.
