A B S T R A C T
Rising food demand, labor shortages, and field variability are pushing crop management toward robust, on- 
device computer vision systems. This review discussed the development of Vision Transformers and Vision 
Mamba (ViM) in the context of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), comparing their architectures and 
drawing their implications for the next generation of agricultural AI. CNNs still remain efficient in baselines for 
local pattern recognition in many simple agri-vision tasks, but struggle with long-range dependencies and 
complex canopy scenes. By introducing global context with self-attention, ViT improves robustness to occlusion, 
scale change, and multimodal fusion. ViM replaces self-attention with selective state-space sequence modeling, 
avoiding quadratic complexity and enabling linear-time scaling for long sequences and high-resolution imagery 
while maintaining competitive accuracy. A synthesis of 2020–2025 studies across disease monitoring, weed/pest 
control, fruit counting and yield estimation, and land/soil/water analysis indicates: (i) ViT is highly likely 
surpasses CNN baselines under cluttered, variable field conditions with sufficient data and appropriate training; 
(ii) ViM provides an effective accuracy-efficiency balance for edge and UAV platforms; and (iii) hybrid designs (e. 
g., CNN combines with ViT/ViM heads or ViM necks in detectors) are more reliable in low light and dense 
canopies. Persistent challenges included limited multi-site data diversity, sensitivity to illumination and occlu­
sion, edge-device energy/latency constraints, and limited interpretability. A forward pathway was outlined: 
standardized domain-shift tests, energy/latency reporting, quantization/distillation for edge deployment, 
uncertainty-aware outputs, and principled multimodal fusion, which can lead ViM-centric or hybrid pipelines to 
practical next step for field-ready agri-vision.
1. Introduction
The global economy depends heavily on the agricultural sector. Food 
demand on the agricultural system rises as the world population keeps 
growing (Attri et al., 2024). Precision farming and agri-technology, also 
referred to as digital agriculture, are emerging as new scientific disci­
plines that use data-intensive methods to increase agricultural produc­
tivity while reducing its environmental impact. Together with big data 
technology and high-performance computing, deep learning (DL) sup­
ports complex, heterogeneous information streams in agricultural 
operational environments.
In agricultural computer vision, convolutional neural networks 
(CNNs) have been widely applied into crop management tasks, 
including weed and pest control, soil and water management, crop 
monitoring and disease detection (Coulibaly et al., 2022). However, 
CNNs are intrinsically local in their receptive fields, which can limit 
performance under long-range spatial dependencies, heavy occlusion, 
and large-scale variation typical of field scenes. The introduction of the 
attention mechanism (Vaswani et al., 2017) led to Vision Transformers, 
which model global context through self-attention and have been 
applied widely in vision domains including medical segmentation 
(Karimi et al., 2021), hyperspectral analysis (Zeng et al., 2023), auton­
omous systems (Lai-Dang, 2024), robotics (Scherl et al., 2025), and se­
curity (Singh et al., 2022). In agricultural settings, ViT and its variants 
have been leveraged for disease diagnosis, crop monitoring, and related 
tasks, contributing to advances in precision agriculture (Xie et al., 2024).
More recently, the computational cost and quadratic scaling of self- 
attention have motivated efficient alternatives. Vision Mamba (ViM)

architecture-level analysis and recent applications (classification,

2. Related surveys
2.1. Paper selection criteria in the survey
The criteria for paper inclusion focused on two principal architec­
tures: vision transformers (ViTs) and mamba models. Relevant keywords 
were identified about modern crop management, particularly focusing 
on precision agriculture, including crop protection (encompassing dis­
ease, insect, and weed detection), crop optimization (focused on yield 
prediction), and resource stewardship for agricultural sustainability 
(covering land, soil, and water management). A search was conducted 
across major digital libraries, including Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, 
the Multidisciplinary Digital Publishing Institute (MDPI), and the IEEE 
Xplore Digital Library. The search timeline, beginning in January 2020, 
was set to capture the critical transition in deep learning architectures, 
covering publications from the pre-transformer era through the initial 
introduction of vision transformers and mamba. The number of papers 
found from the search queries is shown in Table 1.
In this study, the systematic literature review was conducted based 
on the PRISMA 2020 guideline (Page et al., 2021), as shown in Fig. 1. An 
initial search of electronic databases yielded 5,577 results: Google 
Scholar (N = 4706), ScienceDirect (N = 576), MDPI (N = 247), and IEEE 
Xplore (N = 48). After applying database filters and removing dupli­
cates, non-English publications, and records with unavailable full texts, 
3,416 records remained. Although the search string returned a large 
number of papers, many studies did not directly address the research 
questions or were unrelated to agricultural applications. Consequently, 
a manual screening of titles, abstracts, and keywords was performed, 
retaining 207 records. After that, an in-depth review was conducted to 
determine each article's alignment with the research questions and its 
significance to this survey. This evaluation focused on core aspects, 
including objectives, methodology, and findings, ultimately resulting in 
a final selection of 112 papers. Additionally, 11 papers focused on 
computer science were manually added to ensure technical depth 
beyond agricultural applications, bringing the final selection to 123 
articles.
Given that our survey focuses on cutting-edge AI, particularly the 
recent Mamba architecture and novel applications of Vision Trans­
formers, some preprint papers were selected due to their methodological 
significance and their contribution to the evolution of architecture, 
rather than merely based on the reported excellent performance in­
dicators. After combining all the identified papers and removing 
duplication, a total of 123 papers were reviewed. Of the selected papers 
for crop management (91 papers), 30, 25, 24, 13 were related to crop 
disease detection, insect and weed management, crop detection and 
yield prediction, as well as land, soil, and water management, respec­
tively (Fig. 2(a)). While the primary focus of the search was on publi­
cations since 2020, resulting in 112 papers published from 2020 to 
2025, some critical foundational papers published before 2020 (totaling 
11 papers) were also included in this study to ensure comprehensive 
coverage. The percentage of selected articles published in different years 
after 2020 is shown in Fig. 2(b).

3. Current challenges in traditional crop management
3.1. Rising demands for agricultural efficiency
Global agriculture faces increasing food demand due to rapid pop­
ulation growth, climate change, declining agricultural land, soil degra­
dation, and labor shortages. These challenges require transformative 
solutions that can enhance productivity while maintaining environ­
mental sustainability. Traditional farming practices, which heavily rely 
on manual labor and generalized management, are unable to meet the 
precision and efficiency required by modern food systems (Wang and 
Kang, 2025). Furthermore, rapid population growth has become a major 
problem for environmental degradation and a threat to the sustainable 
use of natural resources. Overexploitation, intensive farming, and land 
fragmentation are the main issues that reduce the quality and quantity of 
natural resources (Maja and Ayano, 2021). This growing challenge 
highlights the demand for more efficient land use strategies and the 
production of higher-quality agricultural outputs. Climate change pre­
sents threats to agricultural productivity and global food security, pri­
marily due to the increased frequency and intensity of environmental 
stresses such as drought, heatwaves, frost, and other extreme weather 
events. Without effective adaptation measures, crop yields are projected 
to decline by 7% to 23% under the most severe climate change scenarios 
(Arif et al., 2025). This anticipated reduction shows significant risks to 
both global food systems. To reduce these impacts, it is necessary for 
policymakers and agricultural stakeholders to utilize targeted, science- 
driven strategies that enhance crop resilience, improve resource effi­
ciency, and support sustainable farming practices.
3.2. Environmental impact
Crop management practices can lead to environmental consequences 
if not carefully managed. These major impacts, including chemical use, 
soil degradation, and water waste in irrigation, indicate the need for 
more sustainable, data-driven approaches. Crop spraying, a funda­
mental practice for applying herbicides, pesticides, and fertilizers, is 
traditionally performed uniformly across fields without accounting for 
local variability. This traditional method resulted in great chemical 
waste, up to 60–70%, and environmental contamination (Hussain et al., 
2020; Acharya et al., 2023). Similarly, intensive tillage and monoculture 
systems contribute to soil erosion and the loss of soil fertility by stripping 
away natural ground cover. These impacts have made soil data analysis 
increasingly important for guiding decisions related to land use, climate 
regulation, and environmental planning (T´oth et al., 2013). Methods 
like AFSIS and LUCAS have supported machine learning applications in 
soil analysis, enabling more informed decision-making. However, limi­
tations such as small sample sizes, regional variability, and uneven land 
use data continue to hinder their effectiveness (Veres et al., 2015). 
Additionally, irrigated agroecosystems face a range of challenges, 
including runoff, salinization, erosion, and water shortages (Arbat and 
Masseroni, 2024). Traditional irrigation practices often overlook these 
issues by prioritizing yield over sustainability. Addressing these con­
cerns requires further research and the integration of innovative tech­
nologies to support more resilient and adaptive irrigation strategies.
3.3. Limits of traditional approaches in precision agriculture
While traditional crop management practices have supported agri­
cultural production in decades, it is growing insufficiently for the pre­
cision and efficiency demanded by modern farming. As agricultural 
systems become more complex and resource constraints intensify, there 
is a clear need for smarter, more targeted approaches. For instance, frost 
mitigation methods such as wind machines, sprinklers, and greenhouse 
heating, though effective, are expensive and often out of reach for small- 
scale farmers (Cerd´a et al., 2018; Hua et al., 2024). These systems also 
require accurate weather predictions to avoid unnecessary use and 
energy waste. Similarly, manual weeding remains common but is labor- 
intensive and inefficient, while mechanical methods can cause crop 
damage without precise control (Bakhshipour et al., 2017). Yield esti­
mation typically relies on manual sampling or farmer recall, which can 
introduce significant errors (Sapkota et al., 2016). Pest monitoring also 
depends on physical traps checked by human operators, leading to high 
labor costs and limited data resolution (Preti et al., 2021). Many tradi­
tional farming methods are falling short of modern precision agriculture 
needs, emphasizing the importance of adopting more accurate, efficient, 
and automated solutions. The traditional crop management challenges 
discussed above are summarized in Fig. 3.
4. Visual architectures in agriculture
4.1. Computer vision in crop management
Computer vision is widely used in agriculture to increase produc­
tivity, reduce resource usage, and improve farm management. Agricul­
ture uses several computer vision technologies:
Image Classification: Image classification is employed to categorize 
agricultural images for identifying crop types, plant diseases, and pest 
infestations.
Object Detection: Object detection involves recognizing and local­
izing specific objects within an image, making it essential for tasks such 
as automated harvesting and precision weed control. It enables the 
detection and spatial localization of fruits, vegetables, and weeds within 
complex agricultural environments.
Object Tracking: Object tracking monitors the movement and 
behavior of dynamic entities such as pollinators (e.g., bees) and pests 
across video frames. This facilitates behavioral analysis and ecological 
monitoring, offering insights into species interactions and their impact 
on crop health and yield.
Instance Segmentation: Instance segmentation extends semantic 
segmentation by not only classifying each pixel but also distinguishing 
between multiple instances of the same object class. This is particularly 
beneficial for counting individual fruits, plants, or animals in a given 
image.
4.2. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in computer vision
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have long been the corner­
stone of visual perception in computer vision, providing a powerful 
framework for extracting hierarchical patterns directly from image data. 
The overall architecture of CNN models is fundamentally the same and 
adheres to a fixed paradigm, including an input layer, alternate layers of 
convolution and pooling layers, one or more fully connected layers, 
activation functions, and output layers implemented with a feature 
extractor. Fully connected layers are used in combination with activa­
tion functions to execute tasks such as classification or regression on the 
extracted features. To maximize CNN performance, various regulatory 
units like batch normalization and dropout are also included in addition 
to various mapping functions (Bouvrie, 2006). Fig. 4 shows various CNN 
components.
The strength of CNNs is to encode local spatial patterns and build 
feature maps through stacking multiple layers. In convolutional layers, 
the CNN model extracts local patterns such as edges, textures, or shapes 
from the input image and generates the feature map. Then, in pooling 
layers, the spatial size of feature maps is reduced while retaining 
important information, which makes computation costs lower and 
avoids overfitting. CNN models make them ideal for a variety of vision 
tasks, from classification to segmentation. While early models such as 
LeNet-5 laid the groundwork, the transformative shift in performance 
came with deeper and more structured designs like AlexNet, VGGNet, 
ResNet, Inception, and DenseNet (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014; He 
et al., 2016; Szegedy et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2017). Each of these 
architectures contributed to novel mechanisms, such as residual

In crop management, CNNs address disease diagnosis, weed/pest 
recognition, fruit quality assessment, and phenotyping pipelines, 
particularly when training data are scarce, scenes are simple, or devices 
are constrained (e.g., sprayers, robots, UAVs). Their limitations are well 
known: receptive fields grow only indirectly with depth, so capturing 
long-range dependencies, severe occlusions, or wide scale variation is 
harder than with global models. As a result, CNNs may struggle with 
cluttered canopies or mixed-sensor fusion compared to ViT and ViM, but 
they remain competitive baselines, especially after compression.
4.3. Vision transformer architectures in computer vision
Transformers, originally developed for natural language processing 
(Vaswani et al., 2017), have been successfully adapted to vision tasks, 
offering strong modeling of long-range dependencies through self- 
attention mechanisms. A key component of ViT is the self-attention 
mechanism, which enables the model to weigh the importance of 
different patches when making predictions. Self-attention works by 
comparing all patches to each other and computing attention scores that 
determine how much influence one patch should have on another. 
Multi-head attention enhances this by allowing the model to focus on 
different types of relationships in parallel. Specifically, scaled dot- 
product attention is computed as:

tion, Vision Transformers is an architecture grounded in the self-

shift by modeling images as sequences of non-overlapping patches, 
which are treated similarly to word tokens in NLP (Dosovitskiy et al., 
2021). Each patch is embedded and passed through a series of trans­

attention. The basic idea behind ViT is to divide an image of resolution 
H×W with C channels into non-overlapping patches of size P×P. Each 
patch is flattened into a vector and linearly projected to a common latent 
dimension, producing a sequence of patch embeddings. A learnable

The Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) represents an 
advancement in integrating transformer architectures with object 
detection pipelines. While DETR (Carion et al., 2020) demonstrated that 
transformers could replace traditional object detectors by eliminating 
hand-crafted components like non-maximum suppression (NMS), its 
slow convergence and high computational cost hindered real-time 
applicability. RT-DETR resolves these issues by proposing the first 
real-time end-to-end object detector that successfully bridges the gap 
between detection quality and inference efficiency (Zhao et al., 2024b).
RT-DETR adopted a refined version of DETR’s encoder-decoder 
transformer architecture and introduced several innovations to 
improve speed and accuracy, as shown in Fig. 6. A central component is 
the hybrid encoder, which decouples intra-scale and cross-scale in­
teractions by using attention-based intra-scale feature interaction (AIFI) 
and convolution-based cross-scale feature fusion (CCFF). This design 
significantly reduces the computational burden of standard self- 
attention while preserving spatial context. Moreover, instead of 
relying solely on classification scores to initialize object queries, RT- 
DETR implements an uncertainty-minimal query selection strategy 
that explicitly optimizes the query of uncertainty using both classifica­
tion and localization cues. These queries are then refined through 
transformer decoder layers to predict class labels and bounding boxes.
An important feature of RT-DETR is its support for flexible speed 
tuning by adjusting the number of decoder layers, making it adaptable 
for diverse deployment requirements. It also allows for architectural 
scaling, like YOLO models, offering configurations from lightweight 
models to more accurate, heavier models.
In crop management, Vision Transformers (ViT) have been widely 
applied to plant disease diagnosis (Hassan et al., 2025), weed recogni­
tion (Arthur et al., 2025), fruit detection (Jrondi et al., 2024), canopy 
segmentation (Chen et al., 2025), and growth stage monitoring (Xiao 
et al., 2023). These applications benefit from ViT’s global self-attention, 
which models long-range spatial dependencies and improves robustness 
to occlusion, scale variation, and cluttered field backgrounds relative to 
CNN baselines (Elharrouss et al., 2025). ViT backbones (e.g., ViT, RT- 
DETR variants) have also enabled stronger multimodal fusion with 
ancillary signals such as weather, soil, or hyperspectral cues, enhancing 
early detection and pre-symptomatic screening (Zhao et al., 2024b).
4.4. Vision Mamba architecture in computer vision
With the success of Transformer architectures in natural language 
processing (NLP), recent advancements have focused on building even 
more efficient and scalable models for vision tasks. The Mamba model 
(Gu et al., 2024) introduced a novel approach to sequence modeling that 
addresses some of the key limitations of Transformers, particularly their 
computational inefficiency and limited scalability to long sequences. 
While Transformers has become the foundation model due to their 
strong performance across diverse domains, the reliance on self- 
attention leads to significant memory and computation costs. Mamba 
provided an alternative by building structured state space models 
(SSMs) and offered linear-time computation while preserving the 
expressiveness needed for complex tasks.
The core of Mamba was a selective state space mechanism, where the 
model dynamically adjusts its parameters based on the input. This 
design enables content-based reasoning, and makes the model propagate 
or ignore information depending on the context. Unlike previous SSMs, 
which rely on linear time-invariant (LTI) dynamics and are constrained 
to static computations, Mamba introduces input-dependent selectivity 
that enables fine-grained control over memory and information flow 
along the sequence.
To maintain the model’s efficiency, a hardware-aware algorithm was 
developed in Mamba model that processes sequences in a recurrent 
manner, optimizing memory access across GPU hierarchies. This 
approach avoided storing large intermediate states and leverages kernel 
fusion and parallel scan operations to achieve high-speed inference. As a 
result, Mamba demonstrates up to 5 × faster inference throughput than 
Transformer models of similar size and handles sequences of up to 1 
million tokens.
Vision Mamba (ViM) was proposed based on the Selective State 
Space Model (Mamba) introduced by Zhu et al (2024). It is designed to 
minimize memory overhead by recomputing intermediate activations 
during the backward pass and by avoiding I/O-bound GPU memory 
usage through an SRAM-first strategy. Unlike CNNs, ViM does not rely 
on local receptive fields or inductive biases, and unlike transformers, it 
avoids the quadratic complexity of self-attention. It combines linear- 
time complexity with efficient hardware utilization. This design en­
ables linear scaling with sequence length, offering strong generalization 
to multimodal and gigapixel-level applications. The architecture of ViM 
processes a 2D image into non-overlapping patches, which are then 
flattened and linearly projected into a sequence of tokens. These tokens 
are augmented with positional embeddings and processed by stacked

5. Applications of transformer, Mamba, and CNN-hybrid in crop 
management
The rapid development of artificial intelligence, driven by architec­
tures like CNNs, Transformers, and Mamba, has become a powerful 
force. For applied precision agriculture, CNNs remain indispensable due 
to their efficiency. They are the preferable choice for tasks involving 
low-data regimes, where strict latency is required, and for deployment of 
constrained edge hardware. Their structural simplicity ensures robust 
performance for common, simple classification tasks. CNNs efficiently 
capture local features but often struggle with global context and have 
limited capacity to capture long-range dependencies, especially in 
complex outdoor environments. Transformers addressed this with self- 
attention mechanisms that can model long-range dependencies, 
though they require large datasets and significant computational re­
sources (Ding et al., 2024). Mamba emerged as a promising alternative, 
combining the sequence modeling efficiency of state space models with 
the spatial generalization needed for vision tasks. Its linear complexity 
and global receptive field suit large-scale agricultural applications (Gu 
et al., 2024). However, the early-stage development of Transformers and 
Mamba emphasizes the immediate need for established standard 
benchmarks and robust implementations. This section summarized the 
applications of Transformers and Mamba architecture, highlighting 
their performance and the advantages they offer in various crop man­
agement fields, such as plant disease detection, weed and insect detec­
tion, crop detection for yield prediction and harvesting, and agricultural 
resource management compared to CNNs. We also discussed hybrid 
models that combine CNNs, Transformers, or Mamba architectures, of­
fering a balanced approach to feature extraction and context awareness 
in crop management. The comparison in this survey focused on results 
obtained under identical experimental conditions for valid conclusions. 
However, the metrics cited from different external studies in this review 
may not be directly comparable, as variations in datasets, preprocessing, 
and training protocols fundamentally alter reported values.

With the development of Transformers since 2023, studies have 
investigated and compared the performance of CNNs and Transformers 
in disease detection. For example, Ahmad et al. (2024) proposed a ViT- 
based deep-learning approach for cotton leaf disease detection. The re­
sults show that the ViT model achieved an accuracy of 96.72% for binary 
classification (normal and disease) and 93.39% for multi-class classifi­
cation (bacterial, fungal, viral, and nutrient deficiency), which was 
higher than CNN’s 94.89% and 86.47% for binary and multi-class. Thai

number of attention heads based on ViTs for detecting plant infections 
and diseases, achieving a higher accuracy of 98.0% based on a data

To improve disease detection performance, previous studies have 
capitalized on the strengths of combining ViT and CNNs. For example, 
Wang et al. (2022) developed a deep learning architecture, convolution 
network enlightened transformer (CoNet) by combining the strengths of 
CNNs and ViTs to enhance the detection accuracy of ten tomato diseases. 
By fusing local features extracted by CNNs with global dependencies 
captured by ViTs, CoNet surpassed traditional CNN and ViT models in 
distinguishing subtle variations between plant diseases, achieving an 
average counting accuracy of 96.30%. Christakakis et al., (2024) eval­
uated four deep learning segmentation architectures, U-Net++, PAN, 
MA-Net, and DeepLabV3+, in combination with two ViT encoders, 
MobileViT-S, and MobileViTv2-1.25, for the detection of Botrytis cinerea 
on Cucurbitaceae crops. The authors found that the combination of U- 
Net++ and MobileViTV2-1.25 achieved the highest accuracy and recall 
of 90.6% and 84.8% with a minimal training epoch of 8. Li and Li (2022)
developed a lightweight hybrid model, ConvViT, combining the 
strengths of CNN and ViT for real-world apple leaf disease identification 
with limited computational resources. The model's lightweight design 
was achieved by reducing the computational complexity of convolu­
tional operations through depth-separable convolutions and global 
average pooling before attention operations. This optimization allowed 
ConvViT to achieve an accuracy of 96.85% while using only 32.7% of 
the parameters and 21.7% of the FLOPs of the Swin-Tiny model. 
PlantXViT proposed by Thakur et al. (2022) adopted a VGG16 backbone 
with transformer encoders to perform lightweight plant disease detec­
tion and achieved over 98% accuracy on maize and rice datasets with 
only 0.85 M parameters, promoting the application of real-time 
deployment on edge devices. Si et al. (2024) proposed a CNN-Swin 
dual-branch network with a Feature Interaction Module (FIM) for 
apple leaf disease classification, achieving an accuracy of 97.32% under 
noisy conditions.
To better handle long sequential data in agriculture, Mamba + CNN 
models, e.g., vision mamba and convNeXt (VMC)-Unet, diffusion-driven 
hybrid transfer learning with VMamba (DDHTL-VMamba), GMamba, 
and satellite image time series Mamba (SITSMamba), have been 
deployed in disease detection and demonstrate considerable promise. 
Based on U-Net, a CNN for image segmentation, VMC-UNet integrating 
ConvNeXt and Vision Mamba was proposed to improve lesion segmen­
tation in complex field backgrounds (Wang et al., 2024a). Shi et al. 
(2024) applied VMC-Unet for tomato blight disease spot detection, 
achieving higher performance compared to Unet (90.03% vs 74.44% of 
precision and 85.94% vs 63.30% of recall). Zhang et al. (2025a) pro­
posed the DDHTL-VMamba method, combining VMamba with diffusion 
models and transfer learning techniques. The method was applied to 
detect plant diseases with a small sample dataset and achieved a 3.49% 
increase in accuracy compared to Swin Transformer. Zhang and Mu 
(2024) proposed a GMamba model for segmenting the disease of grape 
leaves. In GMamba, a Co-SSM module characterized by a formulation of 
the state-space model (SSM) and convolution was designed, which can 
process the entire image and globally model the grape leaf disease 
without self-attention compared to Transformers. The results show that 
GMamba yields a 3.28% IoU and 1.65% precision increase over a hybrid 
CNN-Transformer architecture. A SITSMamba model, based on a CNN 
and a Mamba temporal encoder, was proposed for the classification of 
20 crops using satellite images (Qin et al., 2024). The results show that 
SITSMamba achieved the best overall accuracy of 74.16% compared to 
other models (66.97%-73.49%).
To combine the advantages of Transformer and Mamba architec­
tures, previous studies have developed various Transformer + Mamba 
networks for improved disease detection and have exhibited substantial 
potential. For example, Wang et al. (2025c) proposed a network archi­
tecture, LeafMamba, by integrating a state space model (SSM) with an 
optimized self-attention mechanism to identify the apple scab and rust 
diseases, as shown in Fig. 8. LeafMamba efficiently captures multi-scale 
information and improves the spatial resolution of feature maps, thereby 
showing a superior performance (mAP0.5 of 92.5%) than Faster RCNN, 
YOLOv8, and Vision Mamba (mAP0.5 of 82.1%, 88.7%, and 84.3%, 
respectively). Liu et al. (2025b) developed a YOLO-BSMamba network 
by integrating the HCMamba module and SimAM attention to address 
the computational inefficiency of transformers in modeling long se­
quences and reduce background noise interference, respectively. The 
results show that the network for tomato leaf disease detection achieved 
an F1 score and mAP0.5 of 81.9% and 86.7%. Bai et al. (2025) proposed a 
detection model integrated with Mamba and attention mechanisms for 
pine wilt disease. The model comprised three principal components, i.e., 
Mamba backbone network, attention network, and path Aggregation 
Feature Pyramid Network to enhance the detection capacity of multi- 
scale objects, achieving an accuracy of 90.0%, and a recall of 81.8%.

5.2. Insect and weed management
The challenge of insect and weed management in agriculture has 
demanded increasingly efficient solutions in recent years. Transformer 
and Mamba have gained increasing attention with the capability for 
capturing global contextual information and long-range dependencies, 
respectively, and thus comparisons between Transformer, Mamba, and 
CNN have been extensively carried out. For example, Sanjay et al. 
(2023) compared a ViT model and a neural network model for detecting 
and classifying weeds in soybean crops. The ViT model demonstrated 
superior performance, achieving a higher accuracy of 98.83% than the 
neural network (94.58%). Ahmad et al. (2024) developed a UAV 
imagery-based ViT model for automated weed detection in carrot fields, 
achieving an accuracy of 89.4% outperforming U-Net's 85.1%. Remya 
et al. (2024) trained a ViT model with a dataset of cotton fields with and 
without whitefly infestations using 20,000 images. The author indicated 
that the model obtained an average accuracy of 99%, outperforming the 
CNN networks, e.g., SVM, KNN, MobileNet, and VGG, with accuracy 
ranging from 80% to 90%. Castellano et al. (2023) proposed a light­
weight transformer architecture, SplitLawin, to transfer red, green, and 
blue (RGB) weight knowledge to multispectral settings using high- 
resolution drone aerial images for weed mapping in sugar beet fields. 
SplitLawin outperformed SegNet, a CNN architecture for semantic 
image segmentation, achieving a higher F1 score (0.957 vs. 0.828). 
While the temporal shape variations of crops and weeds were not 
considered in this study, the authors suggested the incorporation of 
these time-varying properties within the model for future research. 
Sharma and Vardhan (2023) developed a self-attention ViT-S16 using a 
transfer learning (TL) approach to classify weeds in benchmark datasets. 
The experimental results show an accuracy of 98.54% using ViT-S16, 
higher than the ResNeXt-50 and EfficientNet-B7 models. A groundnut 
vision transformer model (GNViT) developed by Venkatasaichan­
drakanth and Iyapparaja (2024) with 14 million images surpassed all 
other models with a remarkable accuracy of 99.52%. In comparison, 
LeNet, AlexNet, and ResNet achieved accuracies of 66.00%, 72%, and 
62%, respectively. Srivast et al. (2024) investigated the performance of 
classification and object detection algorithms, ViT and EfficientnetB0, 
on unmanned aerial systems-based RGB images of soybean and Palmer 
amaranth weed. ViT and EfficientnetB0 achieved accuracies of 97.69% 
and 93.26%, respectively, but ViT was 2.5 times slower than Effi­
cientNetB0 on inference speed. The author emphasized that the ViT 
model was more likely to be sensitive to the initial set of weights and 
may have required more computational resources due to its self- 
attention mechanisms. Yao et al. (2024) proposed SpectralMamba for 
mix weed detection using hyperspectral images. A piecewise scanning 
mechanism is deployed to transform a nearly continuous spectrum into 
compressed sequences, which can preserve both short-range and long- 
range contextual relationships. Compared to the accuracy of 33.45% 
achieved by CNN, the detection accuracy of the proposed Spec­
tralMamba model increased to 73.74%.
Studies have been exploring the combination of CNNs, Transformers, 
and Mamba architectures to leverage their complementary strengths for 
insect and weed detection. Lucas Orlandi et al. (2024) developed a 
system to detect and classify broadleaf and grassy weeds by combining a 
Transformer model with You Only Look One Sequence (YOLOS) algo­
rithm, a type of CNN. The model achieved an overall precision of 90.7% 
and an average recall of 86.3%. Fig. 9(a) shows an example of a detec­
tion result of broadleaf weeds in green boxes and grassy weeds in red 
boxes. Xu et al. (2024) merged ViT with the YOLOv5 model for crop 
seedling target detection and weed detection, achieving a mean average 
precision of 87.6%, representing a 3.2% increase over the original 
YOLOv5. The authors first applied a detection algorithm and set the 
target crop frame as black to generate an image only containing weeds 
and soil. The weed portion of the image is then color-filtered according 
to a threshold to isolate green pixels, and a weed severity level is 
determined based on the ratio of red (weeds) to black (background) 
pixels, as shown in Fig. 9(b). The authors also developed a spraying 
system equipped with the proposed ViT-based YOLOv5, which can 
achieve a spraying accuracy of 90.32% at a speed of 4 km/h. Similarly, 
Hu et al. (2024) combined YOLOv8 and ViT for cotton weed detection, 
improving detection accuracy, mAP, and recall by 1.45, 0.7, and 0.6% 
compared to the original YOLOv8. Silva et al. (2023) proposed a hybrid 
architecture based on Convolutional and ViT, VT-Net, which can 
perform the segmentation of soybean weeds in images. The proposed 
VT-Net hybrid model was based on a ResNet50v2 feature extraction 
module, consisting of the addition of two convolution blocks and two re- 
sampling blocks. This modification changed the type of projection from 
linear to convolutional before the reception in the MLP heads. Zeng et al. 
(2024) developed a lightweight network, HCFormer, combining a CNN 
and ViT for detecting 12 distinct types of agricultural insects, including 
ants, bees, beetles, caterpillars, earthworms, millipedes, grasshoppers, 
moths, slugs, snails, wasps, and weevils. The proposed model improved 
the average accuracy by 7.8% and 3.55% over SENet and YOLOv8. 
Detection examples of insects such as earwigs, caterpillars, wasps, and 
ants are shown in Fig. 9(c). Kala et al. (2024) developed a convolutional 
vision transformer model by integrating two transformer blocks and one 
CNN block for detecting weed flora in banana plantations. The model 
achieved the accuracies of 97% and 100% after 50 and 200 epochs. 
Wang et al. (2025b) introduced InsectMamba, a novel approach that 
integrates State Space Models (SSMs), Multi-Head Self-Attention 
mechanism (MSA), Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) and CNNs. The 
approach achieved an accuracy of 91% in a dataset with 12 categories of 
agricultural insects. Utku et al. (2025) developed a hybrid convolution 
and vision transformer model (ConvViT), outperforming benchmark 
architectures, such as standalone ViT, VGG-16, ResNet-50, Dense­
Net201, and EfficientNetB0, achieving a classification accuracy of 
93.61%.

complex orchard environments. The model integrates Mamba with three 
novel modules-SS3D (Selective 3D Spatial), RPM (Region Proposal 
Module), and SFPN (Semantic Feature Pyramid Network). The model 
achieved a mAP50 of 94.8% at clustered pears under heavy occlusion. 
Wang et al. (2024b) developed an end-to-end encoder-decoder archi­
tecture based on an efficient sparse transformer for enhancing the 
restoration of low-light wheat images for yield prediction. The explicit 
inter-channel sparse transformer (EIST) introduced sparsity, which fo­
cuses on extracting effective information by filtering out redundant 
image information and focusing on detailed texture and edge informa­
tion. The proposed method achieved a good score of 0.928 in AP 0.5 
with the YOLOv8 method. Ha et al. (2024) developed a ViT-based 
Voting Ensemble network for predicting values of numerical rice yield 
by replacing the classification layer with a regression layer. The 
ensemble regression model based on ViT achieved a mean absolute error 
(MAE) of 72.72 kg/1000 m2.
Due to the challenge of irregular and variable morphology of rape­
seed inflorescences, Li et al. (2025) proposed a semantic segmentation 
framework, Mamba-Deeplabv3+, for rapeseed flower yield estimation 
using UAV imagery (Fig. 10(b)). The proposed network achieved a 
precision of 94.2% and a recall of 94.0% but reached 76.78 M of model 
parameters compared to 45.25 M in Deeplabv3 + due to a diffusion 
model and a Mamba structure. The authors indicated that although the 
proposed model is more accurate than Deeplabv3+ (74.5% of precision 
and 80.5% of recall), the large number of parameters demands 
substantial computational power, which may hinder its deployment in 
real-world applications. To predict the strawberry yield by detecting 
flowers, Wang et al. (2025d) replaced the YOLOv8 neck with a Vision 
Mamba-based module (VMambaNeck) and incorporates Light C2f in the 
backbone. The proposed network, VM-YOLO, achieves superior detec­
tion accuracy and inference speed (mAP of 71.4% and 22.4 ms) in 
strawberry flower detection compared to RetinaNet (mAP of 62.7% and 
30.0 ms). Tang et al. (2024) developed an integrated Transformer and 
Mamba network for low-light conditions to enhance image quality and 
recognition accuracy in wheat recognition tasks. The model achieves 
outstanding performance with a precision of 0.831 and a recall of 0.751.
Although the majority of studies have demonstrated improved per­
formance of ViT and Mamba, Inderka et al. (2024) indicated the oppo­
site results. In the study, the prediction performances of CNN, XGBoost, 
and the Convolutional Vision Transformers (CvT-13) for yield prediction 
of soybean were evaluated using the RMSE in bushels per acre, averaged 
over the years from 2018 to 2021. The results reveal that CNN and 
XGBoost had superior prediction performances than CvT-13. The reason 
could be that the small size of the histograms in the case study might not 
be ideal for Transformers, which excel at capturing long-range de­
pendencies. The author suggested potential ways to improve perfor­
mance by providing larger training datasets and preprocessing the 
soybean field pixel data into a format more suitable for Transformers, 
such as tokenizing the data.
Hybrid models that integrate CNNs with Transformers and Mamba 
have shown promising performance in crop detection for harvesting. For 
example, Olenskyj et al. (2022) proposed an end-to-end deep learning 
approach by utilizing images collected from a moving platform for grape 
yield estimation. The results show that the combination of CNN and 
transformers demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting grape yield 
at 10 m spatial aggregation, achieving a mean absolute percentage error 
(MAPE) of 18% compared to 29.4% for an object detection model.

detection architecture based on Mamba and YOLO. StrawGroot, a

of 0.776 for Strawba YOLO-l and a mAP of 0.745 for YOLO-n. In addition

5.4. Land, soil, and water management
Long-term health and productivity of agricultural resources, e.g., 
land, soil, and water, have attracted more attention in recent years. The 
strengths of ViT in extracting spatial features and Mamba in processing 
long sequential data provide them with significant potential across 
classification, detection, and segmentation tasks in agriculture, partic­
ularly for resource management due to their continuous, long-duration, 
and spatially complex nature.
For land management, Zheng et al. (2025) proposed the Edge Guided

5.5. Hybrids vs. pure models across crop management tasks
Hybrid architectures, i.e., a combination of ViTs, Mamba, and CNNs, 
often outperform their standalone counterparts. This superiority arises 
due to their ability to leverage the complementary strengths of the 
different components. Pure CNNs excel at capturing local, high- 
resolution spatial features due to their inductive biases like weight 
sharing and translation equivariance. Pure ViTs and Mamba, which rely 
on self-attention or selective state space mechanisms, are highly effec­
tive at modeling long-range dependencies and global context. A hybrid 
design typically uses a CNN component to efficiently extract initial, low- 
level features, which are then fed into the ViTs and Mamba block to 
model complex, global relationships across the entire input. The com­
bination is particularly beneficial when dealing with datasets that 
contain a mix of important local textures and contextual information. 
Therefore, hybrid models may achieve higher accuracy and better 
generalization, while requiring fewer computational resources than a 
pure ViT or Mamba model by extracting local features before intro­
ducing the attention mechanism.

6.1. Data availability and quality in crop management
6.1.1. Data limitations
Transformer models, particularly in tasks like plant diseases detec­
tion, crop yield prediction, and weeds classification, heavily rely on 
high-quality, balanced datasets to achieve accurate predictions. Shen 
et al. (2022) in the study on the AAFormer model for aerial agricultural 
image segmentation, highlighted that unbalanced datasets led to over- 
fitting during training and reduced robustness in practical applica­
tions. Rozendo et al. (2024) demonstrated that this data imbalance 
greatly affects the performance of a weed classification task using CNN, 
Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT) and ViT, limiting its generalization 
capability across diverse field scenarios. Moreover, De Silva and Brown 
(2023) suggested that the unbalanced dataset used for multispectral 
plant disease detection adversely impacted the performance of CNN, 
ViT, and hybrid ViT models, particularly when filters like K590 were 
underrepresented. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2023) illustrated that the 
Swin-Unet model struggled with unbalanced datasets in a maize weed 
identification study, leading to overfitting on overrepresented classes. 
These limitations stress the importance of balanced datasets, as imbal­
ances can misrepresent model learning, reducing robustness and real- 
world applicability. Bi et al. (2023) indicated that a limitation of the 
current dataset in a transformer-based model restricts the model’s 
ability to explore the impact of time-series length on prediction 
accuracy.
6.1.2. Data scarcity and limited variability
Agricultural data scarcity is a recurring limitation, as transformer 
models are often trained on localized datasets with limited diversity. 
This constraint reduces their ability to generalize across varying 
agricultural conditions. Liu et al. (2022b) in their study on rice yield 
prediction, found that limited dataset sizes hindered the model’s 
generalization to different growth conditions and regions. Similarly, 
Onoufriou et al. (2023) demonstrated that their Premonition Net for 
strawberry yield model performed poorly in new environments due to 
insufficient variability in the training dataset. Besides, Pacal (2024)
highlighted that the small size of a maize leaf disease dataset constrained 
the transformer model’s capacity to adapt to diverse environmental 
conditions. While the model achieved strong performance in controlled 
environments, its effectiveness significantly declined under varied sce­
narios under field conditions. Similarly, Gao et al. (2024) pointed out 
that the lack of diverse data types and environmental variability in their 
cotton pest detection dataset hindered the model’s scalability and 
resilience.

restricting its feasibility for small-scale farms. Similarly, Bi et al. (2023)
(Bi et al., 2023) indicated that the transformer-based model for soybean 
yield prediction expects more images during the growth stages to 
improve the model’s training performance. However, redundant infor­

increased computation resources. Kalpana et al. (2024) indicated that 
their Residual Swin Transformers model for plant disease recognition 
demanded substantial processing power, making deployment in low-

6.3. Model limitations in vision transformers
6.3.1. Complexity of transformer models
Transformer models require careful hyperparameter tuning to ach­
ieve optimal performance. Wang et al. (2023a) found that their pro­
posed DualSeg model resulted in more time cost for training dataset and 
inference on images due to having larger parameters, compared to the 
traditional single-branch model. Similarly, Inderka et al. (2024) found 
that their Convolutional Vision Transformer model for yield prediction 
was highly sensitive to parameter settings, necessitating extensive trials 
for fine-tuning.
6.3.2. Model interpretability
The complexity of transformer models makes their decision-making 
processes difficult to interpret, which can reduce trust among farmers 
and agricultural practitioners. Salamai et al. (2023) reported that their 
Lesion-aware Visual Transformer for paddy diseases model lacked 
transparency, making it challenging for end-users to understand the 
basis of disease classification. Similarly, Wang et al. (2023b) emphasized 
the importance of explainable AI in their ODP-Transformer for a pest 
classification model, as opaque decision-making processes hindered user 
adoption.
6.3.3. Multi-model limitations
Multi-stage transformer architectures are prone to error propagation, 
where mistakes in earlier stages adversely impact subsequent tasks. 
Wang et al. (2023b) found that their ODP-Transformer for pest classi­
fication encountered cascading errors in the sequential classification 
and description generation processes. Salamai et al. (2023) observed 
that precision agriculture tasks often require multi-task AI solutions that 
monitor multiple factors simultaneously, such as crop health, soil con­
ditions, and environmental data. Their proposed Lesion-aware Visual 
Transformer for paddy diseases, while effective for specific tasks, lacked 
the capability to handle multi-task scenarios effectively. Li et al. (2020)
in their study on A CNN-Transformer Hybrid using multisensor images, 
emphasized the need for the output feature sequences fusion of different 
layers, which can better utilize the relationship between the various 
levels of sequence information and improve the performance of the 
model. Moreover, Bi et al. (2023) demonstrated the potential of multi- 
modal integration in their Transformer-based approach for soybean 
yield prediction but noted that incorporating additional data modalities, 
such as soil and genetic information, could further enhance model 
performance.
6.4. Environmental and contextual challenges in crop management
Transformer models trained under controlled conditions often fail to 
adapt to the dynamic and variable environments of agricultural fields. 
Liu et al. (2022b) found that their rice yield prediction model exhibited 
inconsistent performance under varying environmental conditions, 
reducing its utility for practitioners. The lack of visibility in confidence 
levels further complicates the decision-making process. Moreover, Lu 
et al. (2022) observed that their Swin-Transformer YOLOv5 for grape 
bunch detection model struggled with occlusions and varying lighting 
conditions, leading to missed detections. Similarly, the ViT for weed and 
crop classification model proposed by Reedha et al. (2022) had a 
reduced accuracy issue when faced with environmental changes, such as 
resolutions, luminosity, and plant development phase. Guo et al. (2022)
summarized that their Convolutional Swin Transformer (CST) for plant 
disease detection misclassified targets when background elements 
shared similar visual characteristics. Gao et al. (2024) found that the 
robustness of their transformer model for cotton pest detection in 
extreme environmental conditions, like extreme lighting and occlusions, 
need to be improved. Transformer models perform well in controlled 
settings but often lack robustness in real-world agricultural environ­
ments, which highlights the need for further research into adaptive 
training strategies, uncertainty modeling, and environment-aware ar­
chitectures to ensure the practical utility of Transformers in agriculture.
6.5. Scalability and generalization
Transformer models trained in specific crop types or environmental 
conditions often lack generalizability. Wang et al. (2023a) found that 
their DualSeg for vineyard segmentation performed well on certain 
grape varieties, but the generalization ability of the model needs to be 
further improved. Similarly, Iba˜nez and Monterola (2023) observed that 
their a global forecasting approach for crop production model struggled 
to adapt to new regions or low-production crops.

7. Remaining limitations of Vision Mamba in crop management
While Vision Mamba models have demonstrated strong performance 
across a variety of crop management tasks, multiple limitations remain

He et al. (2025) found that their CountMamba model, although ac­

from 10-meter resolution data, limiting its full potential for spatio- 
temporal crop classification. Additionally, although Zhao et al. 
(2024a) demonstrates that their SRSMamba achieved high detection 
accuracy, its inference speed remains slightly lower than lightweight 
models like YOLOv7s, potentially limiting its deployment in real-time

7.4. Suboptimal real-time performance on edge devices

8.1.2. Improving computational efficiency
It is essential to enhance computational efficiency and make deep 
learning models accessible in resource-constrained environments such 
as small-scale farms or remote regions.
Edge baselines: Models should be benchmarked on representative 
devices (e.g., Jetson Orin Nano/Xavier, mid-range CPU), reporting ms/ 
frame, peak RAM, and energy per inference (J).
Compression pipelines: INT8/FP16 quantization, structured prun­
ing, and knowledge distillation could be incorporated; accuracy deltas 
within 1–3% mAP/IoU relative to FP32 should be targeted.
Hardware-aware design: Use TensorRT/ONNX Runtime export; 
avoid operators that block fusion. Prefer linear-time components (e.g., 
ViM blocks) for high-resolution inputs.
Online/continual updates: Lightweight streaming fine-tuning 
should be employed to adapt to seasonality; catastrophic forgetting 
must be monitored with replay or regularization.
8.2. Medium term: Robustness, adaptation, and efficiency at scale
8.2.1. Enhancing model robustness and versatility
Since agricultural studies span interrelated tasks, reliable field 
deployment requires quantified uncertainty and transparent explana­
tions of model predictions under diverse conditions.
Uncertainty estimation: Predicted probabilities should be cali­
brated (e.g., temperature scaling) and accompanied by simple uncer­
tainty methods (e.g., MC-Dropout or deep ensembles). Calibration 
quality should be reported with standard metrics such as expected 
calibration error (ECE) or the Brier score.
Adverse-condition training: Subsets for low light, motion blur, 
specular highlights, and heavy occlusion should be used; robustness 
should be quantified with stress tests.
8.2.2. Multi-task learning on shared backbones
A single model head could predict multiple outputs (e.g., detection 
+ segmentation + severity, or counting + growth stage) so that errors do 
not cascade between separate models and computation is reduced.
8.2.3. Improving scalability and generalization
Deep learning models often need to operate effectively across diverse 
crops, regions, and environmental conditions. To improve scalability, 
researchers should prioritize designing flexible architectures capable of 
handling diverse datasets and tasks without extensive retraining.
Transfer learning and meta-learning: Source-trained backbones 
should be evaluated with few-shot (≤10 shots) and zero-shot scenarios 
on new crops and regions.
Domain adaptation: Source to target adaptation (unsupervised or 
semi-supervised) should be included; target-only labels could remain 
unseen during adaptation.
Test-time adaptation (TTA): Lightweight TTA (e.g., entropy mini­
mization) should be used for illumination and color shift.
Sensor and resolution shift: Cross-sensor (RGB to multispectral/ 
thermal, UAV to ground) experiments should be designed with explicit 
degradation studies.
8.3. Long term: Multimodal intelligence and standardized deployment
8.3.1. Principled multimodal fusion
Fusion will explicitly shape cross-modal structure (e.g., image­
–weather–soil–management logs) while carrying predictive uncertainty 
through the pipeline, instead of specific concatenation toward archi­
tectures. Late- and mid-fusion designs can be combined with probabi­
listic layers. Confidence, missing-data masks, and sensor quality are 
propagated to the final recommendation. The endpoint is decision 
support: outputs should map to interventions (spraying, irrigation, 
harvest timing) with calibrated risk, estimated benefit, and cost/energy 
trade-offs reported alongside the prediction.
8.3.2. Scalable linear-time modeling
Linear-time modules in the ViM family should be developed for very 
large images (UAV mosaics, field panoramas) and long temporal se­
quences (seasonal monitoring). Goals include: (i) preserving global 
spatial/temporal context with strict memory bounds; (ii) sustaining real- 
time throughput on edge/UAV hardware; and (iii) supporting continual/ 
online updates without catastrophic forgetting. Where appropriate, 
hybrids can delegate local texture to CNN stems and remain ViM blocks 
for long-range reasoning to keep latency and energy.
8.3.3. Open standards and benchmarks
Community benchmarks should be multi-site and multi-season, with 
fixed public splits to prevent overfitting. Each submission should include 
an “edge deployment card” reporting: (i) Domain-shift robustness 
(cross-farm/season/sensor). (ii) Latency, peak RAM, and energy per 
inference on reference devices. (iii) Calibration quality (e.g., ECE/Brier)

9. Conclusions
The developments of self-attention mechanism for Transformers and 
structured state space sequence for Mamba have greatly improved the 
vision tasks in agriculture fields, especially in crop management, 
including weed and pest management, crop counting, disease detection, 
water, soil and land management, and many other visions level research. 
CNNs remain strong baselines for local pattern extraction and con­
strained hardware, while ViT improves robustness in cluttered, variable 
scenes through global context modeling, and ViM offers linear-time 
scaling that favors large images and long sequences on edge platforms.
In this survey, the main discussions are: 1) an overview of CNN, 
Vision Transformer and Vision Mamba core architectural analysis; 2) 
recent ViT- and ViM-based applications (including hybrids) across major 
crop-management tasks; 3) a comprehensive discussion of remaining 
limitations of ViT- and ViM-based models in the agricultural sector, and 
4) suggestions and future directions on standardized domain-shift 
evaluations, energy/latency reporting, compression for deployment, 
uncertainty-aware outputs, and principled multimodal fusion in crop 
management. Along with the analysis, the evidence indicates that ar­
chitecture choice should be task- and context-dependent: CNNs for 
simple scenes and strict on-device budgets; ViT address global context 
and multimodal fusion; ViM or CNN–ViT/ViM hybrids show benefits in 
long sequences, high resolution, or edge throughput conditions. 
Continued progress is most likely to come from open, multi-site 
benchmarks and transparent deployment reporting, coupled with 
linear-time global models and calibrated, multimodal decision support, 
to develop advancing efficient, scalable, and field-deployable agri-vision 
systems.
