A B S T R A C T
Accurate assessment of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) ripeness is essential for the preservation of quality, 
meeting market demands and ensuring customer satisfaction. However, one of the key problems is accurately 
assessing the maturity levels of fruit under varying field conditions. Conventional computer vision models such 
as convolutional neural networks (CNN) demonstrate uneven performance under varying illumination condi­
tions, particularly in arable farms. Further, it requires extensive training that involves fine-tuning entire model 
parameters and lags in global context learning. To address these issues, this work introduces a novel segmen­
tation framework that integrates the SegFormer architecture with the Low-Rank Adaptation (SegLoRA) module. 
The proposed model attained significant performance improvement compared to state-of-the-art (SOTA) 
methods with a mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 83.25 %, an F1-score of 90.07 %, a test accuracy of 
99.19 %, and a balanced accuracy of 93.88 %. Additionally, the computational cost was reduced by 26.98 % 
compared to existing SegFormer models. Further, the deployment on an edge computing device confirmed the 
proposed model’s feasibility in real time, with a minimal prediction delay of 0.065 s per frame. Moreover, its 
incorporation with an approximate yield estimation algorithm enables precise enumeration of harvestable to­
matoes. These results demonstrate the scalability and efficiency of the SegLoRA, adding to the progress in 
automated ripeness detection and agricultural automation for selective harvesting operations.
1. Introduction
Tomato crop is one of the most important crops worldwide and the 
produce is one of the integral components of many cuisines 
(Subramanian, 2016). Millions of people consume tomatoes across the 
globe due to its rich nutritional benefits and taste. Hence, tomatoes are 
an important resource in the agricultural and food industries (Ayvaz, 
2024). Tomatoes are cultivated in arable farmlands as well as green­
houses. Modern greenhouse farming enables higher productivity 
compared to traditional open-field farming methods, but the high initial 
investment, operating costs, limited financial support and lack of tech­
nical expertise discourage small-hold farmers from adopting greenhouse 
farming, leading them to opt for arable farming for tomato cultivation 
(Francis et al., 2020).
Being a climacteric fruit, ripeness level determines the evolution of 
quality parameters of tomatoes throughout their shelf life, and thus, 
their market value. Optimal ripeness ensures the balance of flavour, 
colour and texture which in turn satisfies the consumer demands and 
ensures the quality and freshness (Ge et al., 2024). Colour-based 
detection is the primary approach in estimating the ripeness and is 
considered the primary maturity index. As per the standard established 
by the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), there are six stages of 
ripeness, namely green, breaker, turning, pink, light red, and red (USDA, 
1991). Tomatoes harvested at the appropriate ripening stage result in 
higher profitability and failure to harvest at the right maturity level 
results in reduced quality, market value and in turn reduced profitability 
(Meng et al., 2024). Therefore, the researchers explored modern agri­
cultural technologies, including autonomous harvesting systems, which 
utilize vision systems along with artificial intelligence algorithms to 
estimate the ripeness of tomatoes and make decisions on harvesting

Recently, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely 
adopted for various classification tasks. The basic types of problems 
addressed using CNN include regression, classification, object detection 
and segmentation (Chatterjee et al., 2022; Girshick et al., 2014; Siva­
subramanian et al., 2024; Yadav et al., 2022; Younesi et al., 2024). This 
work, however, narrows its focus to classification, object detection, and 
segmentation. Classification models like AlexNet, VGG-Net, and ResNet 
have been used for ripeness detection and plant health monitoring 
(Halstead et al., 2018; Karthick et al., 2024; Sodjinou et al., 2022). 
However, their reliance on assigning a single label to the entire image 
limits their ability to process complex agricultural scenarios with mul­
tiple objects in varying states or fruits at different ripeness stages. To 
overcome this limitation, object detection-based architectures such as 
YOLO variants, Faster R-CNN and DETR models are employed for 
localizing and classifying multiple objects in an image (Sultana et al., 
2019). For instance, the RT-DETR model is employed for grained ripe­
ness detection of tomatoes. The model showcased improved precision 
and speed addressing challenges such as the detection of small target 
and sample imbalance (Wang et al., 2024a).
Despite their effectiveness in improving speed and precision, these 
models face notable challenges, including difficulty in handling occlu­
sion, overlapping objects, and achieving finer localization (Gunawan 
et al., 2023; Trinh & Nguyen, 2023; Z. Wang et al., 2022). Therefore, a 
pixel-wise prediction is essential for precise detection of the boundary 
and location of the object of interest. This has been addressed through 
deep learning-based semantic segmentation approaches, which not only 
analyse pixel-level information but also learn intricate spatial relation­
ships between pixels.
In semantic segmentation, the image is categorized into segments 
which in turn are used to locate the object of interest and its features 
(Diwan et al., 2023). For training, a pixel-wise annotation mask is 
required along with the raw images.
For instance, the TomatoDIFF model is a diffusion-based semantic 
segmentation method developed for on-plant tomato segmentation. 
While the model excels in varying lighting conditions (artificial light and 
daylight settings) in the greenhouse, it occasionally fails in segmenting 
tomatoes occluded in dense foliage (Ivanovska et al., 2023). Similarly, 
SegNet with a VGG19 backbone was employed to estimate the ripeness 
of tomatoes. Although the model successfully localizes and identifies 
ripe and unripe tomatoes, it falls short when the tomatoes are partially 
visible due to occlusions. Mask R-CNN, Deep Lab V3, and UNET are the 
other common CNN-based segmentation algorithms that are widely 
explored in the literature (L.-C. Chen et al., 2017; Shinoda et al., 2023; 
Weng & Zhu, 2021).
Though CNNs were the predominant approach in implementing se­
mantic segmentation, it has significant limitations in understanding 
global context-based information. This is because convolution opera­
tions rely solely on the kernel dimension, limiting the neighbourhood 
pixels considered in the input image.
To mitigate this, transformer-based models have been recently 
explored (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Rong et al., 2023; Strudel et al., 
2021). Here, the input image is converted to patches for which global 
attention is given using the self-attention mechanisms (Sajith Variyar 
et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2023). Additionally, positional embeddings were 
assigned along with the patches to achieve global attention. Segmenta­
tion Transformer (SETR), Segmenter, UPerNet, and Swin transformer 
are some of the models developed for segmentation (Liu et al., 2021; 
Strudel et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2021).
However, these models face limitations such as computational in­
efficiency, dependency on resolution, reduced interpretability due to 
large parameter sizes, and overfitting. To overcome the drawbacks of 
these SOTA models, SegFormer architecture has been explored recently. 
This model is designed to provide a blend of features that includes robust 
performance, efficiency, flexibility and scalability (Xie et al., 2021). 
SegFormer improves interpretability by using MLP layers that retain 
spatial information and local context. This feature facilitates a simpler 
comprehension of model outcomes and aids in the representation and 
interpretation of segmentation outcomes. While the model has shown 
exceptional SOTA performance for standard datasets such as ADE-20K, 
and Cityscape, it has not been extensively explored in the agricultural 
domain.
This work mainly focuses on deploying SegFormer variants (MiT B0 – 
MiT B5) to efficiently estimate the ripeness of tomatoes using a hybrid 
dataset which consists of images from the greenhouse and arable farm 
conditions. The models were able to achieve SOTA performance due to 
the hierarchical transformer encoder, efficient attention mechanism, 
and decoupled encoder-decoder architecture. Despite having the 
increased performance, as the model scales from B0 to B5, the number of 
parameters significantly increases, leading to higher computational 
costs for fine-tuning.
Hence, this work introduced a low-rank adaptation (LoRA) based 
fine-tuning approach that incorporates two low-rank matrices to the pre- 
trained SegFormer weights (SegLoRA). This enables task adaptation by 
training only the two low-rank weights while keeping the SegFormer 
weights frozen. By strategically freezing the pre-trained SegFormer 
weights, the model can leverage its existing learned representations 
while allowing minimal, targeted updates through the low-rank 
matrices.

Additionally, to assess the approximate yield of harvestable to­
matoes, a mIoU-based tracking algorithm has been implemented for 
counting the number of tomatoes in the post-prediction stage. The 
method keeps track of the predicted harvestable tomatoes using the 
overlap of the segmentation masks in the current and previous frames. 
This count of the harvestable tomatoes is multiplied by the average 
weight of a single tomato to predict the approximate yield.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first approach that integrates 
SegFormer along with the LoRA approach for detecting harvestable to­
matoes and is tested on edge computing devices along with yield 
estimation.
The main contributions of this work are: 
a) Generation of a custom-made tomato dataset that contains Green­
house and Open Farm Images of Tomatoes (GOFIT) comprising 7151 
annotated images classified into four categories: green, turning, light 
red, and red.
b) Incorporation of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) with a hierarchical 
transformer architecture to generate the novel SegLoRA architecture, 
aiming to enhance performance and reduce fine-tuning costs in terms 
of time and computational resources for tomato ripeness estimation.
c) Training and validation of the SegLoRA models to perform the 
ripeness estimation based on the evaluation metrics and compati­
bility on the edge devices.
d) Real-time counting of fully ripe tomatoes to estimate yield during 
ripeness detection.

2. Materials and methods
This work proposes a LoRA-based finetuning for the SegFormer ar­
chitecture to reduce the computational demand in task adaption along 
with an improvement of the model performance. The workflow of the 
proposed work and its implementation steps including the estimation of 
tomato yield is shown in Fig. 1. The work primarily begins with the 
collection of arable farm tomato images. These images are merged with 
existing greenhouse data and are annotated. These annotated images are 
divided randomly into train, validation and test sets in the ratio 7:2:1. 
Further, augmentation methods were applied to improve the general­
izability of the model. These augmentations were specifically applied to 
the training set and utilized as input for training the state-of-the-art 
models such as UNET, DeepLab V3, SegFormer, and the proposed 
SegLoRA. Later, to validate the model’s performance, the trained models 
were evaluated on test set images using a personal computer. Various 
evaluation metrics were then calculated to quantitatively assess their 
effectiveness.
Finally, the best-performing models were deployed on the edge 
computing device where real-time video of tomato was fed as input. 
Upon processing, the models generated precise segmentation outputs, 
which were then overlaid onto the original video stream, enabling real- 
time visual interpretation of the segmentation results.
Additionally, a compatibility analysis of the model on the edge 
computing device was performed. Augmenting this assessment, videos 
with different frames per second (FPS) are given as the input to the 
model to evaluate the latency of the prediction. Finally, the yield esti­
mation is performed by counting the number of harvestable tomatoes. 
The results of integrating the edge computing device with the developed 
model are discussed in section 3.4.
2.1. Dataset
In this work, a custom dataset GOFIT has been created which in­
cludes images from both open arable farms and greenhouses. The 
greenhouse-based images used in this work are taken from the “Real- 
world Tomato Image Dataset for Deep Learning and Computer Vision 
Applications Involving Precision Agriculture” which consists of 1750 
images taken over a period of 1 year for several days (Math 2020). The 
resolution of the obtained images was 1846 × 4000 pixels acquired with 
a smartphone camera. In a greenhouse, plants are grown in a controlled 
environment, where several factors including lighting, nutrition and 
wind are regulated. However, such conditions do not accurately repre­
sent the challenges faced in arable farming, where lighting conditions 
and occlusions vary significantly. Since there is a need for a generalized 
model that can work for both greenhouses and arable farmlands, an 
additional 2456 images were collected from arable farmlands (Local 
field: 10◦52’24.6“N 76◦54’30.9”E) with different lighting conditions 
that enrich the dataset with real-world variability. The arable farm- 
based images of the 4080 × 2296-pixel resolution were taken with a 
smartphone camera. Hence the utilised dataset in our study has 4206 
images in total.
To annotate the image based on the different stages of maturity, 
established standards according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture 
(USDA) (USDA, 1991) were used as a benchmark. According to their 
standard, there are six maturity levels (classes) of tomatoes, namely 
green, breaker, turning, pink, light red, and red. However, since our 
study is mainly focused on harvestable and non-harvestable tomatoes, 
the breaker, turning, and pink stages are non-harvestable and combined 
to form a single intermediate category between green and light red. In 
the revised standard for our study, a total of four maturity levels (ML-1- 
ML-4) were used for annotation of the tomatoes in the created dataset. 
The progression to various maturity levels of tomatoes occurs over 
distinct durations. The transition from the anthesis (flowering) stage to 
ML-1 takes 25–30 days. This is followed by 13 to 28 days for tran­
sitioning from ML-1 to ML-2. Further, it takes 2–6 days to transition from 
ML-2 to ML-3 and finally, 2–3 days to transition from ML-3 to ML-4. 
These durations are highly influenced by environmental factors such 
as temperature and humidity, as well as the specific variety of tomatoes 
(Y. Chen et al., 2024; Newenhouse, 2010). Hence, the ripening period of 
a tomato can vary when greenhouses and arable farms are considered, 
taking an average time of 42–67 days to progress through all maturity 
levels. Farmers and experts were consulted to categorize the tomatoes to 
the appropriate maturity level (ML1-ML4) for annotations. The anno­
tations were performed using the Roboflow online tool. The sample 
annotated images of the greenhouse and open field are shown in Fig. 2.
Further, the annotated dataset was divided into training, validation, 
and test sets, containing 2,945 images, 839 images, and 422 images, 
respectively. After the dataset was split, augmentations were applied 
exclusively to the training set. These augmentations were designed to 
account for varying lighting conditions, camera angles, and motion blur.

2.2. Proposed SegLoRA architecture
In semantic segmentation, the SegFormer model is a significant 
milestone since it can capture long-range dependencies in the input 
images. This serves as a solution for the problems caused by the occlu­
sions in the dataset. The model has a flexible structure when compared 
to the traditional CNN models enabling it to adapt to varying input sizes 
and aspect ratios without many architectural changes. The architecture 
of the SegFormer model is shown in Fig. 4(a).
The model mainly has 3 modules namely overlap patch embedding, 
encoder and decoder. The overlap patch embedding helps in preserving 
the local continuity of the input image whereas the encoder generates 
the multilevel features. Finally, the decoder takes multilevel features 
from different layers and combines global and local attention to provide 
powerful representations.

2.3. Low-rank adaption −LoRA
Inspired by the method of updating only specific elements of the 
weight matrix for the task adaption process, the LoRA method was used 
to fine-tune the pre-trained models. In this method, rather than adapting 
all the weights while fine-tuning, only a few changes in the weights are 
established. Selective updating reduces the computational burden and 
increases the efficiency. For adapting the SegFormer model to the GOFIT 
dataset, a pre-trained weight matrix W0 of dimension d × k is consid­
ered. This weight matrix is pre-trained on the ImageNet-1K dataset. The 
LoRA method builds upon this by focusing on efficient updates to only 
the most relevant parameters. So, when it is utilized for the fine-tuning 
process, it appends an additional weight matrix to the pre-trained 
weight matrix W0. This is represented by Eq. (3). 
Wʹ = W0 + ΔW
(3) 
Specifically, it decomposes the weight updation matrix ΔW into two 
low-rank matrices A and B as shown in Eq. (4). 
ΔW = BA
(4)

Although the segmentation models can recognize and localize the 
harvestable tomatoes, a quantitative approach is required to estimate 
the yield along with tracking individual tomatoes can be helpful for 
farmers to make timely decisions and perform action appropriately. To 
facilitate the above requirement, a yield estimation algorithm was 
incorporated based on the prediction output of the model. The subse­
quent section discusses the developed methodology adapted for the 
yield estimation algorithm.
2.4. Estimation of yield and tracking
Counting and tracking of the tomatoes in the agricultural fields are 
necessary for various tasks such as estimation of yield, future yield 
projections, decision on harvesting and determining the fruit quality. 
Initially, the direct feed from the arable farm is processed by a trained 
model that predicts the output segmentation mask. The specific colour of 
the output mask highlights the harvestable tomatoes. To generate 
bounding boxes around these masks, the maximum and minimum values 
of the segmentation mask are utilized. Each bounding box is assigned a 
unique identifier, which enables tracking of individual tomatoes even as 
frames change. These bounding boxes, along with their identifiers, are 
displayed on the live feed in real time. The flow chart for the proposed 
method is shown in Fig. 7.

farmlands (Tomato Hybrid CO 4, TNAU Tomato Hybrid CO 3) ranges

2.5. Training setup
A personal computer (PC) equipped with an Intel i7 processor, 
NVIDIA RTX 3060 graphics card with 12 GB of GPU memory and 32 GB 
of RAM was used for training and testing the model. The experiments 
were performed using Python 3.11.7 and its associated libraries such as 
Torch 2.2.0 + cu118, Transformers 4.37.2, Scikit-learn 1.1.3, OpenCV 
4.9.0, and PEFT 0.11 to facilitate efficient model training.
A data loader was configured with a batch size of 8 and an input 
image size of 512 × 512. For better model generalization, the training 
data loader is configured with an image shuffle option that rearranges 
the order of images for each training epoch. The ground truth annota­
tions of four maturity levels ML1–ML4 were used for training the model. 
The default hyperparameters for SegFormer were selected based on 
previous studies and modified based on several trials. The learning rate 
used was 0.0006 and the Adam optimizer was used for updating the 
parameter.

model. In this process, the pre-trained SegFormer model is passed to the 
Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) module, where the module-

16 and the LoRA scaling factor is set to 32 (decided based on trial).

3.1. Comparison of computational time
The training time of each model is examined and compared which is 
pictorially represented in Fig. 12. It is observed that the SegLoRA MiT-

39.34 % reduction in training time compared to the SegFormer MiT B0. 
Similarly, the SegLoRA MiT B4 model demonstrates a 26.97 % decrease 
in training time compared to its SegFormer counterpart. This reduction 
in training time is consistently observed across all other SegLoRA model 
variants when compared to their SegFormer counterparts.
Moreover, it can also be observed that despite SegLoRA MiT-B1 
having 13.7 million trainable parameters, its training time is 37.95 % 
less when compared to DeepLab V3 having only 11.82 million trainable 
parameters. Similarly, SegLoRA MiT B1 requires 27.04 % less training 
time compared to UNET, which has 6.63 M trainable parameters.
Overall, when the SegLoRA models are compared with the Seg­
Former counterparts, the reduction in the training time per epoch lies in 
the range of 24 % to 41 %. Specifically, the SegLoRA MiT B0 and B1 
models have a reduced training time in the range of 27 % to 32 % when 
compared to UNET and 37 % to 42 % when compared to DeepLab V3.
The observed reduction in training time can be attributed to the 
integration of a low-rank adaptation module, where the weight 
decomposition reduces the redundancy in parameter updates thereby 
reducing the computation complexity. Moreover, it ensures that only the 
relevant low-rank subspaces of the parameter matrices that contribute 
most significantly during model learning are updated. Further, the 
subsequent section discusses the implementation and evaluation of the 
proposed SegLoRA model on edge computing devices.
3.2. Implementation of the SegLoRA model in the edge device
In this work, two variants of widely used edge computing devices 
(Nvidia Jetson Nano and Xavier NX boards) were utilized to test the 
compatibility of SegLoRA models. Initially, the SegLoRA models were 
deployed on the NVIDIA Jetson Nano, equipped with a 128-core NVIDIA 
Maxwell architecture GPU, a Quad-core ARM Cortex-A57 MP Core 
processor, and 4 GB of RAM, to assess compatibility for real-time seg­
mentation tasks.
The SegLoRA MiT B0 model was tested on field video at 10, 12, 15, 
and 20 FPS rates. The results indicated that the model was able to 
process the video at 10 FPS on the board with a prediction latency of 2.3 
s per frame along with a noticeable degradation in the temporal 
coherence (stability and smoothness of the segmentation video) of the 
segmentation preview. The higher prediction latency and the degrada­
tion in temporal coherence are a result of the model being executed on 
the lower GPU.
Next, the B1 model was tested on the device, it also depicted a pre­
diction latency of 3.34 s per frame along with a considerable degrada­
tion in the temporal coherence of the segmentation preview. 
Furthermore, when other models were tested, the CPU cores were 
exhausted leading to system instability. Additionally, it was also noticed 
that when B0 and B1 models were tested at higher frame rates, the 
model exhausted the entire 4 GB of RAM. Larger architectures demand 
more memory to predict the segmentation masks resulting in system 
instability when Jetson Nano is considered.
Hence, further evaluations were conducted on the NVIDIA Jetson 
Xavier NX board, which is equipped with a 384-core NVIDIA Volta ar­
chitecture GPU featuring 48 Tensor Cores, a 6-core NVIDIA Carmel Arm 
v8.2 64-bit CPU and 8 GB of RAM. On this board when the SegLoRA MiT 
B0 model was deployed, a low prediction latency of 0.065 s per frame 
was generated for the 10 FPS video. This latency was sufficient to 
maintain the temporal coherence of the segmentation preview. How­
ever, as the frame rate increased, the overall processing time for the 
video also increased due to the higher number of frames being processed 
per second, even though the latency per frame remained constant at 
0.065 s. Thus, it shows that the higher frame rate requires processing 
more frames per second leading to the demand of more memory band­
width and GPU resources. Additionally, it is observed that the model was 
able to identify and segment the harvestable tomatoes (in the 14s video), 
identified by the field expert.
Further, when other variants of SegLoRA were tested, the B1 and B2 
models showcased a latency value of 0.067 s per frame and 0.104 s per 
frame. When B3, B4 and B5 models were tested it showcased a slightly

4.1. Case study: Effect of specular reflections and motion blurring
The confusion matrix for the validation and test sets of the proposed 
model reveals that the TP values for the ML-1, ML-3, and ML-4 stages are 
relatively high whereas the ML-2 stage exhibits a notably lower TP value 
(refer to Fig. 10). The higher value of miss-classifications of the ML-2 
class is attributed to various factors such as overlapping colour char­
acteristics, false annotations and specular reflection. The minor differ­
ence in the ripeness levels between the ML-1 and ML-2 classes is 
attributed to their overlapping colour characteristics. Hence, the expert 
may have made false annotations, leading to misclassifications in some 
images as observed in the confusion matrix.
Additionally, the arable farming conditions introduce specular 
reflection to all the classes. The glossy skin of tomatoes in arable farms 
reflects light differently depending on the illumination angle and in­
tensity. This phenomenon alters the perceived colour of the affected 
areas by creating bright or pale spots on the surface, which is a char­
acteristic of specular reflection. However, the impact on ML-3 and ML-4 
classes is relatively minimal due to the distinct colours of each class.
In contrast, ML-2 tomatoes exhibit overlapping colour characteristics 
with the ML-1 class and are more susceptible to specular reflection, as 
the green surface reflects more light. Consequently, these bright regions 
mislead the model, causing it to incorrectly interpret the overall colour 
as being more similar to that of the ML-1 class.

The quantitative comparison shows that, in all evaluation metrics, 
SegLoRA always outperforms all the SOTA models. This superior per­
formance can be attributed to SegLoRA’s ability to balance fine-grained 
feature extraction with global contextual awareness, effectively 
addressing the shortcomings of existing models in handling complex 
backgrounds, subtle colour variations, and extreme illumination 
changes.
For instance, the NVW YOLOv8 model, using adaptive thresholding 
with convolution layers, achieves a 5.4 % improvement in average 
precision compared to its YOLOv8s variant. The method, however, 
suffers from rapid illumination transitions because of its use of localized 
pixel intensity estimates.
Similarly, other SOTA models such as the Convolution transformer, 
trained on the Rob2Pheno dataset (710 images) and PDSI-RT-DETR 
(Wang et al., 2024b), trained on 4.5 times more data, underperform 
against SegLoRA (refer Table 4) which is primarily attributed to the 
limited local feature extraction and ineffective global context handling. 
These challenges are addressed by SegLoRA through its efficient-self 
attention-based segmentation framework and LoRA-based fine-tuning. 
This optimizes the feature representation for detailed, pixel-level 
classification.
This advantage is particularly evident in the segmentation of classes 
like ML-1 and ML-2. For instance, as depicted in Fig. 11, UNET struggles 
to accurately segment these classes due to their similarity to the back­
ground and subtle colour variations. In contrast, SegLoRA segments 
these classes accurately. Fine-tuning with the LoRA module proved its 
capacity to extract the fine features and helped in distinguishing the 
overlapping features of ML-1 and ML-2 classes when compared to SOTA 
methods such as UNET, DeepLab V3 and SegFormer.
Hence, the proposed framework shows substantial promise in accu­
rately segmenting and grading tomatoes according to their maturity 
levels. Additionally, compared to the existing methods that lack imple­
mentation on edge computing devices, this work provides a viable 
approach for real-time and on-site assessment of tomato ripeness. Its 
scalability offers a distinct advantage, allowing SegLoRA to be adapted

5. Conclusion and future Scope
This work mainly addresses the ripeness classification challenge in 
selective harvesting, in particular for tomatoes, using the novel 
transformer-based segmentation model SegLoRA. The study experi­
mentally validates that the incorporation of a low-rank adaptation 
module brings a large boost to segmentation performance using the least 
amount of computation (26.98 % reduction in computational cost). The 
proposed model also offers architectural versatility that enables stra­
tegic implementation choices, by offering ideal efficiency under 
controlled greenhouse environments and better robustness under chal­
lenging arable conditions. The results showcase the importance of 
environmental variables contributing to segmentation efficacy, espe­
cially regarding specular reflection phenomena and motion-induced 
blurring.
The successful deployment of the model on edge computing plat­
forms, notably the Jetson Xavier NX, validates the framework’s viability 
for real-time agricultural applications. SegLoRA architecture demon­
strated its scalability and robustness, and it is a promising solution for 
precision agriculture applications where accurate ripeness assessment is 
critical to minimize harvest timing and management of yield.
While the present work focuses on segmenting the tomatoes to 
different ripeness levels, future research can look into further 
improvement of the model’s resilience to extreme environmental vari­
ations other than specular reflections and blurring, as well as at the 
possibility of combining multi-modal sensing approaches to increase the 
segmentation efficacy in arable farming conditions. Further, future 
exploration includes the integration of polarization-based imaging 
techniques and adaptive pre-processing methodologies.
