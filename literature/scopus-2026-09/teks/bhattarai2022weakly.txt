a b s t r a c t
Flower and fruit count is a critical metric in developing crop-load management and harvesting strategies
during flower/fruit development and harvest seasons. Growers currently rely on their prior experience and/
or manual count in sample areas/trees to estimate the number of flowers/fruits in orchards. In this work, we
propose a simplified yet robust deep learning-based weakly-supervised flower/fruit Counting Network
(CountNet) and investigate its accuracy in commercial orchard images. Unlike detection-based counting
methods, which require individual object detection, CountNet learns from image-level annotation with the
number of objects (flowers or fruits) as input without explicitly specifying the object’s signature and lo-
cation. Experiments were conducted in images acquired in an unstructured commercial orchard environ-
ment. Results showed a minimum Mean Absolute Error (MAE)/Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 12.0/18.4
and 2.9/4.3 for the apple flower and fruit dataset respectively. Activated region/feature visualization
techniques revealed that CountNet is looking into different apple flower/fruit edges and features to make
the count decisions. The results are promising in simplifying the automated methods for flower/fruit
counting which can lead to reduced manual counting in the field, manual image annotation, and compu-
tational complexity and memory requirement of the object counting system.

1. Introduction
Flower and fruit counting in apple orchards is crucial for flower
density estimation, crop-load estimation, and yield prediction,
which helps to optimize crop-load management and harvesting
strategies and resources (e.g. machines and human workers). Fruit
trees often bloom more flowers and set more fruits (also called over-
cropping) than the amount necessary for commercially desired yield
and quality (e.g. fruit size, shape, color, sugar content). Crop-load
management is a balancing act between reducing the number of
fruits adequately to accomplish optimal fruit size and quality, and
satisfactory return bloom without reducing current season yield
unreasonably (Embree et al., 2007; Robinson et al., 2013). Every year,
growers remove excess flowers and immature fruitlets (manually -
using human hands/chemically/mechanically) to minimize over-
bearing and biennial bearing. Flower/blossom density estimation
aids in optimizing and customizing the application of chemical
thinning agents and operation specifications of mechanical thinners.
Furthermore, flower density estimation is considered one of the
earliest indicators of fruit-set/crop-load in orchards. Yield estimation
helps streamline the harvesting labor force, other resources (e.g.
orchard platform), and post-harvest processing strategies. Most of
the field operations, including yield estimation and flower density
estimation, in various tree fruit crops such as apples and cherries,
lack automation. Growers still heavily rely on prior experience and/
or manual fruit counting in sample areas, followed by extrapolation
to estimate count in entire orchards (Marini, 2017; Lacey, 2019;
Gongal et al., 2016). Manual approaches are highly labor-intensive,
laborious, and incur additional costs to the growers. Furthermore,
with decreasing labor availability, the fruit crop industry has been
facing increasingly critical challenges for sustainable crop produc-
tion. There have been reports that farmers are leaving some portion
of their crops in the field due to a lack of farm labor (Mohan, 2017;
Estabrook, 2019). In this context, developing automated solutions
that aid in different aspects of orchard operations such as crop/yield
estimation, harvesting, thinning, and pruning is crucial to reduce
cost and increase productivity and profitability of the tree fruit in-
dustry.
Integration of computer vision in flower/fruit counting has the
potential to automate and simplify the painstaking manual counting
process. However, automation in agriculture has unique challenges

clusters, occlusion of target objects (e.g. fruit or flower), wind, and
variation in lighting conditions. Conventional machine vision sys-

which has shown robust performance when sufficiently large

Unlike robotic picking (or other applications requiring object
manipulation) that requires precise fruit detection and segmenta-
tion, crop-load estimation does not require fruit location as long as
total number of fruit can be counted/estimated in each tree canopy.
Hence, a system directly providing the total fruit count without
explicit detection is sufficient for such applications. On the other
hand, individual flower annotation and detection in challenging and
painstaking task. Chemical/Mechanical apple flower thinning is
performed when around 60–80% of flowers are fully open resulting
in dense flower clusters with substantial occlusions among flowers
in addition to occlusions due to other canopy objects such as tree
trunk, branches, and leaves. Furthermore, detection-based counting
approaches may require intermediate computation blocks including,
but not limited to, region proposal estimation, region of interest
pooling, and non-maximal suppression adding the computational
complexity and memory requirement. Furthermore, object-level
annotation (whether that be for individual fruit or flower) using a
polygon/ bounding box takes a substantial amount of manual an-
notation hours.
This article investigates a regression-based flower/fruit counting
approach in commercial apple orchards (see Fig. 1). The proposed
system leverages image-level annotation and takes a total number of
flowers/fruits in a given image as ground truth to train the neural
network to estimate count. The counting process is weakly su-
pervised in the sense that the neural network learns based only on
the count information without any explicit information on the object
of interest (flowers/fruits) and their location. Based on the given
total flower/fruit count, the network needs to learn to discriminate
and highlight the relevant flower/fruit features while discarding
unnecessary background information. Since the network is weakly
supervised, it makes fewer assumptions about the operating en-
vironment, making it useful for counting applications in different
crop types and cultivars (e.g. green and red apple cultivars). Fur-
thermore, the network looks into highly probable flower/fruit re-
gions irrespective of their spatial footprint (number of pixels
occupied by the object in image space), making the proposed ap-
proach useful for the environment with high level of occlusions
often seen in agriculture (see Fig. 1). To summarize, the following are
the key contributions of this article.

2. Related work
2.1. Flower/Fruit detection and counting
In this section different flower/fruit detection approaches that
have the potential to be extended for the counting problems are
discussed, followed by approaches for direct fruit counting. Machine
vision research in apple orchards during full bloom is almost entirely
focused on flower cluster detection. Dias et al. (2018a) performed
semantic segmentation of flower clusters using region proposal es-
timation followed by CNN-based feature extraction and Support
Vector Machine (SVM) for classification. Improving their previous
work in terms of generalizability, Dias et al. (2018b) proposed CNN-
based feature extraction followed by region refinement to achieve
improved cluster segmentation. Farjon et al. (2019) implemented
Faster R-CNN (FRCNN) to detect flower clusters. Recently, Mask R-
CNN (MRCNN) and MaskScoring RCNN capable of simultaneous
detection and instance-level segmentation have been adopted for
individual apple flower and flower cluster segmentation in early and
full bloom (Tian et al., 2020; Bhattarai et al., 2020). Unlike stone
fruits (e.g., cherries) that develop one flower per flowering bud, each
flowering bud in pome fruits (e.g., apples) can develop 5–7 flowers
in a cluster, making the individual flower detection highly challen-
ging during full bloom.
Fruit detection, segmentation, and localization have received
significant attention from the research community, specifically for
robotic harvesting. Before the widespread adoption of deep learning,
fruit detection involved handcrafted extraction of color, geometry,
and texture features followed by classification/clustering approaches
(Gongal et al., 2015). Recently, using 3D point cloud, Ge et al. (2021)
leveraged fusion of shape and color features followed by a sSVM to
classify and segment fruits, branches, and foliage in apple canopies.
The use of CNN-based feature extractors is currently becoming in-
creasingly popular with improved detection and segmentation ac-
curacy. Implementation of FRCNN/improved versions of FRCNN
ranges from single- to multi-class classification, and detection of
various fruits including apples, kiwis, oranges, mangoes, strawber-
ries (Fu et al., 2020; Gené-Mola et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2020; Liu
et al., 2019; Sa et al., 2016; Wan and Goudos, 2020; Bargoti and
Underwood, 2017). In addition to FRCNN other approaches such as
YOLO and MRCNN have also been implemented and shown im-
proved results in fruit detections (Tian et al., 2019; Gené-Mola et al.,
2020; Liu et al., 2018). Hani et al. (2020) performed U-Net-based
semantic segmentation of apples using pixel-wise classification.
Once the individual fruits are detected, counting is achieved by
simply summing up the individual detection. With the integration of
a CNN-based feature extractor, the detection and segmentation
performance improved substantially compared to hand-crafted fea-
ture extraction methods. However, the increased accuracy comes
with the expense of increased computational complexity, larger
memory utilization, and labor-intensive manual annotation.
To improve and simplify the fruit counting and yield estimation
in orchards, a few approaches have been studied in the past. Given
the segmented and registered image patches, Roy and Isler (2016)
and Roy et al. (2019) used classical Gaussian Mixture model (GMM)
based clustering to count the number of apples in the image. Since
counting was based on the possible fruit segmentation, incorrect
segmentation can compound to the incorrect fruit count. Hani et al.
(2020), and Liu et al. (2018) reported an yield estimation pipeline
involving fruit detection, localization, and counting. While Liu et al.
(2018) detected individual fruit, Hani et al. (2020) solved fruit
counting as an image patch classification problem such that the final
count is the summation of the count in each image patch (Hani et al.,
2018, 2020; Liu et al., 2018). Chen et al. (2017) combined Fully
Convolution Network (FCN) and count convolution network for fruit
blob detection and counting. Given the detected fruit blob from FCN,
a count convolution network was used to extract bounding boxes
around each blob and estimate the intermediate fruit count. Finally,
a linear regression model was used to map intermediate fruit count
estimates within each blob to human-generated ground truth count.
Although there have been some efforts to implement regression-
based counting in other areas, such as tomato counting, tobacco leaf
counting, and banana leaf counting (Rahnemoonfar and Sheppard,
2017; Dobrescu et al., 2019; Farjon et al., 2021), study of regression-

Rahnemoonfar and Sheppard (2017) developed a modified Inception-
ResNet trained with synthetic images for direct counting of tomatoes
without explicit detection. Farjon et al. (2021) developed a counting
network using ResNet-50 as the backbone followed by feature pyr-
amid network to incorporate feature information and estimate count
at each feature scale. Final count was obtained by combining the es-
timation of each feature scales estimated via independent regression
networks. The approaches proposed by Rahnemoonfar and Sheppard
(2017) and Farjon et al. (2021), although provided direct object count
via regression, they lack investigations and insights on whether the
network is looking into correct object (flower/fruit) features to come
up with specific count decision. Dobrescu et al. (2019) replaced the
classification layer of VGG16 with two fully connected layers to ac-
commodate regression-based Tobacco leaf counting and activation
map visualization. The network requires input image size of 224×224.
For the larger input images covering broader areas like an apple tree
canopy, if the feature maps from VGG16 output were flattened and
directly fed to fully connected network, the model size, memory re-
quirement increases substantially (see Section 4.2). Furthermore, this
could result in substantial number of un-activated neurons making the
network unnecessarily bulky (Dobrescu et al., 2019). In this work
VGG16 was used as a backbone to extract features but the inter-
mediate, and backend layers were added to minimize the model size,
improve counting accuracy and training speed by leveraging adaptive
global average pooling.
Counting in commercial tree fruit crops involve various image
processing challenges due to factors such as occlusions, varying
background, changing lighting exposure, unstructured canopies, and
variable crop-load level. Most of the reported work estimates the
object count with some form of detection/segmentation of in-
dividual fruit/blob/image patch providing a multi-stage counting.
Such multistage approaches are complex, time-consuming to pro-
cess, and require substantial computational memory. Algorithms
such as FRCNN and MRCNN also require intermediate processing
such as region proposal estimation, region of interest (ROI) pooling,
and ROI alignment adding the computational complexity. The pro-
posed approach performs direct regression on input images, making
it lightweight, and fast requiring low computational resources. Since
the network is lightweight and computationally simple, it has the
potential to be implemented and operated in hardware systems with
low computational capacity such as cellphones without requiring
cloud computation. For the given count estimation, the experiments
were extended to investigate the highlighted regions and features
contributing to the count estimation. Two popular activation map
visualization techniques: Score-CAM (Wang et al., 2020) and Guided
Backpropagation (Springenberg et al., 2014) were adopted to un-
derstand the functionality of the network. Activation map visuali-
zation helped to understand the activated regions and underlying
features contributing to the final flower/fruit count allowing users to
better understand the results and improve the reliability of the ob-
tained results. The evaluated dataset in this work involves apple
canopy images during bloom and harvesting seasons in the com-
mercial apple orchards. This study opens the possibility to apply
regression-based counting approaches in other commercial fruit
production such as mangoes, oranges, kiwis, pears, and peaches.

2.2. Deep visualization techniques
While the deep neural networks have shown incredible perfor-
mance improvement in a multitude of vision tasks, they are often
criticized for being a Blackbox with a lack of interpretability, and
explainability (Gilpin et al., 2018). In one of the early efforts, Zeilner
and Fergus proposed a multi-layer deconvolutional network that
uses the activation in the feature maps and reverts back to the input
image to determine the parts of images that are responsible for the
activations (Zeiler and Fergus, 2014). Class Activation Map (CAM)
helped solve the visualization with weighted feature maps to obtain
activation in the input image; however, its application is limited to
the neural network architecture with global average pooling and
requires retraining of the classification layer (Zhou et al., 2016).
Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) and Grad-CAM++ (Chattopadhay
et al., 2018) generalized the CAM and computed weights for the
activation maps using the gradients flowing into the last convolution
layer and are applicable irrespective of CNN architectures. Grad-CAM
++ improved the Grad-CAM by introducing pixel-wise weights in
each activation map to compute final weights for developing the
saliency map (Chattopadhay et al., 2018). In this work Score-CAM
(Wang et al., 2020) to highlight activated regions and Guided Back-
propagation (Springenberg et al., 2014) to highlight activated fea-
tures were implemented to investigate the final count. Score-CAM
uses flower/fruit count value to highlight the regions while Guided
Backpropagation leverages positive gradients flowing during the
backpropagation step.
3. Proposed approach
3.1. Network architecture
The proposed fruit Count Network (CountNet) consists of three
computational blocks: feature extraction block, feature reduction
block, and count computation block connected sequentially (see
Fig. 2). CountNet is capable to processes three-channel RGB images
irrespective of size.
3.1.1. Feature extraction block
The feature extraction block of the proposed CountNet consists of
a pre-trained VGG16 (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2014) network as
the backbone. Although different backbone architectures such as
ResNet, ResNeXt, DenseNet are available, VGG16 was selected be-
cause of the simple network architecture. VGG16 is a sequential
network, without skip connections, which helped to simplify and
better understand the network activation via activation map
visualization techniques (discussed in Section 4.3). For the proposed
CountNet, the classification block of the original VGG16 was re-
moved, and new layers were added to estimate flower/fruit count.
3.1.2. Feature reduction block
The feature reduction block consists Global Average Pooling
(GAP) layer. Inspired by the advantages of the GAP in computing
channel attention weights in
squeeze-and-excitation networks
(SENet) (Hu et al., 2018), GAP was used to reduce feature dimensions
by averaging over each feature map. In this work, an adaptive
average pooling method was used which adaptively computes the
kernel size and stride while computing the channel average value.
Since the number of output features from GAP is equal to a number
of input planes, and the Conv5 block of the VGG16 network outputs
constant 512 channel feature map (see Fig. 2), the proposed
CountNet is able to operate on the input image of any size. Fur-
thermore, implementation of GAP after Conv5 feature extraction
block over direct use of fully connected network minimizes model
size, training time, number of training images, and over-fitting by
substantially minimizing the number of optimizable parameters. Let

3.2. Network activation visualization
For activation map visualization, the Score-CAM activation map
visualization technique was implemented. Unlike CAM, which re-
quires network retraining and specifically global average pooling,
Score-CAM is generalizable, does not require re-training, and can
pass through both the convolution and fully connected layers (Wang
et al., 2020). Furthermore, Score-CAM has shown superior activation
map visualization compared to Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ irre-
spective of objects’ spatial footprint. In this work, Score-CAM used
for classification tasks was modified for the proposed regression-
based CountNet (Ozbulak, 2019; Wang et al., 2020). Score-CAM does
not depend on the gradients.The weights for the activation maps
were obtained using each activation map’s contribution in com-
puting the final flower/fruit count. The computed weight value
highlighted the activated regions in each feature map identifying the
highly probable flower/fruit regions. However, the activation map
did not provide information on the activated region’s particular
features contributing to the count. To understand which part/feature
of the activated regions contributed to the final count, Guided
Backpropagation (GB) (Springenberg et al., 2014) method was
adopted. GB uses the backpropagation to visualize the gradients
with respect to the input image, allowing positive gradients flow
while suppressing the negative gradients. Normal backpropagation
allows the flow of gradients even if they are negatively correlated

3.4. Dataset
The datasets used in this experiment consisted of a set of apple
canopy images during the flowering season and two sets of canopy
images close to harvesting (with mature fruit; Table 1). Flower ca-
nopy images (Flower-A) were collected over two growing seasons
from commercial apple orchards during the early to full bloom
period in Washington, USA. Flowers from different apple varieties,
including Scifresh, Honeycrisp, Envy, and Gala grown in four dif-
ferent orchard locations, and two fruiting wall architectures (2D
vertical and V-trellised) were incorporated. Data collection was
performed in daylight conditions without any background manip-
ulation. In order to increase the variability in the dataset, images
were collected using three imaging sensors: Microsoft Kinect v2
Time of Flight (TOF) sensor (1920 × 1080), Stereolab’s ZED2 stereo
camera (1920 × 1080), and Sony RX100 digital camera (5472 × 3648)
in commercial apple orcahrds in Washington, USA. Microsoft Kinect
V2 has a horizontal and vertical field of view of 84.1∘and 53.8∘while
the ZED2 sensor has a larger viewing space with 110∘horizontal and
70∘vertical field of view. Both sensors provide images at a the rate of
30 frames/sec. Images were also collected with Sony RX100 digital
camera that used a 20.2 MP CMOS sensor. Imaging sensors were
positioned in such a way that majority of the canopy space was
covered by the sensor field of view without substantially decreasing
the spatial footprint (area in terms of number of pixels) of the object
of interest (flower/fruit). To achieve this goal, sensors were placed at
~ 0.9 m from tree trunks and ~ 1 m above the ground. Fruit canopy
images (Fruit-A) were also collected over two growing seasons, using
an LG G4 cellphone (1494 × 1992) in three commercial apple orch-
ards in Washington, USA, with multiple varieties grown in fruiting
wall architectures. The images were originally captured for detec-
tion-based crop-load estimation purposes and are publicly available
.1 The images acquired with cellphone have higher resolution com-
pared to both ZED2 and Kinect V2 cameras and have shown the
capability to provide enough detail in object detection tasks that
require more resolution compared to the proposed approach. Ad-
ditionally, the apple fruit dataset publicly released by Gao et al.
(2020) as a part of a multi-class fruit classification experiment was
annotated and used. The dataset consists of images of 800 harvest-
ready apple canopies in 2D fruiting wall architecture (Gao et al.,
2020).2 A set of 630 unique canopy images were extracted by re-
moving similar images with Structural Similarity Index Measure
(SSIM) greater than 95% followed by human verification. The fruit
dataset consisted of 31 and 48 apples on average for Fruit - A and
Gao et al. datasets while the flower dataset consisted of 69 flowers
on average. The ground truth counts were collected using image-
level annotation, specifying the number of flowers/fruits in each
image without identifying the object location and feature informa-
tion. The overall dataset was split into training and test set with 75%
data for training and 25% for testing with a resolution of 1024 (H/W)
x 768(W/H). Validation datasets were obtained by randomly crop-
ping 768 (H/W) x 576 (W/H) size regions from the test set.

4. Results and discussion
4.1. Flower/Fruit count
To evaluate the performance of the proposed CountNet in rela-
tion to models developed in the past, the results obtained with the
proposed model were compared with the same obtained from dif-
ferent approaches utilizing classical and CNN-based hierarchical
feature extraction. All the models used image-level level annotation
that require a total number of objects of interest in a particular
image.
Local Binary Pattern+Kernel Ridge Regression (LBP+KRR): LBP
is a popular texture descriptor that extracts the object features
modifying the neighboring pixel values by comparing them with the
pixel of interest (Wang et al., 2009). The feature vector from LBP was
fed to KRR to estimate the count.

Modified Inception-ResNet is an end-to-end tomato counting net-
work utilizing CNN feature extractor that can provide object count in
a single pass. It consisted of various convolution and pooling layers
along with a stack of modified Inception-ResNet-A and reduction
modules. Modified Inception-ResNet has shown improved perfor-

compared to MSR with 32.6 Million parameters: a ~ 54% (17.7 Mil-
lion) less trainable parameters indicating the potential of improved
performance even for a small dataset. For the apple flower dataset,
the proposed CountNet achieved a mean absolute error of 12.0
( ~ 17% of average # of flower/image). Moving from flower to fruit
counting, a substantial decrease in error (MAE and RMSE) was ob-
served with all four models. CountNet achieved the lowest MAE of
2.9 ( ~ 9% of # of average fruit/image) in the Fruit-A dataset and 3.9
( ~ 8% of average # of fruit/image) in the Gao et al. (2020) dataset.
From Table 2, it was also observed that the MAE and MSE slightly
increased in Gao et al. (2020) dataset compared to the Fruit-A da-
taset with all the networks. This might be because Gao et al. (2020)
dataset (average: ~ 48apples/image) consists a large number of ap-
ples compared to the Fruit-A dataset (average: ~ 31 apples/image).
A scatter plot of predicted count vs ground truth, both as con-
tinuous variables, were plotted to compare the count estimation
provided by the proposed CountNet, LBP+KRR, Modified Inception-
ResNet, and MSR. (see Fig. 3). Additionally, a first-order polynomial
trend-line was fitted to evaluate the estimation quality. Ideally, if the
neural network could perfectly estimate the count, the trend-line
slope would be unity with a null intercept. For practical purposes, it
is desirable to have the trend-line with a slope close to unity and
intercept close to zero. The proposed CountNet performed superior
with larger slope and smaller intercept (smaller over-count) with all
three datasets when compared to the same achieved with LBP+KRR,
Modified Inception-ResNet, and MSR (except overcount in Flower-A
dataset). Observing the trend-line intercept, all the compared algo-
rithms tend to overestimate the flower/fruit counts. We believe all
the models tended to incorporate some of the visible flowers/fruits
in the background tree rows, which were not considered in the
ground truth count.
Additionally correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r-value) was com-
puted to estimate the correlation (count agreement) between the
ground truth and estimated count for all four algorithms (see
Table 3). CountNet performed substantially better compared to LBP
+KRR and Modified Inception-ResNet with higher count agreement
with the ground truth data while showing competitive performance
with MSR. As illustrated in Table 2, Table 3, and Fig. 3, all the com-
pared networks performed better in the fruit dataset than the flower
dataset. For flower counting, the networks showed larger MAE,
RMSE, and over-count and smaller slope values reflecting the added
complexity in flower counting. Although all networks were trained
with a similar number of objects for flower and fruit count (75% of
30936 for flower and 75% of 29753 for fruit), the r-value of the trend-
line for the flower dataset was lower compared to Gao et al. (2020)
dataset. Flower and fruit counting in tree fruit is challenging because
of the inherent complexity due to the biologically driven unique
orchard characteristics and external variability due to weather,
temperature, wind, and lighting conditions. Additionally, flower
counting has added complexity because of the random shape, po-
sition, and orientation of flowers. Unlike fruits with round shapes
irrespective of maturity, flowers exist in early to full bloom stages in
the same canopy, which can have a wide range of shape, texture, and
color features. Furthermore, flowers are densely located in clusters
with a large number of flowers in constrained spaces with a sub-
stantial flower to flower occlusions resulting in random cluster
structures. Oftentimes, multiple clusters are also closely spaced,
resulting in super-clusters thus adding complexity to an already
complex problem. The proposed CountNet even with such a complex
scenarios achieved good results and have promising practical ben-
efits.

4.3. What did the network look for?
As discussed in Section 4.1 the proposed CountNet performed
reasonably well in learning to count flowers and fruits based only on
information on number of objects without explicit feature in-
formation. This is the power of using hierarchical feature learning
and backpropagation. The different layers of VGG16 were instru-
mental in extracting the relevant features which were followed by
global average pooling and fully connected networks to transform
the extracted features into meaningful count estimation.
Once the count results are obtained it is critically important to
analyze and understand if the network is actually looking into the
relevant features to make specific count decision. To further eluci-
date the functionality of the CountNet, two activation map visuali-
zation techniques: one that highlights the activated regions (Score-
CAM) and another that highlights the relevant features within the
activated regions (Guided Backpropagation) were adopted and im-
plemented. Fig. 4 illustrates the qualitative results of the count es-
timation from CountNet (column 1), as well as the activation map
visualization using Score-CAM and Guided Backpropagation for
CountNet (column 2 to column 4). As shown in Fig. 4b, the Score-
CAM heatmap shows that the network was highly activated in the
regions corresponding to the apple flower/fruit locations. The net-
work primarily focused on the front row canopy and learned to
exclude most of the flower/fruit in the background row as well as
other orchard artifacts such as leaves, branches, trellis wire, and the
background sky. Since Score-CAM only visualized the most active
region, Guided Backpropagation was implemented to understand
which part of the apple flower/fruit was activated to contribute to
the final count estimation. Fig. 4d shows some of the critical features
in apple flowers and fruits within the activated regions that con-
tribute to the final count.
As shown in Fig. 4d (row 1 and row 2) and Fig. 5a, the proposed
CountNet processed early and full bloom flowers in distinctive ways
incorporating different feature information to estimate the count.
Fig. 5a shows the close-up view of an apple canopy image, including
a proportion of full bloom and unopened flowers in a cluster. If the
flowers were in early bloom, the most activated features were the
small flower blobs representing unopened flowers. On the other
hand, when the flowers start to bloom, the network looked into
flower edge features. Looking into the Guided Backpropagation re-
sults, it is evident that even if a cluster has both unopened and full
bloom flowers together, unopened buds were activated as blobs
while full bloom flowers were activated with edge features. It is
interesting to see that the network, without any information on
what to look into in such a complicated environment, looked into
some of the crucial features to differentiate flowers from background
objects (e.g. branches, trellis wires. trunk, leaves, sky, canopies in
consecutive rows) in orchards.
As shown in Fig. 4d (row 3 and row 4), for apple fruit images, the
most active details were apple fruit edges(mostly circular) and the
feature information included by those edges. It is also interesting to
see that another non-obvious apple part, the calyx, was activated
when visible, and contributed the final count (see Fig. 5c). For the
given activated region, even if the fruits were partially or sub-
stantially occluded due to leaves, branches, and other orchard arti-
facts, the network learned to exclude those features and activate the
visible fruit regions irrespective of their spatial footprint. Slightly
underexposed/shadowed fruit regions were also activated; however,
the overexposed fruit features were suppressed, which happened to
have a similar appearance as the background sky and the plastic
reflectors.
The apple flower/fruit count and the visualization results from
CountNet are promising for practical applications in agriculture.
Although experiments in apple flowers showed higher MAE and
RMSE, the achieved results are promising, especially for the highly
complicated flower counting problem where individual flower de-
tection is a great challenge. The proposed network, trained with
limited training examples, estimated the count, irrespective of the
object’s spatial footprint, even when the flower/fruits were heavily
occluded. CountNet could be employed in flower/fruit counting ap-
plications that do not require precise detection, segmentation, and

4.4. Failures/Limitations and improvement
The proposed system also has some limitations, reduction/
elimination of which would improve the counting performance
further. Although it was not the common case for apple fruits and
apple flowers in vertical fruiting wall architecture, the network
incorporated flower count contribution from background row in
some instances in V-trellised orchard architecture (see Fig. 6a). In a
V-trellised architecture, canopies are closely spaced and trained in
an inverted pyramid shape, making the background canopy much
closer than in the vertical fruiting wall canopy architecture. We
expect that the implementation of background modeling and re-
moval algorithms and/or an active lighting-based illumination-
invariant imaging system (Silwal et al., 2021) will benefit the
model. Furthermore, the network substantially underestimated
the fruit count in heavily underexposed images (see Fig. 6b).
Through the examination of the dataset, it was found that the
training set constituted limited underexposed images for the
network to learn. The network also incorporated the counts from
apples present on the ground (see Fig. 6c). Deep learning methods
are “dataset hungry” and have the potential to improve perfor-
mance with increased training examples. Furthermore, integration
of the proposed method into a cellphone-based system would be
valuable for commercial apple fruit growers to easily access
technology and strategize crop-load management and harvesting
operations efficiently.

5. Conclusion
In this article, a regression-based network for object counting in
agriculture was proposed. Experiments were conducted to estimate
flower, fruit count, and visualize activated regions and features in
images acquired in an unstructured orchard environment. The pro-
posed method is expected to be an essential step towards auto-
mating applications such as bloom density estimation, crop-load
estimation, and yield prediction in the specialty crop (e.g., tree fruit
crops) industry. Through this study, the following conclusions were
drawn.
• Flower/fruit counting in tree fruit (e.g., apples) could be achieved
using a regression-based convolution neural network with
image-level annotation (e.g. total count of fruit). The regression-
based method simplified the counting process by directly esti-
mating the flower/fruit count without using complex polygon
annotation, explicit detection, non-maximal suppression, and
region proposal computations.
• Incorporation of activation visualization using Score-CAM and
Guided Backpropagation showed that the network was able to
suppress the majority of background information while fo-
cusing on fruit/flower features irrespective of their shape, size,
and texture in a highly occluded environment. The apple
flower/fruit edges and feature information within those edges
were some of the critical information that contributed to the
final count.
In the future, yield estimation using regression-based counting
will be investigated. Furthermore, different approaches will be
investigated to integrate the proposed approach in cellphone app
so that growers would be able to easily access the technology. The
system will also be investigated for improving the performance
algorithmically and tested for wider varieties of fruit and vege-
tables.
