A B S T R A C T  
When performing dense crops target detection and counting tasks in high-resolution images of large scene, 
several algorithms such as the standard Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) and its improved CIoU-NMS and soft- 
NMS are commonly used to improve the de-duplication performance of the prediction frame in objects detection, 
which has a high mean average precision (mAP) but the error boundaries may be large. In this research, an 
improved NMS-based max intersection over portion (MIoP-NMS) algorithm is proposed to address this problem 
and implemented in the YOLOv4 network framework for single-stage target detection. Using banana tree target 
detection statistics with different sparsity levels as an experimental case, the mAP obtained by applying the 
method in the paper reaches about 85%, with a mean counting error of about 1.3%. In comparison with several 
commonly NMS methods, their mAP values are comparable. However, compared to the 10%~25% mean count 
error when using these commonly used methods, the mean count error of the method proposed in the paper is 
reduced by about 8.7%~23.7%. The method in the paper estimates the number of banana trees in dense 
occluded banana forests with about 98.7% accuracy, which is simple and effective, and has significant advan­
tages for accurate detection and counting of dense crops targets in high-resolution images in large scenes.   
1. Intoduction 
To meet the growing demand for food from a growing population, 
growers and managers are under increasing pressure to reduce the cost 
of food production [1]. Recognized by the food and agriculture orga­
nization of the United Nations as the fourth most important food crop in 
developing countries, bananas are the most traded fresh fruit in the 
world and their economic benefits are significant for social develop­
ment. However, bananas are currently grown for the direct purpose of 
producing banana fruit, while the rest of the ancillary products, espe­
cially their stems, are hardly used effectively and even cause environ­
mental pollution. With the arrival of the carbon peak and the 
development of green energy and materials, the use of banana stalks and 
the deep diversification of the banana industry have become a real need 
before us. Meeting these needs without clearing more land requires 
better management of the crops production process and more efficient 
use of resources. Improved decision making in crops management is an 
important tool for improving efficiency, and improved decision making 
requires accurate monitoring of crops growth [2]. 
As a key function in the agricultural production chain, crops moni­
toring is important for yield estimation, weed control, phenotypic or 
disease management, as all these components have a real impact on the 
country’s economy and environment [3]. Convenient and accurate yield 
prediction will directly contribute to the rational use of resources such as 
labor and storage. For example, farmers can use monitoring results to 
decide to reseed in cases of excessive heterogeneity, thus ensuring effi­
cient land use and increased yields. Inadequate crops monitoring, on the 
other hand, can lead to significant waste of valuable resources such as 
water and fertilizer. 
Traditionally, crops monitoring has relied on manual visual inspec­
tion at field level. An important and appropriate way of obtaining yield 
estimates is through crops identification counts. In most cases, it is 
necessary to rely on experienced farmers or experts to count the number 
of plants per unit area and use this in conjunction with plant growth to

limited sampling area is a cumbersome, time-consuming, and costly 
process for operators. The method can also introduce significant un­
certainty due to the limited representativeness of the sampling area and 
the presence of human error. In addition, the method typically does not

Over the past decade, Precision Agriculture (PA) has helped reduce 
physical labor in the field by providing less subjective, cost-effective, 
and robust crops monitoring solutions. Coupled with effective 
decision-making strategies and fine-grained control of inputs and 
treatments, high-precision monitoring has the potential to further 
reduce costs and increase yields, leading to greater return on 
investment. 
However, when faced with large areas of densely planted crops such 
as banana patches, many factors such as high density, heavy shading and 
wide variation in size, shape and position make counting such crops a 
very challenging task. Manual counting, on the other hand, is increas­
ingly unable to meet the demands of modern crops management due to 
its high labor costs, time consuming nature and error prone drawbacks 
[4]. 
Currently, the most promising research tends to develop automated 
and non-destructive remote sensing methods to predict yields by pro­
cessing high-resolution images in large scenarios. Unmanned aerial 
vehicle (UAV) technology provides economical, affordable, and safe 
high-resolution imagery at low altitudes. A single camera image can 
cover a larger field area than a point sensor, allowing a high-resolution 
image survey of the entire site to be acquired in a matter of minutes [5]. 
At the same time, the accuracy of image classification has been revo­
lutionized by the introduction of deep artificial neural networks (ANN) 
[6]. Although aerial data can be collected cheaper and faster using un­
manned aerial vehicles (UAVs), such data collection is almost worthless 
if the acquired images are not converted into relevant information. In 
contrast, deep artificial neural networks provide human-comparable 
accuracy in a variety of image recognition tasks, and by applying ma­
chine vision methods, valuable information can be extracted from 
high-resolution photographs. In the coming years, this combined solu­
tion of low-cost, high-resolution image acquisition using UAVs, sensors, 
computers, and high-precision ANN-based image processing could have 
a further significant impact on crops growth monitoring for precision 
agriculture, offering the potential to develop alternative methods to 
manual counting. Research into the use of such methods for crops 
counting falls into three main categories: image segmentation process­
ing, target detection and direct counting by regression networks. 
Image segmentation processes segment the target based on pheno­
typic characteristics such as color and texture. Combined with counting 
methods, the number of plants or fruits can be calculated. In the related 
field of intensive crops counting, some researchers have used greyscale 
image processing statistics [7,8] to count the number by processing 
object contours and geometric centers, but this method is mostly 
designed for indoor images and is difficult to be directly extended to 
outdoor practical production sites. The UAV combined with multispec­
tral image augmentation statistics method [9] is sensitive to light and 
plant morphology, making it difficult to extend to crops with irregular 
shapes and complex plantation backgrounds, such as bananas. Some 
researchers have generated 3D point clouds for crops counting or size 
measurement based on UAVs with some results [10,11]. However, the 
accuracy of target counting is highly dependent on the accuracy of target 
segmentation, and it is difficult to separate targets when encountering 
high density, mutually occluding and closely occluded crops targets such 
as banana, rice and wheat. Target segmentation must be combined with 
a counting method to obtain the number of targets, and inaccurate 
segmentation can lead to a serious accumulation of counting errors. 
Object detection is a common method for counting by detecting and 
drawing bounding boxes. In addition to the number of stems, informa­
tion about the size and location of the target can be obtained. Ji et al. 
(2021) [12] proposed a detection method combining feature extraction 
and a Least Squares Support Vector Machines (LS-SVM) classifier with 
an F1 score of 88.36%. However, without deep learning, this method 
may limit its direct use for other applications due to the relative lack of 
learning capability. With the development of deep learning in recent 
years, successful applications of neural networks in fruit recognition and 
ripeness classification [13,14] and pest and disease identification [15, 
16] have demonstrated the effectiveness of the method. The UAV 
combined with deep learning object detection methods [17–19] have 
also shown excellent results in crops and fruit detection and counting. 
However, in object detection studies using deep learning, some research 
algorithms resize the image immediately before the input model due to 
the need for deep learning networks, which may lead to a large loss of 
small targets during detection. This is because if the original image is 
large, the size of the small target will be greatly reduced or even 
disappear after resizing. 
Sliding window and image clipping are two commonly used methods 
for counting dense objects in high-resolution images of large scenes 
using object detection. However, repetitive detection between adjacent 
sub-images poses new challenges. Li W [20] used convolutional neural 
networks to identify oil palm trees in high-resolution remotely sensed 
images in a sliding window manner, where all repetitive detection tar­
gets generated within a unit pixel are combined into one target by 
averaging the Euclidean distance. However, this method requires the use 
of satellite remote sensing imagery, which is costly, and image acqui­
sition is susceptible to interferences such as weather and cloud cover, in 
addition to not easily detecting plants with large differences in radius. 
Bruno T. Kitano [18] used U-Net for image segmentation of maize plants 
in high-resolution image, applying morphological operators and Blob to 
detect the number of maize plants. However, this method is difficult to 
count dense crops. Desai et al. [21] (2019) used a sliding window based 
on deep learning to detect flowering regions by counting regions con­
taining ears to predict the number of ears. However, it is not suitable for 
the case of densely growing and different spike sizes of rice. Bipul 
Neupane [22] et al. combined Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for 
detection of banana tree plants with high resolution images. However, 
they only studied seedling plants with a single background and were 
unable to solve the problem of missed and repeated detections due to 
image cropping. In addition, the accuracy of this method would be 
reduced when counting banana plants of different sizes and more 
densely covered growth stages, leading to more false detections and 
larger counting errors. 
Direct counting through regression networks is another commonly 
used method for object counting. Lu et al. [23] (2017) proposed a 
regression network, TasselNet, for direct object counting. However, the 
counting efficiency of this method was unstable in the late stages of 
crops growth. In order to improve the counting accuracy and efficiency, 
they subsequently proposed TasselNetV2+ [24]. Compared to other 
deep convolutional neural networks, TasselNetV2+ reduces the use of 
video memory and will be able to analyze large size images efficiently. 
Similarly, Khaki et al. [25] (2022) proposed WheatNet for wheat head 
counting with an overall prediction error of 8.7%. A disadvantage of 
direct counting through the regression network method is that the 
method can only obtain the object number. Direct counting of fruits with 
more distinctive features in the classification can increase the speed, but 
the range of object frames occupied by the whole crops is important for 
subsequent specific phenotypic analysis, planting operations, and 
growth management. Therefore, the acquisition of bounding boxes is 
necessary. In addition, the size of targets varies considerably even within 
the same plot. Some targets are so small that if the original large image is 
resized immediately before being fed into the detection network, small 
targets are also easily lost in the detection. 
Despite some research on dense crops object detection and accurate 
statistics in high resolution images, most previously proposed moni­
toring methods are limited. For example, methods are only applicable to 
a limited number of well-characterized growth stages. Inaccurate results

The motivation of this research is to develop a fully automated al­
gorithm for calculating the number of dense crops targets and feature 
information using large scene high-resolution RGB UAV images. The 
research contributions includes: First, a sample dataset of banana trees 
in a large scene was constructed by UAV photography and image 
stitching. Experimental tests were also conducted to determine the 
optimal cut size and overlap rate for cutting high-resolution images of 
ripe banana trees for target detection. YOLOv4 [26] was then used as the 
object detection network, and mosaic data increments were taken to 
expand the training set to ensure the speed and accuracy of detection. 
The performance of the developed algorithm is compared with two 
baselines: manually labelled plant objects in UAV images and objects 
detected using traditional algorithms such as NMS, CIoU-NMS and 
soft-NMS. Finally, the NMS based MIoP-NMS proposed in this study can 
effectively remove the duplicate detection frames generated during 
image clipping detection and significantly improve the accuracy of ob­
ject detection counting in high-resolution images. The algorithm ach­
ieves small object detection and counting of closely occluded banana 
trees in high resolution images with a high accuracy of about 98.7%. 
2. Methods and data 
2.1. The main process of the method 
This paper implements the training of a dense crops plant counting 
model based on the YOLOv4 target detection network for large scenes of 
high-resolution images, and the general structure of the study is shown 
in Fig. 1. First, a sample dataset of banana trees in a large scene is 
constructed by UAV photography and image stitching, and data anno­
tation and dataset splitting are performed in the general form of model 
construction. The training set is then augmented with data and model 
training is started. The initial model is then evaluated for performance 
using a validation set and a test set, and if performance does not meet 
requirements, adjustments must be made to the training set and to the 
network structure and parameters. If the performance of the trained 
dense crops counting model meets the requirements, it is then used to 
detect targets in actual high-resolution image slices. In this case, when 
cutting the image for detection, the original high-resolution image of the 
large scene is cut into several sub-images using image cutting in an 
overlapping manner to ensure that the banana tree is completely present 
in at least one sub-image. After the detection results of each image slice 
are obtained by model detection, the results are de-duplicated and 
counted by the MIoP-NMS algorithm, and the de-duplicated prediction 
frames are combined and mapped back to the original high-resolution 
image to obtain the accurate crops plant count. The research results 
are first applied to the detection of dense banana tree targets in high- 
resolution orthophotos of banana forests in large scenes. 
2.2. Data collection and labeling 
This paper uses the banana as an experimental example. All images 
of the banana tree sample dataset were collected in the afternoon of 2 
January 2022, when the weather was cloudy to sunny, at the banana 
plantation in Longan, Nanning, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, as 
shown in Fig. 2(a). The collection was performed by flying a DJI Elf 
quadcopter (DJI Phantom Pro+ V2.0, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a 
digital color camera to 30 m (relative to the ground) above the banana 
forest, and the UAV flew systematically according to the selected plot 
(shown in Fig. 2(b)) and the established direction, with a 30% overlap 
rate for interval photography. The camera was shot vertically downward 
to acquire RGB orthoimages of 4864 × 3648 pixels each with a size of 
7.3 MB, as shown in Fig. 2(c). Most of the collected images were of 
banana trees with normal growth of about 1 year without disease, 
planting density of 120–140 plants/acre, and most of the ideal banana 
plant spacing (calculated at the bottom of the banana stalk) was about 2 
m. Bananas were basically in the overwintering stage, bud differentia­
tion stage and bud gestation stage, which tend to be mature. After the 
flight, a total of 552 banana remote sensing images as shown in Fig. 2(c) 
were collected in this study. Among them, 442 images were used as the 
training set, accounting for 80% of the total sample; 38 images were 
used as the validation set, accounting for 7% of the total sample; 72 
images were used as the test set, accounting for 13% of the total sample. 
Using the UAV mapping and measurement software Pix4D (Lau­
sanne, Switzerland) and SIFT algorithm, the adjacent images in the test 
set of the dataset were stitched together to synthesize a large scene of 
high-resolution banana forest orthorectified remote sensing image with 
a resolution of 4104 × 14,505 pixels and a size of 50 MB as shown in 
Fig. 3. The typical plot picture was also divided into plots of equal size 
on the left and right sides for the purpose of testing the difference of 
crops object detection by the method in this paper for different sparsity 
levels. The left side is a sparse plot with 214 banana trees (manually 
labeled and counted, when 50% of the area of a banana tree is to the left 
of the boundary, it is included in the data on the left); the right side is a 
dense plot with 244 banana trees (also manually labeled and counted). 
The reason for synthesizing the image shown in Fig. 3 is that the current 
effective way to count a large area of banana trees is by first synthesizing 
an aerial orthophoto image of banana trees into a high-resolution image 
of the specified plot, and then manually counting the number of banana

2.3. Data augmentation 
Due to the small number of original sample datasets collected in this 
study, and the high morphological similarity of the banana tree object 
background as well as the banana trees. This may easily lead to over­
fitting of the trained model, which in turn affects the final detection 
effect. Therefore, it is necessary to further expand the sample dataset by 
means of data augmentation to increase the generalization ability of the 
model. 
Unlike the conventional offline augmentation method where all 
images are augmented at once and then put into the network for 
training, we use the online augmentation method. That is, each image 
has a 50% probability of random data augmentation before it is fed into 
the network. When using the "online augmentation" method, it is 
necessary to increase the number of training epochs for the purpose of 
expanding the training set because the number of original samples re­
mains the same. When the probability of data augmentation is set to 
50%, the training epoch will randomly augment 50% of the images. In 
this study, 442 images were used to form the training set, then the 
training epoch is equivalent to expanding 221 images. When the number 
of training epochs reaches 100, it is equivalent to an expansion of 22,100 
images. Various data augmentation methods are used for "online 
augmentation", including random image panning, rotation, color 
changing, flipping, and mosaic data augmentation. Among them, the 
data augmentation effect of pan-chromatic rotation, flip and mosaic data 
augmentation methods is shown in Fig. 5.

If the high-resolution image is directly put into the YOLOv4 model 
for detection, the normal-sized objects in the figure will be scaled to very 
small objects, making object detection impossible. To solve this prob­
lem, we can start from two aspects, one is to modify the model input size, 
and the other is to slice the images using a sliding window-like approach 
and then inspect them one by one. Modifying the model input size im­
plies a larger computational effort as well as a larger GPU memory, and 
the number of high-resolution training sets is scarce, which makes the 
solution less suitable under the limitation of the current level of com­
puter hardware development. So, this study uses an image cutting 
scheme: the high-resolution image is first cut into several smaller-sized 
images, and then detected one by one using the trained object detection 
model.

cut size P and the cut overlap ratio O. The cut size is the pixel value of the 
image being sliced. For banana tree object detection experiments, this

image into a square is that the structure of a normal banana tree grows 
radially from the center outward, showing a circular surface that can 
form an inner tangent constraint with the square; and the square does 
not need to be filled with gray pixels when deflating, allowing the 
YOLOv4 model to perform as well as possible. The cut overlap ratio is

pixels, such as 1200 × 1200 pixels, we can include more banana tree 
objects, even though the slices are scaled to 416 × 416 pixels when put 
into the model, while each banana tree object still has 60 to 200 pixels, 
improving the detection speed without affecting the detection accuracy.

2.5. MIoP-NMS de-duplication algorithm 
Although the cut overlap rate can avoid the missed detection prob­
lem caused by cutting a banana tree into two halves, it cannot solve the 
duplicate detection problem caused by the cut edge just containing the 
local image of the banana tree. As shown in Fig. 9, the upper bounding 
box “banana tree: 0.46′′ and “banana tree: 0.42′′ are both the same ba­
nana tree. The lower bounding box “banana tree: 0.73′′ and “banana 
tree: 0.43′′ are also the same banana tree. The orange line is the cut line 
of the right image slice, and the red mask on the right is the image of the 
overlapping part of the adjacent subgraph. Both “banana tree: 0.42′′ and 
“banana tree: 0.43′′ are duplicate detection problems caused by cutting 
edges containing local images of banana trees. 
Therefore, NMS processing is required for the detected bounding 
boxes. Due to the specificity of the local image of the banana tree pro­
duced by image cutting, its Intersection over Union (IoU) with the 
overall image is less than the standard NMS IoU threshold (0.3 here). If 
the threshold is turned down, it will again affect the de-weighting of 
other bounding boxes. Therefore, the special bounding boxes resulting

4. Results analysis and discussion 
4.1. Impact of training epochs on model effectiveness 
The number of training epochs is related to the amount of data 
generated, but only to the extent that it improves the model. As 
mentioned earlier, the mAP of the model increased from 73.08% to 
87.29% when the number of training rounds increases from 50 to 250 
for the same combination of cut size and cut overlap rate. However, it 
does not improve further and even decreases. In subsequent experi­
ments, the effect of training rounds on the model showed a similar trend. 
It is therefore said that data augmentation is of limited use and that it is 
best to obtain high quality raw data sets in order to obtain a better model 
through data driven approach. 
4.2. Optimal combination of cut size and overlap rate when cutting high- 
resolution images 
It is important to determine a better combination of cut size and 
overlap rate to cut the high-resolution image for different sparse target 
detection and counting. The previous section provides a comparative 
experiment on the effect of different NMS algorithms on counting results 
in different sparse target counting situations. 
A longitudinal comparison of a1, b1, c1, and d1 in Fig. 12 shows that 
with various training epochs of the model and various cut sizes, as the 
cut overlap rate rises, the mAP shows a gradual increase in the cut 
overlap rate 0%~20% stage. This is because the cutting overlap rate 
gradually overcomes the missed detection problem as it rises from 0% to 
20%, so the detection accuracy gradually improves. The mAP shows a 
small fluctuation but overall smooth trend at the cut overlap rate of 20% 
~50%. The cut overlap rate at this stage effectively solves the problem 
of missed detection, and the MIOP-NMS effectively solves the problem of 
duplicate detection, so this stage is the best cut overlap rate range. The 
mAP, on the other hand, shows a tendency to fluctuate significantly in 
the cut overlap rate 50%~80% stage, which is because when the cut 
overlap rate exceeds 50%, a certain image slice constitutes an overlap 
relationship not only with one adjacent image slice, but also with two or 
even more adjacent image slices. Therefore, more duplicate detection 
problems due to image cutting are introduced at this point, resulting in 
an increasing detection error. 
A longitudinal comparison of a2, b2, c2, and d2 in Fig. 12 shows that 
the counting results show a rising and then decreasing trend in the cut 
overlap rate 0%~20% stage, which is because the missed detection 
problem is gradually overcome when the cut overlap rate increases. The 
MIoP-NMS method starts to function gradually after greater than 10% of 
the cut overlap rate, so the count results gradually converge to the true 
value. The counting results showed a small fluctuation but an overall 
smooth trend at the cut overlap rate of 20%~50%, and the counting 
results are closest to the true value of the number of banana tree plants at 
the cut size of 832px, so 832px is the best cut size range. The count 
results, however, showed a gradual increase at the 50%~80% cut 
overlap stage, which was due to the accumulation of duplicate detection 
problems, resulting in count results that were increasingly far from the 
true value of the number of banana tree plants. Moreover, the duplicate 
detection boxes generated in this stage are not effectively removed by 
the MIoP-NMS algorithm as well. In summary, the above results suggest 
that a cut size of 832 px and a cut overlap rate of 20% to 50% would be a 
good choice for detecting and counting dense banana tree targets in 
large scenes with high resolution images. 
4.3. Effectiveness of different NMS for detecting counts of different 
sparsely populated crops 
From Tables 2-4 and Fig. 13, the conventional NMS, soft-NMS, and 
CIoU-NMS can maintain a high mAP, but the counting error is very large 
and is greater in the dense banana tree counting setting than in the 
sparse banana tree counting setting. This indicates that the denser the 
object, the greater the counting error of several traditional NMS algo­
rithms, so it is not suitable for high-resolution dense crops counting. 
MIoP-NMS adds to NMS the strategy of removing if the MIoP is greater 
than a set threshold. Although the mAP in the applied MIoP-NMS case is 
comparable to several traditional NMS algorithms in different sparse 
crops detection counting occasions, the counting error is significantly 
reduced by about 8.7%~23.7% and the combined detection perfor­
mance is well stabilized under different training epochs of the model. 
The mean value of counting error of MIoP-NMS was 1.29% in sparse 
counting occasions, 1.43% in dense counting occasions, and remained 
low at 1.31% in mixed sparse and dense plots. Therefore, for accurate 
counting after dense object detection, this algorithm has obvious 
advantages.

5. Conclusions 
Accurately measuring crops targets in large outdoor scenes is chal­
lenging and important. While some previous studies have addressed 
crops vegetation segmentation for yield estimation, few have addressed 
how to detect dense crops targets from large-scene high-resolution RGB 
UAV images. This paper presents a fully automated and efficient algo­
rithm for computing the number and feature information of dense crops

Using banana crop monitoring, an important cash crop, as an application 
scenario, the error in detecting banana tree targets in large-scene high- 
resolution banana forest orthophotos using this method is about 1.3%, 
which means that the algorithm estimates the number of banana trees in 
dense occluded banana forests with about 98.7% accuracy. This pro­
vides a viable reference for accurate yield estimation under intensive or 
non-intensive growing conditions for bulk crop such as bananas. 
To ensure speed and accuracy of detection, this study used YOLOv4 
as the target detection network and expanded the training set by using 
mosaic data enhancement. However, the key to significantly improve 
the accuracy of dense occlusion target detection counting in high- 
resolution images in large scenes is the use of deep learning and the 
MIOP-NMS extreme value suppression de-duplication algorithm pro­
posed in this paper, which can effectively remove the duplicate detec­
tion frames generated during image cut detection.
