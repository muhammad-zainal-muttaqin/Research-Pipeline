SHD-O2D: a Novel Image Dataset for
Super-High-Density Olive Orchard Yield Estimation
Alessandro Rocco Denarda1,2, Damiano Marchionni3, Francesco Crocetti1, Gabriele Costante1,
Paolo Valigi1, Franco Famiani3 and Mario Luca Fravolini1
Abstract—Extra virgin olive oil is probably the most rep-
resentative agricultural product of the Mediterranean area.
Mechanization and automation in olive production processes
have achieved considerable technological advances, especially
in super-high-density orchards. Nowadays, further progress has
been marked by the integration of modern technologies based
on Artiﬁcial Intelligence, which are rapidly gaining traction
also within the agricultural sector. Speciﬁcally, Computer Vision
systems are emerging as valuable tools for several agricultural
tasks, including crop monitoring, plant disease detection, and
orchard yield estimation. This last fact increasingly points to the
need for speciﬁc image datasets, which are essential for training
these models. In line with these considerations, we present in
this work a novel super-high-density olive orchard image and
data set, as well as its ﬁrst application in training a Computer
Vision system based on a Weakly Supervised paradigm. The
dataset, named SHD-O2D (Super-High-Density Olive Orchard
Dataset) was collected from a single row of Piantone di Mogliano
olive cultivar trees, with 8 images captured per plant under
different conditions: sides of the tree, weather conditions, and the
presence or absence of fruits. The results achieved demonstrate
the effectiveness of applying Computer Vision systems for yield
estimation in olive orchards.
Index Terms—Olive Dataset, Olive Orchard, Super-High-
Density Orchard, Computer Vision, Fruit Detection, Artiﬁcial
Intelligence in Agriculture.

diffusion is due to the signiﬁcant role that extra virgin olive oil
plays in the agri-food chain. Indeed, global olive oil production
and consumption have doubled over the past 30 years [2],
driven by the adoption of new orchard establishment models.
In modern orchards, the main adopted models are intensive,
with a tree density of 200−400 plants/ha, and the super-high-
density (SHD), with more than 1200 plants/ha [3]. While

enhancing the efﬁciency and accuracy of monitoring and yield

In line with the previous considerations, it is important
to highlight the signiﬁcance of yield estimation for farmers,
as this practice is crucial for organizing olive harvesting,
managing manpower, and planning product distribution. Tra-
ditionally, yield estimation was carried out by experts, but this
process is time-consuming and prone to errors. Nowadays,
the ability of CV and Machine Learning (ML) techniques to
alleviate human effort providing accurate results in less time
is widely recognized [5], [6]. AI-based technologies rely on
the availability of speciﬁc datasets required for the developing
fruit-speciﬁc framework, and the lack of such datasets can
limit the adoption of these systems. Therefore, the aim of
this work is to propose a novel image and data set for SHD
olive orchard yield estimation and to provide a preliminary
evaluation of its effectiveness through the application of State-
of-the-Art (Sota) CV-based yield estimation frameworks.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in Section II
the analysis of SotA is revised, as well as the description of
the most relevant methodologies used for fruit yield estimation
tasks; in Section III the image and data set collection and
characterization is well presented; in Section IV, preliminary
yield estimation experiments are reported, carried out by
utilizing a WS SotA framework trained and tested on our
dataset. Achieved results and their qualitative visualization are
ﬁnally described and discussed.
II. RELATED WORK
In literature, several studies and datasets related to fruit
yield estimation tasks are present, with most of them focusing
on the development and application of convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) [7], [8]. These technologies have been tested
and employed in a large variety of fruit orchards, including
mangoes [9], kiwifruits [10], orange [11], and apple [12].
The same approaches have been employed in the olive
orchard context, providing very promising results. In [13]
the authors proposed a CNN capable of detecting visible
olives in digital images of olive trees captured directly in
the ﬁeld, at night-time and with artiﬁcial illumination. This
work was extended in [14], where the authors presented a
computer vision (CV)-based system designed for olive tree
yield estimation within super-intensive olive orchards. This
system utilized visual features exclusively extracted from
RGB images taken from a lateral view and achieved a root-
mean-square-error (RMSE) of 0.9914 kg per sample, with an
overestimation of 2.64%. In another study, [15] proposed a
deep learning (DL)-based olive tree identiﬁcation method for
accurate classiﬁcation and crown-based yield estimation. Their
method was validated over a diverse set of images with a wide
range of samples and ground classes. [16] proposed a system
for early production estimation in olive orchards, combining
image analysis techniques with light drone image acquisition
and photogrammetric reconstruction.
The aforementioned methods are based on the fully su-
pervised (FS) learning paradigm, which means that images
used in the training phase of the network need to be manually
annotated with bounding boxes or pixel-level labels, indicating
the region of the image containing the object and its respective
class. However, manual image labeling is generally a highly
time-consuming practice, which poses a signiﬁcant limitation
to the application of CV-based methods. Moreover, the anno-
tated datasets utilized in SotA works are often too speciﬁc
to be employed for general tasks, and only a few of them
are publicly available [17]. For this reason, recent works have
emphasized the Weakly Supervised (WS) approaches [18]–
[20], which signiﬁcantly mitigate the time and effort required
for dataset collection by employing less restrictive annotations
procedures, i.e., simple image-level labels.
WS approaches have been successfully adopted in detection
and counting tasks [21], although relatively few studies have
focused on their application in agriculture. In [22] an apple
ﬂower-fruit counting network is trained with images annotated
only with the number of objects. [23] applied a WS learning
method to classify, localize, and segment potato defects to
automate quality control tasks. One of the latest works [19],
developed a yield estimation system that detects, locates,
and maps fruits in the orchard, trained with simple binary
labels encoding the presence or the absence of the fruits in
the images. A similar approach was utilized in [24], where
the authors proposed a WS framework based on the [25]
attention modules improving activation map generation and
post-processing for fruit detection.
Previous works underscored the potential of WS approaches
in agricultural applications, offering a more efﬁcient and
scalable alternative to traditional FS methods. In this work,
we introduced a novel dataset for SHD olive orchard yield
estimation and its ﬁrst application in training a WS system.
By leveraging the network proposed in [24], we further tested
the efﬁciency of our dataset to provide a more reliable CV-
based olive estimate (see Fig. 1). Moreover, a graphical user
interface (GUI) is proposed to make the presented dataset and
results publicly available among all trees.
III. THE SHD-O2D IMAGE DATASET
Olive tree images and data were collected in an orchard site
in the Umbria Region, Italy. The orchard consists of seven
years old olive trees spaced 5.0×1.8 m (1111 trees/ha) and
cultivated in a north-south oriented hedgerow, then grown in
an SHD manner [26]. Among the entire orchard, a single row
has been selected for its cultivar homogeneity. The row was
composed of 100 plants of Piantone di Mogliano olive cultivar,
characterized by a medium-large fruit size (2−3 g/olive) with
a bright color.
During the fruit harvesting season (October/November
2023), four sessions of image collection were carried out.
Two sessions were performed before the fruit harvesting, once
in sunny daylight conditions and once in cloudy conditions.

During the harvesting session, the weight of the harvested

average weight of a single olive WA for each plant was
determined by weighing a sample set of 50 olives. Therefore,
the previous data allowed the estimation of the number of
olives produced for each tree, considered as the estimated
Ground-Truth (GT).
Further data were obtained by counting olives in some
samples of the image dataset. In particular, ﬁve out of the
original 100 trees were selected, i.e., plants numbered as 2,
28, 38, 49, and 64. From these, the four images depicting
the trees before the fruit harvesting were cropped into 256
tiles each, and then olives present in each tile were manually
counted. This process allowed the deﬁnition of a scale factor
ks, further detailed in Section IV-C, between the data relating
to the harvested fruits and the number of fruits actually visible
in a plant image. All data for the aforementioned ﬁve trees are

EXPERIMENT

on the SHD-O2D image dataset. Speciﬁcally, the WS yield
estimation framework presented in [24] is ﬁrst well-described
and, afterward, trained and tested for the purpose of olive yield
estimation.

The network optimization is carried out by leveraging the
attention module presented in [25], which is designed for
image classiﬁcation and can identify salient image regions
by amplifying their inﬂuence while suppressing irrelevant
information. During inference, the network’s output consists
of an 8-channel activation map with the same resolution as
the input image, whose pixel values are higher in regions
containing fruits. To obtain fruit detection, the eight channels
of the activation maps are ﬁnally summed up into a single-
channel heatmap H, and the local maxima coordinates P =
{(u1, v1), (u2, v2), ..., (un, vn)}, associated with the presence
of fruits, are computed using the TriCluster Local Maxima
Detector (TC-LMD) detailed in [24], setting the content score
threshold thcs = 18.99.
B. Model Training
The training of the network was carried out using the
Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer with an initial
learning rate of 0.1, multiplied by a decay factor equal to 0.5
every 10 epochs. A batch size of 24 samples was employed,
while the early stopping strategy was used for the best model
selection. The computer used for both training and testing is an

The OliveISAR dataset presented in [18] has been employed
for the ﬁrst stage of training. This dataset comprises 1402
tiles with a resolution of 606 × 403 pixels, randomly picked
from crops obtained from 28 full olive tree images of high
resolution (5456 × 3632 pixels). Tiles were manually labeled
with presence/absence binary annotations and then split into
a training and a validation set composed of 1298 and 104
samples, respectively.
After this initial training stage, the network was ﬁne-tuned
using tiles obtained from a single tree from our dataset.
Particularly, tiles from the eight images of the 2-ID tree, which
were previously labeled during the sc search process, were
employed. Since each full-size image was divided into 256
tiles, the ﬁne-tuning dataset comprises 2048 tiles: 1768 tiles
were used as the training set for ﬁne-tuning the model, while
the other 280 were used as the validation set.
At the end of the training stages, the model was tested
by inferring all the 800 samples of the dataset. Particularly,
each sample was preprocessed by cropping the full-size image
in 256 tiles. The provided heatmaps were then recomposed
generating the full-size heatmap H. Local maxima coordinates
were ﬁnally extracted from H using the TC-LMD method, as
previously depicted.
C. Performance Metrics
In yield estimation tasks, the choice of GT reference value
depends on the cultivation type and the dataset in use. Gener-
ally, in fruit plant datasets, the GT represents the number of
harvested fruits yi for the ith plant. This is then utilized to
calculate the error and related metrics such as Mean Absolute
Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), or Root Mean
Squared Error (RMSE). In CV-based fruit detection tasks, the
actual number of harvested fruits often does not accurately
reﬂect the number of instances visible in a single image of the
tree. To address this discrepancy and establish a correlation
between the number of fruits estimated from the harvested
olive weight and the number of detected fruits provided by the
CV system, a scale factor ks has been deﬁned. The latter was
derived by manually counting olives among dataset images of
ﬁve olive trees, as introduced in Section III.
The main distinction observed when examining the manu-
ally counted fruit values reported in Table I lies between the
samples collected under sunny daylight conditions and those
collected under cloudy conditions. Indeed, the number of visi-
ble olives is higher for samples collected under cloudy daylight
conditions. For this reason, it was decided to determine two
distinct scale factors, one for the sunny daylight condition
(ks

E. Dataset and Result on Graphical User Interface
A graphical user interface has been developed using HTML
and Javascript libraries for visualization of the SHD-O2D
dataset and the preliminary results achieved by the WS
SotA framework (Fig. 4). GPS coordinates of each tree were
collected, allowing their visualization on an Earth satellite
map. Moreover, a pop-up was associated with each tree
using the Folium1 library. Inside each pop-up, the interface
allows the visualization of the eight original dataset images,
corresponding heatmaps provided by the WS yield estimation
framework, and additional images showing CV-based olive
detections. Three sets of buttons allow for the selection of en-
vironmental conditions: (i) the side from which the sample was
taken (East/West); (ii) weather condition (Sunny/Cloudy); (iii)
fruit presence or absence (Before harvesting/After harvesting).
Furthermore, both GT data and CV-based detection results
are presented for each tree, including harvested olive weight,
average olive weight, estimated count, and total number of
detected olives.
The graphical interface, including the whole SHD-O2D
dataset, were made publicly available2.

V. CONCLUSION
In this work, a novel super-high-density olive orchard image
dataset has been presented. Images of 100 trees belonging to
a single orchard row were collected under different conditions
(side, weather, and presence or absence of fruits on trees),
resulting in eight samples for each plant. Moreover, Ground-
Truth data were collected during the olive harvesting phase,
including the total harvested fruit weight and the average
weight of a single olive for each tree.
A CV-based yield estimation framework, based on a weakly
supervised paradigm has been tested using the dataset images,
showing the effectiveness of this approach for applications
related to this kind of orchard. However, there is potential
for further improvement by employing CV systems based on
alternative paradigms. For instance, future development of
this dataset could involve the comprehensive annotation of
fruits using more precise labels, such as bounding boxes or
masks. This would enable the application of more efﬁcient
fully supervised methods, potentially enhancing the accuracy
and performance of yield estimation in such orchard settings.
