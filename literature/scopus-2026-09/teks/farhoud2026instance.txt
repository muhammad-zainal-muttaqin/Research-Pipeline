Egypt ranks among the world’s leading citrus producers and exporters; however, orchard operations remain con-
strained by subjective, labor-intensive manual quality grading and time-consuming yield estimation that require 
specialized expertise. This study presents an AI-driven orchard-intelligence framework for automated fruit qual-
ity grading and dynamic yield estimation under the unstructured conditions of Egyptian orange farms. To address 
the lack of in-ﬁeld public datasets, a domain-speciﬁc instance-segmentation dataset of 618 original pixel-level an-
notated images, expanded to 1,545 augmented training images, and 15 videos was constructed. This dataset was 
used to benchmark single-stage segmentation models (YOLOv8, YOLOv11) against a two-stage (Mask R-CNN), 
alongside a transformer-based bounding-box detection (RT-DETR), under dense canopy occlusion, clustered fruit 
instances, and variable natural illumination. Each ﬁne-tuned model was evaluated across two stages: static qual-
ity grading and dynamic yield estimation. For static grading (Good vs. Defective), YOLOv11l ranked among the 
top tier of evaluated segmenters, reaching mean Average Precision (mAP) of 0.932 with balanced precision-
recall behavior, while maintaining real-time feasibility at 26.1 ms per image. For dynamic yield estimation, a 
multi-object tracking pipeline was implemented using BoT-SORT with camera-motion compensation and a vir-
tual line-crossing counting mechanism. The evaluation reveals a three-way dissociation: the highest-mAP static 
segmenter (YOLOv11l), the best dynamic total counter (RT-DETR, WMAPE = 5.66%, despite the lowest box-
level static accuracy), and the best quality-resolved dynamic grader (YOLOv11s, WMAPE = 6.97% at 14.2 ms 
per frame) are three diﬀerent systems; RT-DETR did not resolve fruit quality reliably under motion. Counting 
accuracy and grading capability are thus separable properties requiring separate evaluation. Overall, the frame-
work enables objective fruit quality assessment and actionable in-ﬁeld fruit counting to support harvest logistics 
and labor planning, providing a practical foundation for smart agriculture in the Egyptian citrus sector.
1.  Introduction
Global food demand is projected to intensify as the world population 
approaches 10 billion by 2050 [1], requiring sustainable intensiﬁcation 
that increases productivity without proportional resource consumption. 
Within this paradigm, citrus is a strategic crop: Egypt is a dominant or-
ange exporter, with annual shipments of approximately 2 million met-
ric tons [2] and a signiﬁcant share of national agricultural exports [3]. 
As shown in Fig. 1, Egypt consistently leads the other major exporting 
origins in annual orange export volume, underscoring its strategic im-
portance in the global citrus supply chain.
Despite this economic importance, Egyptian citrus production still 
depends heavily on manual fruit inspection, grading, and yield esti-
mation. These practices are slow, subjective, and diﬃcult to scale, and 
they are further strained by a steadily declining agricultural workforce

1.1.  Problem statement
Deploying computer vision in real Egyptian citrus orchards is chal-
lenging: fruits are frequently occluded by leaves and branches, clus-
tered with partial overlap, and captured under variable natural illumina-
tion. Moreover, yield assessment is typically conducted using handheld 
moving-camera videos rather than framed still images, so intermittent 
detections and conﬁdence oscillations can fragment tracked identities 
and cause systematic overcounting or undercounting. The core problem 
addressed in this study is therefore the lack of a ﬁeld-validated, end-to-
end pipeline that performs instance segmentation, quality grading, and 
dynamic yield estimation under the unstructured conditions of Egyptian 
citrus farms.
1.2.  Research objectives
The primary objective is to develop and validate an end-to-end com-
puter vision system for automated citrus crop monitoring and yield es-
timation. The speciﬁc objectives are:
• Construct a domain-speciﬁc instance segmentation dataset for 
Egyptian citrus, with 618 original images (expanded to 1,545 aug-
mented training images) annotated into Good and Defective quality 
classes.
• Benchmark YOLOv8, YOLOv11, and Mask R-CNN, together with 
a transformer-based bounding-box detection baseline (RT-DETR), on 
the dataset using mAP@0.5, precision-recall behavior, and inference 
latency to determine the best accuracy-eﬃciency trade-oﬀ.
• Validate dynamic yield estimation by integrating the ﬁne-tuned 
models with a multi-object tracker and evaluating counting relia-
bility on moving-camera videos using Relative Error and Weighted 
Mean Absolute Percentage Error (WMAPE).
2.  Related work
Deep learning methods for fruit recognition generally fall into two 
families. Single-stage detectors perform classiﬁcation and localization 
jointly in one forward pass and favor inference speed; representative 
examples include YOLO [7], RetinaNet [8], and SSD [9]. Two-stage de-
tectors ﬁrst generate region proposals and then reﬁne them, typically 
achieving higher accuracy at higher computational cost, with Fast R-
CNN [10], Faster R-CNN [11], and Mask R-CNN [12] being the most 
widely adopted. More recently, real-time detection transformers such 
as RT-DETR [13] have eliminated non-maximum suppression through 
end-to-end set prediction; however, they operate at the bounding-box 
level and do not natively produce the instance masks required for pixel-
level grading. Within orchard-oriented research, instance segmenta-
tion is increasingly preferred over bounding-box detection because its

2.1.  From detection to instance segmentation
As illustrated in Fig. 3, image classiﬁcation, object detection, and 
instance segmentation form a hierarchy of increasing spatial speci-
ﬁcity. Bounding boxes provide rough localization but overlap heavily 
in clustered canopies, whereas instance masks separate adjacent fruits 
and capture shape information critical for accurate counting and sizing
[14].
Early fruit detection work relied on YOLO variants, including 
YOLOv4 for orange detection [15] and several improved YOLO-based 
models for citrus [16–20], mangoes [21], apples [22], passion fruit [23], 
and capsicum [24]. More recently, YOLOv11 has demonstrated eﬃcient 
real-time crop detection with clear gains over earlier versions [25]. 
However, bounding-box outputs remain insuﬃcient whenever fruits 
overlap or when shape information is needed.
To address these limitations, instance segmentation has gained trac-
tion. Mask R-CNN has served as a benchmark for high-accuracy seg-
mentation on sweet pepper [26], cocoa pods [27], strawberries [28], 
and partially occluded mangoes [29], but its two-stage design is com-
putationally heavy for real-time edge deployment. Single-stage segmen-
tation models such as YOLOv8-seg have extended YOLO with a mask 
branch, producing strong results on strawberries [30], tomatoes [31], 
weeds [32], and apple sizing via 3D reconstruction [33]. Notably, Sap-
kota et al. [6] showed that YOLOv8 outperformed Mask R-CNN on im-
mature green apples (mAP@0.5 of 90.2% vs. 85.0%) while also being 
faster, suggesting that modern single-stage segmenters can oﬀer a strong 
speed-accuracy trade-oﬀ for dense orchards.
2.2.  Multi-class attribute assessment
Beyond localization, segmentation models have been extended to 
classify fruit attributes such as ripeness, growth stage, and quality. Ex-
amples include four cocoa-pod ripening stages with YOLOv8x [27]; ripe 
vs. unripe Gannan navel oranges with YOLOv5-NMM at 93.2% accu-
racy [20]; ripe vs. unripe strawberries with YOLOv8 [30]; ﬁve capsicum 
growth stages [24]; and healthy vs. diseased tomato plants [31]. Multi-
class segmentation has also been used to isolate target fruits from sur-
rounding plant structures relevant to robotic navigation or harvesting, 
such as apples vs. branches and trunks [6], sweet peppers and their pe-
duncles [26], and uncut weeds vs. trunks and poles [32]. These studies 
support the feasibility of training a segmentation model that localizes 
citrus fruits and simultaneously classiﬁes each instance as Good or De-
fective.
2.3.  Yield estimation
Early yield estimation approaches relied on counting detections in 
static images [15,22,29,30]. Although useful for controlled evaluation, 
static-image counting fails in real orchards where moving cameras gen-
erate continuous video streams and the same fruit may be detected 
across many frames, causing severe overestimation [17]. Practical yield 
estimation therefore requires Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) to link de-
tections of the same object over time. Representative tracking-based 
pipelines include centroid-based counting for mangoes [21], YOLOv5s 
+ DeepSORT with virtual-line counting for citrus [17], tracking-based 
grape-cluster counting [34], and YOLOv8n + OC-SORT for passion fruit 
[23].
2.4.  Summary of literature gaps
Table 1 summarizes recent computer vision studies in agriculture 
and highlights several important gaps. While foundational approaches 
such as object detection and instance segmentation are widely used, 
most studies adapt these methods to address only one agricultural objec-
tive, rather than developing integrated end-to-end pipelines. Detection-
based approaches remain common because of their eﬃciency, but they 
do not provide the pixel-level precision required for handling clustered 
or occluded fruits. In addition, attribute-based multi-class classiﬁcation, 
such as fruit quality grading, has received comparatively limited atten-
tion. Yield estimation is also still performed mainly on static images, 
which does not address repeated counting of the same fruit across con-
secutive video frames. Moreover, despite Egypt’s importance as a citrus-
producing and exporting country, only limited work has focused on the 
speciﬁc environmental and orchard conditions of Egyptian citrus farms. 
These gaps provide the main motivation for the end-to-end pipeline pro-
posed in this study.

training of single-stage and two-stage segmentation models, and (iii) 
implementation of a dynamic yield estimation pipeline. The following 
subsections detail each phase and the evaluation metrics used.

Egyptian citrus sector. Acquisition took place in the pre-harvest season 
when fruits had reached commercial maturity and full coloration. A total 
of 618 high-resolution images (3024 × 4032 pixels) were captured with a 
smartphone camera from multiple angles and distances. Close-up shots 
captured ﬁne fruit details and surface defects; wider shots documented 
full-tree fruit distribution; oblique angles were used deliberately to in-
clude oranges partially hidden inside the canopy. Representative sam-
ples are shown in Fig. 5.
3.1.2.  Data annotation
Images were manually annotated in Roboﬂow using polygonal masks 
to exclude background noise and preserve exact fruit contours. Each 
fruit instance was labeled as “Good” or “Defective.” A fruit was assigned 
the Defective class if it exhibited (i) visible disease symptoms, such as 
citrus canker lesions, black spot, or rind rot; (ii) physical damage, includ-
ing punctures, splits, or crushing; or (iii) clearly abnormal discoloration 
or deformation; all remaining fruits were labeled Good. To control anno-
tation subjectivity, the entire dataset was labeled independently by two 
annotators, and every annotation was subsequently veriﬁed by a third 
reviewer who adjudicated all disagreements before the dataset was ﬁ-
nalized. The dataset, training images, contains 376 Good and 239 Defec-
tive instances (615 in total, prior to any oﬄine or online augmentation), 
corresponding to a class ratio of approximately 61:39. Annotations were 
exported in both YOLO format (for YOLOv8/YOLOv11 and RT-DETR) 
and COCO JSON format (for Mask R-CNN). Fig. 6 shows the polygonal 
ground truth corresponding to the samples in Fig. 5.
3.1.3.  Data augmentation
To preclude any data leakage, the 83.3:8.1:8.6 train/validation/test 
split was performed before augmentation. The original dataset of 618 
images was partitioned into exactly 515 training images, 50 validation 
images, and 53 blind-test images. All augmentation described below was 
applied only to the 515-image training partition, while the validation 
and test subsets consist solely of original, non-augmented images. Oﬄine 
augmentation in Roboﬂow expanded the training partition by a 3× factor, 
yielding exactly 515 × 3 = 1,545 augmented training images. Geometric 
transformations (random rotations of ±15◦, horizontal and vertical ﬂips) 
handled camera-orientation variability, while photometric transforma-
tions (random brightness and exposure adjustments) helped the model 
separate true defects from lighting artifacts such as shadows or direct 
sunlight (Fig. 7). Online augmentation during training followed each ar-
chitecture’s best practice: YOLO models used Mosaic augmentation to 
improve small-object detection, while Mask R-CNN used random ﬂip-
ping and multi-scale resizing; aggressive techniques such as Mosaic were 
avoided for Mask R-CNN to preserve the structural context required by 
its Region Proposal Network (RPN).

3.2.  Implementation and training conﬁguration
3.2.1.  Experimental environment
All training and evaluation were performed on a single worksta-
tion with an Intel Core i9-14900HX CPU (2.2 GHz, 32 threads), 16 GB 
of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 GPU (8 GB VRAM). The 
YOLOv8 and YOLOv11 networks were trained with the Ultralytics pack-
age, and Mask R-CNN with Detectron2; two isolated virtual environ-
ments managed dependency conﬂicts. Exact software versions are listed 
in Table 2. To ensure a controlled comparison across all model conﬁgu-
rations, the random seed was ﬁxed (seed = 0) for every training run, so 
that all models were exposed to identical data ordering, augmentation 
sampling, and initialization stochasticity.
3.2.2.  Hyperparameter conﬁguration
Hyperparameters were tuned to the characteristics of the Egyptian 
citrus dataset. Full conﬁgurations are given in Table 3 for YOLO, Table 4 
for Mask R-CNN, and Table 5 for RT-DETR.
YOLO conﬁguration
YOLOv8 and YOLOv11 were trained with AdamW (base learning rate 
1.667 × 10−3), a 3-epoch warmup (bias LR 0.1, momentum 0.9), and co-
sine annealing thereafter. A weight decay of 0.0005 was applied to mit-
igate overﬁtting. The composite loss combined Box Regression, Classi-
ﬁcation, and Distribution Focal Loss, with gains biased toward accurate 
localization in dense canopies. Training was capped at 300 epochs with 
early stopping (patience = 50).
Mask R-CNN conﬁguration
Mask R-CNN (Detectron2) used a ResNet-50 + FPN backbone and 
SGD (momentum 0.9, base LR 0.001, batch size 4 due to memory). Train-
ing ran for 6,400 iterations with a linear warmup over the ﬁrst 640 it-
erations followed by a multi-step decay (factor 0.1 at iterations 768 and 
1024). The RPN was conﬁgured to sample 128 RoIs per image to im-
prove detection under clutter. The ﬁnal checkpoint was selected by the 
lowest validation loss.

3.3.  Dynamic yield estimation pipeline
Static image analysis is limited for real yield estimation because a 
moving camera revisits the same fruit, causing double counting, while 
single viewpoints often hide defects. The proposed dynamic pipeline 
(Fig. 8) extracts frames, applies instance segmentation (YOLO or Mask 
R-CNN), assigns persistent identities via multi-object tracking, and then 
counts each fruit exactly once as its trajectory crosses a virtual line. Ev-
ery crossing also updates the corresponding Good or Defective counter, 
yielding real-time quality-aware yield statistics. The tracking and count-
ing components are detailed below.
3.3.1.  Multi-object tracking integration
BoT-SORT was used to assign a unique persistent ID to each orange 
across frames. It predicts per-track positions with a Kalman ﬁlter and 
associates new detections by IoU overlap. Unlike simpler trackers that 
assume a static camera, BoT-SORT applies Global Motion Compensa-
tion (GMC) to counteract handheld camera motion, which is essential 
for ﬁeld videos. The same tracker conﬁguration (Table 6) was used for 
every segmentation model so that diﬀerences in counts reﬂect detector 
stability rather than tracker behavior.

4.  Results and discussion
This section evaluates the trained models on the Egyptian citrus 
dataset, ﬁrst on static segmentation and computational eﬃciency, and 
then on dynamic video-based yield estimation.
4.1.  Segmentation performance
Fig. 9 summarizes the Precision, Recall, F1-score, and mAP@0.5 
values of every evaluated model. All segmentation models exceed 
mAP@0.5 of 0.88, conﬁrming that both single-stage and two-stage ar-
chitectures can handle the occlusion and clustering typical of Egyptian 
orchards. YOLOv11l (large) was among the top tier of evaluated seg-
menters, attaining mAP@0.5 of 0.932 with balanced precision-recall be-
havior (Precision = 0.88, Recall = 0.865, F1 = 0.87). The transformer-
based RT-DETR baseline recorded a Precision of 0.753, a Recall of 0.860, 
an F1-score of 0.803, and an mAP@0.5 of 0.827. Because RT-DETR func-
tions as a bounding-box detector rather than a mask-level instance seg-
menter, these metrics are computed exclusively at the bounding-box 
level to serve as a cross-paradigm detection reference. Furthermore, 
its detection proﬁle is notably recall-leaning (Recall 0.860 vs. Preci-
sion 0.753), a characteristic whose practical consequences for temporal 
tracking will be discussed in the dynamic evaluation of Section 4.6.
The YOLOv11 gain over YOLOv8 is attributable to architectural 
reﬁnements, in particular the C3k2 bottleneck and C2PSA spatial-
attention modules, which strengthen multi-scale feature processing in 
natural canopies with varied fruit sizes and ﬁlter out leaf/branch clut-
ter, improving Precision.
4.1.1.  Precision-recall trade-oﬀ
Mask R-CNN produced a notably diﬀerent proﬁle: a top-ranked Re-
call of 0.953 paired with a lower Precision of 0.82. This is characteristic 
of two-stage detectors, whose region-proposal stage produces many can-
didates, beneﬁcial for Recall but more prone to false positives on leaf 
and background clutter. YOLO models apply stricter selection and there-
fore achieve higher Precision. Our ﬁndings extend those of Sapkota et 
al. [6] (YOLOv8 > Mask R-CNN on apples, 90.2% vs. 85% mAP): while 
YOLOv11l ranked among the top tier on mAP, Mask R-CNN remains 
practical when maximizing Recall is the priority over strict quality grad-
ing.
4.1.2.  Model complexity and performance scaling
Within the YOLOv11 family, accuracy improves up to the Large 
variant (mAP@0.5 = 0.932) and then drops for the Extra-Large vari-
ant (0.902). With 1,545 augmented training images, the extra capacity 
of YOLOv11x (62.0 M parameters vs. 27.6 M for the Large) is under-
utilized given the size of the training set; the observed drop is therefore 
best interpreted as a data-limited over-parameterization eﬀect, whereby 
the larger model becomes more sensitive to training noise rather than 
learning better features, and its greater depth likely complicates opti-
mization.
4.1.3.  Class-wise quality grading analysis

Good and Defective classes. The detailed class-wise results for all YOLO 
conﬁgurations are presented in Table 7.

tially lower at 0.761. From a quality-control standpoint, this asymmetry 
is favorable, since it minimizes the risk of a defective fruit being missed 
and erroneously accepted as good. Conversely, the Good class exhib-

indicating that fruits classiﬁed as good were rarely mislabeled.

4.2.  Inference speed and real-time feasibility
Per-image latency, decomposed into preprocessing, inference, and 
postprocessing on an RTX 4070 (8 GB), is shown in Fig. 10, and the 
accuracy-latency trade-oﬀ is plotted in Fig. 11.
Mask R-CNN reached 67.8 ms (∼14.7 FPS), dominated by its 62.8 ms 
inference stage, expected for a two-stage pipeline that proposes then 
reﬁnes regions. YOLOv11l processed the same image in 26.1 ms 
(∼38.3 FPS), well above the ∼30 FPS threshold used for real-time ap-
plications. The RT-DETR baseline required 22.1 ms per image (1.1 ms 
preprocessing, 20.5 ms inference, and 0.5 ms postprocessing; ∼45 FPS).
In the accuracy-latency plot, models closer to the upper-left corner 
are preferred. YOLOv11l sits in this region, oﬀering the most eﬀective 
balance: YOLOv11n and YOLOv11s are faster but less accurate, while 
YOLOv11x and Mask R-CNN are slower without a comparable accuracy 
gain. Within YOLOv11, latency grows from 11.2 ms (n) to 41.3 ms (x), 
conﬁrming the expected capacity-speed trade-oﬀ but also showing that 
the largest variant is not the optimal deployment choice.
4.3.  Architectural complexity and resource eﬃciency
Parameter count (Fig. 12), network depth (Fig. 13), and GFLOPs 
(Fig. 14) characterize the suitability of each model for resource-
constrained platforms.
YOLOv11 is markedly more parameter-eﬃcient than YOLOv8 at 
comparable scales. YOLOv11l uses only 27.6 M parameters, versus 
45.9 M for YOLOv8l and 43.9 M for Mask R-CNN. For deployment this 
translates to a much smaller on-disk footprint (≈55 MB for YOLOv11l vs. 
≈90 MB for YOLOv8l), easing integration into embedded systems. Com-
pute cost follows the same trend: YOLOv11l requires 141.9 GFLOPs, 
roughly 46% below Mask R-CNN’s 265.5 GFLOPs and well below 
YOLOv8l’s 220.1 GFLOPs, important for battery-powered platforms. Al-
though YOLOv11 is deeper than YOLOv8 (e.g., 203 vs. 125 layers at 
the Large scale), its design trades width for a lighter, more expres-
sive structure. The RT-DETR occupies an intermediate complexity point: 
approximately 32.0 M parameters (between YOLOv11l’s 27.6 M and 
Mask R-CNN’s 43.9 M), 302 layers (the deepest network evaluated), and 
103.4 GFLOPs (below YOLOv11l’s 141.9 GFLOPs).

ground truth for every benchmark video was established in-ﬁeld by a

orchard. For each benchmark video, the estimator selected a section of 
the orchard and deﬁned a ﬁxed trajectory that was traversed jointly

canopy is excluded from the reference count by construction, exactly as 
it is absent from the model’s input. The quality class of each counted 
fruit was assigned at the moment of counting by the same estimator, 
applying the same visual criteria established for the image annotation 
protocol (Section 3.1.2): visible disease symptoms, physical damage,

the Good class at long camera distances where defect cues fall below 
the resolvable scale. The harvested yield subsequently passes through 
a conveyor-belt sorting stage in which every fruit is inspected at close

removed downstream, whereas a spurious in-orchard Defective count

the dynamic setting should therefore be interpreted as a primary screen-

The framework developed in this study uniﬁes these three ca-
pabilities: instance segmentation, quality-resolved classiﬁcation, and 
tracking-based dynamic counting, within a single pipeline validated on 
a localized dataset from Egyptian orange orchards. The experimental re-
sults demonstrate that each stage operates at a level suitable for practical 
deployment. For static quality grading (Good vs. Defective), YOLOv11l 
achieved a mAP@0.5 of 0.932 at an inference latency of 26.1 ms 
per image, conﬁrming that pixel-level grading is feasible in real time
under dense occlusion and variable illumination. For dynamic yield esti-
mation, the integration of instance segmentation with BoT-SORT track-
ing, camera-motion compensation, and virtual line-crossing logic en-
abled YOLOv11s to achieve a low counting error of 6.97% WMAPE at 
14.2 ms per frame on handheld orchard videos while resolving each 
counted fruit into its quality class. Additionally, the bounding-box RT-
DETR baseline demonstrated strong total-count stability (WMAPE = 
5.66%), although without reliable per-class resolution in the dynamic 
setting (Section 4.6). Taken together, these results establish the pro-
posed framework as an end-to-end system that quantiﬁes and grades 
the harvest in a single pass, extending the methods of prior studies into 
an integrated solution tailored to the operational conditions of the Egyp-
tian citrus sector.
5.  Conclusion
This study delivered a validated, end-to-end computer-vision frame-
work for real-time segmentation, quality grading, and yield estima-
tion in Egyptian orange farms, and constructed a localized dataset of 
618 original images (1,545 augmented training images) and 15 videos 
benchmarking single-stage (YOLOv8, YOLOv11) and two-stage (Mask 
R-CNN) models, together with a transformer-based bounding-box de-

16 

S.A. Farhoud et al.
tection baseline (RT-DETR), under realistic occlusion, clustering, and 
illumination variability.
For static segmentation and quality grading, YOLOv11l was among 
the top tier of evaluated segmenters (mAP@0.5 = 0.932) with real-time 
feasibility (26.1 ms per image), and comparing favorably with Mask 
R-CNN, which achieved higher Recall (0.953) but at the cost of much 
higher latency (67.8 ms) and lower Precision. The RT-DETR’s box-level 
mAP@0.5 (0.827) was below the mask-level mAP@0.5 of every seg-
mentation model, though the two criteria are not directly comparable. 
For dynamic yield estimation with BoT-SORT and virtual line-crossing, 
the best model was not the most accurate one per frame: RT-DETR, de-
spite its lowest box-level static accuracy, produced the lowest overall 
total-count error (WMAPE = 5.66%), although its per-class dynamic 
grading was unreliable; YOLOv11s achieved the lowest error among the 
mask-level segmentation models (WMAPE = 6.97%) while preserving 
quality resolution, and YOLOv11l consistently under-counted. This in-
dicates that temporal detection stability, rather than static mAP, is the 
dominant factor in in-ﬁeld counting reliability in this evaluation.
The principal ﬁnding of this evaluation is therefore a three-way dis-
sociation: the highest-mAP static segmenter (YOLOv11l), the most ac-
curate dynamic total counter (RT-DETR), and the best quality-resolved 
dynamic grader (YOLOv11s) are three diﬀerent systems. Counting accu-
racy and grading capability are consequently separable properties and 
require separate evaluation when selecting a model for deployment. Col-
lectively, the framework oﬀers a practical path to modernize Egyptian 
citrus production by reducing reliance on subjective manual inspection 
and supporting harvest logistics and labor planning.
All results derive from single training runs without dispersion esti-
mates; the counting ranking among the leading models, where RT-DETR 
leads YOLOv11s by 1.31 WMAPE points, therefore rests on single runs.
Future work will prioritize dataset expansion. In particular, the dy-
namic validation set will be extended with videos acquired in orchard 
blocks of higher defect prevalence: only ≈1.4% in the present six videos 
were Defective, which is insuﬃcient to characterize class-wise counting 
reliability. Future work will also integrate depth sensing for 3D fruit siz-
ing and volumetric yield forecasting, and combine the ﬁne-tuned models 
with mobile manipulators for autonomous inspection and robotic har-
vesting.
