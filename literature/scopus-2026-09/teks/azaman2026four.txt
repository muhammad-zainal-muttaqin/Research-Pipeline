Abstract
Oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) ripeness classification is essential for improving the oil ex-
traction rate, oil quality, and mill processing efficiency. However, RGB-based classification
is often limited by insufficient colour information from dark unripe fruitlets and shadowed
regions of the bunch surface. This study evaluated the effect of image input representation
on convolutional neural network (CNN)-based FFB ripeness classification. Four models
with the same CNN architecture were compared using RGB, RGB + infrared (IR), CIEL*a*b*,
and CIEL*a*b* + IR inputs. RGB and IR images were acquired using a dual-camera setup
positioned 3 m from the FFB sample. The CIEL*a*b* + IR model achieved the best overall
performance, with weighted-average precision, recall, and F1-score values of 0.84, 0.81,
and 0.81, respectively, compared with 0.79, 0.76, and 0.76 for RGB-only. The addition of
IR improved model performance by providing complementary near-infrared reflectance
information, while CIEL*a*b* colour-space transformation provided a more discriminative
colour representation. Class activation heat maps showed that the models focused mainly
on fruitlet regions, with the CIEL*a*b* + IR model producing more distinct activation
over ripeness-relevant areas. These findings demonstrate that the proposed four-channel
CIEL*a*b* + IR image representation improves CNN-based FFB ripeness classification by
combining lightness-separated colour information with near-infrared reflectance features,
although underripe FFB remains challenging, where it produced the highest rate of false
positives because of its transitional and heterogeneous characteristics.
Keywords: oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB); ripeness classification; convolutional neural
network (CNN); CIEL*a*b* colour space; near-infrared (NIR) imaging; four-channel image
representation; multimodal data fusion
1. Introduction
Oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) ripeness grading is a critical quality-control pro-
cess in the palm oil industry. The ripeness condition of harvested FFB directly influences
the oil extraction rate (OER), oil quality, free fatty acid (FFA) content, and overall mill
processing efficiency [1–3]. Unripe or underripe bunches may reduce oil yield, whereas
overripe bunches may increase the risk of oil deterioration, including the formation of

free fatty acids [1]. Accurate and consistent FFB ripeness classification is therefore essen-
tial for improving harvesting decisions, mill grading operations, and overall production
quality [2,4,5].
Despite its importance, FFB ripeness grading remains challenging. Conventional
grading is commonly based on visual assessment of fruitlet colour and bunch appearance.
However, such assessment is subjective and can be affected by lighting conditions, shadow,
fruitlet arrangement, and variation in bunch morphology [2,4]. Outdoor image acquisition
introduces additional challenges arising from natural illumination, background complexity,
class imbalance, and variations in FFB appearance [6,7]. The central problem addressed in
this study is the limited and non-uniform colour information available from RGB images of
FFB, particularly for dark unripe fruitlets and fruitlets located in shadowed regions [1,8].
These factors can reduce the visibility of ripeness-related features and make CNN-based
classification less reliable.
RGB imaging is widely used for agricultural product classification because it is simple,
low-cost, and capable of capturing visible colour and texture information [8,9]. Previous
FFB studies have also demonstrated that colour, texture, and their combinations can provide
useful discriminatory information for maturity classification using machine-learning and
image-processing approaches [10–12]. However, RGB images are illumination-dependent
and may not fully capture discriminative features from dark or shadowed regions of
complex biological structures [13]. This limitation is particularly evident in FFB, where
densely packed fruitlets create self-shadowing and uneven illumination across the bunch
surface. Furthermore, RGB pixel intensities are linked to ripeness-related pigment changes,
including chlorophyll degradation and changes in red colour response, but these colour-
based features can still be affected by illumination variation and non-fruit artefacts [14].
Alternative colour representations have therefore also been explored for FFB grading,
including colour-space-based approaches intended to improve the separation of ripeness-
related colour information [15,16].
Infrared (IR) imaging provides additional information beyond the visible colour
spectrum. Unlike RGB imaging, IR imaging captures reflectance characteristics that are
less dependent on visible colour appearance and can therefore provide complementary
information for agricultural quality inspection and fruit maturity evaluation [4,17]. Other
non-visible and active sensing approaches, including thermal imaging and LiDAR-based
reflectance measurements, have similarly demonstrated that maturity-related physical
characteristics of FFB can be detected beyond conventional RGB colour information [12,15].
In the context of oil palm FFB, previous laser-based reflectance studies have shown that
visible and near-infrared wavelengths are sensitive to ripeness-related changes in FFB
reflectance [18]. Recent reviews of FFB ripeness sensing have likewise highlighted the
potential of combining complementary optical and sensing modalities to improve ripeness
assessment [5].
In agricultural image analysis, RGB and NIR data have been combined as four-channel
image inputs to exploit visible colour information and near-infrared spectral responses
for improved deep learning-based interpretation of crop scenes [10]. Similar multimodal
RGB-NIR strategies have also improved fruit detection and agricultural product inspection,
indicating that NIR information provides complementary features for agricultural imaging
tasks [19,20].
During fruit ripening, changes in chlorophyll content, moisture, tissue structure, and
pigment composition influence how near-infrared light is absorbed and scattered by fruit
tissues. These changes can alter the IR reflectance of the fruitlet surface, particularly in
the lower near-infrared region, where ripeness-dependent spectral differences are more
evident than visible colour differences under inconsistent illumination and shadowed

conditions [4,17,18]. Non-destructive optical studies of FFB have also associated spectral

Colour-space transformation may also improve image-based classification.
The
CIEL*a*b* colour space is attractive for agricultural product grading because it separates
lightness from chromatic components, providing a more perceptually uniform represen-
tation of colour [8,13]. Studies using alternative colour representations for FFB maturity
assessment have similarly shown that colour-space transformation can provide useful
ripeness-related information [21,22]. By separating lightness from chromatic information,
CIEL*a*b* may reduce the influence of inconsistent illumination while preserving colour
differences associated with ripening. In the CIEL*a*b* colour space, brightness is repre-
sented by the L* channel, while colour information is separated into the a* (green-to-red)
and b* (blue-to-yellow) channels. As oil palm FFB ripens, the fruitlets gradually change
in colour from darker greenish tones to reddish-orange tones. Separating brightness from
colour information allows the CNN to better distinguish ripeness-related colour variations
while minimizing the influence of uneven lighting conditions. Consequently, CIEL*a*b*
images may provide a more discriminative colour representation than conventional RGB
images for FFB ripeness classification.
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have enabled automatic feature extrac-
tion from image data and have shown strong potential for agricultural classification
tasks [9,23,24]. CNN-based FFB classification has subsequently been explored using mobile
deep learning systems, visual-attention mechanisms, real-time object detection frameworks,
lightweight networks, and transfer-learning approaches, demonstrating the growing poten-
tial of deep learning for automated FFB grading [16,25–28]. However, CNN performance
depends not only on network architecture but also on the quality and representation of
the input image data. For FFB ripeness classification, RGB images alone may limit model
performance because of restricted colour information in dark fruitlets and shadowed re-
gions. Incorporating IR imaging and improved colour-space representation may therefore
provide richer and more robust input features for CNN-based classification.
Previous studies have investigated RGB imaging, multispectral imaging and infrared
sensing, colour-space transformation, traditional machine-learning methods, CNN-based
approaches, and multimodal deep learning strategies for agricultural and oil palm fruit
assessment [2–7,10–12,14–18,21,22,25–29]. These studies demonstrate continuing advances
in colour-texture analysis, alternative colour spaces, non-visible sensing, lightweight deep
learning, real-time detection, and complementary sensing approaches for agricultural
assessment. However, comparative studies that evaluate these image representations using
an identical CNN architecture for FFB ripeness classification remain limited. It therefore
remains difficult to determine whether performance improvements are due to the image
representation itself or differences in network design.
This study investigates the effect of image input representation on CNN-based FFB
ripeness classification. Four input formats were evaluated: RGB, RGB + IR, CIEL*a*b*, and
CIEL*a*b* + IR. The same CNN architecture was used for all models to isolate the effect of
input image representation on ripeness classification performance. Among the evaluated
input formats, the proposed four-channel CIEL*a*b* + IR representation combines comple-
mentary colour and near-infrared information, providing a more comprehensive image
representation for FFB ripeness classification. The main innovations and core contributions
of this work are summarized as follows:

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Image Acquisition Setup
Image acquisition was conducted using two synchronized CMOS cameras (1/2′′
sensor format, 1280 × 1024 native pixel resolution): one dedicated RGB camera (model:
Hikrobot MV-CU013-A0GC) and one monochrome Infrared (IR) camera (model: Hikrobot
MV-CU013-A0GM, equipped with an optical bandpass filter in the range of 700–1100 nm.
This wavelength range includes the red-edge region, where chlorophyll absorption changes
significantly as fruit ripens, providing additional information that cannot be fully captured
by visible RGB images alone [14,19]), both camera manufacturers are based in Hangzhou,
China. The two cameras were mounted side-by-side with a parallel baseline distance of
approximately 8 cm. The acquisition distance measured from the outermost edge of the
camera lenses to the fresh fruit bunch (FFB) was maintained at 3 m, yielding a horizontal
field of view (FOV) of approximately 11.4◦and a focal length of about 32 mm, which
covers typical FFB dimensions of about 45 cm × 36 cm (Figure 1). Dual-camera capture
was controlled simultaneously via custom software built on the camera manufacturer’s
Software Development Kit (SDK) v4.4.1.3, maintaining inter-sensor capture latency below
1.0 s.

2.2. CNN Model Development
CNN models were developed to evaluate the effect of image input representation on
FFB ripeness classification. The evaluated input formats were RGB, CIEL*a*b*, RGB + IR,
and CIEL*a*b* + IR. To ensure a fair comparison, the same CNN classification pipeline
was used for all models; only the input image format was varied. The CNN architecture
comprised convolutional and max-pooling layers for feature extraction, followed by fully
connected layers, a dropout layer, and an output layer for ripeness classification [23].
For the RGB-only model, the original three-channel RGB image was used as input. For
the CIEL*a*b* model, each RGB image was converted into the CIEL*a*b* colour space. The
L* (lightness), a* (green-red), and b* (blue-yellow) channels were then used as the input
to the CNN. For the RGB + IR and CIEL*a*b* + IR models, the corresponding IR image
was combined with the colour-based input to provide additional spectral information.
The CIEL*a*b* + IR model combined the three CIEL*a*b* channels, namely L* (lightness),
a* (green-red), and b* (blue-yellow), with one corresponding IR channel to form a four-
channel input for the CNN. A total of 279 paired (50 Overripe, 65 Ripe, 84 Underripe
and 80 Unripe) RGB-IR images were acquired for the study. Prior to model training, the
ripeness stage of each FFB was determined through visual assessment by two experienced
oil palm fruit graders from the Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) based on visual ripeness
characteristics, including fruitlet colour, fruitlet detachment, overall bunch appearance and
mesocarp color, in accordance with standard harvesting practices.
2.3. Model Architecture
For models combining color and infrared data, early fusion is performed at the input
layer. The 3-channel color tensor (RGB or CIEL*a*b**) and the 1-channel IR tensor are con-
catenated along the channel axis to create a single 4-channel input tensor (4 × 256 × 256).
This fused tensor is fed directly into the first convolutional layer (Conv2D, 4 input channels),
allowing the network to learn joint spatial–spectral features from the first layer onward.
To ensure complete reproducibility, Figure 2 outlines the layer-by-layer architectural
design of the custom RipenessCNN model. The network comprises three feature extraction
convolutional blocks. Each block integrates a 3 × 3 2D Convolutional layer, 2D Batch
Normalization, a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, and a 2 × 2 Max-Pooling
layer with a stride of 2. Spatial feature map dimensions are systematically reduced while
channel capacity increases from 32 to 64 and 128.
Feature maps are aggregated via Adaptive Global Average Pooling into a 128 × 1 × 1
tensor and flattened into a 128-dimensional vector. The classification head consists of a
64-unit fully connected layer regularized by Dropout (p = 0.5) and a final 4-unit linear
output layer.
All models shared the same CNN architecture to ensure a fair comparison among the
evaluated image representations. Using a common architecture allowed any differences in
classification performance to be attributed to the input image representation rather than
variations in network design.
The shared CNN pipeline consisted of convolutional layers, max-pooling layers,
and fully connected layers. The convolutional layers extracted local spatial and textural
features, such as fruitlet colour distribution, bunch surface texture, and ripeness-related
visual patterns. The max-pooling layers reduced the spatial dimensions of the feature maps
while preserving important features. After feature extraction, the learned feature maps
were flattened and passed through fully connected layers. Dropout was applied to reduce
overfitting and improve model generalisation. The final output layer produced the FFB
ripeness classification result.

2.4. Training Configuration
The model was optimized using the Adam algorithm with a base learning rate of
0.001 and trained for a maximum of 100 epochs using a mini-batch size of 64. Dropout
with a probability of 0.5 was applied to dense hidden connections to reduce overfitting,
and two-dimensional mini-batch normalization was used after each convolutional layer.
Early stopping monitored validation loss with a patience of 15 epochs. The dataset was

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Spectral Characteristics of Oil Palm Fruitlets
Figure 3 shows clear spectral differences among unripe, underripe, and ripe fruitlets,
particularly in the lower near-infrared region of approximately 700–780 nm, where sep-
aration among the ripeness stages is evident. This region corresponds to the red-edge
transition, where chlorophyll absorption decreases during ripening. Consequently, differ-
ences in reflectance become more apparent, allowing the ripeness stages to be distinguished
more clearly.
The observed spectral variation is associated with physical changes that occur as
fruit matures, including changes in chlorophyll content, moisture content, tissue structure,
and pigment composition. These changes influence how near-infrared light is absorbed,
scattered, and reflected from the fruitlet surface. Similar reflectance-based sensitivity to oil
palm FFB ripeness has been demonstrated using IR-assisted laser remote sensing, where
selected visible-NIR wavelengths were used to distinguish FFB ripeness categories and
support non-destructive ripeness assessment [19].

The spectral characteristics in Figure 3 provide a physical justification for incorporat-
ing IR imaging into the proposed CNN classification framework. Unlike visible colour,
which is strongly influenced by lighting and shadowing, near-infrared reflectance captures
ripeness-related optical responses that can complement RGB information. By capturing
reflectance information in the wavelength region where ripeness-related differences are
prominent, the IR camera provides additional features that enhance the representation of
FFB characteristics, particularly when combined with CIEL*a*b* colour representation.
3.2. Representative RGB and IR Images
Figure 4 shows representative FFB samples at four ripeness stages: unripe, underripe,
ripe, and overripe. The RGB images show visible colour differences among ripeness stages.
Unripe and underripe bunches generally exhibit darker and less uniformly developed
fruitlet colouration, while ripe and overripe samples show more reddish-orange fruitlets
and more pronounced colour variation across the bunch surface.
The dependence of RGB pixel intensity on pigment-related colour changes has previ-
ously been used to guide physics-informed ripeness classification, indicating that colour
indices can capture ripening trends associated with fruit pigment changes [14]. However,
RGB images may still be affected by shadow, dark fruitlets, specular reflection, and non-
fruit structures. In contrast, the IR images provide complementary information that is not
directly visible in the RGB images. While RGB images mainly represent visible colour and
texture, IR images emphasize reflectance intensity differences across the bunch surface.
These intensity variations may be related to fruitlet surface properties, moisture condition,
physiological changes, or structural differences associated with ripeness development.
Together, the RGB and IR images indicate that FFB ripeness classification can benefit
from both visible colour characteristics and non-visible spectral reflectance features.

3.3. Class Activation Heat-Map Analysis
Figure 5 presents class activation heat maps generated from the trained CNN models
using the four input formats. The heat maps are overlaid on the FFB images to visualize
image regions that contributed most strongly to the classification decision. For the multi-
modal models (RGB + IR and CIEL*a*b* + IR), the activation maps were generated after
feature fusion in the shared CNN backbone, representing the combined contribution of
both colour and infrared information to the final classification. Across the models, activa-
tion regions were concentrated mainly on fruitlet areas rather than the surrounding and
background, indicating that the CNNs learned to focus on biologically relevant ripeness
features, including fruitlet colours, surface textures, and bunch structures.
The RGB-only model mainly attended to visible colour and texture regions on the
bunch surface. However, the attention regions appeared relatively scattered in some
samples, suggesting that RGB information alone may not always provide sufficiently
distinct features for separating adjacent ripeness stages. The CIEL*a*b*-only model showed
attention patterns more strongly associated with colour-discriminative regions, suggesting
that transformation from RGB to CIEL*a*b* helped emphasize chromatic differences related
to ripeness.
For the RGB + IR model, the activation maps indicated that IR information altered the
model focus regions. The model attended not only to visible fruitlet colour areas but also to
regions associated with differences in IR reflectance. The CIEL*a*b* + IR model produced
the most distinct activation over FFB regions, particularly fruitlet areas associated with
ripeness-related colour and reflectance changes. This observation is consistent with the
quantitative results, in which the CIEL*a*b* + IR model achieved the highest weighted-
average precision, recall, and F1-score.
Neither the Color and IR images nor the CNN class activation heatmaps reveal sig-
nificant differences between correctly classified and misclassified samples; both groups
generally follow the trends shown in Figures 4 and 5.

and F1-score were ±0.02, ±0.02, and ±0.02, respectively, compared with ±0.03–0.04 for the
RGB-only model. The lower standard deviations indicate that the proposed CIEL*a*b* + IR
model delivered more consistent performance across different random train–test splits and
was less affected by variations in the training data. This suggests that combining CIEL*a*b*
colour information with IR reflectance produced a more robust feature representation,
enabling the CNN to distinguish ripeness classes more consistently with clearer decision
boundaries. As a result, the model produced more reliable and repeatable predictions than
the other input representations. In contrast, the larger standard deviations observed for
the other models indicate greater variability in classification performance across different
data partitions, suggesting that their extracted features were less effective in separating
neighbouring ripeness classes.
At the class level, IR information substantially improved overripe classification. Over-
ripe recall increased from 0.67 for RGB-only to 0.92 for RGB + IR and 0.95 for CIEL*a*b* + IR,
while the corresponding F1-score increased from 0.77 to 0.90 and 0.95. As the fruit be-
comes overripe, physical changes such as chlorophyll degradation, moisture variation, and
structural changes become more pronounced.
The ripe and unripe classes also benefited from the improved input representations.
For the ripe class, precision increased from 0.75 for RGB-only to 0.91 for CIEL*a*b* + IR,
although recall decreased from 0.87 to 0.78. This indicates that the CIEL*a*b* + IR model was

more conservative in predicting the ripe class, reducing false-positive ripe classifications
while missing some true ripe samples. For the unripe class, F1-score improved from 0.73
for RGB-only to 0.79 for CIEL*a*b* + IR, showing improved balance between precision
and recall.
The underripe class remained the most challenging category across all models, primar-
ily due to its high rate of false positives. Underripe precision was consistently lower than
that of the other classes, ranging from 0.65 to 0.68. This is consistent with the transitional
nature of underripe FFB, which lies between unripe and ripe conditions. Fruitlets within
the same bunch may show uneven ripeness development, resulting in mixed visual and
spectral characteristics. Consequently, underripe bunches may share features with both
unripe and ripe classes, making them more difficult to classify precisely.
Although underripe recall was relatively high across models, ranging from 0.82 to 0.83,
the lower precision indicates that some samples from other ripeness classes were incorrectly
predicted as underripe. This suggests that the models detected many true underripe
samples but had difficulty defining a precise decision boundary for this transitional class.
Overall, the results confirm that both IR information and CIEL*a*b* colour repre-
sentation affect CNN classification performance. CIEL*a*b* alone produced only a small
improvement over RGB alone, whereas the greatest performance gain was achieved when
CIEL*a*b* was combined with IR information. These findings support the proposed four-
channel CIEL*a*b* + IR image input as the best-performing solution among the evaluated
input formats for CNN-based FFB ripeness classification. Nevertheless, underripe FFB
remains a major classification challenge because of its transitional and heterogeneous
ripeness characteristics.

3.6. Statistically Significant Analysis
To determine whether the observed performance improvement of the proposed
CIEL∗a∗b∗+ IR representation over the baseline RGB model is statistically meaningful,
a paired statistical significance test was conducted across the 10 randomized evaluation
splits. Because both model variants were evaluated on the exact same test splits in each run,
the weighted average Precision scores yielded 10 naturally paired observations (n = 10). A

Shapiro–Wilk test performed on the paired differences (CIEL∗a∗b∗+ IR −RGB) confirmed
a normal distribution (W = 0.9471, pSW = 0.6349 > 0.05), where W is Shapiro–Wilk test
statistic and pSW is Shapiro–Wilk p-value. Consequently, a two-tailed paired t-test was
executed, revealing a statistically significant improvement in weighted average precision
for the CIEL∗a∗b∗+ IR model (t(9) = 2.5634, p = 0.0305 < 0.05), with a mean positive
difference of 0.0420 ± 0.0518 (+4.2). This confirms that the gain achieved by integrating
near-infrared images is statistically significant and outweighs evaluation variance across
random dataset splits.
3.7. Computational Efficiency and Training Duration
To evaluate the computational overhead of different models, the average training exe-
cution time across the 10 randomized split runs was recorded for each model representation.
The baseline RGB model required an average training time of 52.8 ± 13.7 min. In compari-
son, transforming the color space to CIEL∗a∗b∗reduced the average training duration to
42.4 ± 5.5 min. Interestingly, integrating the single-channel Near-Infrared (IR) reflectance
layer resulted in even faster convergence across training epochs: the RGB + IR model
averaged 38.8 ± 7.6 min, while the proposed CIEL∗a∗b∗+ IR representation achieved the
shortest overall training duration at 35.3 ± 6.3 min. Notably, the addition of a 4th input
channel did not impose an overall computational time penalty. Rather, this empirical
reduction in training duration suggests that incorporating IR information may enhance
feature discriminability at the input layer, potentially aiding optimization and allowing
the network to reach convergence in fewer epochs. Overall, the proposed CIEL∗a∗b∗+ IR
model achieved a 33.1% reduction in average training time compared to the baseline RGB
model, alongside improved execution stability as evidenced by the reduced standard
deviation across split runs.
3.8. Limitations and Future Work
While this study demonstrates a successful proof-of-concept for 4-channel input fusion
in oil palm fruit ripeness classification, several practical constraints and limitations should
be considered. First, the primary constraint of this study stems from dataset volume and en-
vironmental scope. The current evaluation was conducted on a dataset of 279 paired image
sets collected from a localized plantation site. While repeated stratified/random 80:20 train–
test split stratification and data augmentation were implemented to prevent overfitting and
assess statistical stability across dataset splits, a substantially larger dataset encompassing
diverse oil palm cultivars, multiple harvesting locations, and varying seasonal conditions
is necessary to establish broader operational generalizability.
Furthermore, the experimental setup evaluated single static FFB against relatively con-
trolled background conditions under natural afternoon sunlight. In real-world plantation
environments, automated grading platforms must contend with multi-objective concur-
rency, dynamic leaf canopy shading, harsh sunlight glare, and severe physical fruitlet
occlusion. Additionally, from a hardware deployment perspective, the current dual-camera
setup relies on two separate physical sensors positioned side-by-side. Maintaining channel
pixel alignment across varying working distances requires manual calibration and static
capture protocols. Translating this methodology to mobile field-grading devices or au-
tonomous harvesting machinery will necessitate compact multispectral sensor integration,
optical co-axial beam-splitters, or automated real-time image registration algorithms to
resolve dynamic parallax.
To address these limitations, future research will focus on expanding data acquisition
across dynamic plantation environments and unconstrained field conditions. Upcoming
work will benchmark the lightweight custom network against modern Vision Transform-

as mesocarp oil content extraction, to further refine quality assessment and facilitate end-
to-end integration with automated harvesting systems.
4. Conclusions
This study evaluated RGB, RGB + IR, CIEL*a*b*, and CIEL*a*b* + IR image repre-
sentations for CNN-based FFB ripeness classification using a common model architecture.
The CIEL*a*b* + IR model achieved the best overall performance, confirming that the
proposed four-channel CIEL*a*b*+ IR representation is the most effective input format
among the evaluated models. The improvement is attributed to the complementary roles
of CIEL*a*b* colour-space transformation and IR reflectance imaging: CIEL*a*b* provides
a more discriminative colour representation with reduced dependence on brightness, while
IR reflectance captures ripeness-related physical changes that are not fully represented by
visible colour information alone. Although the proposed representation improved overall
classification performance, underripe FFB remained the most challenging class, where it
produced the highest rate of false positives because of its transitional and heterogeneous
ripeness characteristics.
