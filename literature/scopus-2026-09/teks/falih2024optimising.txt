 
 
INTRODUCTION 
 
The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations 
has reported that grape production in Turkey has presented 
global and annual growths (FAO, 2022). This nation was the 
sixth-largest producer of grapes globally in 2020, with a total 
production of 4.2 million tonnes (or 5.46% of the global 
production rate). Turkey was also the fifth among the largest 
wine-producing regions globally, demonstrating a notable 
growth rate of 5.77% (FAO, 2022). Even though Turkey 
dominates the global sultana (dry grape) export industry with 
a significant market share of 31.3%, the untapped possibilities 
for exporting table and wine grapes remain largely 
unexplored. This country also highlights a negative wine trade 
balance, representing only a 0.02% share of the global wine 
market. The outcome can be attributed to the 1.5% 
contribution of the country to the total global grape exports 
(ITC, 2021).

Grapes are a highly significant fruit with the highest production rates, which are consistently increasing annually. Although 
traditional methods are employed in agriculture for spraying, harvesting, and estimating yield based on workers, these 
techniques are time-consuming, costly, and inaccurate. Hence, alternative technological options are necessary to address these 
challenges. This study proposed a method for autonomously detecting and counting visible grapes using a Red, Green and Blue 
(RGB) sensor in a robotic system for two datasets (grape size and colour). The k-means clustering was utilised to differentiate 
grape pixels from the input image, and the required cluster was automatically selected. This proposed model also incorporated 
Histogram-Oriented Gradients (HOG) to compute shape information as a feature. The classifier was then trained to identify 
grape berries in each window by carefully examining the feature shape by sliding an auto-sized window over a segmented 
image. Consequently, grape counting yielded an average accuracy of 83%, with red grapes achieving 92% accuracy and white 
grapes 76%. On the other hand, bunch counting showed higher accuracy, averaging at 94%, with red grapes at 96% accuracy 
and white grapes at 90%. The reliability of the method in detecting grapes and grape bunches regardless of colour is underlined 
by these results, which also accentuates its applicability in various counting tasks. 
Keywords: k-means clustering, histograms-oriented gradients, grape bunch segmentation, grape berry counting, sliding 
window technique.

Related studies: This section overviews several grape berry 
detection-related studies. A study by Al-Saffar detected silent 
points using the Fast Radial Symmetry Transform (FRST) to 
recognise multi-coloured grapes and bunches (Al-Saffar et 
al., 2022). The study employed various classifier types 
Support Vector Machine (SVM), artificial neural network 
(ANN), and convolution neural network (CNN) based on two 
feature types HOG, local binary pattern (LBP), and a 
combination of both. Consequently, the combined LBP and 
HOG features with the ANN classifier provided the best 
results. The density-based spatial clustering of applications 
with noise (DBSCAN) technique was also used as the final 
stage for grape bunch selection and automatic close bunch 
separation. 
Another study by Al-Saffar applied a technique for detecting 
white and red grapes from image segmentation using k-means 
clustering and auto-channel selection (Al-saffar et al., 2020; 
Wang et al., 2017). The study applied the FRST to the 
segmented image to detect the grape berries, indicating that 
white grapes produced a higher accuracy than red grapes. 
Meanwhile, Liu developed a rapid and robust calibration-free 
technique for counting wine grapes berries to facilitate yield 
estimation (Liu et al., 2020). The method was evaluated on 
529 images collected in the field from different vineyards at 
varying maturity stages. This method achieved an accuracy of 
approximately 89% per bunch. 
A study by Buayai described an end-to-end berry-counting 
method based on a deep neural network (DNN) (Buayai et al., 
2021). The study initially employed a unique data 
augmentation approach to simulate thinning. A location-
sensitive object identification model was then used to 
determine the number of berries. Finally, a collection of 
features and extraction techniques were utilised to detect 
berries, and the number of berries in a bunch was obtained 
and recognised on a single 2D image. Alternatively, Luo 
presented a grape berry detection technique based on edge 
image processing and geometric morphology (Luo et al., 
2021). The study applied corner detection and edge contour 
search algorithms to obtain the ideal contour segment. 
Consequently, the concave point location of the berry edge 
contour could be extracted using the Canny method. This 
finding highlighted that their suggested approach detected the 
concave points of the edge contours of various varieties of 
grapes with an average accuracy of 87.76%. 
Another study by Fernández used a k-means technique to 
perform segmentation tasks on RGB images, classifying stem, 
leaves, background, and grape branches (Fernández et al., 
2013). This process was conducted to develop a robot capable 
of harvesting, spraying, and estimating yield. Likewise, Diago 
applied grayscale conversion to RGB images and utilised 
canny edge with circle detections using the Hough transform 
on filtered images to detect the grape berry borders (Diago et 
al., 2015). The algorithm determined the weight of each grape 
bunch and the quantity of grape berries in each bunch. 
Similarly, Font proposed a yield estimation strategy using 
image segmentation with artificial lighting in high-resolution 
images (Font et al., 2015). The segmentation used two colour 
spaces: RGB and hue, saturation, and value (HSV). 
Consequently, the hue layer exhibited superior performance 
in image segmentation. The number of pixels corresponding 
to the grape cluster was computed and directly converted to a 
yield estimate. This cluster area of grapes was converted into 
a volume using a revolution solid, which was subsequently 
transformed into an estimate. 
A study by Reis presented a method for detecting the centre 
of the grape bunch in colour images and stems involving 
white and red grape berries under natural lighting (Reis et al., 
2012). This process was conducted using colour information 
and morphological operations. Meanwhile, Herrero-Huerta 
accomplished yield calculation from 3D bunches and the 
reconstruction of each bunch (Herrero-Huerta et al., 2015). 
The weight was determined based on the size of each berry 
and the total quantity of berries in each bunch. This approach 
employed images from five different angles to reconstruct the 
arrangement of berries in the bunch. Liu also presented a 
similar approach (Liu et al., 2015). 
Another study by Škrabánek and Runarsson employed a 
sliding window algorithm to identify white berries in a yield 
row image (Škrabánek and Runarsson, 2015). Numerous 
classification strategies involving combining SVM-linear and 
SVM-radial basis function (RBF) were utilised in the study, 
such as HOG and pixel intensities (PI). Consequently, the 
combination of SVM-RBF and HOG with LBP introduced

Dataset: The freely accessible Iceland dataset was established 
by Runarsson (Škrabánek and Runarsson, 2015). This 
information functioned as a training set (T0-3) and consisted 
of 576 full-colour RGB images. Each image possessed a 
dimension of (40 × 40 × 3 Pixel) and presented only white 
grapes. Approximately 288 and 288 images were positive and 
negative, respectively.

Several advantages are presented with pre-processing RGB 
images for object detection, such as histogram equalisation 
(HE) to equalise the colour distribution and colour 
normalisation to reduce the effects of lighting conditions, 
shadows, and colour bias (Dey et al., 2013). These processes 
can improve the accuracy and reliability of object detection 
algorithms. 
Another advantage of pre-processing RGB images is noise 
reduction. RGB images can be affected by noise caused by 
various factors, such as camera sensors, compression, or 
transmission artefacts. Hence, the pre-processing strategy can 
aid in removing or reducing this noise to improve the accuracy 
of object detection algorithms. Image resizing is another 
advantage of pre-processing RGB images. Given that large 
images can be computationally expensive to process, resizing 
them to a smaller size can accelerate the object detection 
process. Therefore, this process can resize the images while 
preserving important details and features. 
Background and foreground segmentation: This study 
employed the k-means clustering algorithm to extract the 
grape bunches from the background (Dhanachandra et al., 
2015). The cluster was specified using Euclidean distance 
(Ali et al., 2022). Meanwhile, the number of clusters (k) was 
determined for two cluster types: white and red grapes. The 
cluster centres with the highest red and lowest blue channels 
were selected for red and white grapes, respectively. 
Pattern recognition: Pattern recognition is crucial to 
computer vision and machine learning. This process identifies 
and classifies objects in images or videos. Hence, object 
detection is an essential application of pattern recognition that 
locates and classifies objects within images or video frames. 
For example, machine-learning-based methods involve 
training a classifier (SVM, weak linear, and Random Forest) 
to distinguish between positive and negative samples using 
handcrafted features (HOG and LBP). This process then 
applies a sliding detection window to the input image and 
categorises each region as either a positive or negative object. 
Generally, this procedure is effective for detecting objects 
with different appearances (Dalal and Triggs, 2005). This 
study employed a cascaded object detector for classifying

Cascaded object detector: The cascade architecture was 
developed by Viola and Jones (Viola and Jones, 2001). This 
architecture trained object detectors to recognise objects with 
diverse properties and aspect ratios. The trained detectors 
were employed to identify objects by systematically moving 
a window across the image. Cascaded detectors consist of c 
weak object classifiers that are arranged sequentially. 
Therefore, the output of one classifier was transmitted to the 
next classifier as supplementary data. Each linear classifier 
was considered a stage, and the final object detector 
integrated these stages with a sliding window detector (see 
Fig. 2). 
 
Figure 2. The cascading object detection model applied to 
grape recognition. 
 
A cascade object detector with 10 stages was trained using 
HOG features. The cascaded classifiers were provided with 
positive images containing the object to be recognised and 
negative images not containing the object as inputs. A sliding 
window search was performed over the segmented images. 
Each window was categorised as positive or negative based 
on window information. Fig. 2 illustrates the machine-
learning approach for detecting grape berries in the 
segmented image (Altaei, 2023). 
The learned HOG pattern was correlated with the HOG motif 
of the test image to detect the rear of grape berries. Several 
parameters must also be chosen optimally for training an 
efficient cascade object detector. These parameters are as 
follows: 
1. Number of stages: The number of stages used depends on 
the number of positive and negative samples used for 
training. The training terminates if the number of positive 
or negative samples is insufficient to fit the next stage. 
2. False positive rate (FPR): A lower FPR presents few false 
detections. 
3. True positive rate (TPR): The minimum rate for each 
stage is chosen to vote as a positive. 
4. Feature: The HOG features are chosen for grape berry 
detection due to their performance in capturing the object 
orientation. Table 1 summarises the parameter values 
used in training the cascade object detector.

presented by Ester could cluster them as one grape bunch (see 
Fig. 3(a)) (Ester et al., 1996). The k-means method introduced 
by Likas was then employed to re-cluster the berry 
information for increasing yield estimation performance and 
minimising the error of the classifier (Likas et al., 2003). 
Conversely, the number of clusters must be determined 
throughout this process.

RESULTS 
 
This study applied the proposed approach to two grape types: 
white and red. Table 3 summarises the findings of this study, 
in which the actual number of bunches and fully visible 
berries are counted manually and compared. The average 
accuracy values with tolerance for the visual red and white 
grape berries detection process were 92.82% ± 0.961% and 
76.71% ± 1.994%, respectively. This outcome suggested that 
the accuracy of the red grapes was superior to the white grape 
because a significant portion of the grape pixels in the image 
shared a similar colour with the leaf and background. 
Furthermore, variable illumination led to misclassification in 
the segmentation part. In contrast, a significant difference in 
the pixel values of the berries was observed between the leaf 
and the background. 
The grape bunches were detected using the DBSCAN 
technique, which relied on the characteristics of the grape 
berries. This technique improved the detection results by 
removing noise and selecting pre-bunches. The neighbouring 
grape bunches were also separated to enhance the accuracy of 
the grape bunch counting process. Despite the illumination 
was not homogeneous, the approach achieved an average 
accuracy above 83%. The performance remained unaffected 
by the colour of the grapes as the outcome was determined 
only by the HOG features (circular shape characteristics). 
This study investigated the grape pixel recognition 
performance (see Table 4). The data indicated an average 
performance of 93.763%. Nonetheless, this significant 
outcome was partially due to many TN pixel detections 
(rather than only correctly labelled pixels) and a high aspect 
ratio. Consequently, the proposed method achieved an 
average positive predictive value (PPV) of 94.99% across 
three datasets. Another analysis also determined that the FP 
pixels were primarily concentrated in nearby clusters. Even 
though the FP rate was high, the FP detections did not 
significantly impact the findings of grape bunch testing. 
The average recall for various datasets was 97.92%, which 
could also be the detection rate. This ratio considered the total 
number of pixels within a grape cluster and the number of FP 
pixels. Thus, the grape recognition performance could be 
optimised by training the classifier with enough images 
(datasets).

encompassing numerous vineyards and grape varietals proved

Only white grapes captured in natural light at a given distance

identification could be used in multiple vineyard management 
tasks. For example, this technique could be an alternative 
application for removing background or leaves. This process 
could enable farm owners to establish a uniform leaf removal 
process across the industry.

DISCUSSION 
 
This study highlighted the different accuracy levels between 
red and white grapes due to colour and illumination 
differences in image processing research involving grape 
analysis. The red grapes exhibited superior accuracy due to 
their significant colour contrast. Likewise, DBSCAN 
enhanced grape bunch recognition by minimising noise and 
obtaining over 83% accuracy, regardless of illumination 
conditions or grape colour. Although the pixel identification 
computed a high overall performance (93.763%), several TNs 
influenced the findings. The clustered FPs also produced 
minimal impact on the grape bunch testing. 
Despite several challenges (insufficient datasets for optimal 
training), further applications for vineyard management 
beyond grape analysis were discovered. The approach 
successfully managed challenges, progressed through the 
detection phases, and effectively distinguished between grape 
and non-grape pixels, as evidenced by the visual findings. 
This outcome suggested that the proposed method helped 
count and yield estimation tasks in wineries. 
 
Conclusion and future studies: This study successfully 
presented grape bunch detection and berry recognition from 
full-colour images. The process involved two different berry 
colours (red and white), and k-means clustering was used for 
image segmentation to separate the grapes from the 
background. The HOG features of a sliding window were also 
categorised with a cascaded object detector consisting of 10 
series classifiers to identify berries. Consequently, the 
average accuracy values of berry recognition and grape bunch 
detection were 83.73% and 94.66% for the test images, 
respectively. 
The duration required was contingent upon the size of the 
image or image resolution and the maximum and minimum 
window sizes of the detector. Therefore, this method could be 
adopted for various real-time applications. For example, 
agriculture robots can be used for yield estimation, spraying, 
and harvesting. This technique can reduce the need for human 
labour, financial resources, and the number of workers 
required. Additionally, this method can improve the speed 
and accuracy of grape estimation. 
Cascade classifiers employ a series of simpler classifiers to 
quickly identify non-grape areas in an image, which are often 
faster than other classifiers. This characteristic enabled 
cascade classifiers to concentrate on potential grape berry 
regions and conduct a more thorough analysis of those areas. 
Furthermore, this method could substantially decrease the 
total computational workload needed for grape berry 
detection and enhance the speed of the operation. Therefore,
