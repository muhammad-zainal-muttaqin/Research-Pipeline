reconstruction

ABSTRACT. Blueberry cluster compactness, maturity, berry number, and berry size are four important traits associated 
with harvestability and yield. In this study, a deep learning instance segmentation model Mask R-CNN was used to 
segment individual blueberries for four cultivars (“Emerald”, “Farthing”, “Meadowlark”, and “Star”) with mature 
properties in 2D images with an iterative annotation strategy. The mean average precision for the test dataset was 71.6% 
under a 0.5 intersection over union (IOU) threshold, and the corresponding mask accuracy was 90.4%. Furthermore, an 
image-capturing system was developed to scan fresh blueberry clusters and photogrammetric method was used for 3D 
reconstruction.  To obtain 3D-2D mask correspondence, 3D faces were projected to 2D masks. The berry number, cluster 
maturity, and compactness were extracted from both 2D images and 3D point clouds, while berry size was estimated from 
3D point clouds using sphere fitting. The accuracy of the counting number from 3D point clouds was above 97%. ANOVA 
of the traits was conducted both in 2D and 3D. The analysis of variance (ANOVA) for the four cultivars showed consistent 
results in maturity of 2D and 3D, while the berry number and compactness showed inconsistent results. This study 
developed an effective method for extracting 2D and 3D blueberry traits for blueberry development monitoring, which 
will help breeders, growers, and packers to objectively evaluate fruit traits related to harvestability and yield.   
Keywords. Blueberry traits, Mask R-CNN, 2D-3D projection

V. darrowi Camp, and other Vaccinium species indigenous to the Southeastern United States) are adapted to areas with mild 
winters.  SHB blueberry production in tropical and sub-tropical environments has become an economically important 
industry in Florida, North Carolina, and Georgia in the Southeastern United States, as well in California, Mexico, Peru,

Australia, northern Chile, and North Africa.  For blueberries, there is a wide variation in fruit maturation among clusters, 
fruit within a cluster, and among branches of the bush.  This means that mature fruit must be harvested selectively after each 
fruit develop a blue color. Therefore, evaluating the maturity of individual blueberry fruits can provide valuable information 
for determining the best harvesting time. Number counting and cluster tightness are also important in the blueberry industry 
and can be used to estimate yield and harvesting strategy. Thus, a reliable method for characterizing these three traits 
(compactness, maturity, and count) and presenting analysis of them would be a valuable tool for blueberry breeders as well 
for blueberry growers.   
Previous literature has shown that although the maturity of blueberry fruits is defined by their color, maturity definitions 
have differed. Gilbert, Schwieterman, Colquhoun, Clark, and Olmstead (2013) classified blueberry fruits into four different 
developmental stages: (A) green, (B) breaker, (C) red, and (D) blue. El Agamy, Aly, and Biggs (1982) defined more stages 
according to fruit color: a) green-mature, b) green+25% pink, c) 50% pink, d) 75% pink, e) pink, f) bluish pink (pink +50% 
blue), g) 75% blue, h) blue, and i) blue-black, in which the maturity stage h) is for commercial picking and stage i) is beyond 
commercial handling. Kalt, McRae, and Hamilton (1995) studied the relationship between surface color and other maturity 
indices (size, sugar, acid, and anthocyanin contents), and their results showed that sugar content was highly correlated with 
the surface color, which indicates that the surface color can represent the berry’s maturity. Yang, Lee, and Gader (2014) 
presented the spectral differences among young fruits (green color), intermediate fruits (red color), and mature fruits (dark 
blue/purple).  In all of these studies, the fruit maturity index was assigned to individual blueberries. For this paper, individual 
berry maturity was classified, and then the maturity ratio of the whole branch was calculated. 
Many studies have used the International Organization of Vine and Wine (OIV) descriptor (International Organisation of, 
Wine, & must analysis, 2015) to classify the  differences among grape cluster compactness, which is,  nevertheless, a 
subjective method for addressing all aspects of grape production. There are also many objective methods to quantify 
compactness. Tello and Forneck (2018) used a quantitative grape compactness index (CI13), which is calculated as the ratio 
of the berry volume over the square of rachis length. Cubero et al. (2015) defined the ratio of the square of the berry perimeter 
over the berry area of the grape bunch for bunch compactness and found that a smaller perimeter with the same area would 
be more compact. The perimeters and areas of grape bunches were extracted from 2D images. Rist et al. (2018) compared 
the correlation of 5 different factors with the OIV grape compactness descriptor, including (a) the ratio of the number of 
berries and grape length, (b) the ratio of total berry volume and the sum of the grape length and grape width, (c) the ratio of 
berry volume and grape length, (d) the difference of convex hull volume and total berry volume, and (e) the ratio of convex 
hull volume and the sum of grape length and grape width.  They found that the order from highest to lowest correlation was 
(b), (c), (a), (e) and (d). All the parameters in these five factors were extracted from the 3D grape bunch point cloud using a 
3D scanner. Hed, Ngugi, and Travis (2009) used the number of berries per centimeter of rachis as a grape compactness 
index. However, these methods did not provide high throughput and required considerable manual operation. 
Various traditional methods have been used for berry detection and classification. One popular method has been to use 
the spectral properties of blueberries. Yang, Lee, and Williamson (2012) measured spectral reflectance of blueberry leaves, 
mature fruits, near-mature fruits, near young mature fruits, and young fruits for different cultivars. Classification models 
were established, and the model achieved more than a 98% accuracy for all leaves and fruits. However, the application of 
this method is too limited, as it only can be used for indoor research with artificial lighting and under a cover that blocks out 
ambient light to distinguish leaves and different maturities of blueberries. One of our goals was to segment individual 
blueberries and assess individual fruit maturity and clusters maturity indices when capturing RGB (red, green, and blue) 
images. 
As a deep learning technique, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown great promise for image classification, 
object detection, and segmentation (LeCun, Bengio, & Hinton, 2015). One successful application of CNN has been to use 
semantic segmentation to classify objects on images at the pixel level(Papandreou, Chen, Murphy, & Yuille, 2015). Since 
Papandreou et al. (2015) proposed semantic image segmentation using CNN and achieved a mean intersection over union  
(mIOU) of over 70% on PASCAL Visual Object Classes (VOC) 2012 dataset, many other models have been developed. 
Long, Shelhamer, and Darrell (2015) proposed a fully convolutional network (FCN), whose output images have the same 
resolution as input images while achieving a mIOU of 62.2% on a PASCAL VOC dataset. FCNs were then improved in 
some advanced models. U-Net was developed based on an FCN with data augmentation, achieving a mIOU of over 92% on 
the biomedical dataset with very few training images (Ronneberger, Fischer, & Brox, 2015). However, U-Net segments all 
objects as a union mask, while Mask R-CNN can predict objects as individual masks. Mask R-CNN combines Faster R-
CNN  and FCN and can achieve a higher accuracy than when only using FCN (He, Gkioxari, Dollár, & Girshick, 2017). 
DeepLab was developed to deal with multiple sizes of objects without reducing the resolution and produced a mIOU of 
79.7% on a PASCAL VOC-2012 (Chen, Papandreou, Kokkinos, Murphy, & Yuille, 2017). DeepLabv3 and DeepLabv3+  
were developed by improving DeepLab and achieved a mIOU of 85.7% and 89% on PASCAL VOC-2012, respectively 
(Chen et al., 2017; Chen, Zhu, Papandreou, Schroff, & Adam, 2018). FastFCN proposed Joint Pyramid Up-sampling (JPU), 
which can extract high resolution feature maps 3 times faster without performance losses (Wu, Zhang, Huang, Liang, & Yu, 
2019). Semantic segmentation for videos was also developed and achieved a mIOU of 83.5% on a Cityscapes dataset and 
82.9% on the Cambridge-driving Labeled Video Database (CamVid) dataset (Zhu et al., 2019).  For this paper, we used

Mask R-CNN to segment individual berries given its superior performance. 
Some semantic segmentation models have been used on berry segmentation. Milella, Marani, Petitti, and Reina (2019) 
used four different CNNs to segment grape bunches, leaves, wood (grapevine trunks, cordons and canes), and vineyard grape 
poles from the background in the RGB images and concluded that using an VGG19 (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014) network 
had the best performance with an accuracy of 91.52%. Santos, Souza, Santos, and Avila (2019) compared Mask R-CNN, 
YOLOv2, and YOLOv3 to detect and segment grape bunches in the field (He et al., 2017; Redmon, Divvala, Girshick, & 
Farhadi, 2015; Redmon & Farhadi, 2018). However, both studies segmented the whole grape bunch but not the individual 
berry segmentation to count berries. To detect and segment individual grapes, Zabawa et al. (2019) used an FCN (Long et 
al., 2015) to detect the edges of a single berry from color images to obtain a single grape berry. Some studies have been 
conducted on individual grape detection and segmentation using deep learning (Grimm et al., 2019; Zabawa et al., 2019), 
but no study has been done on blueberries that have significantly different growth patterns and fruit characteristics from 
grapes. Compared to grape detection, blueberry detection is more challenging because the fruits spread throughout the 
branches and the maturity of fruits in one branch can differ even in one cluster. 
The main goal of this study was to segment individual blueberries from both 2D and 3D and, further, to calculate 
harvestability and yield traits. Specific objectives were to: 
1) Segment individual berries using Mask R-CNN from 2D images with an iterative annotation method and evaluate 
the classification and segmentation performance;  
2) Segment individual berries from 3D point clouds based on the segmented berry masks on 2D images;  
3) Extract blueberry harvestability and yield traits from 2D and 3D data respectively;  
4) Conduct statistical analysis of the traits for four cultivars. 
Methods and Materials 
Data collection 
The data were collected in Alma, Georgia, in the United States in April of 2019 (Figure 1). There were two kinds of data 
collected: 2D images, including the 2D images for berry segmentation model training, and 2D images for trait extraction for 
the four cultivars (“Emerald”, “Farthing”, “Meadowlark”, and “Star”). The 2D images for berry segmentation were captured 
randomly with or without a cardboard background. The 2D images for trait extraction included 26 samples for each cultivar 
with 5 views for each sample. Each sample was rotated 360 degrees and 5 selected views were captured with a cardboard 
background. A cardboard background was used to remove the interference of blueberries from other clusters. Another kind 
of data collected was fresh blueberry clusters. 10 clusters for each cultivar were cut and stored in a cooler in the transportation 
to the lab in Athens, Georgia, in the United States. 
 
 
Figure 1. Data collection for 2D images and 3D fresh blueberry cluster of four cultivars. 
Mask R-CNN based blueberry segmentation and traits extraction pipeline 
We used Mask R-CNN to detect and segment every individual blueberry in the 2D images. Annotation was the first and 
important step in this pipeline (Figure 2) that labelled all of the berries in the training images. The boundaries of all berries 
were to be circled with a polygon line and classified as either a mature berry or an immature berry. However, it was time 
consuming and took about 10 minutes to annotate one image while there were a total of 669 images for annotation.  Thus, 
an iterative annotation method was developed. Firstly, 137 images were manually annotated for training. Then, an initial 
training model was obtained and used to predict and segment the blueberries in the rest images. Manual correction was 

ASABE 2020 Annual International Meeting 
Page 4 
conducted on those segmentation results because some were inaccurate. This saved us two thirds of the annotation time. The 
training configuration is shown in the table in Figure 2. The 669 images were sorted into a training dataset (524 images) and 
a validation dataset (145 images). The “resnet101” was selected as the backbone for the whole architecture. A Tesla V100 
GPU card with 16G memory was used for training because it could process two images at the same time, so the batch size 
was 2 and the images were resized into a square of 1024 × 1024 pixels. A pre-trained model COCO was used as the initial 
weight. The training rate was 0.001 with a 0.9 learning momentum. Rotating, multiplying, and blurring were used to augment 
datasets. After 600 epochs of training, a blueberry detection and segmentation model was obtained. This model was used to 
predict the 520 images of the four cultivars (26 samples per cultivar and 5 views per sample). Because all of the mature and 
immature berries were recognized, the total number of berries in the image was counted, and the maturity was calculated as 
the ratio of the mature berry number and total berry number. According to the segmented berries, a minimum bounding box 
(MBB) was determined, which represents the smallest box used to surround all detected berries. Compactness was defined 
as the ratio of the area of all berries and the MBB. The average results (number, maturity, and compactness) of 5 views were 
used to represent the results for each sample. 
 
 
Figure 2. Mask R-CNN based blueberry segmentation and trait extraction. 
Image capturing system for 3D reconstruction 
The removed fresh blueberry clusters were stored in a refrigerator for two days. In those two days, an image capturing 
system was developed to capture the images of fresh blueberry clusters for 3D reconstruction (Figure 3A). Every blueberry 
cluster was fixed on a step motor with a size reference. Three digital cameras (X-A10, Fujifilm, Japan) were fixed and 
adjusted on three different positions such that the front view, top view and bottom view of the blueberry cluster were 
captured. The three cameras and the step motor were controlled by a controller, so that the step motor rotated 9 degrees each 
time and remained stationary for 1.5 seconds. Then, the three cameras were triggered to capture three simultaneous views. 
After a full revolution, the cameras and the step motor were stopped and waited for the next blueberry cluster. In this way, 
each cluster were captured in 120 images. Agisoft Metashape software was used for the 3D reconstruction, which was time 
consuming (several hours for one cluster). To improve efficiency, the background of the captured images was removed using 
a color threshold, retained only for blueberry cluster mask and size reference. These mask images then were used for 3D 
reconstruction, and the point clouds generated were meshed and textured. 
2D-3D projection for individual 3D blueberry segmentation and traits extraction 
The trained Mask R-CNN model was used to segment the blueberries from the 120 images for each blueberry cluster. 
Since every 3D point correlated to one pixel in the 2D images, every 2D mask obtained in the 2D images corresponded to a 
small patch on the 3D point clouds. However, there were many 2D masks for the same blueberry because multiple images 
contained the same blueberry. To classify the masks belonging to the same berry, overlaps were calculated by all of the 
projected 3D patches. If the overlap exceeded 30%, the two masks were determined to be the same berry. In this way, 2D 
masks belonging to the same berry were classified and the 3D berry was segmented from the whole cluster. For each 
segmented 3D blueberry, each patch was either mature or immature, which was same with every corresponding 2D mask. If 

ASABE 2020 Annual International Meeting 
Page 5 
the number of mature patches was larger than the number of immature patches, this blueberry was considered as a mature 
berry; otherwise, the berry would be considered as an immature berry. The ratio of mature berry number and total berry 
number was calculated as maturity for this blueberry cluster. A 3D minimum bounding box was generated for each 3D 
blueberry cluster. The ratio of blueberry volume and minimum bound box volume was defined as compactness. For each 
segmented 3D blueberry, sphere fitting was used to estimate the berry volume. The actual berry volumes were obtained with 
the size reference. For each blueberry cluster, the ground truth of immature berry number and mature berry number was 
manually counted and the total volume of the blueberry clusters was measured by the method of water displacement.

Results and Discussion 
Trained model performance evaluation 
55 test images were used to evaluate the training model performance (Figure 4). IOU was the intersection over union of the 
detected object and the ground truth. A higher IOU threshold meant a higher requirement for detection. Recall was the true 
positive rate, which is the ratio of true detected blueberries and all blueberries’ ground truth. Precision was the ratio of true 
detected blueberries over all detected blueberries. The mean average precision (mAP) was calculated as the mean precision 
value across all recall values. A different IOU threshold resulted in a different mAP. It can be seen that when the IOU 
threshold was 0.5, the mAP was 0.72. However, when the IOU threshold was 0.95, the mAP was close to 0, which means 
almost all segmented blueberries could not overlap 95% of the ground truth. In most studies, 0.5 is selected as IOU threshold. 
Under a 0.5 IOU threshold, the mask accuracy was 90.4%.

Detection and segmentation results on 2D images 
Overall, individual blueberry detection and segmentation results were good. In Figure 5, (a) represents an outdoor blueberry 
image with a cardboard background and its prediction. There were 8 immature berries and 6 mature berries in the image, 
while the model detected all these berries with correct classes. In Figure 5(b), an indoor blueberry image with a wall 
background was detected, and most blueberries were segmented and classified unless there was a small partial berry that 
was detected with another larger berry. Some errors occurred in the detection and segmentation, while the main types of 
errors were one berry detected as two, missed detection, two berries detected as one, and partial detection, as illustrated in 
Figure 5(c). For one berry detected as two, the example shows a small part of a berry wrongly detected as an individual 
berry and another part of this berry detected as a separate berry. For missed detection, some berries are occluded by the stem, 
leaves, and other berries, and these berries were difficult to detect even by human eyes. When two berries were not separated 
clearly and one berry was partially covered by another berry, they were more likely detected as one berry. For partial 
detection, most parts of the berry were covered by two other berries and the visible part was divided into two unconnected 
parts. Predictably, such berries were detected partially and another visible part of this berry was not detected, possibly 
because of the small area that was visible to the camera. Because of this kind of error, the mask accuracy decreased. In other 
cases, some leaves also were detected as immature berries. The most probable reason for these inaccuracies is that there 
were many berries covered by each other, resulting in only small parts of some berries being visible. Although there were 
some detection errors caused by the inherent limitation of the 2D images, the overall results were promising.

Traits analysis based on 2D images 
The average berry number, maturity, and compactness of five views was calculated as the final results of the berry 
samples. Compared with the manually counted berry number, the detected berry number was underestimated. This is because 
the berry number in one view was less than the true berry number within the whole cluster. The root mean square error 
(RMSE) of the detected berry number was 1.484 among all four cultivars compared with the manually counted berry number, 
which means that approximately 1.5 berries were underestimated.  The average berry number of 26 “Emerald” samples was 
13.812, which had the highest berry number among the four cultivars, while “Star” had the lowest average berry number 
(8.534). “Meadowlark” had the highest maturity (0.651) among the four cultivars, while that of “Emerald” and “Star” were 
in the middle. For the average compactness, “Farthing” was the most compact and “Meadowlark” was the least compact. 
However, the average results could not illustrate the group. Thus, Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for all four 
cultivars (Table 1). Results show that only “Star” had a lower berry number than that of the “Emerald” and “Farthing”. 
“Farthing” was less mature than “Meadowlark”, and the compactness order was “Farthing”> “Emerald” (“Star”)> 
“Meadowlark”. The ANOVA results of these three traits were consistent with that of the average results.

3D reconstruction results and compactness 
All 40 fresh blueberry clusters were scanned by the image capturing system to obtain corresponding 3D point clouds. 
Textures were added to the point clouds to make it look more realistic. Figure 6 shows four examples of 3D reconstruction 
results. Both berries and stems were reconstructed successfully. Below each 3D point cloud, the 3D point cloud with a 
minimum bounding box is presented, which contained the whole point cloud with the smallest box volume, thereby keeping 
the box unchanged regardless of how the point clouds were rotated. The compactness value was relatively small, although 
the blueberry clusters were tight because there was considerable space in the bounding box. To increase the compactness 
difference, an index (e.g. 100) was used to multiply compactness. The results changed to 30.9, 28.2, 26.3, and 22 
respectively. Another option was normalization, i.e., these 4 compactness values can be normalized as 0.288, 0.201, 0.139, 
and 0, respectively. There are many options for making the compactness index more understandable.

3D individual berry segmentation and traits extraction 
After 2D mask-3D patch projection and patch clustering, every individual blueberry was segmented. Figure 7 shows the 
segmentation results for blueberry cluster sample M08. For each blueberry, maturity properties were determined and size 
was estimated based on the sphere fitting. There were 3 immature berries and 11 mature berries, so the total blueberry 
number was 14 and the maturity for this cluster was 0.786 (3/14). The berry number and the maturity were the same with 
the ground truth. Not every individual berry volume was measured by other methods, so the precision of every berry volume 
was difficult to be estimated. However, the total volume of blueberry clusters was measured, so it can be compared with the 
sum of individual berry volumes. The sum volume of M08 was 26.30 ml, while the ground truth was 31.04ml. The reason 
the berry sum was less than the ground truth is that the stems were included when measuring cluster volume by water 
displacement, while they were not included in the sum of all the individual berries. It can be seen that the immature berry 
volumes were relatively small and the mature berry volumes were large because the immature berries have not fully 
developed. In the segmentation of NO.01 and NO.03 presented additional patches because 2D blueberry masks contained 
errors (Figure 5c) that resulted in some stem parts patches of other berries being considered as a segmented berry. The 3D 
model was not complete for most of berries because all berries were so crowed together that the anterior part could not be 
seen and be reconstructed. The average volume accuracy of all blueberry clusters was 89.68% compared to the volume 
measured by the water displacement, while that of the RMSE was 2.95 ml. If there were 15 berries within a cluster, the 
average volume error for one individual berry would have been less than 0.2ml.

Traits analysis based on 3D points clouds 
The berry number, cluster maturity, and berry volume were extracted based on blueberry segmented for every cluster of 
the four cultivars. The average berry number accuracy for all 40 blueberry cluster was 97.3% compared to the manual count. 
The counting accuracy for each cultivar (“Emerald”, “Farthing”, “Meadowlark”, and “Star”) were 96.6%, 96.4%, 97.6%, 
and 98.7%, respectively. It can be seen that “Meadowlark” and “Star” had a higher accuracy than that of “Emerald” and 
“Farthing” because “Meadowlark” and “Star” had lower compactness, which means they were relatively loose and the 
individual berries were easier to separate. The extracted cluster maturity results had a high correlation with the ground truth, 
with a coefficient of determination (𝑅𝑅2) of 0.908 and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.068. The counting error affected 
the cluster maturity results, as it was calculated based on the berry number. 
ANOVA was conducted to do a statistical analysis of berry number, cluster maturity, compactness, and mature berry 
volume among the four cultivars. There was no need to analyze the immature berry volume, because the immature berries 
varied considerably from a very young stage to the near mature stage. Table 2 shows that “Farthing” had a higher berry 
number within a cluster than that of the “Meadowlark” and “Star”. Although the mean berry volume of “Emerald” was about 
4 and 5 larger than that of “Meadowlark” and “Star”, they had no significant difference statistically. “Meadowlark” had more 
mature clusters than that of “Farthing”, while the mean cluster maturity of “Meadowlark” was twice of the “Farthing”. 
“Emerald” and “Farthing” had more compact clusters than that of “Meadowlark” and “Star”. For the mature berry volume, 
“Farthing” had larger mature berries, while “Meadowlark” was in the middle and “Star” had the smallest mature berry 
volume.

Comparison of 2D and 3D traits 
Berry number, cluster maturity, and compactness were extracted on both 2D images and 3D point clouds. Cluster maturity 
generated consistent statistical results for the four cultivars on 2D and 3D, where the berry clusters of “Meadowlark” were 
more mature than that of “Farthing”. The mean berry number extracted from 3D was larger than that of 2D for all cultivars 
because berry number in 2D reflected only one view of the blueberry clusters. The berry number in 2D showed that “Emerald” 
and “Star” had significant difference while “Farthing” and “Meadowlark” had no significant difference. However, the berry 
number in 3D showed that “Emerald” and “Star” had no significant difference, while “Farthing” and “Meadowlark” had a 
significant difference. The compactness values in 3D were lower than that in 2D because the compactness in 2D was the 
ratio of areas while the compactness in 3D was the ratio of volumes. The statistical results in 2D show that the compactness 
order for four cultivars was “Farthing”>”Emerald”=”Star”>”Meadowlark”, while in 3D it shows that the order was “Emerald” 
=”Farthing”> “Meadowlark”=”Star”. The traits extracted in 3D should be more reliable than those from 2D because it 
reflects complete information in 3D while reflecting only partial information in 2D. However, sample number should be 
increased to obtain more convincing statistical results. 
Conclusions 
This study investigated a deep learning segmentation Mask R-CNN model for segmenting individual blueberries and 
extracting harvestability and yield traits in 2D images of blueberries. The classification accuracy was better than 0.7 at a 0.5 
IOU threshold with a 0.9 segmentation accuracy. The berry numbers extracted from segmented 2D blueberries showed that 
‘Star’ had a lower berry number, ‘Farthing’ was less mature but was most compact, and ‘Meadowlark’ was the loosest in 
comparison to the other three cultivars. This study also provided the individual 3D blueberry segmentation pipeline 
combining the segmented 2D masks and 3D point clouds. The counting accuracy based on 3D segmentation was larger than 
97%. Among four cultivars, the cluster maturity of “Farthing” was the lowest, the compactness of “Emerald” and “Farthing” 
was higher than that of “Meadowlark” and “Star”, “Farthing” had the most berry number, and “Star” had the smallest mature 
berry size. The trait of blueberry number have potential applications for yield estimation with maturity. The compactness 
and berry size analysis can be incorporated into a breeding scheme in which genotypes are selected for machine 
harvestability. In future studies, blueberry traits will be extracted efficiently in-situ in the field.
