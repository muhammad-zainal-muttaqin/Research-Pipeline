Abstract The present paper aims to study image processing algorithms to ac-
celerate and facilitate the evaluation of the harvest condition in tomato farms.
In order to achieve this, two diﬀerent Deep Learning models are trained and
combined with counting methods to produce a harvest monitoring system for
embedded applications using an Intel® Movidius
TM and an aﬀordable RGB
camera. The ﬁrst model detects the location of cherry tomato clusters, while
the second estimates the fruit’s maturity. The results are compared to a base-
line implementation based on segmentation. Next, a multiple counting method
based on regions of interest is applied to the detected clusters in videos to count
the tomatoes at diﬀerent maturity stages. In order to produce a more robust
counting, a tracking system is implemented which uses temporal information to
ﬁnd the unique tomato clusters in videos. In the evaluation stage, the obtained
location results indicate an Intersection over Union (IoU ) of about 89% when
using the MobileNetV1 as a feature extractor and choosing the appropriate
location anchors. The maturity estimation results indicate better performance
for the trained algorithm as compared to the baseline, providing a root mean
square error of 7.7%. The best results were obtained when combining the fully
learned solution with the tracking system, correctly counting the majority of
the tomato clusters at multiple maturity stages.

1 Introduction
The large demand and competitive market for fruits brings challenges to farm-
ers, such as optimizing the costs and eﬀectiveness of manual labor for harvest-
ing and reducing the percentage of fruit loss [23]. Hiring more seasonal manual
workers or paying overtime may improve the production, but increases the
costs or aﬀects the worker’s health at the farm when working for extended
hours [30]. The eﬀectiveness of manual harvesting and the percentage of fruit
loss are linked to the monitoring and control of the fruit maturation during
harvest [23].
Fruit harvesting is a time-consuming task because each worker needs to
observe and touch the fruits in order to evaluate their maturity and decide
whether to harvest on the current or next days. Some technological methods
use custom non-destructive crop monitoring sensors aiming to solve this prob-
lem by measuring the fruit ripeness [7,36]. The detection is based on fruit
reactions that take time to take eﬀect and is aﬀected by other variables (e.g.
climate and fruit variety) that lower the robustness. Some variety of cherry
tomatoes are considered ready for harvest when the skin changes its color from
greenish to reddish or the tomato closest to the stem comes out in hand. How-
ever, in some farms (e.g. Dutch style greenhouses), these fruits may not be
harvested individually, being necessary to harvest the entire cluster. The har-
vest decision is made after manually estimating the ripeness that takes into
account all the tomatoes on that cluster, which can be demanding in large
farms. Each cluster is packed to be sold in the supermarket even when the
maturities are mixed.
Relevant approaches that estimate the maturity of fruits [2,9,12,19], which
can produce immediate results, involve vision systems with image processing
or machine learning techniques. A vision system applied in real agricultural
environments can provide useful fruit information to improve the harvesting
decision. For single images, the system needs to be able to handle problems
such as occlusion, stability of light source, agricultural background clutter and
fruit scale variation to detect the fruits and estimate their maturity accurately.
A recent practical application concerns a vision system, which works day and
night on plant rows, to estimate the maturity in tomatoes and harvest individ-
ually each tomato with a robot-arm1. In related work [21], a machine vision
system was developed for identifying tomato clusters in sets of three stereo
images (aiming to reduce occlusion), captured by special sensors. The vision
system was able to identify the tomato clusters ready for harvest. In order for
the system to work properly, the images were captured during night (being
sensitive to inﬂuence of sun-light) and the sensors needed to be close to the

fruits. Therefore, only a few tomato clusters may be visualized in each of the
image sets collected.
We implemented an automatic visual system to monitor the maturity in
cherry tomato clusters on plant rows, that only requires a common RGB cam-
era and an embedded computer, being relatively cheap and easily attached to
embedded systems for statistical data collection or even automatic harvest.
For harvesting robots, a single operation may be suﬃcient for harvesting a
tomato cluster [22]. The camera can stay relatively far to the fruits and cap-
ture images containing many fruits. Our system is robust to occlusion and
works in diﬀerent conditions of illumination, being able to monitor many clus-
ters simultaneously in sequences of images. The system is based on supervised
Deep Learning (DL) techniques, being able to generalize its response to diﬀer-
ent fruits when feeding the system with new labeled data. The dataset used in
this work was collected from scenes captured on a tomato farm (greenhouse),
located in Vestfold county in Norway, covering three plant rows.
Our system is composed of three parts: fruit detection, maturity estimation
and counting subsystems. The detection and the maturity estimation subsys-
tems are combined to provide the cluster’s locations and maturation rates in
videos for the application of the counting subsystem, implemented using a
tracking algorithm that separates the diﬀerent clusters using their coordinates
in the image and their maturity information in multiple frames. Because the
tracking algorithm monitors the clusters in diﬀerent positions, the ﬁnal matu-
rity estimate is improved. Based on the information collected by the system,
it is possible to quickly identify the best tomato plantation row for the har-
vest in a given day. Additionally, due to the possibility of fast data collection,
continual data collection can be used in the analysis for harvest prediction in
the following days and to identify possible tomato growing problems.
The system can automatically monitor multiple tomato clusters on plant
rows at the same time, using any conventional RGB camera and a vision pro-
cessing unit. It is able to monitor the continuous maturity in tomato clusters
and quantify the clusters in real conditions, handling occlusion phenomena.
The fruit’s continuous maturity is updated during tracking, increasing its ro-
bustness. We show that the vision system is fast enough for real-time pro-
cessing in an embedded solution. The self-collected video samples and the
datasets on three plant rows for tomato cluster detection, maturity estimate
and counting will be made publicly available.

cussions. Section 3 presents the methods to obtain and label the agricultural
dataset used in this work and the diﬀerent approaches to locate, rank and
quantify the clustered fruits. Section 4 describes our experiments and section
5 evaluates the results. The last Section 6 presents conclusions and motivates
future work. The Appendix A shows additional results related to tracking with
occlusion.

2 Background
Deep convolutional neural networks are learning algorithms, relevant in the
ﬁeld of Deep Learning, predominantly applied to images and have been widely
used since the advances in GPU technology enabled their success at the Ima-
geNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge [24]. They have been devel-
oped for many image recognition tasks (e.g. classify, locate or even segment
the images) with outstanding results. Compared to classical image processing
methods for image recognition [49,50], CNNs do not need heavy pre-processing
speciﬁc to the dataset (instead, they use e.g. convolutions and max-pooling
operators) because they learn how to process the image and generalize to new
data. Convolution is a parallel operation that uses multiple kernels (trainable
ﬁlters) that learn how to extract images features. Subsequently, max-pooling
simpliﬁes the image by calculating the image max response using predeﬁned
square-cell grids. Commonly, CNNs are divided into many convolutional lay-
ers followed by downsampling operations (e.g. max-pooling) in which the ﬁrst
layers extract low-level features such as edges and blobs. The next layers ex-
tract mid-level features such as texture, up to high level-features (speciﬁc
features to the dataset). The number of feature maps generated in each layer
is proportional to the (predeﬁned) number of kernels for that layer. After the
last convolutional layer, a conventional neural network is usually attached by
means of a fully connected layer. The neural network is composed of neurons,
which each have trainable parameters, that learns how to combine the fea-
tures maps of the last convolutional layer to predict results (e.g. classiﬁcation,
regression or both).
The following subsections present the background information related to
our research for object detection, maturity level as well as tracking and count-
ing.
2.1 Object Detection
Standalone CNNs can robustly solve the image recognition problem, but the
machine intelligence, in this case, does not learn how to understand scenes.
In other words, the machine is not able to classify and locate diﬀerent ob-
jects in the same scene by itself as humans do. Researchers have developed
methods based on CNNs (i.e. Regions with CNN features [11]) to improve
the scene understanding issue in machines, aiming at real-time applications
(R-CNN [11], Fast-RCNN [10] and Faster-RCNN [38]). These methods can
detect objects reasonably well, but the inference time in videos is still too slow
in usual GPUs (around 5 frames per second for the fastest method) for real-
time applications. Important algorithms in the research area were developed
to solve these problems, such as ”You Only Look Once” (YOLO) and ”Single
Shot MultiBox Detector” (SSD) [37,27]. The ﬁrst algorithm can increase the
frames per second (FPS) on inference up to 150, but compromises the accu-
racy of the results. The second algorithm highly improves the accuracy, but

inferring at 22 −60 FPS which is enough for real-time applications. In the
SSD algorithm, starting from the ﬁrst convolutional block and following to
the last block, the feature maps decrease in terms of resolution, but increase
in terms of depth. For each feature map extracted from the convolutions at
diﬀerent layers, the algorithm uses a shared regressor and a layer dedicated
classiﬁer. Consequently, the object detection is done at multiple scales being
able to handle objects at diﬀerent sizes [27]. In order to label a dataset for the
SSD model, each object to be detected must have a class identiﬁcation and its
coordinates position in the image. As with the other methods, in the training
stage, SSD also uses anchors to learn the bounding box coordinate oﬀsets at
multiple scales, based initially on which anchors correspond to a given label
object (i.e. ground truth information). The SSD architecture is explained in
more detail in Sec. 3.2.
In a recent paper, Santos et al. [42], discussed the scene understanding
problem and implemented YOLO-based object detection algorithms to de-
tect ﬁve diﬀerent grape varieties in images. In the same paper, an instance
segmentation algorithm is evaluated for detection purposes. The instance seg-
mentation fulﬁlls the same role as object detection, but additionally classiﬁes
each pixel of the image for each instance. This brings advantages such as the
exact location of a grape’s pixels in an image, but also disadvantages such
as the time-consuming labeling and high processing required (i.e. advanced
GPUs). Similarly, Liu et al. [28] implemented a deep learning algorithm to de-
tect oranges and apples. Instead of object detection, a Fully-CNN (FCNN) was
used to segment fruit pixels. FCNNs have the advantage of being extremely
fast, but they are not able to detect the objects as instances, being necessary
to apply additional methods to separate the objects into instances. Another
approach for fruit detection, Halstead et al. [12], adapted the Faster-RCNN
(FRCNN) to detect sweet peppers. Sun et al. [45] proposed a method based
on a Feature Pyramid Network [25] to localize tomato organs in images that
outperformed the traditional FRCNN. In the present work the SSD algorithm
is used to eﬃciently detect tomato clusters in images, taking advantage of its
well-performing architecture for real-time applications.
2.2 Maturity Level
In the literature, fruit maturity estimation is commonly treated as image clas-
siﬁcation and trained with Machine Learning (ML) models. Kaur et al. [19]
trained classical ML algorithms (e.g. SVM [16]; Random Forest [6]) to identify
ﬁve maturity stages of tomatoes (i.e. green mature, turning, pink, light red and
red). Training classical models is faster than DL and does not require a GPU,
but image pre-processing (e.g. feature extraction) is highly recommended for
the models to produce reasonable results.
Many authors implemented standalone CNNs as a solution for maturity
level identiﬁcation. Faisal et al. [9] implemented DL models (e.g. ResNet [14];
VGG-19 [44]) to identify ﬁve maturity levels on date fruits (i.e. immature,

applied transfer learning to these models in order to classify papaya fruits

we modiﬁed standalone CNNs (explained in detail in Sec. 3.1.2) to produce
continuous maturity values (between 0% and 100%) for the detected tomato
clusters. Therefore, the maturity levels can be used as continuous values or
can be easily adjusted by discretization into N levels after their estimation by
the CNN.
2.3 Tracking and Counting
In a single scene, where multiple objects are present, the inference of the
detection models described in subsec. 2.1 generates a single identiﬁcation for all
the objects in the scene (i.e. standalone CNNs), or diﬀerent identities for each
static object (i.e standalone object detection algorithms). In dynamic scenes,
the standalone detectors are not able to generate a unique identiﬁcation for
each object due to the inability to perform tracking (which would not permit
the application of fruit counting and monitoring).
A few authors mentioned in subsec. 2.1 implemented tracking methods
to add functionality for dealing with objects in dynamic scenes to their re-
spective detectors and, consequently, perform counting tasks. Santos et al.
[42] implemented a tracking method that used the detectors as a base. Their
tracking method uses a three-dimensional (3D) association technique (based
on Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [13]) for tracking and counting the grape va-
rieties in sequences of images on orchards rows. Starting with a similar 3D
reconstruction approach, Liu et al. [28] implemented a frame-to-frame track-
ing solution based on the combination of the Hungarian Algorithm [31] and
SfM to track and count oranges and apples on diﬀerent rows. SfM is a com-
puter vision technique that uses a decent amount of computational processing
and, when combined with a detector model for tracking, should considerably
decrease the FPS. Halstead et al. [12] proposed a tracking via detection ap-
proach that uses the intersection over union (IoU) of the bounding boxes as
well as the quality estimation to track and count the sweet peppers.
Our counting subsystem takes into account the two-dimensional position of
each tomato cluster in the image and the continuous maturity information ex-
tracted from the detection and maturity subsystems. The maturity is updated
by our system at each image in a sequence which provides a smooth, unique
identiﬁcation for each cluster and a precise ﬁnal maturity. The combination of
our subsystems results in a fast-processing vision system that can also work

3 Method
In this section, we begin by describing how the data were collected and labelled
to be used in our diﬀerent approaches for tomato clusters localization and their
maturity estimation. Subsequently, we describe the model for object detection
and the baseline for the estimation followed by the main regression model.
Next, we discuss the implementations for counting the clusters in videos.
3.1 Preparing and Labeling the Dataset
A color camera was coupled to a pipe rail trolley in order to continuously
capture scenes on the farm by following a unidirectional horizontal movement,
covering three diﬀerent plant rows. A portion of the scenes is used to create
a dataset consisting of 254 images (containing multiple tomato clusters in
each image) which are used to train supervised deep learning algorithms and
measure their performance. The images obtained are labelled for the learning
methods for fruit detection and the maturity estimate, resulting in two datasets
presented in the following subsections. An example scene, which illustrates
some diﬀerences between tomato clusters that must be dealt with is shown in
Fig. 1.

3.1.3 Tracking and Counting
The scenes mentioned in subsec. 3.1 were obtained from three diﬀerent videos
(Table 3), in which each video is related to a plant row. The videos are used
in the tracking and counting algorithms to dynamically count the fruits. In
order to obtain a ground truth, we counted the unique tomato clusters for each

to a total of 328 unique tomato clusters.
3.2 Learning Algorithm for Cluster Detection
In order to locate and detect multiple tomato clusters in the same image, tak-
ing into account the processing speed and accuracy, we trained the SSD [27]
object detection algorithm. This model is capable of real-time detection, being
able to eﬃciently detect completely or partially occluded objects. The SSD al-
lows the use of pre-trained networks to form the convolutional base. There is
a trade-oﬀbetween speed and accuracy depending on which pre-trained archi-
tecture (e.g. VGGNet [43], ResNet [15], MobileNet [18] and AlexNet [24]) is
used. We opted to use the MobileNet V1 [18] architecture as the base of the
SSD because it has optimized convolution operations in order to achieve good
performance in real-time applications. The MobileNet V1 has 28 layers formed
by 13 convolution alternate blocks, being the main operations in the block the
Depthwise Separable Convolutions (DSC). The basic idea of this convolution
is to mitigate the computational cost required by full 3D convolution by sepa-
rating the spatial and feature-wise convolutions. The DSC is thus divided into
two diﬀerent convolution layers: Depthwise Convolution (used for ﬁltering)
and Pointwise Convolution (used to combine features). Instead of applying
convolutions to whole images and feature maps channels at the same time,
the depthwise convolutions layer applies smaller convolutions in each channel
separately, producing features with the same number of channels as its input
which are then stacked together. The pointwise convolution layer takes the

As mentioned before, the SSD model uses a shared regressor and layer
dedicated classiﬁers to accomplish the object detection task. In order to train
the algorithm for this task, the model needs to minimize a weighted combined
loss function (i.e. multibox loss) that is divided into a location loss and a
conﬁdence loss. The multibox loss function is minimized by updating the SSD
trainable parameters using an optimizer (e.g. (Adam [20] or RMSprop [3]). The
ﬁrst loss function measures how close the predicted bounding box coordinates
(based on the set of anchors) are to the ground truth. The second loss function
computes how conﬁdent the model is in relation to the predicted class, given
the ground truth, in a predicted bounding box [27]. In this paper we used
the detector to ﬁnd a single class, therefore the conﬁdence loss function is
simpler. During the training phase, the SSD uses initially a matching strategy
that ﬁnds the best set of anchors to be used for each ground truth to train
the network [27]. The regression part of the model predicts the bounding
box oﬀset coordinates (∆(cx, cy, w, h)) based on the best anchors found. For
multiple bounding boxes in the same image, the anchors are handled based on
a matching approach [47].
The pre-trained network to be used as a base for the feature extraction is
usually split in two parts and the layer depth of each part is chosen manually.
After obtaining the feature maps generated by the ﬁrst part, a layer that
contains the regressor and a convolutional classiﬁer is applied. The same logic
is applied to the second part and consecutively to auxiliary convolutional layers
as shown in Fig. 2. As a consequence, multiple bounding boxes pointing to the
same object, at diﬀerent scales, are generated for each predicted class. The
results are combined using non-maximum suppression to remove the detections
with location and conﬁdence scores lower than predeﬁned thresholds.
3.3 Cluster Evaluation
After obtaining the bounding boxes for each cluster of tomatoes, it is intended
to identify how ripe the clusters are. In order to complete this task, two dif-
ferent approaches were implemented and tested. The ﬁrst approach (baseline)
consists of implementing a segmentation algorithm, based on the Ohta and
Otsu algorithms with color space transformation [32,33,52] to calculate the
level of maturation using the segmented pixel areas. The second approach is
related to the use of CNNs adapted to provide a regression output which role
is to estimate the level of maturation. In both cases, the methods are applied
to each cluster sub-image inside the bounding boxes.

3.3.1 Baseline: Image Analysis Method
The main idea to use image analysis algorithms is to separate by image seg-
mentation the reddish and greenish tomatoes in a real environment, taking
advantage of their colors and ﬁltering out the agricultural background. The
major advantages of these algorithms are the processing speed and the pos-
sibility of their direct application without the need for labeling, training the
data and availability of GPUs.
Some challenging factors of segmenting tomatoes in images are conditions
of illumination and variations in object color. Another considerable problem
concerns the complex background of a tomato farm, with color intensities
(green leaves colors) close to the color of the green tomatoes. A practical and
eﬃcient solution was introduced by Wei et al. [52] and has been used in practice
on agricultural environments [26,29] as well as in this paper as a baseline. The
solution consists in segmenting red and green fruits under complex agricul-
tural background. The method initially applies a color space transformation,
converting a RGB image into Ohta and Wei [32,52], which highlights fruit
objects and mitigates the agricultural background. Subsequently, the method
segments reddish and greenish fruits by applying an adaptive threshold color
segmentation algorithm [33]. As discussed by the authors, the best Ohta and
Wei features for fruit extraction are I′

3.3.2 Learning Method
In order to estimate the maturation levels more robustly, we use CNNs that,
in simple words, learns how to ﬁlter eﬃciently the images contained inside
the bounding boxes extracting features that it considers important. Instead of
training the CNN from scratch, we use the VGG-16 and MobileNet pre-trained
networks [43,18]. The VGG-16 and MobileNet architectures diﬀer mainly in

Also, a transfer learning [35] technique is used, which allows the transfer-
ring of pre-trained parameters learned on the ﬁrst ”Y” layers. Next, we use a
ﬁne tuning method that permits the freezing of layers, marking the ”Y” layers
as non-trainable (frozen) and the next layers are marked as trainable during
model learning. Similar to Tenorio et al. [48], we used a statistical dispersion
method in order to choose the best ”Y” layers to be frozen. The advantages
of using transfer learning and freezing only the ﬁrst ”Y” layers are the faster
training time and the signiﬁcant lower number of trainable parameters which
also prevents overﬁtting, thereby allowing us to train on our relatively small
regression dataset.
The original pre-trained networks were trained using the ImageNet dataset
for the Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge [41] which contains a thou-
sand classes used for binary classiﬁcation. The pre-trained output layer con-
tains a number of neurons equal to the number of classes and uses the softmax
activation function, therefore the classiﬁcation of a given input image is made
choosing the neuron with higher activation. As mentioned before, for the ma-
turity estimate, we are looking for a continuous value instead of a binary value.
Thus, we modiﬁed the output layer for regression, keeping a single neuron and
changing the activation function to sigmoid. Consequently, the output value
of this neuron varies between 0 and 1 according to the maturation rate range.

3.4 Tracking Clusters in Videos
For a static image, the cluster detection algorithm can identify each tomato
cluster separately. However, when using a video (i.e. multiple frames), the
same algorithm may switch their identiﬁcation at each frame depending on
which cluster it identiﬁes ﬁrst. In other words, the detection algorithm allows
to count the clusters in a static image, but it is not possible to count them in
a video, thus a tracking system is necessary.
In order to track individually each tomato cluster in videos, we have im-
plemented a logic algorithm that takes advantage of each cluster’s centroid
coordinates (x, y) related to the image and its rank (r) computed in the last
N frames of video. Our tracking algorithm, based on multiple frames, brings
advantages, such as occlusion robustness and multiple counting avoidance for
the same cluster.
We use a Degree of Compatibility (DoC) computed using a weighted Eu-
clidean Distance, as shown is Eq. 5, to measure how closely matched the clus-

6 Conclusions and Future Work
In this paper, diﬀerent approaches for fruit monitoring were implemented and
tested proving their eﬀectiveness. The learning techniques showed the robust-
ness to adverse conditions as well as the precision of the convolutional neural
networks for object detection and regression, when tuned properly. The ob-
ject detection model could locate precisely the majority of tomato clusters on
the test set. The baseline implementation based on segmentation was relevant
to identify the tomato ripeness based on their appearance for clusters that
contain mostly red tomatoes, but not so accurate for some mixed and green
clusters. However, when using a CNN as a regressor for the same task, the
maturity estimation was quite precise.
The counting system in videos based on region of interest is a simple and
fast method to count objects, but it has limitations which lowers its precision.
On the other hand, when the system was based on tracking, the algorithm was
able to successfully identify the diﬀerent clusters and consequently perform the
counting with higher precision.
The processing speed, in general, is suﬃcient and to help the farmer to
quickly identify the maturity using common CPUs, improving the harvest
eﬃciency. The embedded solution using Movidius was slower, but still useful in
resource-constrained environments. If more performance is required, (desktop)
GPUs provide an easy upgrade path.
Training two separate learning models (a detector and a regressor) has the
advantage of being able to combine diﬀerent architectures and easily observe
the individual results as well as the possibility to tune each one more precisely,
but it is less eﬃcient in terms of computational cost compared to a single
model that performs both tasks. Besides the object location and class, the
object detection algorithm can be adapted to provide the maturation rate
by substituting the multiple classiﬁcation layers for regression layers and also
changing the loss function and the metrics. This approach was not considered
in this paper because the VGG16 performed better for maturity estimation
and MobileNetV1 performed better for cluster location, but the approach can
be considered for other diﬀerent CNN architetures [46,15,24].
Also, the use of recurrent networks for instance segmentation could be
tested for detection and tracking. Using trained instance segmentation algo-
rithms, it is possible to detect and segment each object sequentially being also
robust to occlusion [39].
Instead of using regression or segmentation to estimate the maturity, pixel-
wise semantic segmentation deep learning algorithms could be used for this
task, being robust to image adverse conditions (e.g. SegNet[1], U-Net [40] and
DeepLab [8] )
For future work, the tracking system could also be improved by using
additional information to identify unique clusters such as the prediction of the
next position of a given cluster based on adaptive Kalman ﬁlters, similar to
the object tracking implementation by Weng et al. [53].

If there is a need for locating and estimating other kind of fruits, vegetables
or fruits with similar shape the learning algorithms for cluster detection and
evaluation can be retrained (after the labeling procedure) and the tracking
algorithm can be tuned for counting. However, the baseline method for fruit
maturity is speciﬁc to red, green, reddish and greenish fruits.
In further work, more classes besides the maturation classes can be added
to identify overripe tomatoes as well as diseases and plagues, similar to Brahimi
et al. [5] and Wang et al. [51]. The tomato clusters in these conditions can be
automatically skipped for counting and this information can be provided to
farmers.

We also veriﬁed some tracking results for occlusion cases in the videos and their impacts
on maturation rate when using the DDT conﬁguration. The results are shown in the ﬁgures
A.10-A.12.
It can be noticed that the object detection algorithm on Fig. A.10 predicted the bound-
ing boxes even with occlusion, but the maturation rank was aﬀected when the occlusion
was occurring which decreased the rank value for a while. On the other hand, our tracking
algorithm uses the rank information between 20 frames and calculates a smoothed maturity
function which mitigates this rank discrepancy.
The same eﬀect happens in Fig. A.11, the rank estimate was higher in the ﬁrst image
because of the cluster behind, but the rank discrepancy is also mitgated by the tracking
algorithm.
The Fig. A.12 results shows a moment when the object detection algorithm loses the
detection due to occlusion, then detects the cluster again. The tracking system could identify
correctly the two detections as the same cluster. An important point is that in the uncropped
image, ﬁve other clusters were being tracked.
