Abstract
This scoping review synthesizes 35 studies on sensor technologies and artificial intelligence
for fruit detection, classification, and quality assessment, contrasting Latin American and
international research traditions. The analysis suggests notable differences in technolog-
ical approaches between the included Latin American and international studies: Latin
American research tends to emphasize in developing accessible, practical solutions using
classical computer vision and low-cost hardware, while international studies more fre-
quently employ through deep learning architectures, multi-modal sensing, and complete
robotic automation systems. Across the included studies, relatively limited attention was
given to AI-assisted decision support for agricultural practitioners, insufficient considera-
tion of inclusivity, and the scarce integration of environmental sustainability into intelligent
sensing system design. The review identifies that only 8 of 35 studies originate from
Latin America, suggesting an uneven geographical distribution of the available evidence.
The included studies generally reported high accuracy values, yet these findings must be
interpreted with caution given the reliance on curated datasets that may not represent
real-world variability. The reviewed evidence suggests that future research may benefit
not from one approach dominating the other, but from a thoughtful integration of com-
plementary strategies, including knowledge transfer, edge computing democratization,
and human-centered design. Overall, this review suggests that the ultimate goal extends
beyond accuracy metrics to the transformation of agricultural practices that enhance food
security, economic development, and environmental sustainability across the global agri-
cultural landscape. It is important to note that this work does not propose or validate a new
fruit detection algorithm but rather synthesizes and critically evaluates existing scientific
evidence regarding sensor technologies and artificial intelligence applied to fruit detection
and quality assessment.

1. Introduction
The global agricultural industry stands at a technological crossroads, where the con-
vergence of artificial intelligence, sensor technologies, and computer vision promises to
transform traditional practices that have remained largely unchanged for generations [1].
Among the most promising applications of these technologies is the automated detection,
classification, and quality assessment of fruits—a domain that holds immense economic
significance for both developed and developing nations [2]. The ability to non-destructively
evaluate fruit quality, detect defects, assess ripeness, and predict shelf life using sensor-
based systems represents a paradigm shift from subjective, labor-intensive manual inspec-
tion to objective, rapid, and scalable automated solutions [3].
Computer vision, as a subfield of artificial intelligence, has emerged as a particularly
powerful tool for fruit quality assessment. By enabling machines to interpret and analyze
visual information from the world, computer vision systems can replicate—and, in many
cases, surpass—human capabilities in detecting subtle defects, classifying fruits by size
and color, and assessing ripeness with remarkable consistency [4]. When combined with
advanced machine learning techniques, particularly deep learning architectures such
as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), You Only Look Once (YOLO), and Vision
Transformers (ViTs), these systems achieve accuracy levels that approach or exceed 99% in
many applications [5]. The integration of complementary sensing modalities—including
near-infrared spectroscopy, hyperspectral imaging, and 3D scanning—has further expanded
the capabilities of these systems, enabling assessment of internal quality attributes such
as soluble solids content, internal defects, and chemical composition that were previously
accessible only through destructive testing [6].
The importance of these technologies extends far beyond academic curiosity. Globally,
the fruit industry faces mounting pressures to reduce post-harvest losses, ensure food safety,
meet increasingly stringent quality standards, and optimize supply chain efficiency [7].
The Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that approximately one-third of all food
produced for human consumption is lost or wasted annually, with fruits and vegetables ex-
periencing particularly high loss rates due to their perishable nature [8]. Automated quality
assessment systems offer a pathway to dramatically reduce these losses by enabling rapid,
accurate sorting at multiple points along the supply chain—from harvest to processing
to retail [9].
In Latin America, these technologies hold particularly transformative potential. The re-
gion is a major global exporter of fruits, with countries like Ecuador, Colombia, Mexico,
Peru, and Chile supplying bananas, pineapples, passion fruit, avocados, grapes, and count-
less other fruits to markets worldwide [10]. The quality and consistency of these exports di-
rectly impact the economic livelihoods of millions of farmers, processors, and exporters [11].
However, the adoption of advanced sensor technologies and AI in Latin American agricul-
tural sectors has been uneven, constrained by limited access to sophisticated equipment,
computational resources, and technical expertise [12]. This technological gap, if left un-
addressed, threatens to undermine the region’s competitiveness in increasingly quality-
conscious global markets [13].
Despite the rapid proliferation of research in this domain, the literature remains
fragmented and poorly synthesized. Studies emerge from diverse disciplinary back-
grounds—computer science, agricultural engineering, food technology, and robotics—each
employing different methodologies, reporting different performance metrics, and address-
ing different crops and applications [14]. This fragmentation makes it difficult for re-
searchers, practitioners, and policymakers to gain a comprehensive understanding of
the state of the art, identify best practices, or recognize critical gaps in the evidence
base. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, the stark contrast between research

To address these gaps, this scoping review aims to synthesize the existing evidence on

American and international contributions, we seek to explore the technological landscape,
identify strengths and limitations of different approaches, and identify potential directions
for a more integrated, accessible, and impactful research. This review moves beyond simple
cataloging of techniques to critically examine how different contexts, resources, and priori-
ties shape the development and deployment of fruit detection technologies. We analyze
not only what has been reported but also areas that have received comparatively limited
attention—particularly concerning inclusivity, sustainability, and the human dimensions of
technology adoption.
To guide this scoping review, we propose the following overarching questions, which
emerged from preliminary analysis of the literature and reflect the comparative, critical,
and forward-looking orientation of our investigation.
•
RQ1 (Technological Approaches): What sensor technologies and computational tech-
niques are being employed for fruit detection, classification, and quality assessment across
different geographical contexts, and what are their reported performance characteristics?
•
RQ2 (Regional Contrasts): How do Latin American and international research tradi-
tions differ in their technological approaches, resource utilization, research priorities,
and implementation contexts for fruit detection technologies?
•
RQ3 (Agricultural Applications): Which fruit species and agricultural stages (pre-
harvest vs. post-harvest) are addressed across the literature, and what patterns emerge
regarding research priorities and economic contexts?
•
RQ4 (Critical Gaps and Blind Spots): What potential research gaps emerge regard-
ing AI-assisted decision support, inclusivity, environmental sustainability, human-
centered system design, and the transition from laboratory prototypes to real agricul-
tural deployment?
By addressing these questions, this scoping review aims to provide a comprehen-
sive, forward-looking synthesis that informs both research and practice. We seek to move
the conversation beyond the “what” of sensor technologies and AI to the “so what” and
“for whom”—analyzing the technological, economic, and social dimensions of fruit detec-
tion technologies. Ultimately, we aim to understand not just whether these technologies
can improve fruit quality assessment, but whether they may contribute to a more equi-
table, sustainable, and resilient agricultural future for both Latin America and the global
community. Our review is guided by the premise that technology must serve human
needs—enhancing the capabilities of agricultural professionals, supporting the livelihoods
of farmers, and ensuring that the benefits of innovation are shared equitably across the
global agricultural landscape.
In contrast to traditional systematic reviews that primarily synthesize evidence ad-
dressing narrowly defined research questions [15,16], this scoping review aims to provide a
broader understanding of the technological ecosystem surrounding AI-based fruit detection.
Readers should therefore interpret the present work as a comprehensive mapping of sens-
ing technologies, computational approaches, regional research priorities, methodological
gaps, and emerging opportunities rather than as a quantitative comparison of algorithmic
performance. This broader perspective enables the identification of research trends and
future directions that extend beyond conventional effectiveness-based evidence synthesis.

scoping review.

2.1. Similarities and Differences with Our Scoping Review
While previous reviews have provided valuable, albeit fragmented, insights into specific
facets of the field, they collectively fail to offer a holistic, critically comparative, and contex-
tually aware synthesis. For instance, general reviews like those by Ukwuoma et al. [18] and
Sawant et al. [20] have comprehensively cataloged deep learning techniques and their impres-

sive performance, often exceeding 99% accuracy on curated datasets. However, these works
are largely techno-centric, focusing on algorithmic proficiency without systematic examination
of the geographical, economic, or human factors that shape technological adoption. Similarly,
surveys on specific applications, such as ripeness classification [22] or citrus diseases [21],
offer deep dives into narrow sub-domains but lack the breadth to identify cross-cutting blind
spots. Furthermore, all the reviews in Table 1—including the systematic literature reviews
by Rojas Santelices et al. [19] and Espinoza et al. [25]—operate without a critical regional lens,
treating the global research landscape as a monolithic entity and thus failing to acknowl-
edge the stark disparities in resources, research priorities, and implementation contexts
that characterize different parts of the world.
In stark contrast, our scoping review differs by its critical, comparative framework
that explicitly contrasts Latin American and international research traditions. Crucially,
our scoping review adopts a broader analytical perspective that integrates technologi-
cal, geographical, and implementation-oriented dimensions. Beyond cataloguing sensing
technologies and AI models, we compare regional research ecosystems, highlight method-
ological limitations, examine deployment readiness, and discuss cross-cutting themes such
as explainable decision support, inclusivity, accessibility, and environmental sustainability.
By focusing on this regional comparison, our work provides a broader overview of the
situation in Latin America, highlighting apparent strengths in contextual adaptation and
its significant limitations. This analysis pinpoints critical areas needing further exploration,
thus covering methodological gaps prevalent in the region which may help foster a more
equitable and impactful global research agenda.
2.2. Emerging Physics-Based and Biosensing Technologies for Fruit Quality Inspection
Recent advances in food quality inspection have extended beyond conventional com-
puter vision systems toward physics-based sensing technologies capable of assessing
internal physicochemical properties without destructive sampling. Optical spectroscopy,
fluorescence imaging, hyperspectral imaging, near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy, surface
plasmon resonance (SPR), electrochemical biosensors, and dielectric sensing have emerged
as complementary approaches for evaluating fruit freshness, soluble solids content, inter-
nal bruising, microbial contamination, pesticide residues, and biochemical composition.
Unlike RGB imaging, which primarily captures external appearance, these sensing tech-
nologies provide information about internal quality attributes that are often invisible to
conventional cameras.
Artificial intelligence has increasingly been integrated with these sensing modalities to
improve feature extraction, pattern recognition, and predictive modelling. Deep learning
architectures, support vector machines, random forests, and ensemble learning algorithms
have demonstrated promising performance when processing high-dimensional spectral
or biosensing data. Consequently, the combination of advanced sensing technologies
with AI represents an emerging direction toward comprehensive, non-destructive food
quality inspection systems capable of simultaneously evaluating both external and internal
fruit characteristics.

3.1. Rationale for Selecting a Scoping Review
This study adopted a scoping review methodology because the primary objective
was to comprehensively map the current landscape of sensor technologies and artificial
intelligence applied to fruit detection, classification, and quality assessment rather than to
estimate the effectiveness of a single intervention. The identified literature encompasses
substantial heterogeneity in sensing modalities, computational algorithms, agricultural
contexts, evaluation metrics, target fruit species, and validation protocols. Such method-
ological diversity prevents meaningful statistical aggregation of results and therefore makes
a meta-analysis inappropriate.
Unlike a conventional systematic review, which generally focuses on answering nar-
rowly defined effectiveness questions, a scoping review enables the identification of tech-
nological trends, research gaps, emerging sensing modalities, geographical disparities,
and future research opportunities across a rapidly evolving multidisciplinary field. This
approach is particularly suitable for synthesizing evidence originating from computer sci-
ence, agricultural engineering, robotics, food technology, and precision agriculture, where
study designs and outcome measures differ considerably.
Consequently, the objective of this review is not to determine which algorithm per-
forms best under identical experimental conditions, but rather to provide a comprehen-
sive map of current technological developments, compare regional research trends be-
tween Latin America and the international community, identify methodological limitations,
and propose future directions for more accessible, robust, and sustainable AI-enabled
agricultural systems.
3.2. Databases and Search Strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted across Scopus and IEEE Xplore
to identify peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings relevant to sensor
technologies and artificial intelligence for fruit detection, classification, and quality as-
sessment published between January 2012 and May 2026. Google Scholar was used as a
complementary source to capture grey literature and conference proceedings not indexed in
Scopus or IEEE Xplore. All database searches were executed to ensure temporal consistency
across sources.
The search strategy employed Boolean combinations of terms related to sensor tech-
nologies, artificial intelligence, and fruit quality assessment. Given the interdisciplinary
nature of the topic, which spans computer science, engineering, agricultural science, and food
technology, Scopus and IEEE Xplore were selected to ensure coverage of both technical and
agricultural contributions. Google Scholar was used to broaden coverage and mitigate poten-
tial database bias by capturing studies indexed outside traditional disciplinary boundaries.
The search strategy incorporated terms related to sensing modalities (including com-
puter vision, machine vision, hyperspectral imaging, near-infrared spectroscopy, and RGB
imaging), computational techniques (including machine learning, deep learning, artificial
neural networks, convolutional neural networks, and YOLO), and agricultural applica-
tions (including fruit detection, fruit classification, quality assessment, ripeness detection,
and defect identification). Boolean operators were used to combine these terms, ensur-
ing comprehensive retrieval of the relevant literature. All this information is presented
in Table 2.

Given the extremely large volume of results retrieved from all databases—especially
from Google Scholar, which returned 228,000 records—a pragmatic and targeted screening
strategy was adopted, consistent with previously published scoping reviews facing similar
retrieval volumes [28,29]. Results from each database were sorted by relevance, and the
first 300 records from each source were screened based on title and abstract.
This threshold was chosen for three primary reasons.
•
First, methodological guidance for scoping reviews acknowledges that comprehensive
screening of all retrieved records is often impractical and that feasibility-based strate-
gies are both legitimate and widely adopted when clearly reported [28,30]. Manual
screening of 242,310 records would be prohibitively resource-intensive and would
exceed the capacity of a scoping review, which is designed to map the literature rather
than exhaustively synthesize all available evidence.
•
Second, relevance ranking in academic databases—particularly in Scopus and Google
Scholar, which employ citation-based algorithms—typically places the most pertinent
studies at the top of search results [31]. The methodological literature on systematic
searching has demonstrated that literature searching is subject to the law of dimin-
ishing returns; after a certain threshold, further searching yields increasingly fewer
relevant papers [32,33]. Consequently, there comes a point where the rewards of
further searching may not be worth the effort required [34].
•
Third, this approach allows for a manageable yet sufficiently representative sample to
understand the broad technological trends and regional differences in the development
of fruit detection technologies, both in Latin America and globally. As Pham et al. [29]
observed in their analysis of 272 scoping reviews, substantial variability exists in how
researchers handle large retrieval volumes, and pragmatic screening strategies are
commonplace in the methodology.
Although combining three complementary databases reduced the risk of selection bias,
screening only the first 300 relevance-ranked records from each source may have excluded
relevant studies appearing beyond this threshold. Consequently, the findings presented in
this review should be interpreted as a synthesis of the most accessible and highly ranked
evidence rather than an exhaustive representation of all published research. This limitation
should be considered when interpreting both the regional comparisons and the identified
research gaps.
The search was restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings
published in English or Spanish, ensuring accessibility and quality standards. Conference
proceedings were included given the rapid pace of technological development in this field,
where significant advances often appear first in conference venues.

deployment include the commercial sorting machine developed by Fan et al. [61], which

the infield grading and sorting system by Zhang et al. [55], validated at throughputs of

to field is technically feasible, yet they also reveal the substantial engineering effort and

Nevertheless, promising developments in edge computing, exemplified by Srinivasagan
et al. [69] and Shi et al. [45], suggest that the gap between research and practice may nar-

Orin Nano for blueberry maturity detection, demonstrating that Transformer-based architec-
tures can be effectively deployed on edge devices through model pruning and quantization
techniques. These developments are particularly significant for Latin American contexts, where
the affordability and accessibility of edge computing platforms could democratize access to
advanced AI capabilities without requiring substantial infrastructure investment.
5. Discussion
The application of sensor technologies and artificial intelligence for fruit detection,
classification, and quality assessment represents one of the most dynamic frontiers in
agricultural technology. As we examine the global landscape of research in this field, a fas-
cinating dichotomy emerges between the approaches adopted in Latin America and those
pursued in other regions of the world. This divide is not merely geographic but reflects
fundamental differences in research priorities, available resources, institutional support,
and the specific agricultural challenges that each region faces. Through a comprehensive
analysis of the scientific literature, we can trace the contours of these two technological
worlds, understanding their respective strengths, limitations, and the potential pathways
toward a more integrated and impactful future. Unless otherwise indicated, the following
interpretations represent the authors’ synthesis of the reviewed evidence and should not
be interpreted as direct findings reported by the individual primary studies.
5.1. Why a Scoping Review Provides Added Value
The added value of the present scoping review lies in its ability to integrate evidence
originating from highly heterogeneous technological domains. Rather than ranking algorithms

according to isolated performance metrics, the review suggests broader research patterns
that would remain largely invisible within a conventional effectiveness-focused systematic

deployment readiness, accessibility of AI technologies, and underexplored dimensions such
as sustainability, inclusivity, and human-centered agricultural innovation. Consequently, this
review provides researchers, policymakers, and practitioners with a strategic overview of the
field while simultaneously identifying priorities for future research.
5.2. The Latin American Approach: Practical Innovation Amidst Constraints
The research landscape in Latin America suggests a distinctive technological phi-
losophy shaped by resource awareness and a deep commitment to solving real-world
agricultural challenges. Rather than pursuing cutting-edge performance at any cost, re-
searchers across the region have cultivated a pragmatic approach that prioritizes accessibil-
ity, affordability, and contextual relevance. This pragmatic orientation has given rise to a
body of work characterized by clever adaptation of classical computer vision techniques,
the creative use of low-cost hardware platforms such as Raspberry Pi and Arduino, and a
persistent focus on crops of regional economic significance. While this approach may not
achieve the same benchmark performance as its international counterparts, it indicates
remarkable ingenuity in delivering deployable solutions that address genuine industrial
needs within realistic technological and economic constraints.
It is important to emphasize that the smaller number of Latin American studies identified
in this review should not be interpreted as a methodological imbalance but rather as an
accurate reflection of the current state of scientific production retrieved through the predefined
search strategy. This finding itself constitutes one of the principal contributions of the review,
highlighting regional disparities in research capacity and technological development.
5.2.1. A Philosophy of Pragmatic Problem-Solving
Selected Latin American research in fruit detection technologies exhibits a deeply
pragmatic philosophy. Researchers in this region have consistently demonstrated remark-
able ingenuity in developing effective solutions despite significant resource constraints.
The work emerging from countries like Ecuador, Colombia, Mexico, and Peru reflects a
fundamental understanding that agricultural technology must be accessible, affordable,
and directly relevant to local needs. This is not merely a constraint but has become a
distinctive strength, fostering a culture of innovation that prioritizes practical deployability
over technological sophistication for its own sake.
The research of [42] on dried chili pepper classification perfectly exemplifies this
approach. Rather than employing the most advanced deep learning architectures, these
researchers developed an effective artificial neural network system using straightforward
feature extraction from 8-bit grayscale histograms. The system, while achieving what
might be considered modest by international standards, directly addressed a genuine
industrial need in Mexico: the automated classification of chili peppers according to
official quality norms. This focus on solving real problems with available tools, rather
than chasing benchmark performance, represents a fundamental characteristic of Latin
American research philosophy.
Similarly, the work of [38] on pineapple ripening assessment in Colombia indicates
how sophisticated results can be achieved through clever application of relatively simple
techniques. By employing HSV color space analysis and Otsu thresholding, they achieved
high classification accuracy for Perolera pineapples. This study exemplifies how Latin
American researchers have developed deep expertise in classical computer vision tech-

5.2.2. Building Complete Systems from Accessible Components
One of the most striking features of Latin American research is the emphasis on creat-
ing complete, integrated systems using widely available, low-cost hardware. This approach
is particularly well exemplified by [37], who developed a comprehensive quality manage-
ment system for Cavendish bananas using Raspberry Pi and Arduino components. Their
work involved capturing over a thousand augmented images over six months, processing
them with a combination of classical techniques and machine learning regression mod-
els. The resulting mobile application could predict remaining shelf life in real conditions,
addressing a genuine need in Colombia’s banana export industry.
This systems-level thinking is further demonstrated by [35] in their work on passion
fruit mass and volume estimation. By combining multiple feature extraction methods with
PCA and LDA, they created a comprehensive system that could estimate both mass and
volume from digital images. The integration of these various techniques into a coherent
workflow, implemented using open-source tools like OpenCV and QT Creator, reflects the
Latin American emphasis on creating practical, deployable solutions rather than isolated
technological demonstrations.
5.2.3. The Agricultural Context: A Focus on Export Crops
The crops selected for study in Latin America reveal much about the region’s agricul-
tural priorities and economic dependencies. The focus on export-oriented fruits such as
bananas [37], pineapples [38], passion fruit [35], and chili peppers [42] reflects the impor-
tance of agricultural exports to Latin American economies. This focus has both advantages
and limitations. On one hand, it ensures that research is directly relevant to major economic
sectors and addresses genuine industrial needs. On the other hand, it may lead to a neglect
of subsistence crops and smallholder agriculture, which remain critically important for
food security and rural livelihoods in many Latin American countries.
The research on dried white mulberry by [63] represents an interesting variation on
this pattern. While conducted in Iran rather than Latin America, the approach shares
many characteristics with Latin American research: the use of accessible RGB cameras,
manual expert grading as a gold standard, and the successful application of ANNs and
SVMs to achieve perfect classification accuracy. This work indicates that the pragmatic,
resource-conscious approach characteristic of Latin American research has global relevance
for agricultural contexts where advanced sensing infrastructure is not available.
5.3. The International Approach: Pushing Technological Boundaries
The international corpus analyzed in this review should not be regarded as a homoge-
neous body of research. Rather, it encompasses studies conducted in countries with diverse
agricultural practices, economic resources, technological infrastructures, and research pri-
orities. Consequently, the patterns identified in this section should be interpreted as trends
emerging from the reviewed literature rather than as universal characteristics of all in-
ternational research. This distinction is particularly important because many small- and
medium-scale producers outside Latin America also face challenges related to affordability,
accessibility, local adaptation, and limited technological resources, despite operating in
different geographical contexts.
In contrast to the pragmatic resource-conscious philosophy that characterizes Latin
American research, the international research community—particularly in technologically
advanced nations and rapidly developing economies like China—has embraced a paradigm
defined by technological ambition and boundary-pushing innovation. This approach is

5.3.1. The Pursuit of Cutting-Edge Performance
Research conducted outside Latin America, particularly in technologically advanced
nations and rapidly developing economies like China, exhibits a markedly different charac-
ter. Here, the emphasis is squarely on pushing the boundaries of what is technologically
possible, employing the most sophisticated algorithms and sensing modalities available.
This research culture is supported by greater access to computational resources, larger
research budgets, and stronger connections to commercial technology sectors.
The work on vision transformers (ViT) by [58,60] exemplifies this pursuit of cutting-
edge performance. Rather than relying on classical computer vision techniques, these
researchers have adopted the latest deep learning architectures, originally developed for
general computer vision tasks, and adapted them for fruit quality assessment. The Multi-
Task NoisyViT developed by Esfandiari Fard and colleagues, which achieves near-perfect
accuracy for both fruit type classification and freshness detection, represents the kind
of sophisticated multi-task learning that is only possible with substantial computational
resources and large datasets.
Similarly, the EMBS-DETR architecture developed by Yan et al. [52] for tomato maturity
detection indicates how researchers outside Latin America are able to combine multiple ad-
vanced techniques—frequency-domain convolution, parameter-free attention mechanisms,
and multi-scale feature fusion—to achieve exceptional performance while simultaneously
addressing computational efficiency. This simultaneous pursuit of both accuracy and ef-
ficiency reflects a maturity in the research ecosystem that allows for optimization across
multiple dimensions.
5.3.2. The Integration of Robotics and Automation
Perhaps the most dramatic distinction between international and Latin American
research lies in the integration of fruit detection technologies with robotics and automation
systems. While Latin American research has largely focused on quality assessment as a
standalone task, international research increasingly addresses the complete agricultural
automation pipeline from field detection to robotic harvesting and sorting.
The lithic picking robot developed by Wang et al. [50] represents this integrated
approach in its most sophisticated form. This system combines YOLOv8-based fruit
and branch segmentation with binocular stereo vision for 3D localization, D-H inverse
kinematics for robotic arm control, and an innovative air-blowing obstruction removal
unit. The result is a complete robotic system capable of navigating natural environments,
identifying obstructions, and successfully harvesting fruit clusters. This indicates that such
integrated systems are approaching practical viability.
The robotic apple sorting system developed by Zhang et al. [55] similarly indicates the
power of integrating sensing with physical automation. Their pitch-variable screw conveyor
system, combined with machine vision for size and color grading, achieved remarkable
sorting accuracy exceeding 99% even at high throughputs. This level of performance,

5.3.3. Sensor Fusion: Combining Multiple Modalities
International research has also led the way in combining multiple sensing modalities
to achieve more comprehensive fruit assessment. While Latin American research has
primarily relied on conventional RGB imaging, international studies increasingly incorpo-
rate near-infrared spectroscopy, hyperspectral imaging, 3D scanning, and other advanced
sensing techniques.
The work of Zhao et al. [56] on apple internal quality assessment exemplifies this multi-
modal approach. By combining SSD-based machine vision for target detection with Vis/NIR
spectroscopy for internal quality measurement, they created a robotic system capable of
simultaneously grasping fruit, detecting spectral characteristics, and classifying by soluble
solids content. The integration of these complementary sensing modalities allowed for com-
prehensive quality assessment, including both external appearance and internal properties.
Similarly, Hu et al. [64] demonstrated the power of 3D sensing for bruise detection in
apples. By converting irregular 3D mesh data from NIR scanning into 2D feature maps, they
were able to apply state-of-the-art CNNs to the classification problem, achieving exceptional
accuracy. This innovative approach to data representation allowed them to leverage the
full power of deep learning for a fundamentally 3D sensing problem, highlighting how
advanced sensing technologies enable new analytical approaches.
Future research should move beyond conventional RGB image analysis toward
multimodal sensing systems integrating computer vision with spectroscopy, biosensing,
and other physics-based sensing technologies. Such sensor fusion approaches could sub-
stantially improve robustness by simultaneously evaluating external appearance, internal
physiological conditions, and biochemical composition. Artificial intelligence will play
a central role in integrating heterogeneous data streams into unified decision-support
systems suitable for precision agriculture and automated quality inspection.
5.3.4. Edge Computing and Deployment Readiness
A notable trend in international research is the growing emphasis on making sophisti-
cated AI systems deployable on edge computing devices. This focus on practical deployment,
while still requiring substantial technical sophistication, represents an important convergence
with the pragmatic concerns that have always motivated Latin American research.
The work of Shi et al. [45] on blueberry maturity detection exemplifies this trend.
By applying random channel pruning and TensorRT FP16 quantization, they reduced
the model size and achieved real-time inference on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano. This
remarkable efficiency allows the system to operate in natural orchard environments with
limited computational resources, bringing sophisticated AI capabilities to the field.
Similarly, the TinyML-based shelf life estimator developed by [69] indicates how
extreme efficiency can be achieved. By deploying a neural network regression model on
an Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense microcontroller, they created a handheld device that can
predict date fruit freshness with remarkable accuracy using minimal memory and power
consumption. This approach represents a vision of truly democratized agricultural AI that
could have enormous impact in both developed and developing economies.
While cloud-based AI platforms provide virtually unlimited computational resources,
they frequently depend on stable Internet connectivity, centralized data transmission,
and remote processing. These requirements are often difficult to satisfy in agricultural
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15080518

environments, particularly in rural regions where network infrastructure may be limited
or intermittent.
Edge computing addresses these limitations by executing AI inference directly on
embedded devices located near the sensing platform, such as drones, mobile robots,
autonomous tractors, smartphones, Raspberry Pi systems, NVIDIA Jetson modules,
or TinyML microcontrollers. By eliminating continuous communication with remote
servers, edge computing substantially reduces inference latency, communication costs,
and bandwidth requirements while improving data privacy and operational reliability.
Another important advantage of edge computing is its ability to provide real-time
decision support. Fruit harvesting, robotic manipulation, grading, and quality inspection
frequently require immediate responses that cannot tolerate delays associated with cloud
communication. Local inference therefore enables rapid detection of fruit maturity, defects,
and harvesting readiness while simultaneously reducing dependence on Internet availability.
From a sustainability perspective, edge computing also contributes to lower energy
consumption by reducing continuous data transmission between sensing devices and
cloud servers. Furthermore, distributed processing improves system resilience because
agricultural operations can continue even during temporary communication failures. Nev-
ertheless, cloud computing remains valuable for computationally intensive tasks such
as large-scale model training, centralized data management, and long-term agricultural
analytics. Consequently, future intelligent agricultural systems will likely adopt hybrid
cloud–edge architectures that combine the strengths of both paradigms.
5.4. Critical Analysis: Strengths, Weaknesses, and Blind Spots
Having examined the distinct technological philosophies and research priorities that
characterize Latin American and international approaches to fruit detection, it is essential
to subject both traditions to critical scrutiny. Each approach possesses genuine strengths
that deserve recognition—the Latin American model’s commitment to accessibility and con-
textual relevance, and the international model’s technological sophistication and systems
integration. Yet, both traditions also face significant limitations that constrain their impact
and reach. More troubling still, both share critical blind spots that have been systematically
neglected across the literature: the near-total absence of attention to human factors such
as AI-assisted decision support, the limited consideration of inclusivity and accessibility
for diverse user populations, and the persistent failure to address the environmental sus-
tainability of AI-based agricultural systems. This critical analysis seeks to illuminate these
strengths, weaknesses, and omissions, providing a foundation for charting a more balanced
and responsible path forward.
5.4.1. The Latin American Model: Strengths and Limitations
The Latin American approach to fruit detection research possesses distinctive strengths
that deserve recognition. First and foremost, the focus on practical, deployable solutions
using accessible technology ensures that research outcomes can actually benefit agricultural
practitioners. The systems developed by researchers like [35,37] are not merely academic
exercises but represent genuine attempts to address industry needs within realistic techno-
logical and economic constraints.
Second, the depth of expertise in classical computer vision techniques that Latin
American researchers have developed is itself a valuable resource. These foundational
techniques remain relevant, particularly for applications where large training datasets
or substantial computational resources are not available. The ability to achieve high
accuracy with relatively simple approaches, as demonstrated by [38,39], represents a form
of technological wisdom that should not be undervalued.

Third, the emphasis on crops of regional economic importance ensures that research
directly benefits local agricultural sectors. This contextual relevance is often lacking in
international research, which may focus on crops or conditions that are not representative
of agricultural realities in developing economies.
However, the Latin American approach also faces significant limitations. The relative
scarcity of advanced sensing equipment—hyperspectral cameras, NIR spectrometers, 3D
scanners—limits the range of problems that can be addressed. While remarkable results
can be achieved with conventional RGB imaging, certain quality attributes can only be
assessed with more sophisticated sensing modalities.
Additionally, the smaller datasets and simpler models used in much Latin American
research may limit generalizability. Systems developed with hundreds or thousands of sam-
ples may not perform as robustly when deployed in diverse conditions as systems trained
on tens of thousands of images from multiple environments. The challenge of achieving
reliable performance across the full range of natural variability remains a significant hurdle.
5.4.2. The International Model: Achievements and Excesses
The international approach to fruit detection research has achieved remarkable tech-
nological feats that should not be dismissed as mere academic exercises. The development
of architectures like [52] EMBS-DETR and [45] BR-DETR-Prune, achieving exceptional
accuracy with efficient deployment, represents genuine engineering achievement. These
systems are approaching the point where practical deployment in commercial agriculture
becomes feasible.
The integration of robotics with sensing technologies, as demonstrated by [50,55],
represents perhaps the most significant advance in agricultural automation. These systems
demonstrate that complete automation of harvesting, sorting, and quality assessment
is technically achievable, with profound implications for agricultural labor, food waste
reduction, and supply chain efficiency.
However, the international approach is not without its excesses. The pursuit of
incremental improvements in classification accuracy, often at substantial computational cost,
raises questions about the practical value of such efforts. When a system already achieves
high accuracy, is the additional research effort required to reach even higher performance
genuinely worthwhile? In many cases, the constraints on practical deployment are not
technological but economic, social, and institutional. Moreover, the high accuracy values
reported throughout this review should not be interpreted as the performance of a single
unified detection model. Instead, they represent outcomes reported independently by the
original studies under their respective experimental conditions. Therefore, the objective of
this review is to critically compare published evidence rather than to estimate the predictive
performance of a novel AI algorithm.
There is also a risk that the international research agenda becomes disconnected from
the needs of the majority of the world’s agricultural producers. The focus on high-value
crops, advanced sensing equipment, and sophisticated automation may produce solutions
that are inaccessible to smallholders and farmers in developing economies. This disconnect,
if not addressed, threatens to exacerbate agricultural inequalities rather than reduce them.
5.4.3. Research Funding as a Driver of Technological Development
One factor that may partially explain the technological differences observed between
Latin American and international studies is the availability of research funding. Al-
though a formal quantitative comparison of funding sources was beyond the scope of
this review, qualitative examination of the included studies suggests that many inter-
national projects benefit from large-scale governmental programs, national innovation
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15080518

Computers 2026, 15, 518
28 of 44
agencies, and industry-supported initiatives. Such financial support facilitates access to ad-
vanced sensing equipment, robotic platforms, high-performance computing infrastructure,
and multidisciplinary research teams.
Conversely, several Latin American studies emphasize the development of affordable
solutions based on commercially available hardware, including Raspberry Pi, Arduino,
conventional RGB cameras, and open-source software frameworks. Rather than repre-
senting a technological weakness, this strategy reflects an adaptation to local economic
conditions and indicates a strong commitment to developing deployable solutions suitable
for regional agricultural contexts.
Future systematic reviews could explicitly extract funding information and quantita-
tively investigate possible relationships between funding sources, technological sophistica-
tion, publication impact, and deployment readiness across different geographical regions.
5.4.4. Shared Blind Spots
Remarkably, both Latin American and international research share some significant
blind spots. The near-total neglect of AI-assisted decision support in the technical literature,
even as a human factors consideration, represents a striking omission. Given that the
ultimate goal of agricultural automation is to augment human capabilities, attention to
how such systems affect and interact with human competencies—including mathematical
and analytical skills—seems essential.
Similarly, the limited attention to inclusivity and accessibility in technical development
is troubling. The observation that only two studies address learning difficulties among
students or agricultural professionals suggests that the human dimensions of technology
adoption are being overlooked. If automated fruit detection systems are to be adopted by
diverse populations with varying educational backgrounds and abilities, their design must
account for human factors beyond simple usability.
The environmental sustainability of AI-based agricultural systems is another area
of neglect. While many studies demonstrate impressive performance, few consider the
energy consumption of training and deploying AI models, the lifecycle environmental
impacts of electronic components, or the potential for technology to enable more sustainable
agricultural practices. As the environmental costs of AI become increasingly recognized,
this blind spot will demand urgent attention.
5.5. Why High Laboratory Accuracy Does Not Always Translate to Field Performance
Although many reviewed studies reported classification accuracies exceeding 95%,
these values were frequently obtained using carefully curated datasets collected under
controlled laboratory conditions. Such datasets often exhibit homogeneous illumination,
standardized camera positioning, limited background complexity, and balanced class
distributions. Consequently, trained models learn highly discriminative features that may
not remain robust under real agricultural conditions.
Field deployment introduces substantially greater variability, including changing sun-
light intensity, shadows, rain, dust, wind-induced fruit motion, partial occlusions caused by
leaves or branches, variable viewing angles, differences in fruit maturity, seasonal changes,
sensor noise, and camera calibration inconsistencies. These factors collectively generate
distribution shifts between training and operational environments, causing degradation of
model performance.
Another important challenge concerns dataset representativeness. Many publicly
available fruit datasets contain thousands of carefully labelled images captured under ideal
acquisition conditions but fail to represent the full diversity encountered in commercial
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15080518

29 of 44
orchards. Consequently, models frequently exhibit overfitting to laboratory environments
despite excellent validation metrics.
Robust deployment therefore requires not only larger datasets but also more repre-
sentative datasets collected under diverse environmental conditions. Data augmentation,
domain adaptation, transfer learning, self-supervised learning, continual learning, and mul-
timodal sensor fusion have emerged as promising strategies for improving generalization
across heterogeneous agricultural scenarios.
Future research should prioritize long-term field validation involving multiple or-
chards, seasons, climatic conditions, fruit varieties, and imaging devices. Benchmark
datasets should progressively evolve from laboratory collections toward realistic agricul-
tural datasets capable of better representing operational deployment conditions.
5.6. The Path Forward: Integration, Democratization, and Human-Centered Design
The critical analysis of Latin American and international research traditions suggests
not a hierarchy of superior and inferior approaches, but rather complementary strengths
that, if thoughtfully integrated, could transform the field of fruit detection technology.
The path forward lies not in one paradigm supplanting the other, but in a deliberate synthe-
sis that harnesses international technical sophistication while preserving Latin American
commitments to accessibility and contextual relevance. This vision of integration rests
on three interconnected pillars: the democratization of advanced technologies through
knowledge transfer and edge computing, the continued cultivation of locally adapted
solutions that address regional agricultural needs, and a fundamental reorientation toward
human-centered design that prioritizes educational outcomes, inclusivity, and environ-
mental sustainability. By pursuing this integrated approach, researchers can develop
technologies that are not merely more accurate or efficient, but genuinely transformative
for agricultural communities worldwide.
5.6.1. Technology Democratization and Knowledge Transfer
The most promising future direction lies in democratizing the advanced technologies
developed in international research and making them accessible to the Latin American
research community and agricultural sector. The recent trend toward edge computing
and model efficiency, exemplified by [45,69], is particularly encouraging in this regard.
These developments show that sophisticated AI can be deployed on increasingly afford-
able hardware, making advanced sensing and analysis capabilities accessible to resource-
constrained users.
Knowledge distillation, as demonstrated by [44] in their work using YOLOv8, offers a
pathway for transferring capabilities from large models to lightweight implementations.
By using a larger teacher model to guide a smaller student model, researchers can achieve
performance approaching that of state-of-the-art systems with dramatically reduced com-
putational requirements. This approach could allow Latin American researchers to leverage
the developments of the international research community while operating within their
own resource constraints.
5.6.2. Contextual Adaptation and Indigenous Innovation
However, technology transfer is not sufficient. The Latin American research commu-
nity must continue to develop locally adapted solutions that address the specific needs of
the region’s agricultural sectors. The focus on regionally important crops, the use of acces-
sible hardware, and the integration of systems for real agricultural contexts are strengths
that should be preserved and enhanced.
There is particular opportunity in developing open-source platforms and shared
datasets that can support research across the region. The success of open-source tools

like OpenCV and Python libraries in Latin American research indicates the power of
collaborative, accessible resources. Building on this foundation, regional research networks
could create shared repositories for agricultural imagery, develop standardized benchmarks,
and coordinate research efforts to maximize impact.
5.6.3. Human-Centered AI, Inclusivity, and Sustainability
Although considerable progress has been achieved in improving the technical perfor-
mance of AI-enabled fruit detection systems, future agricultural innovation should not be
evaluated solely according to prediction accuracy. Intelligent sensing technologies must
also address broader societal, economic, and environmental challenges to ensure long-term
adoption and sustainable impact.
Human-centered AI represents an important research direction because agricultural
practitioners require more than numerical predictions. Farmers, inspectors, and supply chain
operators benefit from interpretable decision-support systems capable of explaining why fruits
are classified into particular quality categories. Such explainability improves user confidence,
facilitates technology adoption, and supports transparent agricultural decision-making.
Inclusivity is equally important because many agricultural regions, particularly in devel-
oping countries, have limited access to high-performance computing infrastructure, expensive
sensing equipment, and specialized technical expertise. Consequently, future intelligent agri-
cultural systems should be designed to operate on affordable hardware platforms, support
multiple languages, accommodate users with different levels of digital literacy, and remain
accessible to smallholder farmers as well as large agricultural enterprises.
Environmental sustainability also represents a fundamental consideration for next-
generation AI-enabled agriculture. Intelligent sensing systems can contribute to reducing
food waste through earlier detection of defects and optimal harvesting decisions while si-
multaneously decreasing unnecessary pesticide application, improving resource utilization,
reducing transportation losses, and supporting precision agriculture practices. Further-
more, energy-efficient AI models and edge computing architectures contribute to reducing
the environmental footprint associated with large-scale artificial intelligence deployment.
Future research should therefore evaluate intelligent agricultural systems using mul-
tidimensional criteria that extend beyond predictive accuracy, including interpretability,
accessibility, affordability, environmental impact, deployment readiness, and long-term
societal benefits.
6. Limitations of This Scoping Review
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this
scoping review.
•
First, the evidence base was characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity.
The included studies differed considerably in sensing technologies, computational ap-
proaches, target fruit species, dataset characteristics, validation protocols, evaluation
metrics, and experimental settings. This diversity precluded direct quantitative com-
parison across studies and made a formal meta-analysis inappropriate. Consequently,
the present review provides a qualitative synthesis of the available evidence rather
than a statistical comparison of algorithmic performance.
•
Second, most included studies evaluated their proposed methods using curated lab-
oratory datasets or controlled experimental environments. Although these studies
frequently reported high predictive performance, such results may not fully reflect the
variability encountered under real agricultural conditions, including changing illu-
mination, occlusion, weather conditions, fruit maturity variability, and differences in
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15080518

Computers 2026, 15, 518
31 of 44
acquisition hardware. Therefore, reported performance metrics should be interpreted
with appropriate caution.
•
Third, the geographical distribution of the included studies was uneven, with only
eight studies originating from Latin America. Accordingly, the regional comparisons
presented in this review should be interpreted as patterns emerging from the available
evidence rather than as definitive representations of all research conducted in Latin
America or internationally.
•
Fourth, although a comprehensive search strategy was implemented across Scopus,
IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar, the exceptionally large number of retrieved records
(242,310) required the adoption of a pragmatic screening strategy in which only the first
300 relevance-ranked records from each database were screened. While this approach
has been adopted in previous scoping reviews facing similarly large retrieval volumes,
potentially relevant studies appearing beyond this threshold may have been missed.
Consequently, the present review should be interpreted as a synthesis of the most
highly ranked and accessible evidence rather than as an exhaustive representation of
the entire body of published literature.
•
Finally, although this review identifies emerging themes related to AI-assisted decision
support, inclusivity, environmental sustainability, and human-centered system design,
these aspects were explicitly addressed in only a limited number of the included
studies. Therefore, these observations should be interpreted as emerging research
opportunities rather than definitive conclusions regarding the current state of the field.
Despite these limitations, the authors believe that the present scoping review makes a
meaningful contribution to understanding the technological landscape of fruit detection
and quality assessment, the regional disparities in research capacity and priorities, and the
critical gaps that must be addressed to achieve a more equitable, sustainable, and impactful
agricultural technology future. These limitations should not be viewed as fatal flaws but
rather as inherent characteristics of scoping review methodology and as opportunities
for future research to address the identified gaps with more rigorous, comprehensive,
and quantitative approaches.
7. Conclusions and Future Work
7.1. Conclusions
This scoping review critically examined 35 studies on sensor technologies and artificial
intelligence for fruit detection, classification, and quality assessment, synthesizing evidence
from both Latin American and international research traditions. The analysis suggests a
rich and diverse technological landscape, yet one characterized by significant disparities in
resources, priorities, and research cultures that have profound implications for agricultural
development worldwide. Importantly, the regional differences identified in this review
should not be interpreted as rigid distinctions between Latin America and the rest of the
world. Similar technological, economic, and infrastructural challenges are also encountered
by many smallholder and medium-scale producers across Europe, Asia, Africa, and other
regions. Consequently, affordable, adaptable, and human-centered AI solutions represent a
global priority rather than a challenge unique to any particular geographical area.
The contrast between Latin American and international approaches to fruit detection
technology is not a competition between inferior and superior methods, but a reflection
of different contexts, priorities, and constraints. Latin American research tends to empha-
size developing accessible, practical, and contextually relevant solutions, demonstrating
remarkable ingenuity with limited resources. International research pushes the boundaries
of what is technologically possible, creating the sophisticated algorithms and integrated
systems that represent the future of agricultural automation. The greatest opportunities lie
https://doi.org/10.3390/computers15080518

not in one approach dominating the other, but in their integration. The democratization of
international research advances, the contextual adaptation of technologies for local needs,
and the mutual recognition of strengths and limitations can create a virtuous cycle of inno-
vation. By combining international technical sophistication with Latin American pragmatic
problem-solving, we can develop fruit detection technologies that are both powerful and
accessible, sophisticated and sustainable, advanced and human-centered.
The ultimate goal is not simply better classification accuracy or more efficient automa-
tion, but the transformation of agricultural practices in ways that enhance food security,
economic development, and environmental sustainability. Achieving this goal will require
the full participation of researchers from both Latin America and the international commu-
nity, working together to ensure that the benefits of sensor technology and AI are shared
equitably across the global agricultural landscape. Future AI-enabled agricultural platforms
are expected to increasingly adopt hybrid edge–cloud architectures capable of combining
real-time local inference with centralized model training and long-term data analytics.
7.2. Future Work
Several limitations of this scoping review should be acknowledged. First, although the
multi-database search strategy and relevance-based screening were designed to balance
comprehensiveness with feasibility, the screening of only the first 300 relevance-ranked
records from each database may have excluded relevant studies. While previous method-
ological work supports this approach for mature research topics, the potential for missing
less-cited but relevant studies cannot be excluded. Second, the present review did not
perform a formal meta-analysis, and the reported performance metrics should therefore
be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive comparative evidence. Third, research
funding information was not systematically extracted during protocol development, pre-
cluding a statistical comparison of financial support mechanisms between Latin American
and international research. Future reviews could address these limitations by employing
more comprehensive screening strategies and including formal funding analyses .
