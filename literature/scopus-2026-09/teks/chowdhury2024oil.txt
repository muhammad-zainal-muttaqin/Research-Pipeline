Oil Palm Fresh Fruit Branch Ripeness 
Detection Using YOLOV6 Algorithm 
Alvi Khan Chowdhury, Wan Zailah Binti Wan Said, 
and Sarah ‘Atifah Saruchi 
Abstract Malaysia is one of the largest palm oil producers and exporters in the 
world. As a result, the efﬁcient production of palm oil plays a major role in the 
Malaysian economy. Currently, oil palm fresh fruit branches (FFBs) are harvested 
by human graders at oil palm plantations based on the fruit surface colour and the 
number of loose fruits on the ground as a measure of the ripeness level. However, 
solely relying on human graders would result in misclassiﬁed bunches due to factors 
such as the position of the FFBs on the trees, the height of the trees and incorrect 
count of the number of loose fruits on the ground. Therefore, this study proposed an 
automated oil palm FFBs ripeness detection via a deep learning algorithm, namely 
You Only Look Once Version 6 (YOLOV6). YOLOV6 is used to detect oil palm 
FFB ripeness levels based on the surface colour of the oil palm fruits. Real images 
of FFBs at oil palm plantations are taken to be classiﬁed into four classes: unripe, 
underripe, ripe, and overripe. Each class differs from other classes of oil palm FFBs 
based on their colour. The datasets are trained using two YOLOV6 models which are 
YOLOV6s (small) and YOLOV6m (medium). The proposed algorithm performance 
is analysed based on the precision, recall, F1 score, mean average precision, training 
time, inference speed, frames per second and non-maximum suppression time. The 
YOLOV6m model outperformed the YOLOV6s model, according to the investiga-
tion. For the YOLOV6m model using 100 training epochs, the achieved precision, 
recall, f1 score, mAP(50), and mAP(50–95) is 36.9%, 30%, 33.1%, 36.9%, and 
16.5%, respectively. The YOLOV6m model automatically detected oil palm FFBs 
accurately with a predicted bounding box including objectness score for all the four 
classes of palm oil fresh fruit branches which includes unripe FFB, underripe FFB, 
ripe FFB, and overripe FFB, respectively. It is expected that the ﬁnding of this study 
can be applied as a real implementation at an oil palm plantation in future.

Introduction 
The demand for food, cosmetics, chemicals, and fuel goods, which are in high demand 
due to a population that is always expanding, is driving the continued growth in the 
global use of palm oil. Oil palm is the ﬁnest choice among the crops for the vegetable 
oil industry due to its outstanding land use efﬁciency (4–5 tonnes of oil per hectare) 
and extended life cycle (30 years). Compared to rapeseed and soybean, oil palm 
produces 3.5 and 10 times more vegetable oil, respectively. Because of its high 
production and small land use demand, palm oil is a superior oil crop [1]. 
Figure 1 demonstrates the predicted palm oil production graph in Malaysia until 
2035, and this graph was demonstrated by authors in [2]. The majority of palm oil is 
produced in Malaysia and Indonesia. Despite having its roots in West Africa, oil palm 
has become widely grown in these nations because of their exceptionally favourable 
climate and soils. At 115 and 98 tons/year, respectively, Indonesia and Malaysia 
produced the most fresh fruit bunches (FFB) from oil palms in 2018. In 2018, the 
combined FFB output of the two nations accounted for 78% of global output and 85% 
of output in the top ten producing nations. As a result, these two nations provided 
85% of the world’s palm oil, which is their primary source of revenue and a means 
of eliminating poverty. Oil palm ﬁelds in Indonesia and Malaysia have grown over 
the past thirty years as a result of these socioeconomic advantages [1].
Nowadays, oil palm fresh fruit branches (FFBs) are harvested by human graders 
as per the fruit surface colour and also the number of loose fruits on the ground as 
a measure of the ripeness level. However, relying heavily on human graders would 
result in misclassiﬁed bunches due to factors such as the position of the FFBs on 
the trees as some of the FFBs might be hidden behind the branches of the trees, 
the height of the trees as on taller trees the FFBs might not be clearly visible to 
the human graders, unclear vision because of the surrounding weather, lightning 
conditions, incorrect count of the number of loose fruits on the ground, and so on. 
These misclassiﬁcations might result in the harvesting of unripe FFBs, which as 
a result would reduce the proﬁts of the oil palm plantation companies due to the 
production of low-quality palm oil [3]. 
In order to maximise the production and proﬁts of palm oil companies in Malaysia, 
this study proposed to take real-time images from plantations in Malaysia and apply 
the You Only Look Once Version 6 (YOLOV6) algorithm to detect oil palm FFB 
ripeness levels based on the surface colour of the oil palm fruits. The oil palm FFBs 
dataset would be classiﬁed into four classes, namely unripe, underripe, ripe, and 
overripe. Each class differs from other classes of oil palm FFBs based on their colour 
[4]. The collected datasets are trained using two YOLOV6 models, namely YOLOV6s 
and YOLOV6m. Then, a comparison of precision, recall, F1 score, mean average 
precision, training time, inference speed, frames per second, and non-maximum

Literature Review 
A computer vision method called object detection recognises and categorises a 
speciﬁc object in a speciﬁc environment. The main objective of object detection 
is to locate instances of each object in digital images or in real-world situations, 
separate them, and analyse their necessary elements for real-time predictions [5]. 
A subset of standard CNN vision algorithms is image localization. With discrete 
numbers, these algorithms forecast classes. When drawing a bounding box around 
a target object, the algorithm in object localization predicts a set of four numbers, 
namely the x coordinate, y coordinate, height, as well as width [6]. 
Image localization is essentially a regression problem, with the output being the 
x and y coordinates surrounding the desired object to create bounding boxes. A 
picture is an input. The output encircles a desired object with numbers for “x,” “y,” 
height, and width. Object detection is a challenging issue that combines the ideas of 
classiﬁcation and localization in images [6]. 
In the last decade starting from 2011 to 2020, a lot of research papers on oil 
palm fruits using deep learning had been published by researchers worldwide. Major 
deep learning algorithms applied in the oil palm industry include algorithms like

A precise CNN model enables the execution of difﬁcult tasks. The model recog-
nises an object in a test image by assessing a classiﬁer for that speciﬁc object 
across numerous positions and scales. Once the target object gets spotted, a predicted 
bounding box shall display in the scene. Furthermore, a deep CNN model can detect a 
wide range of object classiﬁcations. Multiple convolutional layers and pooling layers 
are included in the deep CNN model. The massive amount of feature maps could aid in 
identifying additional features in an image, such as edges and lines. AlexNet, ResNet, 
and DenseNet are examples of well-known pre-trained CNN models. The CNN 
model’s capability would be used by the autonomous harvesting system to detect 
other objects such as stalks and fronds. Meanwhile, advancements in the graphic 
processing unit (GPU) speed up the computer graphics workload, allowing the CNN 
model to operate in real time. With the introduction of “You Only Look Once” 
(YOLO), the goal is to improvise on real-time object detection. YOLO computes 
several bounding boxes and predicts class probabilities for those boxes using a 
single neural network for each box. While training on entire images, YOLO directly 
enhances detection performance. When compared to traditional object identiﬁcation 
approaches, the uniﬁed framework has various advantages [8]. 
YOLOV6 was released to the deep learning community in June 2022. It was a 
state-of-the-art innovation in the ﬁeld of computer vision at that time. To create 
the YOLOV6 algorithm, the YOLOV6 developers modiﬁed the previous version 
network’s backbone and neck to EfﬁcientRep Backbone and Rep-PAN Neck. After 
that, various features were separated from the ﬁnal head by decoupling the network 
head [9]. The YOLOV6 algorithm signiﬁcantly outperformed the YOLOV5 algo-
rithm in a number of areas, including the ability to detect small objects, anchor-free 
model training, and increased stability and ﬂexibility [10]. 
In Fig. 2, the YOLOV6 framework architecture shall be observed. As previously 
mentioned, the YOLOV6 architecture is divided into three parts namely EfﬁcientRep 
Backbone, Rep-PAN Neck, and ﬁnally, Efﬁcient Decoupled Head. The backbone 
network design plays a key role in the efﬁciency and effectiveness of the detection 
model.
In the case of the YOLOV6 backbone, the researchers designed an efﬁcient re-
parameterisable backbone later named the EfﬁcientRep backbone. For the small 
models of the YOLOV6 algorithm, the major backbone component was RepBlock 
during the training phase. Furthermore, during the inference phase, each RepBlock

gets converted to stacks of 3 × 3 convolutional layers named as RepConv with the 
ReLU activation function. The researchers discovered that the cost of computation 
and the number of parameters in the single-path plain network increase exponen-
tially as the model capacity is further increased. Hence, for building the medium 
and large models of the YOLOV6 algorithm, a revised CSPStackRep was utilised 
[9]. The CSPStackRep consists of three 1 × 1 convolution layers as well as a 
stack of sub-blocks which consists of two RepVGG blocks [11] or RepConv with a 
residual connection. In order to boost performance without excessive computational 
costs, a cross-stage partial (CSP) connection was adopted. It has a more concise 
outlook and addresses the equilibrium between speed and accuracy when compared 
to CSPRepResStage [12]. 
In the case of the YOLOV6 Neck, the researchers adopted the modiﬁed PAN 
topology [13] from both YOLOV4 [14] and YOLOV5 [15] as the base of their 
detection neck. Furthermore, they replaced the CSPBlock utilised in YOLOV5 with 
RepBlock for the small YOLOV6 models and CSPStackRep Block for the large 
YOLOV6 models and thereafter, adjusted the width and depth accordingly. As a 
result, the researchers named the neck of the YOLOV6 the Rep-PAN [9]. 
In the case of the YOLOV6 Head, the researchers adopted a hybrid-channel 
strategy to make a better efﬁcient decoupled head compared to the FCOS [16] and 
the YOLOX [17]. In speciﬁc terms, they reduced the number of the middle 3 × 3 
convolution layers to only one. The width of the YOLOV6 head was jointly scaled 
by the width multiplier for the neck and the backbone of YOLOV6. As a result, 
these changes immensely reduced computational costs to achieve a lower inference 
latency [9]. 
The YOLOV6 algorithm family has a list of models, namely the YOLOV6n (nano) 
model, YOLOV6s (small) model, YOLOV6m (medium) model, and YOLOV6l 
(large) model. This study utilised YOLOV6s and YOLOV6m models. The single-
path structure works better for the YOLOV6s model than the multi-branch structure 
in terms of accuracy and speed. As a result, a CSPStackRep Block with a channel 
coefﬁcient of 1/2 serves as the building block for the YOLOV6s model. Despite 
having more FLOPs and parameters than the multi-branch structure, the single-path 
structure might be faster to operate because of its higher level of parallelism and 
relatively smaller memory footprint. Multi-branch structures outperform single-path 
structures for the YOLOV6m model in terms of accuracy and speed. As a result, a

Methodology 
3.1 
Dataset Collection and Labelling 
The image collection of the oil palm FFB was collected from a plantation located in 
Selangor, Malaysia. The image dataset was divided into four classes, namely unripe, 
underripe, ripe, and overripe. The total number of images collected for the research 
purpose was 80 images. The dataset was divided into two sets which were the training 
and testing datasets with a split ratio of 80% and 20%. 
Figures 3, 4, 5, and 6 illustrate the different categories of oil palm fresh fruit 
branch classes considered for the model training and evaluation processes, namely 
overripe oil palm FFB, ripe oil palm FFB, underripe oil palm FFB, and unripe oil 
palm FFB. After collecting the image dataset of oil palm FFBs, the images were 
labelled using the makesense.ai website and then converted into the YOLO format 
text ﬁles. The image bounding box label text ﬁles in the YOLO format consists of 
ﬁve parameters: class, Xcenter coordinate, Ycenter coordinate, width, and height 
[18]. The class parameter is used to recognise the desired palm oil FFB. In YOLO 
format, the class numbering starts from 0. In this case of four palm oil FFB classes, 
the class numberings are from 0 to 3, respectively, for each of the four FFB classes. 
The other four parameters, namely Xcenter coordinate, Ycenter coordinate, width, 
and height are utilised to create the bounding box around the desired object class of 
oil palm FFBs.
3.2 
Training and Evaluation Methods 
The two YOLOV6 models were trained for 100 epochs using the tesla K80 graphics 
processing unit (GPU). The batch size of the training model was set to 16 batches. 
The image size of the training model was 416 × 416. The pre-trained YOLOV6 
weights were utilised to train the models. Both models were evaluated based on the 
following parameters; the precision of the models, recall of the models, F1 scores of 
the models, mean average precision of the models, the training time of the models, 
inference speed time, frames per second and, non-maximum suppression time of 
the models, and lastly, the detection accuracy of all the models based on the unseen 
image dataset.

Results and Discussion 
Table 1 states the results obtained from the YOLOV6s and YOLOV6m trained 
models. The precision for both YOLOV6s and YOLOV6m models were 24.2% and 
36.9%, respectively. The recall for both YOLOV6s and YOLOV6m models were 
30.6% and 30%. The F1 scores for YOLOV6s and YOLOV6m were 27% and 33.1%, 
respectively. For YOLOV6s, the mean average precision is 30% for 100 epochs using 
the Tesla K80 GPU, whereas the mean average precision of YOLOV6m is 36.9% for 
100 epochs using the Tesla K80 GPU. In the case of training time, the YOLOV6m 
model took more time to train for 100 epochs than the YOLOV6s model. For infer-
ence speed, the YOLOV6s model was faster than that of the YOLOV6m model. 
For frames per second, the YOLOV6s model performed better than the YOLOV6m 
model. In the case of the non-maximum suppression time, the YOLOV6m model was 
slightly quicker than the YOLOV6s model. In the case of unseen image dataset detec-
tion, it can be stated that the YOLOV6m model performed better overall compared 
to the YOLOV6s model for three of the four categories of oil palm ripeness stages. 
Overall, the YOLOV5m model was chosen as the best model by considering all the 
factors with the major evaluation parameters being model precision, F1 score and 
the unseen images dataset detection accuracy, and ﬁnally, all these major evaluation 
parameters formerly mentioned compensate for another major evaluation parameter 
which is the frames per second parameter with a value of 54 FPS.
Figures 7, 8, 9, and 10 show the bounding box detection results on all the unseen 
datasets classes using the YOLOV6m model which are overripe oil palm FFB, ripe oil 
palm FFB, underripe oil palm FFB, and unripe oil palm FFB. Based on the precision 
result, improvement can be done by collecting more datasets for training purposes. 
Even though the current model managed to classify the ripeness level, a study on the 
enhancement of its precision value must be considered in future to produce a more 
reliable model.
