a b s t r a c t
Strawberry production in hydroponic systems requires precise and spatially explicit information on fruit load, 
maturity, and size to support harvest planning and quality control. This study presents an integrated computer 
vision and GNSS RTK framework that detects individual strawberries, estimates their maturity and physical di-
mensions, assigns fruits to operational size–maturity categories, and generates high-resolution yield maps in an 
open-ﬁeld hydroponic system. RGB-D images are processed with YOLOv8-seg and a BoT-SORT-based tracking 
module to obtain instance-level masks, from which a color-based ripeness index and 3D point clouds are derived. 
After DBSCAN-based ﬁltering, fruit length and width are computed in metric space and combined with ripeness 
percentage to assign each strawberry to one of nine operational size–maturity categories using predeﬁned thresh-
old rules. The system was evaluated in a commercial hydroponic crop in Arcabuco, Boyacá, Colombia, achieving 
accurate segmentation, with median IoU values up to 0.83 for bounding boxes and 0.71 for masks, and mean 
absolute errors of 2.51 mm in length and 1.85 mm in width with respect to Vernier caliper measurements. Yield 
maps aggregated in 1 m2 cells and by crop row revealed marked spatial variability in fruit density, maturity 
state, and size–maturity composition, allowing the identiﬁcation of zones with a high concentration of fruits 
ready for harvest versus areas dominated by immature fruits. The proposed framework provides a practical and 
low-cost decision support tool for hydroponic strawberry management and may be adapted to other high-value 
horticultural crops after recalibration under diﬀerent crop architectures, cultivars, and environmental conditions.
1.  Introduction
Accurate estimation of yield and maturity in high-value horticultural 
crops such as strawberries represents a fundamental technical and lo-
gistical challenge to improve harvest eﬃciency, reduce losses, and en-
sure product quality standards. In hydroponic systems, where plant den-
sity is higher and growing conditions are more controlled, data-driven 
planning becomes even more critical, especially in environments with 
limited labor or narrow commercial maturity windows [1]. However, 
continuous monitoring of fruit status remains predominantly manual, 
subjective, and costly.
In response to this need, the scientiﬁc community has made consider-
able progress in developing computer vision and deep learning systems 
to detect, classify, and characterize fruits across diﬀerent crops. In a pi-
oneering work, Swain et al.[2] developed a system to estimate yield in 
wild blueberries based on visual counts from images, achieving a corre-
lation of 𝑅2 = 0.97. More recently, Safre et al.[3] applied YOLOv8 and

Beyond detection, various strategies have been developed to es-

Tamilarasi and Muthulakshmi[4] proposed a system to predict in-

studies. Chu et al.[6] used a laser-camera scanner for three-dimensional 
localization in apples, achieving mean localization errors between 6.9 
and 11.2 mm, signiﬁcantly lower than those obtained with commercial 
RGB-D sensors like RealSense, which averaged 21.5mm.

Yield mapping has become a key tool in precision agriculture. Apolo-
Apolo et al.[7] used UAVs to map citrus distribution, obtaining a pre-
diction error of 6.59%, while [8] introduced a neural network that uses 
16 visual fruit descriptors from olive trees to estimate yield with only 
a 2.64% overestimation compared to actual weight. Safre et al.[3] also 
integrated YOLO and BoT-SORT for tart cherry counting, validating the 
results with real weights and achieving an average error of 10 kg per 
tree.
Despite these advances, the simultaneous integration of segmenta-
tion, maturity/size estimation, and georeferenced fruit mapping remains 
limited, especially in low-structure crops like strawberries, where fruits 
occur in various maturity stages, with high occlusion and signiﬁcant 
variability in color and size [9–11]. While some studies have applied the 
Segment Anything Model (SAM), Transformers, or multi-view strategies 
to improve segmentation and tracking [12,13], their application under 
real ﬁeld conditions and their linkage to GNSS RTK coordinates remains 
incipient.
In the speciﬁc case of hydroponic crops, the literature is even more 
scarce. Sharmin et al.[1] reviewed yield prediction models for hydro-
ponic lettuce, highlighting that most approaches lack spatial data in-
tegration, and that the production-control capabilities oﬀered by hy-
droponics have not been fully exploited by computer vision systems. 
Similarly, studies on grapes [14], tomatoes [15], and bananas [16] un-
derscore the need to model intra-plot variability in attributes such as 
maturity and weight to improve harvest planning, but these approaches 
have not been fully extended to structured hydroponic strawberry sys-
tems.
However, hydroponic strawberry crops should not be regarded 
merely as a planting method. From a computer vision and preci-
sion agriculture perspective, these systems introduce particular op-
erating conditions: high fruit and plant density, fruit overlap, par-
tial occlusion by leaves, support structures, repetitive backgrounds 
from adjacent rows, and illumination variability under open-ﬁeld con-
ditions. These characteristics increase scene complexity and compli-
cate not only individual fruit detection, but also reliable maturity 
estimation, dimension measurement, and spatial localization of each
fruit.
This work builds upon a previously developed RGB-D and GNSS RTK 
framework for hydroponic strawberry detection, segmentation, track-
ing, counting, and fruit-density yield mapping [17]. In the present study, 
that perception module is used as a validated input stage, and the main 
contribution is to extend it toward fruit-level characterization through 
maturity estimation, depth-based size measurement, operational size–
maturity categorization, and enriched yield maps.
In this context, this study proposes a ﬁeld-validated yield monitoring 
and mapping methodology for hydroponic strawberry crops. The system 
includes:
• Detection and segmentation of strawberries using deep learning 
models with RGB-D sensors.
• Estimation of fruit maturity through colorimetric analysis and fruit 
size through depth-based measurement.
• Georeferencing of each detected fruit using GNSS RTK coordinates.
• Rule-based assignment of fruits into operational size–maturity cate-
gories.
• Generation of yield maps in 1 m2 grid cells with aggregated indica-
tors per zone, including fruit count, maturity, size, and size–maturity 
composition.
This proposal addresses a key gap in the scientiﬁc literature by of-
fering a practical, automated, and adaptable tool for spatially explicit 
fruit-level monitoring in hydroponic strawberry crops.
2.  Materials and methods
Fig. 1 presents the framework devoted to strawberry feature extrac-
tion and yield mapping. Starting from RGB-D images georeferenced with 
GNSS RTK, the workﬂow is organized into ﬁve tightly coupled compo-
nents: (i) data acquisition and localization, where RGB-D crop images 
are captured and associated with precise spatial coordinates; (ii) detec-
tion and segmentation, responsible for identifying and isolating each 
strawberry as an independent instance in the images; (iii) feature ex-
traction, in which quantitative attributes are derived for each fruit, such 
as ripeness percentage and geometric dimensions; (iv) rule-based size–
maturity categorization, which integrates ripeness and size information

2.1.  Data acquisition and localization
The proposed system was implemented in an open-ﬁeld hydroponic 
strawberry crop located in Arcabuco, Boyacá, Colombia (Lat: 5°45’46.3" 
N, Lon: 73°26’46.9" W; altitude: 2739 m a.s.l.), using an Intel RealSense 
D435i RGB-D camera and an Ardusimple simpleRTK2B GNSS RTK sys-
tem operating in a Base–Rover conﬁguration. The camera and rover 
receiver were mounted on a telescopic monopod with a lateral view 
towards the crop rows, at an approximate height of 1.2 m above the 
ground, a lateral oﬀset of about 0.6 m with respect to the row, and a 
tilt angle close to 25◦. Data acquisition was carried out in the morning 
(between 8:00 and 11:00 h), under partially cloudy conditions, in or-
der to reduce abrupt changes in illumination. Under these conditions, 
the plants exhibited diﬀerent fruiting stages, with fruits of various sizes 
and ripeness levels. A total of 1626 RGB-D images were captured at a 
resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels and a frame rate of 7 fps, which allowed 
dense coverage of the crop and adequate spatial continuity for subse-
quent analysis stages.
Camera control and data ﬂow management were performed in 
Python using the pyrealsense2 library and Intel RealSense SDK 2.0 
tools. The detailed preprocessing of depth images (application of dec-
imation ﬁlters, disparity–depth transformations, spatial and temporal 
ﬁlters, and hole ﬁlling) has been described in a previous work by the 
authors on strawberry detection and counting using georeferenced RGB-
D images in the same hydroponic system [17]. In the present article, 
this preprocessing is assumed as an input stage, and one additional step 
speciﬁcally related to feature extraction is highlighted.
Fig. 2 illustrates the background removal process, where the already 
ﬁltered depth information is used to discard pixels that fall outside the 
expected range of the target crop row. Based on the depth map, a dis-
tance interval associated with the furrow where the plants and fruits of 
the analyzed row are located is deﬁned. Pixels with depth values below 
or above this interval are marked as background and masked out, thus 
removing distant rows, support structures and other elements that are 
not part of the fruit analysis. Simple morphological ﬁltering operations 
are applied to clean small disconnected regions, so that the resulting 
image mainly preserves the foliage and fruits of the row of interest. This 
procedure reduces scene complexity and improves the robustness of the 
subsequent detection, segmentation and feature extraction stages, in a 
manner consistent with other vision-based fruit yield mapping systems 
that integrate RGB-D sensors [18,19] and GNSS RTK [20,21].
In parallel with RGB-D image acquisition, localization data from 
the GNSS RTK system on the simpleRTK2B rover were continuously 
recorded. The base receiver was placed at a point with known coor-
dinates and transmitted real-time diﬀerential corrections to the rover 
through a radio link in the 902–927 MHz band. This Base–Rover conﬁgu-
ration compensates for atmospheric and geometric signal errors, achiev-
ing centimetre-level positioning accuracy. The acquisition system was 
programmed with independent execution threads for the GNSS and the 
camera, synchronized by shared timestamps so that each RGB-D image 
was associated with a corresponding GNSS RTK position.
To quantify the eﬀective accuracy of the GNSS RTK system under 
the actual operating conditions, a static experiment was carried out by 
keeping the rover at a ﬁxed point for an extended period. The resulting 
localization coordinates were represented in a planar coordinate system, 
generating a dispersion pattern around the true position. Fig. 3 shows 
the dispersion of GNSS data at a ﬁxed location, with the resulting point 
cloud and reveals a maximum radius of approximately 5.61 cm, which 
characterizes the variability of the system in Base–Rover mode. This dis-
persion is relevant because it directly propagates to the spatial accuracy 
of the fruit-level yield maps.
Although this centimeter-level accuracy characterizes the localiza-
tion of the acquisition platform and propagates to the yield maps, it 
should not be interpreted as the ability to spatially resolve adjacent fruits 
separated by only a few centimeters within the same plant or fruit clus-
ter. In this system, individual fruit separation is primarily performed 
in image space through instance segmentation and temporal tracking, 
whereas GNSS RTK provides the geospatial reference for each image 
and its associated detections. Therefore, RTK accuracy is adequate for 
the target spatial scale of this study, namely crop-row analysis and ag-
gregation in 1 m2 grid cells, but it is not intended to replace visual 
discrimination of closely spaced fruits within the canopy.
Finally, the GNSS coordinates were transformed from latitude–
longitude to a projected coordinate system in metres, speciﬁcally UTM 
(Universal Transverse Mercator), as commonly employed in precision 
agriculture applications [22,23]. This transformation allows each im-
age and each detected fruit to be located within a Cartesian reference 
frame suitable for spatial analysis and yield map generation. The acqui-
sition and localization scheme described in this section forms the basis

2.2.  Detection and segmentation
The detection and segmentation stage receives as input the RGB-D 
images that have already undergone the preprocessing and background 
removal described in the previous section. On these images, an instance 
segmentation pipeline is implemented, allowing each strawberry to be 
individually identiﬁed at the pixel level and associated with its corre-
sponding depth information and geographic location.
For strawberry detection, four variants of the YOLOv8 model with 
instance segmentation capabilities were used: YOLOv8n-seg, YOLOv8s-
seg, YOLOv8m-seg, and YOLOv8l-seg. The construction of the dataset, 
the annotation protocol, the partition into training, validation, and test 
subsets, and the training details of these models were presented in de-
tail in [17]. In the present manuscript, YOLOv8-seg is not introduced 
as the main algorithmic novelty, but as a previously developed percep-
tion module that provides fruit masks, bounding boxes, tracking iden-
tiﬁers, and georeferenced detections. The methodological contribution 
of this study is to extend this perception stage toward fruit-level fea-
ture extraction, maturity and size estimation, operational size–maturity 
categorization, and enriched yield mapping.
Dataset annotations were performed at the instance level, delineat-
ing each visible strawberry in the image with polygons that follow the 
fruit contour. From these polygons, the corresponding bounding boxes 
are also derived. This dual representation (polygon masks and bounding 
boxes) enables the evaluation of both object detection performance and 
instance segmentation quality.
During inference, each preprocessed RGB image is fed into one of 
the YOLOv8-seg models. For each detected strawberry, the network out-
puts the class label (strawberry), a conﬁdence score, a bounding box, 
and a binary mask that delineates the fruit at pixel level. The result-
ing set of detections per image constitutes the input for the temporal 
tracking module. Tracking of strawberries along the image sequence 
is performed using a tracking-by-detection strategy based on the BoT-
SORT algorithm, which associates detections across consecutive frames 
by combining motion and appearance information. Each strawberry is 
assigned a track identiﬁer (track ID) when it is detected for the ﬁrst 
time, and this identiﬁer is maintained while the fruit remains visible. If 
a track stops receiving associations for a predeﬁned number of frames, 
it is considered terminated. This mechanism prevents double counting 
when the same strawberry appears in multiple consecutive frames with 
small viewpoint changes or partial occlusions.
The Fig. 4 summarizes the overall operation of this stage. In the ini-
tial part of the ﬁgure, a set of RGB images is shown after depth ﬁltering 
and background removal, so that only the target crop row remains vis-
ible. The ﬁgure also shows the result of instance segmentation, where 
strawberries are detected with their corresponding masks and bounding 
boxes. Subsequently, the tracking process is depicted, where each straw-
berry is followed along the sequence by its track ID, ensuring that the 
system identiﬁes it as the same object in diﬀerent frames and enabling a 
unique count for each fruit. Finally, the ﬁgure illustrates the individual 
extraction of strawberries: for each detection, the RGB image is cropped 
using the mask, the aligned depth map is cropped in the same region, 
and the associated information is organized in a structured ﬁle system.
More speciﬁcally, for each detected strawberry, three main types of 
information are stored:
• The segmented color image of the fruit, obtained by cropping the 
fruit region in the RGB image using its instance mask.
• The depth map corresponding to the fruit, cropped from the original 
depth image aligned with the RGB image, using the same instance 
mask.
• The detection metadata, including the mask (as a binary image), the 
track identiﬁer (track ID), and the georeferenced location of the fruit, 
obtained from the GNSS RTK system.
The localization information of each fruit is stored in a .csv ﬁle, 
where, among other ﬁelds, the image identiﬁer, the track ID, the pro-
jected coordinates (UTM), and additional labels required for subsequent 
stages are recorded. This procedure produces a dataset of georeferenced 
strawberry instances, in which each fruit is represented by its RGB ap-
pearance, its local 3D structure (through depth), and its spatial position 
within the crop. These data form the basis for the feature extraction 
stage (Section 2.3) and the yield mapping stage (Section 2.5).

2.2.1.  Evaluation metrics
To quantitatively evaluate detection and segmentation performance, 
geometric metrics were computed on the test subset for both bound-
ing boxes and instance masks. Speciﬁcally, F1-Score, interpreted as the 
Dice coeﬃcient between the predicted and ground-truth regions, and 
Intersection over Union (IoU), which measures the overlap ratio be-
tween both regions, were used. These metrics were computed for each 
prediction–ground-truth pair and summarized using the median, mean, 
and standard deviation to characterize both typical performance and 
model variability under diﬀerent scene conﬁgurations, illumination con-
ditions, and occlusion levels.
In addition to detection and segmentation quality, the com-
putational performance of each YOLOv8-seg variant was evalu-
ated using total inference time, average inference time per image, 
frames per second (FPS), and peak GPU memory usage. These met-
rics allow the geometric accuracy and computational eﬃciency of 
YOLOv8n-seg, YOLOv8s-seg, YOLOv8m-seg, and YOLOv8l-seg to be 
compared. Fig. 5 qualitatively illustrates the comparison between 
predictions and ground-truth annotations for bounding boxes and
masks.
Overall, the detection and segmentation stage establishes the link 
between the preprocessed RGB-D sequences and the subsequent mod-
ules for feature extraction, rule-based size–maturity categorization, and 
yield mapping. This stage ensures that each strawberry is localized, seg-
mented, tracked over time, and associated with its depth information 
and georeferenced location.
2.3.  Feature extraction
For each strawberry detected and segmented in the previous stage, 
the system extracts two main groups of features:
• A ripeness indicator, associated with the predominance of red tones 
on the fruit surface.
• The physical dimensions of each strawberry (width and length, in 
millimeters), computed from the depth map and the intrinsic pa-
rameters of the depth camera.
These features are individually computed for each fruit and stored 
together with its georeferenced location, forming the basis for the subse-
quent operational size–maturity categorization and for the construction 
of yield maps.
Methodologically, feature extraction relies on two key inputs pro-
duced by the detection and segmentation stage:
• The segmented RGB image of the strawberry, where the background 
has been removed and only the fruit remains.

2.4.  Rule-based assignment of strawberry size–maturity categories
Once the features of each fruit, speciﬁcally ripeness percentage and 
physical dimensions, have been computed, each strawberry is assigned 
to one of nine operational categories that combine maturity and size in-
formation. This stage uses a transparent rule-based categorization proce-
dure, in which the extracted fruit descriptors are converted into discrete 
agronomic classes to support spatial interpretation, yield mapping, and 
harvest planning.
The categorization is based on two main axes:
• Ripeness, expressed as the percentage of ripe fruit area obtained 
from the masks of ripe and unripe regions (Section 2.3.1). From this 
continuous value, three levels are deﬁned:
– Unripe: ripeness ≤20%,
– Partially Ripe: 20% < ripeness ≤60%,
– Ripe: ripeness > 60%.
• Size, derived from the physical dimensions Fruit_Width and
Fruit_Length (in mm), as described in Section 2.3.2. Three size 
ranges are deﬁned:
– Small: width < 20 mm or length < 30 mm,
– Medium: 20 mm ≤ width < 30 mm and 30 mm ≤ length < 40 mm,
– Large: width ≥30 mm and length ≥40 mm.
Combining the three ripeness levels with the three size ranges yields 
nine size–maturity categories. Each fruit receives a category identiﬁer 
between 0 and 8, which enables aggregation by spatial cell and crop row 
during yield mapping. These categories are used as operational indica-
tors to summarize spatial crop variability, distinguishing zones domi-
nated by developing immature fruits from zones with larger ripe fruits 
of greater interest for selective harvesting.
The thresholds used in this study should be interpreted as opera-
tional criteria deﬁned for the evaluated monitoring system, rather than 
as universal commercial standards. They were selected to produce agro-
nomically interpretable groups compatible with the measurement scale 
of the RGB-D system. When the system is transferred to other production 
contexts, the thresholds can be recalibrated or aligned with cultivar-
speciﬁc traits, local commercial grading criteria, grower requirements, 
or postharvest grading standards. The distribution of fruits per category 
in the original dataset is shown in Table 1.

3.  Results and discussion
3.1.  Evaluation of performance metrics on the test dataset
The results in this section summarize the performance of the de-
tection and segmentation module used as the perception stage of the 
system. Since the original training and validation of the YOLOv8-seg 
models were previously presented, these metrics are reported here to 
contextualize the quality of the masks, bounding boxes, and detections 
that feed the new stages of this manuscript: maturity estimation, size 
measurement, rule-based size–maturity categorization, and yield map-
ping.
Table 2 summarizes the quantitative performance of the four 
YOLOv8-seg variants on the test dataset, for both bounding boxes and 
segmentation masks. The metrics reported follow the deﬁnitions intro-
duced in Section 2.2.1 and are complemented by the boxplots in Fig. 9, 
which illustrate the distribution of F1-Score and IoU values across all 
test instances.
For bounding boxes, a clear trend is observed as model complex-
ity increases from YOLOv8n-seg to YOLOv8l-seg. The median F1-Score 
increases from 0.81 to 0.90, while the median IoU rises from 0.73 to 
0.83. Similarly, the mean values improve (F1-Score from 0.78 to 0.85, 
IoU from 0.70 to 0.79), indicating that larger models not only achieve 
better best-case predictions, but also increase the average overlap be-
tween predicted and ground-truth regions. The standard deviations for 
F1-Score and IoU remain within a relatively narrow range (approxi-
mately 0.13–0.16), suggesting that these gains are not obtained at the 
cost of higher instability in more complex scenes. The upper row of Fig. 9 
shows that the interquartile ranges tend to shrink for YOLOv8m-seg and 
YOLOv8l-seg, with higher medians and fewer low outliers, consistent 
with more reliable localization of strawberry instances.
For segmentation masks, the same monotonic trend holds, although 
with slightly lower absolute values, as expected for a more demanding 
task than box-level detection. The median F1-Score for masks increases 
from 0.73 (YOLOv8n-seg) to 0.80 (YOLOv8l-seg), and the median IoU 
from 0.63 to 0.71; in parallel, the mean F1-Score and IoU improve from 
0.70 to 0.77 and from 0.61 to 0.68, respectively. These results indicate 
that the segmentation head of YOLOv8-seg can capture fruit contours 
with good accuracy despite occlusions, overlapping fruits, and appear-
ance variability. The lower row of Fig. 9 shows that, although the disper-
sion of mask-based metrics is somewhat higher than for bounding boxes, 
the distributions corresponding to YOLOv8m-seg and YOLOv8l-seg are 
clearly shifted toward higher overlap values, conﬁrming their superior 
ability to delineate the eﬀective fruit area used later for ripeness and 
size estimation.
In addition to detection and segmentation quality, Table 2 reports 
the computational performance of each model. As expected, there is 
a clear trade-oﬀ between accuracy and inference speed. For bounding 
boxes, YOLOv8n-seg and YOLOv8s-seg achieve the highest processing 
rates, with 34.48 and 35.45 FPS, and mean inference times close to 
28–29 ms per image. In contrast, YOLOv8m-seg and YOLOv8l-seg re-
duce the processing rate to 22.40 and 13.70 FPS, with inference times of 
44.63 and 73.00 ms per image. A similar behavior is observed for masks, 
where the additional cost of the segmentation head further decreases 
FPS (from 25.51 FPS for YOLOv8n-seg to 13.57 FPS for YOLOv8l-seg). 
GPU memory usage remains within moderate ranges for all conﬁgu-
rations (on the order of tens to a few hundred MB), which facilitates 
deployment on standard GPU platforms.

erating points depending on the requirements of the target system. If

resentation not only conﬁrms the location of high-load zones, but 
also reveals which types of fruits dominate in each sector. In sev-
eral dense regions, most strawberries belong to the “Unripe–Small” or 
“Unripe–Medium” categories, suggesting strong future production po-
tential rather than immediate harvest availability. Conversely, there are 
patches with moderate density but a higher presence of “Ripe–Medium” 
and “Ripe–Large” fruits, which correspond to zones of particular inter-
est for early or selective harvesting. The joint analysis of density and 
category maps allows areas with high fruit load but still immature fruits 
to be distinguished from less dense areas containing fruits closer to har-
vest.
3.3.2.  Average vs. individual ripeness
Fig. 12 compares two complementary ways of representing fruit ma-
turity. In the left panel, the Average Ripeness per Cell map associates each 
1 m2 grid cell with the mean ripe-area percentage of the fruits it con-
tains, as deﬁned in Eq. (26). This produces zones of higher or lower 
average ripeness, which can be interpreted as zones of the ﬁeld that are 
phenologically ahead of or behind the rest of the plot.
The right panel shows the Individual Ripeness map, where each straw-
berry is displayed at its georeferenced position and colored according 
to its ripeness percentage, using the same color scale as in the cell-
based map. It becomes clear that a cell with high average ripeness may 
be composed almost entirely of mature fruits, but it may also corre-
spond to a mixture of very ripe strawberries with others that are still 
green. Likewise, some cells with intermediate average ripeness con-
tain a mosaic of states, in which unripe, partially ripe, and ripe fruits
coexist.

This comparison highlights the importance of considering both ag-

3.3.3.  Fruit count by size and maturity class
A third group of maps explores the interaction between size and ma-
turity. Fig. 13 presents a 4 × 3 arrangement of yield maps. The three 
columns correspond to the aggregated maturity classes Unripe, Partially 
Ripe, and Ripe, while the four rows represent All sizes, Small, Medium, 
and Large, respectively.
In the ﬁrst row (All sizes), the maps show the global spatial distri-
bution of unripe, partially ripe, and ripe fruits per square meter. As ex-
pected from the category statistics, unripe fruits dominate large portions 
of the plot, consistent with the overall phenological state of the crop. 
However, when moving to the second, third, and fourth rows (Small, 
Medium, and Large), important diﬀerences emerge. In the “Unripe–
Small” maps, extended bands with very high counts appear, indicating 
zones where fruit load is high but still at early stages of development. 
In contrast, the “Ripe–Large” maps reveal more localized patches, typ-
ically in sectors where plant vigor and microenvironmental conditions 
have favored more complete fruit development.
These maps support decisions such as: (i) prioritizing for harvest 
those areas where high density coexists with a predominance of ripe 
medium or large fruits; (ii) identifying zones that should be left for a 
later harvest (areas dominated by “Unripe–Small” categories); and (iii) 
recognizing sectors that might require speciﬁc agronomic interventions 
to homogenize the crop status.
Complementarily, Fig. 14 shows the same 4 × 3 arrangement of size–
maturity combinations, but using interpolated surfaces instead of dis-
crete cells. Each map represents a continuous color ﬁeld obtained by 
spatial interpolation of the counts per 1 m2 cell. This continuous repre-
sentation smooths transitions and makes spatial gradients in fruit con-
centration more evident. In particular, it helps identify zones of high 
density for speciﬁc size–maturity combinations and zones of gradual 
transition where the composition of the fruit changes along the plot. 
These interpolated maps are used as visual aids to interpret spatial 
trends; quantitative interpretation is based on the original counts com-
puted in the 1 m2 grid cells, since interpolation may produce smoothed 
or intermediate values that do not correspond to direct fruit-count
observations.

how internal variability is structured, thus oﬀering a robust basis for 
harvest planning and decision making in precision agriculture.
4.  Conclusion
This work presented an integrated RGB-D and GNSS RTK pipeline for 
hydroponic strawberries that links instance segmentation, feature ex-
traction, rule-based operational size–maturity categorization, and yield 
mapping at the individual fruit level. The YOLOv8-seg models provided 
fruit masks, bounding boxes, and detections with suﬃcient quality to 
feed the subsequent stages of size estimation, ripeness estimation, and 
spatial distribution analysis under real ﬁeld conditions.
By projecting depth information into 3D and ﬁltering point clouds 
with DBSCAN, the proposed method estimated strawberry length and 
width with mean absolute errors of 2.51 mm and 1.85 mm, respectively, 
relative to Vernier caliper measurements. These results indicate that the 
system can characterize fruit dimensions at the individual level with suf-
ﬁcient accuracy to support operational categorization and spatial aggre-
gation. By combining these dimensions with visible ripeness percentage, 
each fruit was assigned to one of nine size–maturity categories using the 
predeﬁned rule-based procedure described in the methodology.

The yield maps derived from georeferenced fruits revealed marked 
spatial variability in density, visible ripeness, and size–maturity com-
position, both at the 1 m2 grid scale and along crop rows. These maps 
make it possible to identify zones with high concentrations of harvest-
priority fruits, areas dominated by developing immature fruits, and spa-
tial patterns potentially associated with management diﬀerences within 
the crop.
The system was implemented and applied as a processing pipeline 
on ﬁeld-collected data. Although the evaluation was conducted in a sin-
gle commercial hydroponic crop, the results show the potential of the 
approach as a decision-support tool for harvest planning in precision 
horticulture. Transfer to other cultivars, planting conﬁgurations, or en-
vironmental conditions will require recalibration of maturity and size 
thresholds, as well as renewed validation of the perception module. Fu-
ture work should also compare more recent segmentation architectures 
and evaluate the system under diﬀerent levels of occlusion, illumination, 
and crop density.
