A B S T R A C T  
Automated and accurate counting and ripeness level estimation play key roles in the precision management of 
cherry tomatoes in greenhouses. This study presents an enhanced framework based on tracking by detection to 
simultaneously estimate the count and ripeness levels for cherry tomato bunches in greenhouses. Ripeness value 
is determined by the proportional relationship between the number of ripe and unripe fruits in a tomato bunch. 
To accurately detect fruit kernels in small sizes and blurred ROIs, a lightweight detector called NanoDet is 
employed. To reduce ripeness estimation errors caused due to shading, the ripeness value with the highest 
frequency is selected as the estimate when multiple detections of the same bunch are detected. The ripeness 
values are categorized into five levels and combined with the counting results. The study’s results demonstrate 
that it achieves 92.79% accuracy in counting and 90.84% accuracy in estimating ripeness levels. The improved 
algorithm has a processing rate of 15 frames per second. These results demonstrate the potential and value of the 
enhanced method for counting cherry tomatoes and estimating their ripeness levels.   
1. Introduction 
Tomato is an important crop whose total world production has 
reached 187 million tons as of 2020 [1]. In China, more and more to­
matoes are grown in smart greenhouses. This mode of cultivation re­
quires intensive labor inputs and refined management [2,3]. In factory 
production, effectively managing cherry tomatoes depends on rapidly 
and accurately estimating tomato clusters counts and ripeness. How­
ever, there is still a lack of methods to automate these tasks, which 
contrasts with their labor-intensive and cost-increasing manual coun­
terparts. Hence, there is an urgent need for automated methods that are 
accurate, reliable and capable of counting cherry tomato bunches and 
estimating ripeness levels in factory production management [4,5]. 
In the agricultural production environments, the utilization of visual 
sensors to acquire images is a mainstream and relatively inexpensive 
approach [2,6,7]. However, extracting crop and environmental infor­
mation from these images requires taking into full consideration the 
specific characteristics of the crop’s production environment. Fig. 1 il­
lustrates the effects of lighting on changes in brightness and darkness, 
the mutual obstruction between fruit clusters, and the potential 
misidentification of fruit clusters in adjacent ridges. Furthermore, the 
main stem visible in the figure can obstruct the fruit clusters, conse­
quently impeding the recognition and detection of the individual fruit 
grains. When it comes to the detection of fruit clusters, their relatively 
large pixel area in the image facilitates their recognition and detection, 
but is also makes them susceptible to confusion with tomato clusters on 
nearby ridges. However, when it comes to ripeness estimation, indi­
vidually detecting the ripeness of each fruit grain with precision would 
be not only time-consuming and labor-intensive, but it could also lead to 
inaccurate detection due to variations in lighting and obstruction [8,9]. 
Thus, there are still significant challenges in employing visual methods 
for counting tomato cluster and estimating ripeness [10]. 
With the rise of computer vision techniques in recent years, re­
searchers have tried to detect fruit and ripeness by various methods. 
Traditional methods, including directional gradient histogram, various 
clustering methods, and Hough transform typically rely on features such 
as color, shape, and texture for detection [11]. However, in complex 
agricultural scenes, these methods are often lack accuracy due to light

convolutional neural networks are highly effective in extracting object 
features, allowing them to mitigate the negative effects of the afore­
mentioned issues to a certain extent [10]. Hence, researchers have 
widely adopted deep learning for detecting and counting crops. Zhang 
et al. [12] employed a combination of the SIFT image matching method 
to generate a panoramic view of a holly tree, followed by the utilization 
of the YOLOX algorithm to detect and count all the whole fruit on the 
entire tree, demonstrating robustness in dealing with shadows, occlu­
sions, and incomplete contours. Hu et al. [13] initially employed Faster 
R-CNN to distinguish individual tomato fruits from overlapping tomato 
clusters. Subsequently, they proceeded to isolate the fruits from the 
background by using the H and S components in the HSV color space, 
thereby enhancing the accuracy of final detection. Besides fruit detec­
tion, researchers have employed multi-object tracking algorithms to 
perform fruit or crop counting, particularly in row-monopoly crop 
production [14,15]. Multi-object tracking (MOT) algorithms are widely 
applied in various fields, particularly in production setting, and pri­
marily involve analyzing the appearance and quantity of objects 
captured in video images without prior knowledge [16]. At present, the 
majority of MOT algorithms adhere to the concept of object detection 
followed by tracking, which relying on the accuracy of two key com­
ponents: target detection accuracy and tracker accuracy [17]. The uti­
lization of advanced object detectors can significantly enhance the 
capability to capture objects in complex environments. However, cur­
rent trackers have an issue with objective ID conversion, resulting in 
counting errors that necessitate enhanced measures for improved fruit 
counting [18]. In fruit ripeness detection, the color feature on the sur­
face of tomatoes serves as valuable information, enabling fast and 
nondestructive detection of fruit ripeness [4,19]. Therefore, Begum and 
Hazarika [20] employed a pre-trained deep learning model to classify 
individual fruit ripeness. The models was further trained on a tomato 
dataset with three distinct ripeness levels, resulting in a detection ac­
curacy of 97.37 %. In a laboratory experiment, Wan et al. [21] employed 
computer vision techniques to extract the contours of a number of to­
matoes at three ripeness stages. Furthermore, they obtained color 
feature values for five additional sampling points within each contour, 
which were subsequently used as inputs for ripeness prediction in a 
BPNN network. This approach achieved a prediction accuracy of 
99.31 %. However, both methods underwent testing in a controlled 
laboratory setting, and there are still many challenges in the real con­
ditions. Consequently, researchers endeavored to conduct ripeness 
detection of tomatoes in real environments. An improved YOLOv4 
achieved the estimation of ripe and unripe fruits with an accuracy of 
94.44 % and a detection rate of 10.71frames per second in a real envi­
ronment. Wang et al. [22] further enhanced the performance of 
detecting ripe and green fruits in real environment by improving Faster 
R-CNN. This modification led to an average algorithmic accuracy of 
96.14 %. Nonetheless, all of the aforementioned methods solely detect 
the ripeness of individual fruits within the entire image, without 
attaining an estimation of an entire bunch of tomatoes. 
In this study, it is desired to realize both counting and maturity level 
estimation of tomato clusters. This goal can be realized by employing a 
tracking-by-detection algorithm. The framework based on tracker by 
detection can be extended. In this study, we design an additional ripe­
ness estimation module, which can perform the detection of tomato 
bunch ripeness without compromising the effectiveness of target 
detection and tracking. In addition, the tracker by detection method 
objectively suffers from the defect of ID conversion. Therefore, to 
improve the accuracy of counting results and ripeness estimation, it is 
necessary to adopt appropriate strategies to reduce false detections and 
to fuse counting results with ripeness information. Table 1 lists some 
studies similar to the research in this paper. Fruit location detection and 
ripeness assessment are commonly employed in the information sensing 
component of harvesting robots [23,24]. Conversely, detection of spe­
cific plant organs is typically employed for acquiring detailed crop in­
formation [25]. In recent years, the application of trackers in 
combination with target detectors for crop counting, yield estimation 
and density estimation has gained increasing attention [14,15]. None­
theless, studies simultaneously addressing both fruit yield estimation 
(quantity statistics) and ripeness estimation are scarce, with only one 
study identified that achieved quantity estimation and ripeness esti­
mation of an individual tomato by analyzing ripeness information 
through HSV color space analysis [26]. The paper did not further inte­
grate the quantity information with the ripeness information, but only 
obtained the two types of information separately. In addition, the 
maturity information was only analyzed at the qualitative level and no 
breakthrough was made at the quantitative level. The difference be­
tween our study and these studies is that this paper simultaneously re­
alizes whole bunch ripeness estimation and bunches counting of cherry 
tomatoes. The combination of YOLOv8 and Deep Sort for bunch number 
estimation has also not been studied. We also applied a very lightweight 
detector for detecting the number of unripe and ripe tomato kernels, and 
designed a very simple function to map the number ratio to a ripeness 
value between 0 and 1. Additionally, we design a separate information 
fusion module for fusing bunch count information with ripeness 
information. 
In conclusion, this study aims to propose a method for simulta­
neously counting cherry tomato bunches and estimating their ripeness 
levels in greenhouses. The advanced YOLOv8 [27] target detector is 
used to improve the detection accuracy. Followed this, Deep Sort [28] is 
utilized for ID identification of each bunch. Meanwhile, the lightweight 
detection network NanoDet [29] is used to detect ripe and unripe fruits 
within the bunches identified by YOLOv8. The maturity of the entire 
tomato cluster is represented by the ratio of mature fruits to immature 
fruits. Subsequently, the region counting method [18] is used to reduce 
the counting errors due to ID conversion. The ripeness value with the 
highest frequency in different frames of the same fruit bunch is used as 
the detection result to reduce ripeness errors. Furthermore, the final 
ripeness values obtained through the aforementioned method were 
categorized into five levels from low to high. The ripeness information is 
fused and processed along clusters IDs information to achieve the esti­
mation of ripeness levels and counting for tomato bunches. Fig. 3 il­
lustrates the reasoning process of our proposed method. The main 
contributions of this work can be summarized: (1) We extend a frame­
work based on tracker by detection to enable simultaneous counting and 
estimating ripeness levels for cherry tomato bunches. (2) We develop a 
simple module for fast estimating the maturity of bunches without 
affecting counting. (3) We design an information fusion module to 
effectively mitigate counting errors caused by ID jumps and errors in 
maturity estimation caused by occlusion.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Data acquisition 
The data were collected within Hongfu Agricultural Tomato Pro­
duction Park in Daxing District of Beijing, China (116.283601 E, 
39.603538 N), during the period from December 2021 to March 2022. 
The greenhouse follows a standard Dutch greenhouse growing pattern 
and produces cherry tomatoes called Hongfushi. The cherry tomatoes 
are cultivated in rows, with a spacing of 1.5 m between each row. Each 
tomato bunch typically contains around 10–12 fruits. For this study, the 
RealSense D435i (Intel, Santa Clara, America) was employed as the 
acquisition device, which was mounted on a raised platform cart to 
capture image and video data. The D435i integrates an RGB camera 
capable of capturing 1280 × 720 pixels and video frames at a rate of 30 
FPS per second. The D435i features an integrated IMU that employs an 
accelerometer and gyroscope to determine the camera’s positioning and 
orientation. With a horizontal viewing angle of 69◦and a vertical 
viewing angle of 42◦, the RGB camera captures a wide scene. The 
RealSense D435i camera connects to the PC through USB 3.0, delivering 
high-speed data transfer and power. Additionally, Intel offers a software 
development kit (SDK) that endows developers with powerful APIs and 
tools for accessing camera data, performing calibration, and imple­
menting computer vision algorithms. The SDK streamlines the devel­
opment process and simplifies integration with other algorithms. 
Therefore, we chose D435i as the device for data acquisition. The 
camera was positioned approximately 0.6 m horizontally from the to­
mato bunches and 1 m vertically from the rail height. The camera was 
rotated by 90 degrees to allow a wider field of view in the height di­
rection, thus obtaining images and videos with a resolution of 
720 × 1280. To ensure sample diversity, images were taken at three 
periods of the day (morning, midday and afternoon) and various poses of 
fruit bunches (see Fig. 2). 
In the above way, 2127 original images with tomato bunches were 
collected as the dataset for as the dataset for detecting tomato bunches. 
1382 images with various types of tomato bunches were cropped and 
selected from the collected images as a dataset for estimating ripeness. 
Both tomato clusters detection and ripeness estimation data were 
divided into training set and validation set in the ratio of 8:2. We used 
LabelImg [30] to label these data by manual annotation. Additionally, 
several videos were also acquired as data for later count and maturity 
estimation verification. The captured images and video frames were

2.2. The proposed framework of counting and estimating maturity levels 
for tomato bunches 
We proposes a workflow that enables both counting and maturity 
grading for tomato clusters. It mainly consisted of the following com­
ponents: an object detector for detecting tomato bunches, an object 
tracker, a ripeness estimation module, and an information fusion mod­
ule. In the object detection module, YOLOv8n is used to mark the fruit 
bunches in the video frames. The object tracker is used to identify 
whether the fruit bunches in different video frames are the same fruit 
bunch and to mark ID numbers. The ripeness estimation module is used 
to estimate the ripeness value of each bunch. The information fusion 
module converted the tagged IDs into the number of bunches and fused 
them with the bunch maturity grades information. The flowchart of the 
whole framework was shown in Fig. 3. First, sequential frames are 
inputted into YOLOv8n to generate the detection results. These results 
are then used as input for NanoDet, which is responsible for identifying 
ripe and unripe grains. The ratio of ripe to green fruit in the same bunch 
is calculated to determine the ripeness of the tomatoes within the clus­
ter. Simultaneously, the fruit bunches are processed by the Deep Sort 
tracker module, which employs techniques such as Kalman filtering, 
Hungarian algorithm, and ID identification tagging. Next, the tracking 
results and ripeness values are fed into the information fusion module. A 
region counting method is used to filter out redundant ID numbers and 
determine the number of fruit bunches. The ripeness predictions are

2.2.1. The object detector for tomato bunches 
Accurate bunch detection directly plays a crucial role in determining 
the effectiveness of subsequent trackers and ripeness estimation of 
bunches. To enhance counting precision and estimation of ripeness, the 
current state-of-the-art object detector YOLOv8 is utilized for fruit 
bunch detection. YOLOv8 builds upon the successful performance of 
previous YOLO series detectors and inherits YOLOv5 directly. The 
network structure in the top right corner of Fig. 3 presents the network 
structure of YOLOv8, in which the C3 module in the original Backbone 
and Neck is substituted with the C2f module, and the convolution layer 
in Neck is modified. The prediction head adopts the widely-used 
decoupled-head and replaces anchor-based with anchor-free method. 
Additionally, YOLOv8 incorporates various techniques to further 
enhance its performance, including optimizing the loss function and 
implementing the task-aligned assigner for sample matching. 
2.2.2. Tracking tomato bunches 
For the counting task, the accuracy of the counting task is related to 
how to effectively track the same object appearing in multiple consec­
utive frames. Due to the potential similarity in appearance among 
different tomato clusters, it is necessary to assign different IDs to these 
bunches for trajectory tracking. Nowadays, multi-target tracking strat­
egy based on object detection is the mainstream tracking strategy. The 
tracker utilizes a Kalman filter to process the trajectories of fruit 
bunches, and a Hungarian algorithm is employed to estimate their 
correlation. [31]. However, the Kalman filter alone is not sufficient to 
cope with changes in the appearance of the same fruit bunch within 
different frames (see Fig. 6), and significant changes due to occlusion 
[28]. Deep Sort improves accuracy by calculating the depth features of 
the bounding boxes of each fruit bunch to enhance the information 
regarding its action and appearance. This, to some extent, reduces the 
occurrence of ID jumps when fruit bunches are occluded or experience 
significant appearance changes across frames. Therefore, we employ 
Deep Sort to track fruit bunches in different frames. The main processing 
flow of the Deep Sort algorithm is illustrated in the lower right corner of 
Fig. 3. Initially, the bounding box is detected using YOLOv8n. Then, the 
tracked objects undergo feature extraction, and the similarity and IOU 
matching of targets in adjacent frames are computed. If a successful 
match is found, the information is updated using Kalman filtering, and 
future position and velocity are predicted. The data is then correlated, 
and an ID is assigned. If the number of unsuccessful matches exceeds a 
predefined threshold, the information of the unmatched bounding box is 
deleted. 
2.2.3. Maturity estimation module 
Step 4 shown in Fig. 3 illustrates the ripeness estimation module and 
the tracker module. In the maturity estimation module, the ROIs of the 
cropped bunches can be obtained after step 3, but not the ripeness of the 
tomato bunches. Therefore, we designed a module for estimating the 
ripeness of a tomato bunch. First NanoDet detects the number of ripe 
and unripe fruits in a fruit bunch, and then obtains the ripeness value of 
the tomatoes in that bunch using our designed mapping function. We 
describe the NanoDet detector and the mapping function separately 
below.

A. Ripe and unripe tomatoes detector 
To efficiently estimating the ripeness of the entire tomato bunch, we 
simplified the process by evaluating the proportion of green and red 
fruits. Since YOLOv8n detector accurately determines the position of 
fruits bunches in each frame. Therefore, ripeness detection is conducted 
in the region of interest (ROI) in each frame, reducing background 
interference and enhancing the efficiency of detection. The size of the 
ROI region varies based on the differences in size among the fruit 
bunches (as illustrated in Fig. 9). This results in potentially blurred 
outline boundaries of the tomato grains in a cluster, which hinders 
subsequent detection. Consequently, selecting a detector appropriate for 
this scenario is necessary. While YOLOv8n can achieve this task, its 
network architecture is not well-suited for small input images. In this 
study, we utilize the lightweight target detector NanoDet in conjunction 
with a ripeness determination method for tomato bunch. Initially, the 
fruit bunches detected by the tomato bunch detector are fed into 
NanoDet for the classification of ripe and unripe fruits. Subsequently, 
the ripeness of the entire tomato bunch is determined based on the ratio 
of red to green fruits. NanoDet is a lightweight detector consisting of 
lightweight backbone, FPN, and detection head. The backbone utilizes 
ShuffleNetv2 [32], a lightweight model. As shown Fig. 5, during the 
feature fusion stage, Ghost Bottleneck block [33], depth-separable 
convolution, and reduction of the number of channels of the features 
are incorporated into PAN [34] to enhance feature fusion and reduce 
computational requirements. 
First, all convolutions in the Path Aggregation Network (PAN) are 
replaced with 1 × 1 convolutions to align the feature channel di­
mensions (Fig. 5 shows this substitution in the Rd module). Bilinear 
interpolation is used for upsampling to reduce computation, while 
depth-separable convolution is used for downsampling. In the prediction 
head, depthwise-separable convolution replaces the normal convolu­
tion, and the number of convolution stacks is reduced from four to two. 
The computation for bounding box regression and classification initially 
share the same set of convolutions and are subsequently split into two 
separate processes. During the training stage, the algorithm utilizes a 
lighter and simpler training assistance module called assign guidance 
module (AGM) along with dynamic soft label assigner (DSLA) to address 
the optimal label matching problem in the lightweight model. In this 
paper, the chosen version of the model is NanoDet-m-Plus out of several 
available versions provided by NanoDet. Fig. 4 illustrates the model 
structure of NanoDet-m-Plus. The red dashed box part in the figure is 
solely used for weight updates during training and is not utilized for 
forward propagation in testing and prediction. This approach signifi­
cantly enhances the training effectiveness and reduces the inference 
load.  
B. Quantitative relationships mapped to ripeness values 
The ripeness composition of a bunch of tomatoes may be as follows, 
consisting entirely of ripe fruit grains, partially ripe and unripe fruits, 
and entirely of unripe fruits. In fuzzy logic,a membership function maps 
input values to a membership value between0 and 1, indicating the 
degree of belong to a fuzzy set. In this study, we aim to map the quan­
titative relationship between unripe and ripe fruits to a ripeness value, 
which differs from the approach in fuzzy logic. This ripeness value 
directly represents the ripeness of a bunch of fruits, with the defined 
scale between 0 and 1. We have developed a function (Eq. (1) to accu­

By the aforementioned methods, we successfully determined the ID 
information and ripeness of each bunch of tomatoes. Although Deep Sort 
can reduce the number of ID jumps, overly complex agricultural sce­
narios will still cause ID jumps [17]. Consequently, this leads to an ID 
number larger than the actual number of tomato bunches, resulting in a 
significantly increase in counting errors. However, the utilization of the 
region counting method [18] can effectively enhance counting accuracy. 
Furthermore, in the actual detection, there are some instances of both 
complete and incomplete shading. Additionally, the appearance of the 
same tomato bunch varies across different viewpoints of frames, as 
depicted in Fig. 6, resulting in variations in the number of detected 
bunches. Hence, a reliance solely on the ratio of detected results would 
introduce a certain degree of detection error. To minimize the error in 
ripeness detection, we determine the final detection result by selecting 
the value with the highest frequency of occurrence among the ripeness 
detection results of the same tomato bunch across different frames.  
B. Maturity levels classification 
Similar to the quality grading of fruits, it is usually necessary to grade 
the ripeness because the ripeness grade determines the picking condi­
tions, transportation and storage conditions, and the freshness of to­
matoes at the time of consumption [4,35]. Regarding the classification 
of tomato ripeness levels, there are currently mainstream methods of 
categorization such as two-level division, three-level division, five-level 
division, and six-level division [35–37]. These classification methods are 
only for the ripeness classification of individual fruits. However, when 
considering the maturity classification for an entire bunch, only whether 
bunches can be picked or not is taken into account in the case of auto­
matic picking. This is not sufficient for more accurate yield estimation 
and storage management. 
Therefore, in this study, the ripeness level of an entire bunch is 
classified into one of five ripeness levels based on the distribution of the 
estimated values between 0 and 1, with reference to the five-level di­
vision [37]. In order to provide data support for harvesting and storage 
management, the ripeness grade of an entire bunch is therefore classi­
fied into one of five grades of ripeness. Examples of five grades of tomato 
bunches are presented in Fig. 7, with two example images listed for each 
grade. It is clear to see that the proportion of ripe fruit in a tomato bunch 
gradually increases. This signals the practical application of expressing 
the ripeness of an entire bunch of tomatoes based on the percentage of 
ripe fruit. At the time of harvesting, a bunch with a rating of 5 was ready 
for picking, even though one or two kernels might not be fully ripe 
(communication with the greenhouse staff also confirmed that a bunch 
with a rating of 5 was ready for picking). If this maturity scale is com­
bined with the ripening cycle of tomato bunches, it is possible to accu­
rately estimate the number of tomato bunches of each grade in the entire 
greenhouse. This will provide the exact number of manpower or picking 
robots needed to pick the bunches that meet the picking requirements in 
the entire greenhouse, preventing yield loss due to missing the picking 
period, and providing important data support for more refined 
greenhouse management. During picking, both laborers and robots will 
inevitably damage the fruits. These fruits may not be marketed or stored. 
Accordingly, non-destructive inspections and yield estimates can be 
compared to the number of damages, which in turn provides the basis 
for future yield and transportation and storage management. In addi­
tion, since standard commercial cherry tomato production greenhouses 
can precisely control the crop’s nutrient supply, temperature, humidity, 
light, etc., it is also possible to extrapolate the results of yield estimation 
to determine whether or not these regulatory measures are justified. 
3. Experiments and results 
3.1. Evaluation metrics and experiment conditions

3.4. Bunches maturity detection results 
To obtain a better detection accuracy, a total of 1382 different fruit 
bunches were quantified and their size distribution was plotted on a 
scatter plot shown in Fig. 9. The horizontal axis represents the height 
distribution of the bunches in the images, while the vertical axis rep­
resents the width distribution of the bunches in the images. The pixel 
width of the fruit bunches in the images mostly range between 100 and 
150, while the height mostly range between 200 and 400 pixels. 
Therefore, selecting a suitable model has a significant impact on the 
recognition effect based on the size distribution. This study trained 
several popular models at the size of 384 × 384, as well as the NanoDet 
model at the original size of 320 × 320. The effects of each model is 
compared, and their respective test results are presented in Table 5. 
Although the YOLO series models perform well, they exhibit weaker 
performance than NanoDet at smaller scales. This indicates that Nano­
Det is more effective in recognition at small scales, and its computational 
power requirement is also lower. 
As shown in Figs. 10 and 11, the tomato grains were detected for 
both images using different models and the results were visualized. The 
actual number of grains to be detected in Bunch 1 is 10. Although 
NanoDet did not detect all the grains, it achieved the best result, 
detecting 8 grains. In contrast, YOLOv8n and YOLOx_tiny detected 6 and 
7 kernels, respectively. In Bunch 2, 10 kernels actually needed to be 
detected. NanoDet detected all of them correctly, while YOLOv8n 
detected 9 kernels and YOLOX_tiny detected 11 kernels, misdetecting 
the stem in the background as fruit grains. For the recognition of fruit 
grains within the cropped fruit bunch region, the imaging may be blurry, 
and a model with good performance may not always achieve the ex­
pected results for the recognition of small-sized images; instead, Nano­
Det is more adapted to this detection task. In some cases, the acquired 
video stream may have degraded imaging due to jittering problems, 
resulting in local areas where the pixels of object boundaries are not very 
clear (see Figs. 10 and 11). This adds difficulty to the model detection 
and recognition. In this case, how to choose a suitable model or improve 
networks is the main issue.

3.6. Discussion 
The current work suggests that expanding the framework based on 
tracker by detection in estimating the maturity levels and counting for 
tomato clusters is an effective improvement strategy. During clusters 
detection, since the proportion of fruit clusters in the whole image is 
relatively high, occlusion does not cause significant disturbance to the 
detection of fruit clusters’ positions. Therefore, we choose YOLOv8n as 
the tomato bunches detector, which can have the better balance in terms 
of operating efficiency and detection performance. In the future, it is 
possible to attempt improving YOLOX-nano to achieve higher perfor­
mance while maintaining the lowest computational requirements. This 
would allow the algorithm to run more smoothly in edge systems. 
In maturity estimation, we utilize a lightweight NanoDet to detect 
the quantity of mature and immature fruits, and then map the rela­
tionship of quantity to a maturity value between 0 and 1 using a simple 
function. The occlusion between the main stem, fruit bunches, and 
within the fruit bunches is an unavoidable issue when detecting the 
number of fruit grains. The occlusion between the main stem and fruit 
bunches may render the fruit grains undetectable, consequently 
affecting the estimation of maturity. The same fruit bunch may exhibit 
different postures across different frames (perspectives), which can also 
result in occlusion within the fruit cluster. Such variations lead to 
changes in the detected quantity. The occlusion between the main stem 
and fruit bunches hampers an accurate counting of fruit grain quantity, 
and altering perspectives may not fundamentally solve this problem. 
Additional manual intervention may be required, which could increase 
the cost of crop inspection and management. On the other hand, 
adjusting the perspective can potentially help detect the accurate 
quantity of fruit grains by addressing occlusion within a fruit bunch. In 
the information fusion module, it is necessary to effectively integrate 
tracking information and maturity values. While Deep Sort can reduce 
ID switches, it still cannot completely eliminate them. Therefore, this 
study adopts a region counting method to convert the number of ID 
numbers into the number of tomato bunches to improve the counting 
accuracy. At the same time, a maturity value is transformed into one of 
five levels, and the maturity level is determined based on the frequency 
of occurrence, which mitigates the maturity estimation error due to the 
posture variations between the same cluster in different frames and 
occlusions within the bunch. ID jumps also introduce estimation errors 
in maturity because of an ID number corresponds to a maturity value. In 
future work, it is necessary to optimize the estimation results of maturity 
while considering the issue of ID jumps, which may require further 
optimization of the tracker. 
Tomato clusters cultivated in the facility greenhouse examined in 
this study exhibit a more standardized growth pattern compared to other 
agricultural scenarios, with fixed growing heights and distances from 
the medium. This characteristic has piqued the interest of researchers in 
the field of automated inspection and harvesting of tomatoes. However, 
the effectiveness and scalability of deep learning-based vision methods 
rely heavily on diverse and comprehensive datasets. In agricultural 
settings, data collection is a time-consuming and labor-intensive pro­
cess, further complicated by stringent data protection, which makes is 
challenging to obtain publicly available, high-quality data. Regrettably, 
the limited dataset we managed to acquire did not fully encompass the 
complexities associated with tomato growth. Furthermore, during our 
data collection and experimentation, we encountered the unavoidable 
issue of vibration in the acquired dynamic video data, resulting in lower 
imaging quality compared to static images. Therefore, it is imperative to 
conduct further sensitivity analysis of the proposed method, specifically 
investigating the impact of different scene characteristics (such as oc­
clusion and video imaging quality) on bunches counting and ripeness 
estimation. 
4. Conclusion 
This study proposes a framework that simultaneously achieves the 
counting of cherry tomato bunches and the estimation of their ripeness 
levels. It accurately estimates the yield and distribution of cherry to­
matoes in an accurate manner. In the proposed method effectively uti­
lizes the extension of a framework based on tracking by detection. The 
framework included a ripeness estimation module that categorizes the 
detected ripeness values into five levels. To mitigate count maturity 
estimation errors, a fusion module is designed to integrate count infor­
mation and maturity level information. The proposed method can 
perform inference at an average speed of 15FPS, with the bunch 
counting accuracy of 92.79 %, and the ripeness level estimation accu­
racy of 90.84 % without counting errors (84.29 % with counting errors). 
The experiments demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed 
approach in estimating the number of tomato bunches counting and 
maturity levels. However, there are some limitations in this study, as 
occlusion can still interfere with accurate maturity estimation. In addi­
tion, there is room for improvement in the overall algorithmic process. 
In the future, further optimization of the entire algorithm framework 
and its components can be helpful for accurate yield estimation and 
maturity level assessment of cherry tomatoes, thereby generating posi­
tive impacts on more precise production and storage management.
