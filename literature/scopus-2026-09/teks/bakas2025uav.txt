In recent years, arid and semi-arid regions have faced severe and persistent drought, with rainfall at most 200 
mm per year. In addition, intensive irrigation practices in agribusiness areas aimed at boosting production further 
increased irrigation water consumption. These practices call for innovative applications that enable optimized 
yield estimation and tree health monitoring. This paper aims to predict crop yield in a citrus orchard farm 
using UAV imagery and Deep Learning approaches. It emphasizes the use of a lightweight Tiny U-Net model 
for tree detection and a CNN-based architecture for crop yield estimation based on vegetation indices and 
in-situ measurement data. The study was designed to provide a cost-effective solution for precision orchard 
management and monitoring under climate stress. The CNN model outperformed other machine learning models 
in yield prediction, achieving the highest coeﬃcient of determination (R2) of 88%. The Tiny U-Net architecture, 
developed for semantic segmentation and counting, eﬀectively distinguished individual citrus trees and rows. The 
model reached high accuracy, with overall precision and recall reaching 94.74% and 94.88%, respectively, and 
maintained a low inference time of 12.55 ms, making it suitable for real-time and on-boarding processing. The 
segmentation output enabled an accurate counting of both trees and rows, with a R2 exceeding 99%, confirming 
the reliability of the model for structural orchard analysis. The pipeline supports precision agriculture through 
reliable and high-resolution yield monitoring, enabling informed decision-making for citrus orchard management 
and resource optimization.
1. Introduction
The agriculture sector in the Mediterranean region is a fundamen­
tal core of the worldwide economy [1]. Many agricultural regions, es­
pecially in arid and semi-arid zones, are renowned for crop rotation, 
terraced landscapes, and traditional water management strategies [2]. 
These methods have maintained a varied agri-system incorporating an 
extensive diversity of fruits, vegetables, and grains [3]. Nonetheless, 
the sector is increasingly threatened by the impacts of climate vari­
ability eﬀects, including frequent droughts, high-temperature events, 
water deficits, and spatial distribution of precipitation [4--6]. Agricul­

socio-environmental dynamics reveals how rising emissions, human vul­

impacts, strain agricultural systems, and heighten the urgency for adap­

use and managing irrigation practices. However, conventional methods 
based on manual sampling or historical averages are generally time­
consuming, imprecise, and inadequate for variations within large or­
chards.
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), have revolutionized agricultural 
monitoring through their flexibility in image acquisition time, high spa­
tial resolution, and independence from cloud cover [23]. In the latest 
advances, UAVs have demonstrated strong potential to address the lim­
itations of ground-based surveys and lower-resolution satellite imagery, 
allowing more eﬃcient and precise measurement of crop characteris­
tics [24]. Through the vegetation spectral indices, UAV data provides 
valuable plant vigor, water stress, and nutrient content indicators, be­
ing fundamental for understanding spatial variability and forecasting 
orchard performance [25--27]. Recent studies have demonstrated the ef­
fectiveness of leveraging UAV imagery integrated with AI techniques for 
mapping and monitoring the canopy health [28], tree distribution, and 
biophysical parameters, which are critical indicators for yield prediction 
and tree counting [29]. For instance, Melnychenko et al. [30] developed 
a multi-UAV deep learning system for instantaneous fruit detection and 
counting, obtaining a mean average precision (mAP) of 86.8% while 
maintaining low error rates even under challenging weather condi­
tions, thus demonstrating robust performance in orchard environments. 
Similarly, Shammi et al. [31] used multispectral UAV data to monitor 
soybean development and predict yield across diﬀerent growth stages. 
Their approach incorporated 14 vegetation indices (VIs) and several ML 
algorithms, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest 
(RF), and Linear Regression (LR). Among the VIs tested, Normalized Dif­
ference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Canopy Cover (CC), and Normalized 
Area Vegetation Index (NAVI) were identified as the most reliable for 
early-stage yield prediction. The yield models attained root mean square 
error (RMSE) values ranging from 134.5 to 511.11 kg/ha, while cross­
validation RMSEs ranged from 605.26 to 685.96 kg/ha, highlighting 
both the potential and sensitivity of ML-based yield prediction when us­
ing limited training data. Vijayakumar et al. [32] developed three citrus 
yield prediction models using UAV and ground-based imagery, finding 
that combining aerial imaging data with ground-level fruit detection and 
counts produced the most accurate predictions, with Model-2 achieving 
a MAPE of 23.45%, outperforming UAV-only approaches. Likewise, Al­
abi et al. [33] used UAV multispectral imagery in combination with 
texture features Gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) and vegeta­
tion indices to estimate soybean yield across breeding trials in Nigeria. 
The results showed that textural information slightly outperformed tra­
ditional vegetation indices, and ML models like Random Forest and 
Cubist achieved R2 values up to 0.89. Meanwhile, Khodjaev et al. [34] 
improved wheat yield prediction by combining vegetation indices, so­
lar radiation, soil properties, and plant height using hyperparameter­
optimized ML models, with Random Forest and XGBoost outperforming 
other methods showing a 7--12% improvement over traditional linear 
models, highlighting the importance of feature selection and tuning in 
yield forecasting. In other context, Zheng et al. [35] proposed a real­
time UAV-based citrus fruit counting system designed to overcome the 
common challenges of illumination variability and dense occlusion in 
orchard environments. Their pipeline integrates a custom UAV platform, 
a flight control application (FlyCounter), and a novel Fruit-YOLO detec­
tion model combined with an Illumination-Adaptive Transformer (IAT) 
for image enhancement under low-light conditions. To track fruits across 
frames, they employed the DeepSORT algorithm for video-based object 
tracking. The system demonstrated strong performance, accomplishing 
a mean average precision (mAP) of 0.929, along with high precision 
(0.898) and recall (0.854) in enhanced images, improvements of 1.7%, 
4.6%, and 3.5% respectively, over unenhanced inputs. The counting er­
ror (MAPE) ranged from 4.36% oﬄine to 12.66% online, making it one 
of the most eﬃcient pipelines for citrus counting in both daytime and 
nighttime scenarios, with signiﬁcant potential for large-scale orchard 
deployment.
This study was conducted to contribute to the growing field of preci­
sion agriculture by developing and testing a UAV-based framework for 
citrus tree detection, canopy segmentation, and fruit yield prediction 
using deep learning techniques. The UAV imagery was employed for 
citrus tree detection and simultaneously predict fruit yield within the 
orchard, oﬀering a high-resolution and eﬃcient approach to monitor 
orchard structure and productivity. The aerial images captured the tree 
placement, canopy size, and vegetation health of individual trees, al­
lowing accurate segmentation and spatial analysis across large orchard 
areas. In the proposed method two key tasks were addressed. First, a cus­
tom lightweight Tiny U-Net model was developed to extract individual 
tree and row shapes from RGB imagery, oﬀering fast and precise seg­
mentation suitable for real-world agricultural environments. Second, a 
1D Convolutional Neural Network (Conv1D) was employed to estimate 
citrus yield, based on vegetation indices and in-situ yield data. Several 
experiments were carried out to evaluate and identify the most eﬃcient 
and scalable approach for real-world applications in precision orchard 
management.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Study area description
The study area is located within the Souss-Massa basin of the central 
western part of Morocco, a region widely recognized for its agricultural 
productivity and crop diversity [36]. The area is known for its semi-arid 
climate, with an average annual precipitation of 200 mm/year [37,38]. 
This experimental field was carried out at an agricultural business citrus 
orchard in the Sebt El Guerdane region, approximately 54 km east of 
Agadir (Fig. 1).
Geographically, the site coordinates are situated between 30◦21’20''N 
and 30◦21’40''N latitude, and 8◦58’35''W and 8◦59’15''W longitude. 
The orchard covers an area of 13 ha, cultivated with Citrus Esbal 
Clementina trees, and is equipped with a drip irrigation system pro­
viding water for daily needs with a rate ranging between 1 and 3.5 
mm/day, depending on atmospheric demand, phonological cycle, and 
water availability. This system supplies water directly to the root zone, 
thereby achieving maximum water utilization and reducing surface 
runoff and evaporation loss. During periods of high evapotranspira­
tion, particularly July and August, irrigation is supplied at larger rates, 
while reduced amounts are supplied between October and December, 
indicating lowered crop water requirements. Moreover, pruning is prac­
ticed during the early stages of tree growth to shape the canopy and 
establish strong structure, generally a single fruiting pruning at the 
end of the dormant phase (late winter or early spring) for adult trees 
that are productive after harvesting. Additionally, a formative prun­
ing for young plantations to control their structural growth is done, 
at times a maintenance pruning also is managed following fruit set in

2.2. Methodology
The methodology followed in this study integrates a multifactor anal­
ysis approach by merging ground-based yield measurement, UAV-based 
multispectral imagery, vegetation index calculation, and deep learning 
techniques for citrus yield prediction and tree detection. The workflow 
initially began with the data collection phase, during which both mul­
tispectral drone imagery and ground-based yield data were obtained 
from the experimental orchard. Several vegetation indices such as the 
Normalized Diﬀerence Vegetation Index (NDVI), Normalized Diﬀerence 
Water Index (NDWI), and Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index (SAVI) were 
computed from high-resolution UAV multispectral images to describe 
canopy vigor and structure. Among the calculated vegetation indices, 
NDVI was chosen as the primary indicator for yield estimation because 
it reliably reflects plant health, water stress, canopy vigor, chlorophyll 
concentration, and photosynthetic activity, key factors influencing cit­
rus productivity. Previous studies have consistently confirmed its eﬀec­
tiveness for predicting yield and biomass in perennial crops [39--41].
The NDVI values were derived from multispectral UAV orthomosaics 
processed in Agisoft Metashape software through image alignment, geo­
referencing using ground control points (GCPs), and the generation of a 
dense point cloud. Subsequently, the digital elevation model (DEM), dig­
ital surface model (DSM), and final orthomosaic image were produced, 
along with the multispectral bands. The citrus rows were delineated as 
polygon features, and a row shapefile was generated to match the field 
plots where yield measurements were performed. Non-vegetation pixels 
were excluded by applying a vegetation threshold (NDVI > 0.2). The 
mean NDVI value for each row was calculated using the Zonal Statis­
tics tool, and the resulting NDVI dataset was spatially correlated with 
the corresponding field yield measurements. In addition to NDVI, seven 
other vegetation indices, such as Normalized Diﬀerence Water Index 
(NDWI), Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index (SAVI), Enhanced Vegetation 
Index (EVI), Normalized Diﬀerence Red Edge Index (NDRE), Green Nor­
malized Diﬀerence Vegetation Index (GNDVI), Chlorophyll Index Green 
(CIgr), and Leaf Area Index (LAI), were derived from the multispectral 
data to capture complementary information related to canopy structure, 
pigment content, and water stress. The concatenated dataset was sub­
sequently divided into training and evaluation subsets to develop and 
assess data-driven models for yield prediction.
In parallel, RGB UAV imagery was used for tree detection and canopy 
delineation through semantic segmentation. The tree crowns were man­
ually annotated to generate ground-truth labels for model training. 
The annotated dataset was then augmented through geometric trans­
formations, including rotation,cropping, and scaling, to enhance data 
diversity and improve model robustness. The dataset was then divided 
into training and testing subsets to ensure reliable model evaluation. 
The segmentation model was later integrated with the yield estimation 
model to produce a spatially explicit yield map at the individual tree 
level. This methodology provides a systematic approach that combines 
remote sensing, GIS-based spectral analysis, and artificial intelligence 
techniques for citrus yield estimation and tree mapping (Fig. 2).

2.3. Data
2.3.1. Data collection
Drip irrigation is a common practice in citrus orchards for water 
use eﬃciency and maintaining crop productivity under restricted water 
availability. In this study, citrus yield data were analyzed from sepa­
rate four rows, each consisting of 15 trees and irrigated using diﬀerent 
strategies taking in consideration the crops water needs represented by 
the Crop Evapotranspiration (ETc).
• X/2 strategy: The row is irrigated with half of the crop water re­
quirements (50% ETc).
• X strategy: The row is irrigated with the crop water needs (100% 
ETc).
• 3X/2 strategy: The row is watered with 1.5 water requirements 
(150% Etc).
• 2X strategy: The row’s irrigation water needs are doubled (200% 
Etc).
The yield data was collected from each tree within a row under each 
irrigation strategy, enabling a coherent analysis of the impact of dif­
ferent irrigation levels on crop and fruit yields [14]. For each selected 
tree, a representative sample of approximately ten fruits was collected 
and individually weighed. The average fruit weight obtained from this 
sample was then multiplied by the total number of fruits on the same 
tree to estimate the total yield per tree. This procedure was repeated 
for all trees within each row, allowing the computation of the mean 
yield per row, as summarized in Table 1, alongside the corresponding 
NDVI values for each irrigation strategy. In addition to yield data ac­
quisition, RGB images were used in order to perform tree detection and 
counting in citrus orchards. The dataset was required for estimating tree­
level productivity and spatially analyzing orchard structure. The UAV 
ﬂight mission was conducted using the DJI Matrice 300, a professional­
grade drone commonly used in precision agriculture; it features robust 
ﬂight performance, obstacle avoidance, and weather resistance. In our 
case, each flight lasted between 25 to 35 minutes, enabling complete 
coverage of the targeted orchard area. For this purpose, the drone was

2.4. Model architecture
2.4.1. Tree detection and counting
The implemented architecture consists of three main components: 
an encoder and a decoder that has two output channels, one for each 
class (trees and rows). Input images are initially passed through the en­
coder, which comprises a sequence of DoubleConv blocks and MaxPool­
ing layers. Each DoubleConv block includes two convolutional layers 
Fig. 6. Proposed Tiny U-Net architecture for semantic segmentation of citrus 
trees.
(Conv2D), each followed by a batch normalization layer and a ReLU 
activation function. The resulting feature maps are then fed into the 
decoder, which consists of a ConvTranspose2D layer for upsampling, 
followed by a concatenation with the corresponding feature map from 
the encoder and then a DoubleConv block. This process is repeated, with 
another ConvTranspose2D layer, another skip connection with an ear­
lier encoder block, and another DoubleConv block. Finally, the decoder 
ends with an output layer producing a double-channel image, each cor­
responding to the two classes. Fig. 6 illustrates the detailed architecture 
and the flow of data through the encoder and the decoder.
To further explore the performance of the proposed architecture, 
which employs a single decoder that generates two output channels 
simultaneously, a similar architecture with a dual-decoder approach 
was implemented. In this variant, each decoder is dedicated to a spe­
cific channel and outputs a mask for one of the corresponding classes, 
as shown in Fig. 7. This variation enables more specialized decoding 
per class while maintaining the same underlying architecture and input 
encoding. Model training was conducted using a defined set of hyper­
parameters, as summarized in Table 3. They were carefully chosen to 
ensure stable training and eﬀective performance using our dataset for 
tree recognition and counting for the segmentation task:
• Image size: All input images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels to 
standardize input dimensions and preserve adequate spatial infor­
mation.
• Batch Size: A batch size of 8 was employed to balance memory 
limitations with the ability to learn from multiple examples in each 
iteration.
• Learning Rate: The learning rate was set at 0.001, a standard value 
employs gradual and stable convergence throughout the training 
process.
• Number of Epochs: The models were trained for 200 epochs, which 
allowed suﬃcient iterations for the models to learn and adapt to our 
dataset.
• Loss Function: The Mean Squared Error (MSE Loss) was used to 
measure the pixel-wise squared error between the predicted and 
ground-truth masks, handling segmentation as a regression problem 
to make it suitable for our application purposes.

4. Discussion
The proposed methodology demonstrated high eﬀectiveness across 
segmentation, counting, and yield estimation tasks. It consistently de­
livered accurate results even in the presence of noisy or low-quality 
UAV imagery, underscoring the robustness of the pipeline. The Tiny 
U-Net model accomplished a segmentation accuracy of 96.88%, en­
abling a reliable spatial delineation of individual trees, as the counting 
stage attained an impressive R2 of 99.63%. For yield estimation, the 
CNN model trained on vegetation indices and ground truth yield data 
attained an R2 of 88%, eﬀectively capturing yield variability of crop 
physiological traits at the per-tree level. These results demonstrate that 
our approach enables eﬃcient, spatially accurate citrus yield prediction 
without the need for complex multi-angle or in-season fruit observabil­
ity. Zhu et al. [50], relied on multi-camera views and pruning events, 
meanwhile Apolo-Apolo et al. [51] focused on fruit detection and size 
estimation using Faster R-CNN and LSTM models. By comparison, our 
yield model solely necessitates vegetation indices and is suitable for 
early estimation and applicable to embedded architectures. In [52], a 
segmentation method was proposed using L*a*b* color space-based for 
citrus yield estimation, achieving 91.69% fruit counting accuracy and 
a R2 of 98%. This approach relied on more controlled imaging setup, 
with camera-object distances influencing detection accuracy. For exam­
ple, an advanced study combining UAV and Sentinel-2 data imagery 
demonstrated that fusing multispectral and structural data using Ran­
dom Forest modeling signiﬁcantly improved canopy nitrogen content 
estimation and its yield correlation in citrus orchards, achieving a R2
of 80% [53]. Similarly, Mwinuka et al. [54] reported that transformed 
diﬀerence vegetation index (TDVI) was particularly sensitive at detect­
ing leaf moisture content during initial growth stages. However, GNDVI 
and NDVI reacted more sensitively to nitrogen status. The combination 
of those indices is reflected to distinguish interaction between irriga­
tion and nitrogen application, a requirement for crop optimization and 
yield prediction. While, Suarez et al. [55] predicted yields at orchard

underscore the necessity of selecting context-appropriate architectures 
and developing innovative solutions to overcome persistent challenges 
in scale, visibility, and model generalization. Our approach oﬀers a more 
spatially detailed perspective by estimating yield at the individual tree 
level, enabling localized analysis of variation across the orchard. 
Table 8 contrasts three lightweight segmentation models, Yolo11n­
seg, Double Decoder Tiny U-Net, and our proposed Tiny U-Net. The 
Yolo11n-seg model shows moderate eﬀectiveness, especially in recall, 
with only 49.60% for tree class and 31.85% for row class, and an 
overall average precision of 74.11% and a recall of 40.73%, with the 
slowest inference time at 57.2 ms making it unsuitable for precise seg­
mentation tasks. On the other hand, the Double Decoder Tiny U-Net 
demonstrates signiﬁcantly improved segmentation results, reaching an 
overall precision of 90.59% and recall of 89.97%, with a weak inference 
time of 38.54 ms. At the class level, it achieved 89.27% precision and 
88.62% recall for the tree class, and 91.90% precision and 91.32% re­
call for the row class. Although the model achieves reliable results for 
both classes, the inference time decreases its overall reliability. In our 
case, the proposed Tiny U-Net architecture attained the highest perfor­
mance in all aspects, with an overall precision of 94.74% and recall of 
94.88%. Speciﬁcally, the model reached 92.36% precision and 93.30% 
recall for the tree class, and 97.11% precision and 96.47% recall for 
the row class, proving its robustness for real-time applications with a 
practical inference time of 12.55 ms. Despite the promising results, the 
proposed methodology presents numerous constraints that should be 
addressed in future work. Firstly, the model was not tested on trees 
that are spaced apart or located in irregular planting patterns, which 
may aﬀect segmentation accuracy and yield estimation reliability. More­
over, the segmentation results were satisfactory, further refinement of 
the model could enhance its precision, particularly in complex or clut­
tered scenes. Similarly, the yield estimation model could benefit from 
incorporating additional variables such as soil quality, irrigation data, 
or phenological stages to improve prediction accuracy across varying or­
chard conditions. These constraints, along with potential environmental 
and operational factors such as UAV flight limitations and image quality 
under changing weather conditions, may aﬀect the results.
5. Conclusion
This study demonstrates the signiﬁcant potential of integrating UAV­
based multispectral imagery with deep learning techniques for tree-level 
citrus yield estimation. A complete pipeline was developed to employ a 
Tiny U-Net architecture for tree and row segmentation, coupled with a 
CNN-based model leveraging vegetation indices for yield prediction. The 
proposed approach achieved high accuracy while maintaining process­
ing eﬃciency and cost-effectiveness, enabling the generation of detailed 
yield maps at the individual tree scale. It also exhibited an excellent 
robustness over tree density variation and noisy scenes, underscoring 
its reliability for orchard monitoring and decision support, by eﬀec­
tively combining spatial and spectral features to support data-driven 
decision-making in precision farming. Furthermore, the segmentation 
component of this work is readily transferable to other applications, in­
cluding canopy surface estimation, tree health assessment, and per-tree 
water consumption analysis, applications particularly relevant in arid 
and semi-arid regions. Nevertheless, the current study remains limited 
by its reliance on NDVI data collected from a single orchard and its po­
tential sensitivity to seasonal variability.
Future research should aim to broaden the applicability of the pro­
posed approach by evaluating its performance in orchards with lower 
planting densities and across diﬀerent crop types and tree species with 
varied crown architectures. Another key direction is the development of 
inline processing directly on UAV platforms using lightweight embedded 
systems, enabling real-time yield estimation during field operations and 
reducing dependence on high-performance architectures. Further work 
is also needed to enhance robustness under challenging field conditions, 
such as tree occlusions, variable lighting, and scale diﬀerences, which 
remain critical factors aﬀecting accuracy in UAV imagery. In addition, 
integrating the pipeline into operational decision-support systems would 
provide farmers with actionable per-tree yield insights, strengthening its 
role as a practical tool for orchard management. Finally, incorporating 
multi-temporal data, multi-sensor fusion, and transfer learning across 
orchards could further enhance model generalization and adaptability. 
Collectively, these eﬀorts would strengthen the value of the proposed 
approach as a scalable and flexible solution for precision agriculture, 
supporting sustainable farm management across diverse environmental 
conditions.
