Yield mapping
Yield mapping for citrus fruit is a challenging task due to factors such as varying illumi-
nation conditions, clustering, and occlusions. Mapping the yield for immature citrus fruit
presents an additional challenge that is the colours of fruit and leaves are almost identical.
Commonly used machine vision techniques using colour cameras become less effective for
immature citrus fruit detection. This study explores a novel active thermal imaging
method to tackle the problem of colour similarity between immature citrus fruit and
leaves. In this study, a thermal camera was combined with a water spray system that
applied water mist to citrus trees. The water mist caused temperatures of both the fruit
and leaf surfaces to change but at different rates. Multiple parameters of the spray system
were experimented with the goal to induce as much temperature differences as possible
between fruit and leaf surfaces. The combined system was tested in a citrus grove for fruit
detections. Deep learning models were built based on the active thermal imaging system
and tracking and fruit counting algorithms were created to count fruit in thermal videos. A
mean average precision of 87.2% was achieved by the models and an accuracy of 96% was
achieved when comparing the number of fruit counted by the algorithms with the true
number of fruit counted manually in the ﬁeld.

Introduction
Yield data, as an important indicator of crop health and a direct
measure of proﬁts, is perhaps the most important information
for farmers. Therefore, sensing and monitoring yield data has
been attempted and efforts to improve the speed and accuracy
of yield monitoring have been made. In general, autonomous
yield monitoring has been well-developed for grain crops, by
measuring the weight or volume of crops when harvesting (Lee,

Schueller, & Burks, 2005; Schueller & Bae, 1987; Searcy,
Schueller, Bae, Borgelt, & Stout, 1989). For tree crops, autono-
mous yield monitoring remains a difﬁcult problem partially due
to lacking mechanical harvesting solutions. One early attempt
was made by Schueller, Whitney, Wheaton, Miller, and Turner
(1999). In the study, a system was developed to record the lo-
cations of containers of harvested citrus fruit and to generate
yield maps for the citrus grove. Later, more attempts were
made using machine vision methods. Chinchuluun and Lee
(2006) used two colour cameras to acquire images of citrus
canopies from a ground vehicle and identiﬁed ripe fruit from
those images. Instead of using a ground vehicle, a mini heli-
copter was used by Macarthur et al. (2006). The study calculated
the areas of orange pixels to estimate the yield directly. An
important advantage provided by the machine vision methods
is that yield can be estimated before harvest. Thus, studies have
been conducted to identify immature fruit by cameras to create
early yield map.
The main challenges for identifying immature green fruit
are the colour similarities between fruit and leaves and
various illumination conditions. Taking citrus fruit as an
example, multiple methods and algorithms have been created
(Kurtulmus, Lee, & Vardar, 2011). created a novel feature
named ‘eigenfruit’ from colour images of green citrus can-
opies. The feature was combined with circular Gabor textures
to differentiate fruit from leaves. A correctly identiﬁcation rate
of 75.3% was obtained. Also using colour images (Sengupta &
Lee, 2014), and (Zhao, Lee, & He, 2016) developed similar
methods, that combined feature and colour information to
separate green fruit and leaves. Correctly identiﬁed fruit rates
of 80.4% and 83.4% were obtained by the two studies,
respectively. Other than using colour images (Kane & Lee,
2007), used multispectral imaging method and (Okamoto &
Lee, 2009) tested hyperspectral imaging method for this task.
Limited by the image qualities from multispectral and
hyperspectral cameras, these studies did not show better re-
sults than studies using colour images.
To solve the problems of colour similarity and various il-
luminations, better techniques should be utilised. Among
possible solutions, thermal imaging shows great potential.
Bulanon, Burks, and Alchanatis (2008) studied the temporal
temperature variation in citrus canopy under natural envi-
ronment. Thermal images of citrus canopies were acquired for
24 h. Due to the differences in thermal properties, fruit pre-
sented different temperatures from leaves. The relatively
large temperature difference was found in the afternoon,
around 16:00. They also did a preliminary test on fruit detec-
tion using the thermal images, which demonstrated the po-
tential of thermal imaging with limited time range. Later, the
same research team (Bulanon, Burks, & Alchanatis, 2009) built
a multi-modal system using a thermal camera and a colour
camera for fruit detection and obtained improved results
comparing to using a thermal camera alone. A similar multi-
modal system was also built by (Wachs, Stern, Burks,
Alchanatis, & Bet-Dagan, 2009) for apple detection. The
study achieved a recognition accuracy of 74%. Although
demonstrated the potential, these studies couldn’t achieve
high
accuracies
using
thermal
imaging.
More
effective
methods should be developed to transform the potential into
real applications.
The system developed in this study utilised an active
thermal imaging method that an external stimulus was
applied to the surfaces of tree canopies to induce temperature
contrast between fruit and leaves. The goal is to create a novel
thermal imaging system that eliminates some of the limita-
tions when using colour imaging and traditional passive
thermal imaging methods. Speciﬁc objectives include 1. to
develop an active thermal imaging system for fruit detection;
2. to determine parameters of the system and provide rec-
ommendations under different conditions; and 3. to achieve
high detection speed and accuracy for real-time applications.
2.
Materials and methods
This section describes (1) the design of the thermal imaging
platform; (2) the experiment on determining related parame-
ters; (3) the development of fruit detection and counting
algorithms.
2.1.
The design of the platform
The platform consists of three sub-systems which are the
driving system, the spray system, and the imaging system as
shown in Fig. 1. The driving system was a modiﬁed golf cart on
which the spray system and the imaging system were
installed. The spray system included a water tank, a 24-V DC
electric pump, three standard nozzle mounts, hose, and two
12-V DC car batteries. The imaging system included a thermal
camera (A655SC, FLIR, Wilsonville, OR, USA), a metal frame for
mounting the camera and a laptop (Precision M4700, Dell,
Austin, TX, USA).
The tasks the platform had to perform were spraying water
onto the citrus canopies to cause temperature differences
between fruit and leaves, then taking thermal videos of the
canopies for fruit detection. Therefore, the spray system and
the imaging system should work collaboratively. The ﬁrst
consideration in the design was the positions of the nozzles
and the camera. For the nozzles, given a typical spray angle,
80 or 100, the width of the overlapping area on the citrus
canopies should be no less than 0. As shown in Fig. 2, given the
distance between nozzle and citrus canopies, C, the nozzle
spacing B and spray angle q determines the width of the
overlapping area A. To ensure A is always bigger than 0, a
small spray angle, q equal to 80, and a short distance, C equal

2.2.
Experiments for determining parameters
There are numerous factors that could affect the results of the
active thermal imaging method. The type and model of the
nozzles, the optimum time to take thermal videos, the relative
humidity ðRHÞ, the ambient temperature ðTambient), and the
sunlight conditions are the most important ones. Therefore,
experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of those
factors.
The experiments were designed to evaluate the tempera-
tures of fruit and leave surfaces with respect to time after
being sprayed with water. For each experiment, one nozzle
model was selected to be mounted on the spray system. In this
study, TeeJet® (TeeJet® Technologies, Wheaton, IL, USA) ﬂat-
fan and full-cone nozzles were used. The pressure of the spray
system was set at the nozzles’ rated pressure, 276 kPa. The
platform travelled at a low speed of 0.5 m s1 to sweep the
target citrus canopy while temperatures were recorded by the
thermal camera ﬁxed on a stationary tripod. Figure 3 shows
the

The experiments were conducted under different weather
conditions. For each experiment, the RH, Tambient, wind speed,
sunlight situation (sunny side or shady side), the model of the
nozzle, average distance between the nozzle and the canopy,
and average fruit size were recorded. After the acquisitions,
fruit and leaf areas were sampled from the thermal videos and
their temporal temperatures were extracted. A python pro-
gram was written to analyse their temperature changes and
calculated two values, the maximum temperature difference
between fruit and leaves and the time to reach the maximum
value.
2.3.
Statistical analysis

of different factors. There were three hypotheses proposed by
this project, (1) the maximum achieved temperature differ-

with the amount of water applied in unit area; (2) Tmax has a
positive correlation with the Tambient; (3) Tmax has a negative
correlation with the RH. To test the hypotheses, a linear model
was created as Eq. (1), and multivariate linear regression was
performed to test the signiﬁcance of each independent
variable.

2.4.
Data collection using the active thermal imaging
method
During the data collection process, the thermal camera was
mounted at the end of the system (Fig. 1). All detailed param-
eters, including locations of the nozzles and the camera,
models of the nozzles, etc., were determined by the experi-
ments in section 2.2 and will be discussed in the results sec-
tions. The platform was controlled at approximately 0.5 m s1
while the nozzles were sweeping the canopies. The frame rate
of the thermal camera was set at its highest value which was 25
frames per second (FPS). The platform travelled linearly from
the beginning of a row to the end and returned to spray the
other side of the trees. The experiments were done at random
time periods between 10:00 AM and 3:00 PM on both sunny and
cloudy days. In total, data were collected for 20 rows.
2.5.
Training deep learning models
Two deep learning models, Faster ReCNN (Ren et al., 2015) and
Single Shot Multibox Detector (SSD) (Liu et al., 2016), were
tested for fruit detections. In Faster ReCNN, the ﬁrst 5
convolution layers of the VGG16 model were used for feature
extraction. Both methods were based on the deep learning
architecture Caffe, therefore, the preparations of training data
were very similar.
Firstly, training images were extracted from thermal
videos by sampling one frame per 20 frames, which ensured
that two sampled images had no overlap. Secondly, fruit lo-
cations in each image were labelled and annotation ﬁles were
created along with the labelled images. A total of 289 thermal
images
were
labelled.
Thirdly,
labelled
images
were
randomly divided into training-validation set and testing set
with a ratio of 4:1. Finally, three folders, an ‘Annotations’
folder, a ‘JPEGImages’ folder and a ‘ImageSets’ folder, were
created to contain the annotation ﬁles, image ﬁles and text
ﬁles that determined the ratio of training-validation and
testing data.
The training data were used as input for both the Faster
ReCNN and SSD to train models. A high-performance laptop
(Precision 7720, Dell, Austin, TX) with an 8 GB GPU (Quadro
P4000, NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to train the
models. For both the Faster ReCNN and SSD models, the
training took 50 h. By the end of the training processes, the
models were saved for fruit detections.
2.6.
Fruit detections from videos
The fruit detection algorithm consisted of three steps being
detecting, tracking, and counting. The trained deep learning
models were used to detect fruit in each thermal video frame.
Then, a tracking algorithm which was created based on the
Lucas-Kanade optical ﬂow method to track the locations of all
detected fruit. This information was used in the counting al-
gorithm, which counted the total number of fruit while avoi-
ded counting the same fruit multiple times. The three steps
were integrated as one algorithm. The algorithm could pro-
cess the video and output the numbers in real-time. Figure 4
shows the process of the fruit detecting, tracking and count-
ing algorithm.
3.
Results and discussion
3.1.
The temporal temperature changes of citrus
canopies
The temporal temperature changes of citrus canopies after
water spray were analysed by comparing the mean tempera-
tures of fruit areas and leaf areas. A low-pass ﬁlter was applied
to all the signals to remove high-frequency noises. Then, the
mean temperature of leaf areas subtracted from the mean
temperature of fruit areas, because fruit areas had higher
temperatures than leaf areas after water spray. Figure 5 shows
the typical temperature differences between fruit and leaf
areas in this process. To quantitatively measure the temporal
temperature curves, two measurements were used, being the
maximum difference between mean fruit and mean leaf
temperatures (Tmax) and the time it takes to reach the
maximum value as shown in Fig. 6. The orange line repre-
sented the piecewise linear ﬁt of the curve based on con-
strained least square method. The constraint used in this step
was that the ﬁrst and third lines had slope equal to 0.
3.2.
Determining appropriate parameters of the system
Based on the collected data from the experiments, multiple
steps were performed to determine the optimum ranges of
different parameters to achieve the maximum temperature
difference between fruit and leaf areas within the least
amount of time.
Firstly, a decision was made to select ﬂat-fan nozzles over
full-cone nozzles by comparing the Tmax between the nozzle
types and visually looking at the recorded thermal videos. If a
video showed more visual evidence that the canopies were
cooled uniformly, the nozzles associated with the video would
be rated with higher scores. As shown in Table 1, the Tmax and
the time to reach them were calculated when different nozzles
were used. The spry qualities were also quantiﬁed for each
nozzle. The results in the table showed that the ﬂat-fan nozzles
were superior to the full-cone nozzles for this research.
Secondly, statistical analysis was performed to test the
relationships
between
different
parameters
and
the
maximum temperature differences. The parameters con-
cerned included nozzle’s ﬂow rate, RH, Tambient, the distance
between the canopy and the nozzles and vehicle’s travel
speed. The ﬂow rate, the distance between the canopy and the
nozzles and vehicle’s speed were used to calculate the amount
of water applied per unit area using Eq. (2).

4.
Discussion
The goal of this research was to design an active thermal
imaging system that could effectively generate signiﬁcant
temperature differences between fruit and leaves when no
difference was presented and to help achieve a high fruit
detection accuracy. Possible factors that could have an impact
on the results were tested to build a reliable system. Based on
the
results,
the
recommendations
include:
(1)
apply
0.75e1.5 l m2 water to the surface of canopies; (2) high Tambient,
above 30 C, is the best for this method (3) lower RH (e.g. below
70%). has a positive impact on achieving higher temperature
differences, however, is not necessary. (4) There is no need to
avoid sunlight for this method. The contributions of this study
were that (1) an innovative active thermal imaging method
was created to visually separate fruit and leaves in thermal
images; (2) comprehensive statistical analyses were per-
formed to evaluate the impacts of factors in the design and
weather; (3) recommendations were made based on the
analysis for future design of such systems.
Utilizing active thermal imaging for object detection is a
novel method that has never been done in the ﬁeld of agri-
culture. The intention was to reduce the difﬁculty of image
and video processing through innovative designs of hardware.

5.
Conclusion
An active thermal imaging system was developed for creating
temperature differences between fruit and leaves and a
detection model was trained to count the number of imma-
ture fruit from thermal images of citrus canopies. Firstly,
Three hypotheses were tested and proved to be correct: (1)
amount of water applied in unit area is positively correlated
with the maximum temperature between the fruit and leaves
(Tmax); (2) the Tambient has a positive correlation with Tmax, and
(3) RH has a weak negative correlation with Tmax. Then, two
deep learning models, Faster ReCNN and SSD, were trained
using thermal video frames and achieved average precisions
of 87.2% and 85.5%, respectively, in detecting fruit from testing
images. When combined with tracking algorithms, the Faster
ReCNN based algorithm achieved 96% accuracy in counting
the fruit.
