A B S T R A C T
Accurate counting of small dense Chinese cherry (prunus pseudocerasus) is crucial for yield estimation and 
resource management but remains challenging due to background interference, morphological variation, and 
fruit adhesion ambiguity. A Fruit-Oriented Perception and Sharpening Network (FOPSN) is proposed to address 
the challenges by fruit concentration-adaptation and distribution sharpening. Specifically, Progressive Spatial 
Aggregation Encoder (PSAE) concentrates fruit to reduce background interference through region-aware 
refinement, and Adaptive Similarity Module (ASM) leverages morphology guided adaptation to achieve cherry 
recognition of diverse phenotypes. Meanwhile, Interleaved Channel Reconstruction Decoder (ICRD) resolves fruit 
adhesion ambiguity through dual channel rearrangement to sharpen fruit boundaries. FOPSN achieves a mean 
absolute error of 2.097, a root mean squared error of 2.838 and frames per second of 73, outperforming existing 
object detection and density map methods. Moreover, feature map confirms PSAE effectively highlights cherry 
regions against complex backgrounds, and density map visualization demonstrates the robust adaptation of ASM 
to diverse fruit morphologies and powerful boundary sharpening capability of ICRD. The proposed method 
provides a robust and reliable solution to count small and dense Chinese cherries under challenging orchard 
conditions, including complex backgrounds, morphological variations, and fruit adhesion ambiguity, and this 
approach can be integrated into practical orchard workflows to provide yield estimation, cultivation regulation, 
and harvest scheduling in field environments.
1. Introduction
Chinese cherry (prunus pseudocerasus) is widely cultivated in China 
for the luscious taste and high economic benefits, with an annual yield 
exceeding 1.6 million tons across over 3.5 million mu (Zhang et al., 
2023). In cherry cultivation, accurate fruit counting under complex or­
chard conditions provides a critical perception basis for practical or­
chard management. Reliable fruit counting results from orchard images 
can be incorporated into routine orchard workflows to determine fruit 
quantity and thereby support yield estimation, cultivation regulation, 
and harvest planning in dynamic environments(Devanna et al., 2025; 
Rong et al., 2025).
Traditional fruit counting methods, including random sampling and 
manual measurement, are typically laborious and unreliable. With the 
advancement of computer vision, object detection and instance 
segmentation provide a versatile solution for automated fruit counting. 
Zhao and Liu (2024) applied the faster region-based convolutional 
neural network to count ripe apples and achieved a regression rate of 
89.10 %. You only look once version x (YOLOX) achieves a yield esti­
mation accuracy exceeding 96 % for holly fruit counting in complex 
orchard conditions (Zhang et al., 2022). Wu et al. (2023) utilized 
DeepLabV3+ to finely segment sparse banana bunches during the 
debudding period and calculated the bunch count using classical image- 
processing algorithms, achieving a detection accuracy of 86 %. How­
ever, specific studies on dense fruit clusters with heavy overlap have 
reported notable degradations in counting methods based on detection 
and instance segmentation, with object detection achieving an average 
precision (AP) of 60 % and instance segmentation an AP of 59 %, 
highlighting missed and merged instances as common errors (Du & Liu, 
2023; Shen et al., 2022). This issue is exacerbated in small fruits like

Density map regression enables dense fruit counting by marking each 
object as a point and applying Gaussian kernel smoothing to create a 
density map that estimates spatial distribution without relying on pre­
cise boundary detection (Bhattarai et al., 2024; Gan et al., 2023; Zhang 
et al., 2025a,b; Zhang et al., 2024a,b). Lin et al. (2022) applied a density 
map to count male litchi flowers and reported a mean absolute error 
(MAE) of 16.29 and mean squared error of 25.40. Tian et al. (2019)
utilized a density map regression method based on a modified con­
volutional neural network to count pigs and achieved a MAE of 1.67 and 
root mean squared error (RMSE) of 2.13. Ranjan et al. (2021) introduced 
exemplar-based similarity modeling and subsequent density estimation 
to boost robustness. Yuan et al. (2023) utilized unmanned aerial vehicle 
images to generate blossom density maps for apple orchards and applied 
them to yield estimation. Cheng et al. (2023) developed a feature 
enhancement network based on density map regression for dense goji 
berry yield estimation and achieved a MAE of 9.62 and MSE of 22.47. 
Nevertheless, density map approaches remain limited in natural orchard 
conditions. Li et al. (2023) reported that in scenarios with severe fruit 
adhesion ambiguity and high morphological variability, MAE and RMSE 
reached 26 and 36, respectively. Cheng et al. (2025). found that under 
complex background conditions, fruit counting produced MAE and 
RMSE of 29 and 37, and accurate yield estimation was not possible. 
Therefore, background confusion, morphological variability, and fruit 
adhesion ambiguity undermined density estimation of small and dense 
Chinese cherries.
Du et al. (2023) introduced perceptual scope refinement to alleviate 
background interference, yet their refinement remains coarse in com­
plex orchard environments. Liu et al. (2024) and Zhou et al. (2024)
employed dynamic adaptation and discriminative embedding to 
enhance morphological robustness, but their approaches focus primarily 
on global representation adjustment and fail to address local corre­
spondence mismatches that arise with tiny or irregular fruits. Recent 
studies enhanced output contrast but fail to reorganize channel infor­
mation to recover detailed structural clarity in dense clusters and thus 
leave density responses indistinct when fruits adhere closely (Chen 
et al., 2022; Guan et al., 2024).
Herein, we propose the Fruit-Oriented Perception and Sharpening 
Network (FOPSN) to use fruit concentration-adaptation and distribution 
sharpening to achieve accurate counting of small dense Chinese cherries 
in natural orchard. The contributions of this work are as follows: 
(1) Progressive Spatial Aggregation Encoder (PSAE) facilitates 
region-aware refinement to concentrate fruit regions.
(2) Adaptive Similarity Module (ASM) uses morphology guided 
adaptation for robust fruit phenotype recognition.
(3) Interleaved Channel Reconstruction Decoder (ICRD) leverages 
dual channel rearrangement to improve fruit distribution clarity.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data acquisition
Image acquisition was conducted at multiple sites. In April 2024, 369 
images of the ‘Jiahong’ and ‘Rainier’ cherry cultivars were collected at 
Yueshan Cherry Orchard, Guangde, Anhui Province. In March 2025, 130 
early-stage images of the ‘Mingzhu’ cherry cultivar were collected at 
Wannian Cherry Orchard, Hefei, Anhui Province. All images were 
captured using a Nikon Z30 camera under natural illumination, with the 
camera positioned at a height ranging from 1.6 to 1.7 meters and an 
elevation angle between 30 and 45 degrees. A fixed focal length of 24 
mm was used, with exposure controlled in aperture-priority mode. ISO 
sensitivity was set to 125. A total of 499 high-resolution images of 4176 
× 2344 pixels were acquired.
2.2. Data processing
The dataset was firstly partitioned into 75 % for training and 25 % for 
testing with a fixed seed of 42 to ensure reproducibility. The dataset was 
then augmented with blurring using Gaussian blur with kernel size of 5 
and standard deviation of 1.5, overexposure by increasing brightness 
with factor of 1.3, and foggy enhancement simulating fog with density 
parameter of 0.5 (Fig. 1). The dataset comprised a total of 2,006 images.
2.3. Image annotation
Labelme supports annotating cherry counting images with two 
forms. In each image, every cherry is framed with a bounding box, and 
its width and height are recorded (Fig. 2A); meanwhile, each cherry 
target is marked with a point, and three targets are randomly selected as 
references (Fig. 2B). These annotations support detection and density 
map counting to ensure automated counting accuracy. For density map 
annotation, the point coordinates are extracted to create a point map. 
The size of the Gaussian window is determined as the average distance 
between each point and its nearest neighbor in the image. This window 
generates the target density map. The standard deviation σ of the 
Gaussian distribution is set to one-fourth of the window size, following 
common practice in density regression, which ensures adaptive match­
ing between the density map and the original cherry image (Ranjan 
et al., 2021; You et al., 2023). Assuming the position of the ith cherry 
center in the image is (xi, yi), the density map is calculated as:

2.4.1. Progressive spatial aggregation encoder (PSAE)
Existing methods struggle to focus on fruit regions due to background 
interference. Deformable convolution used learnable perceptual 
refinement of sampling locations to enhance attention to object regions 
(Zhu et al., 2019). Wang et al. (2022) employed region targeted ag­
gregation to enhance strawberry detection in complex environments. 
Combining fruit size information with offsets can better focus on fruit 
regions. Progressive Spatial Aggregation Encoder (PSAE) concentrates 
cherry fruit through region-aware refinement (Fig. 4).
Specifically, PSAE applies convolution and pooling operations to 
extract basic feature. We introduce a novel Foreground-Aware Convo­
lution (FAC) that dynamically adjusts the receptive field based on 
exemplar size, enabling precise alignment with foreground fruit regions 
while suppressing background interference. Furthermore, PSAE in­

2.4.3. Interleaved channel reconstruction decoder (ICRD)
Smoothing effect of upsampling fails to address adhesion negativity. 
Gao and Huang (2024) and Feng et al. (2023) explored channel inter­
action to purify feature representations and reinforce structural infor­
mation. Channel interactions can be combined with various 
rearrangement methods to improve edge delineation. As shown in Fig. 6, 
ICRD applies a novel dual channel rearrangement to sharpen object 
edges restored in regions with high target density.
Channel rearrangement is performed by detail preservation unit 
(DPU). DPU initially partitions the input Fn∈RHn×Wn×Cn into four channel 
groups Fn

existing cosine similarity and k-means with four clusters, where similar 
channels are combined to enhance local representation and restore 
blurred boundaries. Cluster-number ablation is in Table S2.

adjacent elements are interleaved across groups and in turn reinforces

3.2. Images of cherry
The images of cherry in natural orchard environments are illustrated 
in Fig. 7. In overexposed (Fig. 7A), underexposed conditions (Fig. 7B and 
C), complex background leads to boundary ambiguity and suppressed 
contrast between target and background. The resulting decline in visual 
clarity makes cherry distinction difficult. In environments with heavy 
foliage (Fig. 7D–F), the interweaving of cherries and leaves, combined 
with occlusion by dense branches, induces irregular shapes character­
ized by distorted contours, limited visibility, and fragmented edges. In 
cases of dense mass (Fig. 7G and H), fruits often adhere closely to one 
another, a phenomenon we refer to as fruit adhesion ambiguity, where 
the absence of clear separation between adjacent cherries leads to 
boundary ambiguity and increases the difficulty of distinguishing indi­
vidual fruits. Therefore, variations in illumination, morphological 
inconsistency, and fruit adhesion ambiguity create major obstacles for 
accurate cherry counting in natural environment.

3.4. Cherry counting based on density map regression
To evaluate the effectiveness of FOPSN, we compared it with five 
density map methods, FamNet (Ranjan et al., 2021), CSTrans-B (Gao & 
Huang, 2024), BMNet+ (Shi et al., 2022), PSGCNet (Gao et al., 2022), 
and LOCA (Đuki´c et al., 2023), as shown in Table 2. FamNet demon­
strated the counting performance with an MAE of 7.983 and an RMSE of 
12.759. Among the remaining four methods, the MAE ranges from 2.301 
to 3.082 and the RMSE falls between 3.113 and 4.165. These results 
reflect a similar level of performance. In contrast, FOPSN secured the 
lowest MAE of 2.097 and RMSE of 2.838 and delivered the most accurate 
counting results. Compared with the best competing method LOCA, 
FOPSN reduces MAE by 8.9 % and RMSE by 8.8 %. Fig. 9 presents the 
qualitative comparison of density maps for cherry counting. FamNet 
engendered severe undercounting as a result of its weak correlation 
representation and limited ability to localize objects accurately (He 
et al., 2024). In the case of BMNet+, the density maps appeared blurred 
and adhesive due to its limited adaptability to spatial variations (Gao & 
Huang, 2024). PSGCNet encountered similar problems because its scale 
selection mechanism failed to effectively distinguish between densely 
and sparsely distributed regions (Wang et al., 2024a,b). Although 
CSTransB produced cleaner visual outputs, its accuracy drops in areas 
where fruit shapes become irregular under occlusion, as the attention 
module tends to respond to surrounding background textures (Quan 
et al., 2025). LOCA provided better localization but still reduces density 
precision in the presence of shape variation, as its prototype adaptation 
mechanism struggled to retain fine spatial information (Xu et al., 2025). 
From Table 2, FOPSN exhibits relatively moderate parameter scale, 
FLOPs, and memory consumption, and attains superior counting 
accuracy to demonstrate an effective balance between performance and 
efficiency. Additionally, the test samples are divided according to oc­
clusion level, density level, and lighting condition, and the corre­
sponding error statistics for each subset are reported in Table S5. To 
further evaluate applicability across different fruits, we directly evalu­
ated FOPSN and several density map networks on a grape dataset (Zhang 
et al., 2025a,b) that was not used during training. As shown in Fig. S1 
and Table S6, FOPSN obtained the lowest MAE and RMSE among all 
methods, and this result indicates that FOPSN maintains stable counting 
performance under distribution shift and demonstrates robustness when 
applied to unseen fruit data. In summary, FOPSN produces concentrated 
density responses and maintains signal intensity in regions.
3.5. Ablation studies
To evaluate the contribution of each component in our method, we 
progressively add PSAE, ASM, and ICRD to framework on the cherry 
dataset and present the results in Table 3. The baseline, without PSAE, 
ASM, and ICRD, achieves an MAE of 2.756 and an RMSE of 3.872. 
Equipped with PSAE, framework records an MAE of 2.512 and an RMSE 
of 3.789. Region-aware refinement in PSAE enhances fruit regions and 
mitigates background interference. PSAE with ASM further lowers the 
MAE to 2.178 and RMSE to 3.045. Morphology guided adaptation in 
ASM improves fruit phenotype recognition and addresses morphological 
variation. The combined operation of PSAE, ASM, and ICRD achieves the 
highest precision with an MAE of 2.097 and an RMSE of 2.838, 
improving counting accuracy under complex natural scenes, although it 
increases parameters and FLOPs. Additional results under different 
random seeds are shown in Table S7.
3.5.1. Impact of PSAE
To validate the effectiveness of PSAE, we gradually incorporated 
CSFF and FAC and evaluated their effects on the cherry dataset, as 
detailed in Table 4. Without CSFF and FAC, the network yields an MAE 
of 2.560, RMSE of 3.615, 29.95M Params, and 47.82G FLOPs. The use of 
CSFF alone reduces MAE to 2.315 and RMSE to 3.245, while increasing 
Params to 30.21M and FLOPs to 49.15G. After adding FAC, the network 
achieves the optimal accuracy, with an MAE of 2.097, RMSE of 2.838, 
31.20M Params, and 58.90G FLOPs.
Fig. 10 illustrates the changes in the feature response at each stage. 
At the early stage, the feature map shows fruit regions in a faint yellow 
tone (Fig. 10A), a reflection of insufficient spatial discrimination. The 
network fails to establish clear boundaries between the fruit and its 
background. CSFF aligns and concatenates shallow and deep charac­
teristics to enhance spatial detail preservation and semantic enrichment. 
The image presents a chromatic progression from pale yellow to an 
orange-tinted hue, a transformation indicative of enhanced focus on 
salient regions alongside a more comprehensive semantic representation 
of the target area (Fig. 10B). Subsequently, FAC refines characteristic 
extraction to achieve the highest activation intensity and precise con­
centration on fruit regions, manifested in a dominant red tone 
(Fig. 10C). PSAE, formed by the integration of CSFF and FAC, 
strengthens attention to fruit and suppresses background interference.

3.5.3. Impact of ICRD
We compare DPU in ICRD with Bilinear Interpolation and Pixel 
Shuffle to assess its effectiveness (Table 6). DPU achieves the lowest 
MAE of and RMSE. It reduces MAE by 15.4 % and RMSE by 12.7 % over 
Bilinear Interpolation, and by 8.8 % and 11.1 % over Pixel Shuffle, 
respectively. Additionally, in terms of model complexity and computa­
tional cost, DPU demonstrates superior efficiency. Its Params are 
31.20M, a reduction of 2.77M compared to Bilinear Interpolation and a 
reduction of 4.38M compared to Pixel Shuffle. The FLOPs of DPU are 
58.90G, lower than those of Bilinear Interpolation and Pixel Shuffle. The 
decrease amounts to 1.33G compared to Bilinear Interpolation and 
2.78G compared to Pixel Shuffle. Fig. 12 compares density maps 
generated by Bilinear Interpolation, Pixel Shuffle, and DPU. White cir­
cles highlight improved regions in these maps. Bilinear Interpolation 
yields density responses in fruit adhesion ambiguity regions that are 
abnormally pale and often become indistinguishable from the back­
ground or disappear entirely (Fig. 12A). This phenomenon arises due to 
its oversimplification of spatial structures and its inability to maintain 
gradient continuity. As a result, the method fails to preserve localized 
contrast. Pixel Shuffle produces moderately enhanced density signals, 
but the representations in adhesive regions remain fused, making it 
difficult to distinguish closely distributed targets (Fig. 12B). This 
blending occurs because the method lacks spatial correspondence dur­
ing upsampling and fails to preserve the structural independence of 
adjacent instances. DPU produces sharper and more accurate density 
representations in fruit adhesion ambiguity areas (Fig. 12C) through 
dual channel rearrangement that enables precise spatial reconstruction.
3.5.4. Impact of Gaussian kernel standard deviation
The choice of Gaussian kernel standard deviation plays a critical role 
in density map generation. To assess its influence, we test different 
settings where σ is one-third, one-fifth, and one-fourth of the window 
size. The results are summarized in Table 7. When σ is one-third, the 
network reports an MAE of 2.145 and RMSE of 3.189. When σ is one- 
fifth, the errors are 2.312 and 3.367. In comparison, σ is one-fourth of 
the window size and achieves the lowest errors, with an MAE of 2.097 
and RMSE of 2.838. The results demonstrate that σ is one-third produces 
density maps that are excessively smoothed so that local peaks flatten 
and the discrimination of closely spaced fruits decreases (Fig. 13A), σ is 
one-fifth produces density maps where the peak region becomes too 
small so that the model fails to capture sufficient contextual information 
and the counting results suffer from underestimation (Fig. 13B), and σ is 
one-fourth maintains a proper balance between sharpness and conti­
nuity (Fig. 13C).

detection and density map techniques. These approaches offer practical 
value for orchard management compared with the limited feasibility of 
manual counting. However, their effectiveness decreases in orchard 
environments where foreground distractions such as branches and 
leaves interfere with recognition, morphological diversity across growth

stages complicates adaptation, and dense clustering of small cherries 
leads to adhesion ambiguity. These challenges remain the primary 
bottlenecks to achieving reliable yield estimation. Fruit-Oriented 
Perception and Sharpening Network (FOPSN) is proposed to enable 
precise counting of small and dense fruits through fruit concentration- 
adaptation and distribution sharpening.
Progressive Spatial Aggregation Encoder (PSAE) employs region- 
aware refinement to enhance spatial perception for precise fruit locali­
zation. Unlike conventional frameworks with narrow receptive fields 
that overlook small fruits (Yu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024a,b), PSAE 
gradually integrates information from multiple stages under region- 
aware guidance. This strategy enlarges contextual perception and re­
tains precise spatial cues so that the network achieves more reliable fruit 
localization in complex backgrounds.
Adaptive Similarity Module (ASM) performs morphology guided 
adaptation by weight and offset coordination to adjust sampling pat­
terns tailored to diverse cherry shapes. Liu et al. (2024) proposed an 
adaptive strategy with dual constraints and dynamic feature selection to 
address scale and shape variations in apple leaf diseases, but its inability 
to model spatial contextual correlations limits the recognition of lesions 
with ambiguous boundaries. Zhou et al. (2024) used a Contrastive 
Learning Scheme with category and prototype losses to improve classi­
fication and adapt to similar blossom morphologies, yet limited gener­
alization reduced its effectiveness when applied to blossoms with 
irregular morphologies. ASM explicitly models local spatial correspon­
dences together with contextual cues and thereby enables reliable 
recognition of cherries that exhibit ambiguous or irregular morphology.
Furthermore, Interleaved Channel Reconstruction Decoder (ICRD) 
resolves fruit adhesion ambiguity by dual channel rearrangement to 
sharpen fruit boundaries. Although CDMENet (Li et al., 2023) designed 
auxiliary tasks to classify spatial regions by density and introduced a loss 
function to enhance the contrast between different density levels, it 
exhibited persistent density peak blurring in severe adhesion. ICRD 
differs by directly reconstructing channel representations in an inter­
leaved manner and thus restores distinct density responses while pro­
ducing sharper and more separable boundaries even under extreme 
adhesion conditions.
Despite these advancements, fixed angle images miss heavily 
occluded fruits and restrict complete spatial perception, leading to 
missing density responses and underestimation in heavily occluded re­
gions. Representative failure examples are shown in Fig. S2. Fruit 
counting in video sequences under natural scenes remains to be further 
explored. In addition, the model parameters and operational efficiency 
can be further optimized for on-site orchard application. In the future, 
multi-view image acquisition could recover incomplete fruit observa­
tions caused by occlusion through geometric consistency across view­
points. Optical flow tracking will be combined with video counting to 
reduce duplicate counting. Furthermore, employing convolutional 
decomposition strategies and knowledge distillation techniques can 
realize lightweight models.
5. Conclusion
In this study, FOPSN is designed to count Chinese cherry in natural 
orchard through fruit concentration-adaptation and distribution sharp­
ening. Among FOPSN, PSAE employs region-aware refinement to sup­
press background interference, while ASM achieves morphological 
adaptation through weight and offset coordination. Finally, ICRD 
sharpens fruit boundaries by dual channel rearrangement to resolve fruit 
adhesion ambiguity. FOPSN achieves an MAE of 2.097 and an RMSE of 
2.838, and surpasses object detection and density map methods. Feature 
visualization demonstrates PSAE effectively highlights cherry regions 
under complex backgrounds, and the density map confirms the adap­
tation of ASM to morphological diversity and boundary sharpening by 
ICRD. In a word, FOPSN delivers accurate and robust counts for dense 
Chinese cherries in images by addressing complex backgrounds, 
morphological diversity, and adhesion ambiguity, and thereby facili­
tates orchard management tasks. In the future, multi view spatial 
completion, video based temporal modeling, and lightweight model 
deployment will be explored to enhance counting reliability and prac­
tical usability.
