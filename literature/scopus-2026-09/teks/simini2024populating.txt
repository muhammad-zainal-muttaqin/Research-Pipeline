Populating Digital Orchards: Evaluation of a 3D
Positioning Algorithm for Semi-Automated Data
Collection and spawning

Abstract—Precision Orchard Management tasks require high-
grade precision representation of plants information to unleash
the potential and the efficiency of involved operations. In order to
improve this, especially when autonomous vehicles are involved,
a 3D orchard reconstruction is particularly useful. This work
aims to exploit RGBD images to estimate the local 3D position
of fruits on a plant, with respect to the trunk position. Besides
being a useful information to map plant production distribution,
it can also be used to localize real objects in virtual environments,
such as digital twins. The process can serve as a foundation
of numerous monitoring activities, thinning procedures and
autonomous harvesting tasks.
Index Terms—On-line monitoring, computer vision, digital
twins, precision orchard management, simulation environment
I. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM DEFINITION
Monitoring the crop status and creating prescription maps
are fundamental for all Precision Agriculture (PA) applica-
tions. In tree crops, three-dimensionality complicates variabil-
ity mapping, as vertical information (i.e., plant height) is often
excluded from conventional 2D maps, reducing data accuracy.
However, proximal sensing using side-view ground computer
vision (CV) applications and autonomous agricultural vehicles
can include 3D positional information, particularly when using
depth-cameras (RGBD).
Recently, numerous image analysis approaches based on
RGBD information have been investigated due to their lower
computational requirements compared to point cloud analysis.
Adding depth information alongside color allows CV algo-
rithms to both identify and locate plant physiological traits.
Previous studies have explored CV applications on RGBD
streams, focusing on extracting physiological parameters such
as canopy volume and porosity [1], crop load, fruit size [2],
[3], and fruit growth rate monitoring [4]. While depth maps
enabled precise remote sensing, the positional information
from depth images was not exploited in these researches.
To enhance the spatial accuracy of 3D orchard recon-
structions, for digital twin development, matching spatial and
physiological information is crucial. A RGBD camera was
utilized in [5] to develop a CV algorithm for trunk detection,
localization, and sizing in real-time. Precisely geo-referencing
each trunk allows linking physiological attributes to specific
positions, requiring positional data in a 3D local coordinate
system with the trunk origin.
Precise positioning of fruits in 3D space can aid in mapping
field variability at the single-plant level, enabling farmers to
implement optimal crop load management practices. For ex-
ample, clustering statistics based on estimated XY Z positions
can inform where fruits are overly aggregated, guiding targeted
hand-thinning applications.
Positioning information can also enhance autonomous fruit
harvesting [6], [7] and the development of accurate digital tree
models for digital twins, which can be fundamental simulation
environments that can help train robots to perform effectively
in real-world scenarios [8].
In this context, the present study aimed to develop an
algorithm to precisely estimate the local 3D coordinates of
detected fruits relative to the trunk position. Potential appli-
cations of this algorithm include: i) generating realistic world
files for simulation environments to train and develop picking
robots; ii) exploiting positioning information to determine fruit
proximity in 3D space for better management during thinning
or harvest. Knowing fruit proximity and positioning is crucial,
as sub-populations within the canopy produce varying fruit
quality depending on exposure to light (i.e., X and Y coordi-
nates) and altitude (i.e., Z coordinate). In addition, mapping
fruit position and features can prevent poor quality at harvest;
furthermore, an accurate digital model with precise crop load
and fruit position data is a powerful tool for simulations and
forecasting yield quantity and quality.
This study could represent an important step in the de-
velopment of digital twins in agriculture: the collected data
by proximal sensing CV algorithms that automatically feed
physiological parameters connected with spatial information
can enable a new paradigm for digital orchard creation,
enhancing farm digitalization and data-driven management.

II. MATERIALS AND METHODS
This trial aimed at evaluating the performance of a 3D
positioning algorithm capable of adding information (e.g.,
physiological) to each annotated object in an image within a
3D local coordinate system (XYZUp). The coordinate system
has its origin at the base of the tree trunk.
The algorithm evaluation was performed using both 2D and
3D setups to mimic different tree training systems with lower
(2D) or higher (3D) volume expansion
The evaluation was conducted under both laboratory and
field conditions to better investigate the real applicability of
the developed system. The laboratory trial was first carried
out to evaluate the algorithm’s performance in a controlled
environment, i.e., with clear and defined object positions in the
frame, constant lighting conditions (no sunlight interference),
no object occlusion (e.g., due to leaves), and no object
movement (e.g., wind effect). In this best-case scenario, the
algorithm’s performance in positioning and annotating infor-
mation was assessed. Subsequently, a field trial was conducted
to investigate the algorithm’s performance in a real-world
scenario, accounting for higher complexity.
A. Laboratory setup
In the laboratory trial, an artificial tree similar to the
one used by [9], was used to asses algorithm positioning
performances. The artificial tree (dimensions: 1 m x 0.5 m
x 2 m - Fig. 1) was built with a simple geometry that
facilitated the manual collection of the reference Cartesian
coordinates of each hung object. The structure included five
horizontal bars (i.e., first-level branches) installed to extend
along the x-axis with x[m] ∈[−0.5, 0.5]. Along the z-axis,
the bars were installed every 0.30 m top-to-bottom, defining
five elevation levels (z[m] = [2.0, 1.7, 1.4, 1.1, 0.8]). With that
characteristics, the XZ plane was defining the 2D set-up (Fig.
1). To allow the switching to a 3D set-up (Fig. 3), each bar
was added with two additional wood sticks (i.e., second-level
branches) of 0.50 m length, mounted with a folding joint that
enables/disables to turn into a 3D space by extending the
y-axis with y[m] ∈[0, 0.5]. All the first- and second-level
branches were equipped with a centimeter scale measuring
tape, intended to facilitate the definition of the xyz coordinates
of each hung object. The choice of switching from a 2D to a
3D space, was done to test the hypothesis that a 2D-like tree
training system could improve the in-orchard performance of
the positioning CV application, similarly to what stated in [10].
At trunk-level, a pink tag was placed on a wood plate
to facilitate the recognition of the trunk origin during image
processing phases. The origin of the local coordinate system
XYZUp was associated to the bottom-left corner of the pink
tag, which was placed at 0.127 m above ground-level (Fig. 1).
Objects, with various known dimensions (i.e., apples and
tennis balls), were hung on the artificial tree at pre-defined
positions (pos-id) ranging from 1 to 15 in the 2D set-up,
and from 1 to 25 in the 3D set-up. Specifically, objects with
pos-id ∈[1, 15], were hung at each first-level branches at
x = [−0.5, 0.0, 0.5] m, while pos-id ∈[16, 25] were generated

by adding an object (i.e, apples, tennis balls) to each second-
level branch. The use of both tennis balls and apples was meant
to investigate the positioning errors related to the effect of
regular or irregular shapes, similarly to what done in [3].
Two different background scenarios were tested on the
artificial tree: a leafy background and a black background
(see Fig. 1 and Fig. 3). The purpose was to assess the
impact of background variation on object correct positioning,
either by enhancing the contrast between the objects and
background (black background) or by simulating more realistic
field conditions (leafy background).
B. Field setup
Aside of the laboratory trial, in-field testing was conducted
to evaluate algorithm performances in real-world scenarios.
Also here, both the 2D and 3D setups were evaluated by
testing two different tree training systems: the ’Spindle’ system
with a more voluminous 3D-like architecture and the ’Planar
Cordon’ [10] with a simpler, standardized 2D-like architecture.
For each training system, three fruits were selected and tagged
(pos-id ∈[1, 3]) from five trees. Pos-id were chosen according
to what done for laboratory trial pos-id=1 placed in the higher
part of the canopy, while Pos-id=2 and Pos-id=3 in the middle
and lower part respectively. As for laboratory setup, a marker
was placed on the trunk to facilitate its recognition during the
image processing phase. For the in-filed setup, tennis-balls
objects were not included as later explained in the discussion
section.
C. Data collection and preparation
The data collection consisted in collecting, with an Intel
RealSense D435i, RGBD pictures of the artificial tree with
objects hung at each pos-id. Following each picture, the
ground-truthing Cartesian coordinates of each object were
defined in the local XYZgt coordinate reference system. The
latter is imposed by the use of the Intel RealSense stereo-

camera, which defines the image as the XY plane and the
distance as the Z coordinate.
Color and depth images of the laboratory setup were pro-
duced with the stereo-camera set to generate data with the best
resolution and accuracy for both RGB (1080 x 1920) and depth
(480 x 848) streams. Differently, in the field test the resolution
of the color image was reduced to 1280 x 720, since this
could be a reasonable high-resolution for in-field applications
matching storage requirements with resolution and processing
time.
Images were collected exploiting the software from Intel
(RealSense Viewer), which made possible to directly align
depth-to-color images, resulting in the same color resolution
for both RGB and depth images. The same process could
also be done exploiting the ROS2 framework. The stereo-
camera was physically placed and levelled at 1.1 m height
on a tripod, that was positioned at 1.5 m from the artificial
tree. To completely include the artificial tree in the frame, the
camera was oriented to have, in XYZgt, its larger field of view
parallel to the y-axis (i.e., tree height).
With this methodology, 27 couples of aligned RGBD im-
ages, containing 405 objects, were collected in the 2D setup,
while 12 couples of images containing 300 objects were
collected in the 3D setup.
A ground truth dataset was generated by including the
manually measured xyz coordinates in the XYZgt, and the
size and weight of each object. Object size and weight were
collected with the purpose of having attributes to be included,
as physiological parameters, during the automated spawning.
For field trial, images were collected following the same
approach with the same software, camera orientation and
parameter defined above; just after the images collection, per
each tree, camera distance from the trunk and height from the
ground were measured, as well as XYZgt coordinates of each
fruit, from the trunk origin. The distance of the trunk marker
from the ground was recorded per each tree. Lastly, average
values of size and weight were exploited to test the ability
of the algorithm in correctly adding attributes to each object,
after its positioning.
From field data collection, a total of 5 couples of aligned
RGBD images, containing 15 objects, were collected for both
the planar cordon and the spindle tree training systems.
D. Image Processing
Prior to applying the positioning algorithm, all RGB im-
ages were set to portrait mode and manually annotated with
bounding box polygons (bbox). Annotations had the purpose
of correctly identifying and localising the objects (trunk origin,
fruits, tennis balls) in the images, with the aim of properly
linking the ground truth dataset with the images. Only for
trunk annotation, a dedicated marker was placed both on
artificial and real trees to facilitate the trunk origin defini-
tion that could be altered by weeds or uneven ground. The
annotation task, could be replicated by Convolutional Neural
Network (CNN) trained to detect specific objects, as done in
[3] and [11]. Images were annotated using the Supervisely
[12] web platform which made possible to include, within
its JSON-based annotation format, additional information for
each labelled object. Those included the pos-id to be used
as the link with the ground truth dataset. Consequently, the
annotations were designed to be the starting point for the
positioning algorithm.
E. Positioning algorithm
The positioning algorithm was developed in Python 3.10
and made available at https://github.com/ECOPOM. The im-
plemented pipeline estimates objects relative position with
respect to the trunk origin in the XYZUp space starting from
the RGBD images and their annotations. In order to do that,
the processing pipeline includes the following steps:
1) by exploiting the color camera intrinsic parameters (fo-
cal length, principal point coordinates) and the depth
matrix, the pixel, P(u, v), deprojection is computed,
thus pixels are transformed into spatial coordinates,
P(x, y, z). The resulting coordinates, in millimeters,
have the y-axis representing the vertical distance be-
tween P(x, y, z) and the principal point, while the z-
axis is the depth distance of P(x, y, z) and the principal
point;
2) to better exploit annotations, along with spatial coordi-
nates, a six-channels tensor (6Dimage), with same width
and height as the color image, is created to host for each
P(u, v) the x, y, z spatial coordinates and the 8-bit red,
green and blue color coordinates;
3) by exploiting the manual annotation, the algorithm ex-
tracts, for each image, the trunk origin in pixels, T(u, v).
Then, with a translation matrix (TM, eq. 1), the spatial
coordinates stored in 6Dimage are centered at T(x, y, z).
This process moves the origin of the image from the
camera to the trunk, within the XYZgt space;

F. Automated Spawning
Following the definition of O(x, y, z), the object’s descrip-
tion defining the pos-id, was accessed from the annotation.
This allowed to get from the ground truth dataset its attributes
such as the object size, weight and reference position. These

information were then coded into python f-strings and added
as inertial, collision and visual XML tags of the respective
model. All the base meshes used to populate the digital twin,
such as the models of the artificial tree, apple and tennis ball,
were shaped in Blender 4.2.0 and exported as collada files.
Particularly, the apple and tennis ball models had predefined
dimensions, which had to be scaled to the target object size
as defined in the ground truth dataset. As a consequence, also
scaling factors had to be inserted into the f-strings to make
the digital twin accurate. To increase the accuracy of both
the digital twins, the average color of the object, accessed
trough the 6Dimage, is converted into RGBA color space
and added as a visual property of the model. The mesh to
associate with the object is defined based on the object’s class
(e.g., apple, tennis ball) as specified in the annotations. Each
model is then added to the Gazebo .world file containing the
digital twin of the tree (Fig. 1). For a better comparison of
the positioning performances, all the object-related attributes
were added to both a reference and an estimated digital twin,
with the only difference in the coordinates used to position
the models on the digital tree (i.e, reference and estimated
positions respectively).
G. Data Analysis and Evaluation
Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the Euclidean and
Manhattan distance (from here on referred to as ED, MD)
between the reference and estimated position of each object
was computed, as well the MBE (Mean Bias Error) for the
single axis. The computed errors were then explored to find
relations with the setup (i.e., 2D, 3D), the background, the type
of object (i.e., apple, tennis ball), the position of the object in
the frame (i.e, pos-id) and the object dimensions.
A visual comparison of each color image, with its respec-
tive reference and estimated digital twin was performed; the
.world files related to the reference and estimated digital twin
were inspected and compared to verify the proper attributes
association of each object.
Processing time needs were tested to understand possible
real-time application: testing consisted in measuring the time
needed to process all the images for 7 times and computing
average processing time parameter; testing was performed on a
gaming laptop (Intel-i7 CPU, 32GB RAM, 1TB NVME SSD,
Nvidia RTX-3070 GPU)
III. RESULTS
A. Laboratory trial
Results showed a positioning error, expressed as MBE, of
+0.001 m along the x-axis, -0.010 m on the y-axis, and +0.043
on the z-axis (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).
The data analysis, demonstrated that a 2D setup (RMSE:
MD ±0.057 m, ED ±0.045 m) reduces the positioning error
with respect to a 3D setup (MD ±0.072 m, ED ±0.056 m).
As an average, objects positioned with a pos-id ∈[1, 15] (i.e.,
2D setup) have higher positioning accuracy (Mean Absolute
Error, MAE: 0.055 m) with respect to objects with a pos-id ∈
[16, 25](i.e., 3D setup) (MAE: 0.0854 m). For pos-id ∈[1, 15]
the positioning error seemed to slightly decrease with increas-
ing pos-id, so passing from objects positioned in the higher
part of the artificial tree to objects in the lower part (Fig.
2). This was tested, and no correlations were found between
pos-id and positioning errors, neither for pos-id ∈[1, 15] nor
pos-id ∈[16, 25].
The effect of background and object type on the positioning
error was tested. Background type showed no relationship
or alteration with positioning performance (data not shown),
while object type demonstrated that in the 2D space, tennis
balls have higher errors (RMSE: MD ±0.058 m, ED ±0.047 m)
with respect to apples (RMSE: MD ±0.054 m, ED ±0.043 m),
but in the 3D space, apples are positioned with less accuracy
(RMSE: MD ±0.090 m, ED ±0.068 m) with respect to tennis
balls (RMSE: MD ±0.063 m, ED ±0.051 m).
Regarding object size, all the three object dimensions were
evaluated for Pearson’s correlation against the positioning er-
ror, but results showed no relationship between the considered
variables: r values found were always < 0.16.
The visual analysis showed the ability of the developed
algorithm to correctly create, starting from both 2D and 3D
setup images, a digital representation of the artificial tree (Fig.
3) while properly positioning and associating size, weight (data
not shown) and color features (Fig. 3) of each annotated object.
Regarding time requirement, a code to loop 7 times the data
analysis was exploited. In total, 312 data packages (including
annotations, color and depth images process) summing 5640
object to be positioned were analyzed, resulting in a total
processing time of 125.876 s; Average processing time was
336 ms/pack and 3.5 ms/obj.
B. In-filed test
When moving in field, the algorithm reported a MBE of -
0.038 m along the x-axis, +0.108 m on the y-axis, and +0.177
m on the z-axis (data not shown). 2D setup (i.e., Planar Cordon
system) showed to reduce the positioning error with respect to
3D setup (i.e., Spindle system), confirming laboratory results
also in-field (2D RMSE: MD ±0.460 m, ED ±0.370; 3D
RMSE: MD ±0.607 m,ED± 0.380 m).
Also if with a minimum resolution and a lower replication
number (i.e., only 3 positions per each tree analyzed), the
effect of object position was analyzed and no correlation
was found. Despite that, a trend was detected: objects in
the top part of the canopy (>1.70 m) showed higher error
compared to the ones in the middle and lower canopy zone
with a cumulative absolute error of 0.418, 0.306, and 0.284 m
respectively.
The visual analysis to evaluate the correct creation of a
digital representation was carried out and, as for the laboratory
trial, the algorithm correctly associated object information
together with its position (Fig. 4).
The analysis of the background, object type and size, on the
positioning error was not investigated for the in-field trial, as
later explained in the discussion section.
Regarding time requirement, the same code was exploited
and a total time of 15.757 s was needed to 7-times loop the

IV. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Per each digital representation, in both 2D and 3D setups,
objects were correctly spawned in terms of object type (tennis
ball, fruits and trunk origin) and position identifier. All the
objects were showing the same sizes as reported in the ground
truth dataset and the color of all the objects passed the visual
check ensuring the representativeness of the digital twin. The

Regarding the positioning performances of the algorithm,
a 2D set-up resulted in being easier to be digitized, thus
supporting what stated in [10], regarding how planar training
system, such as the planar cordon, can empower the appli-
cation of computer-vision algorithms in orchard. This was
expected since, when working in a 2D-like reconstruction,
the perspective effect is negligible, while when dealing with
objects at different depth levels in the scene, the perspective
effect become an important factor to be considered. Also
field test results confirmed this fact, showing a positioning

performances improved from 0.010 to 0.210 m (i.e., up to 30
%) , thanks to the 2D training system.
As above mentioned, the effect of background, object type
and size was not evaluated in field. This was done since
effect of these variables, in laboratory conditions, showed
to not effectively alter the positioning error. In addition, the
positioning error related to object type was negligible (<0.03
m), when considering real-world application.
Results showed a wide difference in terms of performance
between laboratory and in-field trials, with errors increasing of
almost tenfold (MD and ED RMSE). This behaviour was ex-
pected due to the difficulties that computer vision applications
can encounter in field. Regarding the developed algorithm,
some in-field conditions highly affecting its performances are:
the difficult light conditions affecting both object identification
and depth estimation (and consequentially object position-
ing); the orchard weed management, since weed can blind
the camera impeding the trunk detection and/or its distance
estimation, for example. Other limitations can be related to the
computational needs, that should be kept as low as possible
when dealing with in-field, real time application.
Considering this latter point, the performance obtained
seems adequate to a real-time utilization: the average time per
package mainly represents the time needed to create and spawn
the digital representations, which will not occur in an in-field
data collection. So the 3.5 ms needed to process each object
appear to be a good result, considering a possible analysis of
280 obj / s approx.
When discussing the positioning performance obtained, it
is worth to make some considerations regarding the manual
ground truth collection. Considering the resolution at which
the manual ground truth position was collected (0.01 m), this
bring an uncertainty to the measurements of ± 0.01 m; this
uncertainty is present in each of the dimensions considered in
the setup resulting in a MD and ED uncertainty error of ± 0.02
and ± 0.03 m per 2D and 3D setup respectively; these values
are similar to the performances obtained in laboratory trial. In
addition, the operators ability in collecting the ground truth
should be considered: from internal testing, asking different
operators to collect in-field XYZup position coordinates of the
same fruit showed an uncertainty in a range of 0.050 - 0.200 m
(i.e., generally 10% of the measured distance) due to operators
measuring error.
Thus, the system’s positioning performance (< 0.18 m) is
an interesting achievement compared to manually collected
data, especially considering the precision required to localize

Spatial canopy resolution is typically utilized at levels of
0.5–1 m span (e.g., lower, middle, upper zones for the z-axis;
near or far from the trunk for the x and y coordinates).
Therefore, a good level of accuracy was achieved in the field
testing, making automated data collection with positioning
information feasible.
In conclusion, this paper investigated the performance of a
3D positioning algorithm to facilitate the automation of field
data collection for digital orchard representation. The algo-
rithm successfully created digital models of the investigated
trees by accurately populating them with target objects and
their associated information. Positioning performances showed
error of < 0.18 m per axis, in the worst case. This error
level showed to be in-line with uncertainty present in manually
collected ground truth data.
Despite these promising results, further improvements might
be needed to reduce the effects of perspective distortion and
to incorporate the estimation of object orientation (i.e., yaw,
pitch, and roll) to achieve full 6D pose estimation. Additional
enhancements could include positioning objects on the tree
when the trunk origin is not clearly visible in the images.
