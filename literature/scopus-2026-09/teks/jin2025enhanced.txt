A B S T R A C T
The growing demand for automation in the apple-harvesting industry remains challenging due to the complex 
and dynamic nature of orchard environments. This study presents an enhanced deep learning model designed to 
improve the accuracy and adaptability of recognition algorithms for robotic arm-based harvesting. Specifically, 
an optimized You Only Look Once (YOLO) v8n model was developed by integrating a dilation-wise resi­
dual–dilated re-parameterization block module, a generalized feature pyramid network, and the Scylla 
Intersection-over-Union loss function. The enhanced model was trained and evaluated on a comprehensive 
dataset, achieving precision, recall, F1 score, and mAP50 values of 81.43 %, 68.48 %, 74.40 %, and 81.68 %, 
respectively. These results indicate improvements of 1.06 %, 1.42 %, 1.28 %, and 1.61 % over the original 
YOLOv8n, while preserving comparable model parameters, computational efficiency, and detection speed. 
Furthermore, the enhanced model demonstrated superior overall performance compared to YOLOv5, YOLOv6, 
and RT-DETR. To validate its adaptability and robustness, the enhanced model was rigorously tested against the 
original YOLOv8n model diverse conditions, including varying growth stage, lighting environments, field of 
view, and levels of occlusion. In outdoor field experiments conducted under cloudy, low-light, and artificial 
lighting conditions, the model achieved localization errors of 2.43 mm (X-axis), 3.70 mm (Y-axis), and 1.28 mm 
(Z-axis), representing reductions of 19.27 %, 12.67 %, and 23.05 %, respectively. Furthermore, counting ac­
curacy improved to 69.39 %, reflecting a 2.42 % increase over the original model. The results demonstrate the 
enhanced model’s reliable performance and heightened precision for robotic arm-based apple harvesting in 
complex and challenging orchard environments. The study also provides a comprehensive analysis of the model’s 
strengths, limitations, and avenues for future research. Ultimately, this work contributes to advancing agricul­
tural automation, paving the way for smarter, more efficient, and sustainable farming practices.
1. Introduction
As a key player in the global agricultural economy, apple production 
significantly enhances sustainability and efficiency. Apples are culti­
vated across diverse climates, ranging from temperate regions in North 
America and Europe to the varied conditions in Asia and the Southern 
Hemisphere [1]. This widespread cultivation contributes substantially 
to agricultural GDP, export revenues, and rural employment [2]. How­
ever, the labor-intensive nature of apple harvesting, coupled with a 
declining agricultural workforce, has hindered efficient market delivery, 
highlighting the urgent need for technological solutions to reduce 
reliance on manual labor. Advances in precision agriculture have been 
instrumental in addressing these labor challenges, ensuring the indus­
try’s long-term viability and reinforcing its crucial role in global agri­
culture [3,4].
Apple harvesting, a critical phase in agriculture with significant 
economic impact, has evolved with technological advancements, pro­
gressing from manual picking to mechanical harvesting, semi- 
automated picking, and fully automated picking [5,6]. Manual pick­
ing, ideal for small-scale orchards and scenarios requiring high-quality 
fruit, is inefficient and labor-intensive [7,8]. Mechanical harvesting, 
favored by large-scale orchards, employs a shaking method whereby

Semi-automated assisted picking involves using platforms to assist 
multiple workers with manual picking and also incorporates conveyors 
to collect the picked fruit. This approach enhances the efficiency of 
manual picking and reduces physical strain but is limited in efficiency 
and remains relatively labor-intensive [9]. Fully automated harvesting 
systems, which integrate machine vision and end-effectors to identify, 
locate, and harvest apples, encompass both drone-based and 
ground-based robotic arms. These technologies pivotal in reducing labor 
costs and increasing productivity. Drone-based systems introduce 
innovative capabilities but are constrained by challenges such as limited 
battery life, low payload capacity, high maintenance demands, and 
susceptibility to environmental factors like wind and rain, which can 
affect their stability and overall performance [10,11]. On the other 
hand, ground-based robotic arms offer notable advantages, including 
greater load capacity, superior operational stability, consistent work 
efficiency, and improved safety. These strengths have been extensively 
validated in various studies [12–14]. Nevertheless, ground-based sys­
tems fac obstacles such as technical complexity and limited adaptability 
to diverse operational conditions, which hinder their widespread 
adoption. As such, fully automated harvesting technologies remain a 
focal point of ongoing research and development, with efforts aimed at 
overcoming existing limitations and enhancing their practicality for 
real-world applications.
Deep learning–based techniques employ advanced neural network 
architectures to precisely analyze fruit characteristics in complex or­
chard environments, significantly improving automatic detection ca­
pabilities for robotic vision–based harvesting. These methods offer the 
stability needed to adapt to diverse environmental variations, making 
them highly suitable for dynamic agricultural applications [15–17]. 
Object detection algorithms are generally divided into one-stage and 
two-stage approaches. One-stage methods, such as the You Only Look 
Once (YOLO) series [18–24], are celebrated for their efficient, 
end-to-end boundary box localization and classification, enabling 
real-time detection. In contrast, two-stage methods, such as R-CNN [25], 
Fast R-CNN [26], Faster R-CNN [27,28], Mask R-CNN [29,30], R-FCN 
[31], TridentNet [32], SPP-Net [33], involved an additional region 
proposal step prior to classification, which often results in slower 
detection speeds [34]. The YOLO series has emerged as a leading solu­
tion for real-time apple detection due to its integration of multi-scale 
features, attention mechanisms, and lightweight architectures [35]. 
Numerous studies have demonstrated its enhanced accuracy, efficiency, 
and adaptability for apple harvesting tasks. For instance, Kuznetsova 
et al. [19] implemented preprocessing and postprocessing techniques to 
optimize the YOLOv3 algorithm for picking robot vision systems, 
achieving an average apple detection time of 19 ms. Similarly, Chen 
et al. [20] proposed the Des-YOLOv4 algorithm for real-time apple 
detection in natural environments. By integrating DenseNet, they 
reduced network parameters and improved feature reuse, attaining a 
remarkable mAP of 97.13 % with a detection speed of 51 frames per 
second. Building on theses advancements, Chunbo et al. [21] proposed 
an improved YOLOX-s network to further enhance apple detection ac­
curacy in natural scenes. Their approach incorporated a self-attention 
module, an additional detection head, and enhanced convolutional 
branches, leading to a superior feature fusion and precision. Yang et al. 
[22] improved YOLOv7 for dense apple detection by incorporating 
preprocessing techniques, MobileOne modules, parallel channels, and 
an auxiliary detection head. Their model achieved notable increases of 
6.9 % in accuracy, 10 % in recall, and 5 % in mAP, outperforming other 
YOLO models by up to 14 %. Liu et al. [23] further advanced YOLOv5 s 
for Fuji apple maturity and diameter recognition. By integrating 
VoVGSCSP, GSConv, and GAM modules for enhanced feature fusion and 
leveraging color-depth data for contour-based diameter estimation, the 
models achieved an impressive 98.7 % accuracy, including 97.4 % for 
low-maturity apples. This performance significantly surpassed Faster 
R-CNN, Mask R-CNN, YOLOv7, and the original YOLOv5s. Similarly, Ma 
et al. [24] optimized YOLOv8n with ShuffleNetv2, Ghost modules, 
Squeeze-and-Excitation blocks, and Wise-IoU to improve efficiency in 
apple monitoring. Their enhancements resulted in 94.1 % precision and 
91.4 % mAP, achieving superior precision and speed compared to Faster 
R-CNN, Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD), YOLOv5 s, YOLOv7-tiny.
Advances in visual processing have significantly accelerated the 
development of agricultural robotics, with object localization and 
counting being crucial for effective vision-based robotic grasping [36]. 
Recently, various 3D object localization techniques have been devel­
oped to enhance target positioning and counting accuracy for robotic 
apple picking. These techniques include the use of stereo vision cameras, 
structured light cameras, time-of-flight cameras, lidar cameras, and 
infrared depth cameras [15]. Andriyanov et al. [37] used the YOLOv3 
architecture with a RealSense D415 depth camera to estimate apple 
positions intelligently; however, the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 
7–12 mm in the coordinate direction poses challenges for successful 
grasping. Zhang et al. [29] developed an apple positioning system using 
Mask R-CNN and a Bumblebee XB3 camera, achieving a coefficient of 
variation (CoV) of 5.28 mm and a positioning accuracy of 99.49 %. 
However, the system’s main limitation is its slow processing, requiring 
0.91 s per image. Abeyrathna et al. [38] integrated a RealSense D455 
camera with deep learning into a robotic system to position and count 
apples efficiently in a simulated orchard. Using the EfficientDet algo­
rithm, they achieved an RMSE of 1.54 cm along the Z-axis and a 
maximum counting accuracy of 86.6 % with YOLOv5 and YOLOv7. 
However, this method only accounts for the Z-axis position, neglecting 
the X-axis and Y-axis, and does not consider the impact of varying 
weather and lighting conditions either. In addition, integrating YOLOv8 
with depth cameras has facilitated detection and localization for other 
crops. Paul et al. [39] demonstrated this by combining YOLOv8 with the 
RealSense D455, achieving a mAP of 0.967 for capsicum detection, 
0.614 for growth stages, and 0.790/0.771 for peduncle box and mask 
detection, respectively. Localization errors were minimal, with de­
viations of only 8 mm longitudinally, 9 mm vertically, and 12 mm 
laterally. Furthermore, the tracking algorithm achieved an accuracy of 
94.1 %, underscoring its potential to enhance robotic harvesting 
capabilities.
Robotic arm-based apple harvesting involves significant challenges 
due to the complex and dynamic environments in orchards [14]. Issues 
such as narrow fields of view (FOVs), frequent occlusion by leaves and 
branches, fluctuating lighting, and the diverse colors and sizes of apples 
complicate detection and localization [40,41]. These factors result in 
poor detection accuracy, low counting precision, and considerable 
positioning errors, leading to misalignments that can damage both the 
fruit and the trees [38]. Although advanced vision technologies have 
improved the ability of robotic arms to recognize, localize, and operate 
under varying conditions, these challenges remain unresolved, necessi­
tating further optimization of existing solutions. Therefore, enhancing 
the robustness and adaptability of detection algorithms, along with 
improving the precision of robotic positioning, is essential for devel­
oping reliable, efficient, and commercially viable robotic arm-based 
apple-harvesting systems [10,42].
The YOLO model consistently outperforms numerous other detection 
algorithms, especially when operating within identical computational 
resources and time constraints. Its exceptional balance of real-time 
processing efficiency and accuracy makes it a preferred choice for 
many applications. Among its iterations, YOLOv8 represents the latest 
and most advanced version available at the time this study’s develop­
ment [43]. The objective of this study is to develop an integrated vision 
system that combines an enhanced version of the original YOLOv8n 
architecture with RGB-D sensing technologies to improve the accuracy 
of the detection, localization, and counting of apples in complex or­
chards for robotic arm-based apple harvesting. In summary, the key 
contributions of this study are as follows: (1) development of a

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Overall framework
The enhanced YOLOv8n leverages advanced neural network archi­
tectures and sophisticated training techniques to significantly enhance 
harvesting accuracy and performance. The overall framework used in 
this study, illustrated in Fig. 1, comprises three main steps:
(1) Construction of dataset for apples in complex orchards: Images of 
apples were sourced online. Blurry images were discarded, and existing 
labels were reviewed, re-annotated, and converted into a unified TXT 
format. The dataset was then divided into training, validation, and test 
sets.
(2) Development and comparative analysis of deep learning models: 
The original YOLOv8n network was enhanced by integrating the 
dilation-wise residual (DWR)–dilated re-param block (DRB), general­
ized feature pyramid network (GFPN), and Scylla intersection-over- 
union (SIoU) modules. Ablation studies were conducted to evaluate 
the contribution of each enhancement to the model’s performance. The 
enhanced model was systematically compared against YOLOv5, 
YOLOv6, Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR)-L, and the orig­
inal YOLOv8n using test images across diverse conditions, verifying 
significant improvements in detection accuracy, robustness, and overall 
performance.
(3) Apple detection, localization, and counting: Real-world experi­
ments were performed in outdoor environments under diverse weather 
and lighting conditions. Utilizing an eye-in-hand system, a robotic arm 
equipped with a depth camera captured images and computed the 3D 
coordinates of the centers of detected apple bounding boxes. Impor­
tantly, all 3D coordinates were represented within the camera coordi­
nate system, independent of the robotic arm’s base frame. Localization 
errors and counting accuracy were analyzed before and after the en­
hancements to the original YOLOv8n model, facilitating the

2.2. Robotic arm-based harvesting system
Considering the workflow requirements, the robotic arm-based 
apple-harvesting system, depicted in Fig. 2, is designed with several 
key components. The system includes an unmanned ground vehicle 
(UGV) platform, a robotic arm with an end-effector, an RGB-D camera, a 
low-level controller, a high-level controller (computer), and an emer­
gency power supply. The UGV platform is an AgileX Robotics-Bunker 
(AgileX Robotics, Shenzhen, China), on which an Elite CS66 six- 
degrees-of-freedom robotic arm is mounted along with its controller, 
the ERB2E2K0–220/110 (Elite Robots, Suzhou, China). The end-effector 
of the robotic arm is a flexible, adaptive, servo-driven four-finger gripper 
(Easy Gripper, Dongguan, China). The vision system utilizes active ste­
reo vision with the RealSense D455 (Intel Corporation, California, USA) 
RGB-D camera. This camera, equipped with two stereo infrared sensors, 
an RGB sensor, and a dot projector [44], is mounted on a bracket 50 cm 
above the gripper and moves in conjunction with the robotic arm. The 
high-level controller (computer), featuring an Intel (R) Core (TM) 
i7–11370H processor running at 3.3 GHz, operates the control system 
via the Ubuntu 20.04 Linux operating system. The detailed specifica­
tions of the main equipment are listed in Table 1.
2.3. Creation of apple dataset
2.3.1. Data collection and processing
To improve sample diversity and ensure dataset integrity, the apple 
dataset, consisting of images from orchards under varied environmental 
conditions, was obtained from publicly available online sources 
[45–47]. The dataset was carefully curated by manually removing 
blurry images, and each labeled image underwent a thorough review 
using the LabelImg software tool [48]. During this process, labels cor­
responding to spoiled apples were discarded, and bounding boxes with 
significant discrepancies were re-annotated. For consistency, all labels 
were standardized under a single category named “apple” and the label 
formats were uniformly converted to TXT files.
2.3.2. Image diversification and dataset segmentation
A total of 8759 apple images, captured across diverse orchard en­
vironments, were utilized, as depicted in Fig. 3(a)–(j). These images 
encompass various stages of apple development: immature (green), 
nearly mature (red with green), and mature (red) apples. The dataset 
was captured under a diverse range of lighting conditions, including 
natural light, overexposure, low light, and artificial light. It also in­
corporates varying levels of occlusion, ranging from no occlusion to 
minor and major occlusions (where more than 50 % of the apple surface 
is obscured). The images span different FOVs, such as close-up, medium, 
and wide shots, and were captured at various times of day (morning, 
noon, and evening), weather conditions (sunny, cloudy, and post-rain), 
and light directions (front, side, back, low, and artificial lighting). For 
this study, sunny and cloudy conditions with front, side, and back 
lighting were assessed for daytime scenarios, while low light and arti­
ficial lighting were evaluated for nighttime conditions. This study in­
corporates a broad dataset variability to rigorously assess the model’s 
robustness across diverse environmental conditions. The Hold-out vali­
dation method was employed, dividing the dataset into distinct subsets 
for training, validation, and testing, ensuring objectivity and general­
izability in model assessment. Specifically, 6244 images (71 %) were 
allocated for training, 1675 images (19 %) for validation, and 840 im­
ages (10 %) for testing. The split ratio, derived from empirical guidelines 
and relevant literature [38,49,50], provides sufficient data for effective 
model training, hyperparameter optimization, and independent evalu­
ation. The dataset, consisting of 8759 images with high sample diversity, 
meets variability requirements for robust model development. Further­
more, the absence of significant class imbalance eliminates the need for 
data augmentation to correct distributional biases. Consequently, this 
study relied on the original dataset for training, maintaining scientific 
rigor while optimizing deep learning performance.

2.4.2. DWR–DRB module
The DWR–DRB algorithm effectively handles the challenge of 
detecting apples in complex backgrounds by merging broader contex­
tual information with multi-scale features. This fusion of global struc­
tural context and fine-grained local details across various resolutions 
enhances both recognition accuracy and robustness, particularly in 
challenging scenarios, making it highly suitable for apple orchards. As

proposed by Zhu et al. [53], the DWR–DRB module combines

(1) DRB
The DRB enhances object detection accuracy and speed, particularly 
in high-resolution images, by adeptly handling complex boundaries and 
multi-scale features. It incorporates dilated convolutions to expand the 
receptive field without increasing the number of parameters, thus being 
able to capture more contextual information. Additionally, the use of re- 
parameterization ensures robust feature extraction during training 
while simplifying the complex network structure to an equivalent con­
volutional kernel during inference. This approach reduces computa­
tional load while preserving feature representation, thereby improving 
both detection accuracy and efficiency [54].
As illustrated in Fig. 5, the structure of the DRB [53] used in this 
study processes the input layer through three distinct paths: the standard

re-parameterized convolution path. These paths are aggregated through 
addition or concatenation, followed by a nonlinear activation and a 
residual connection. The DRB structure consists of multiple parallel 
convolutional layers with varying dilation rates (r = 1, 2, 2, 3), utilizing 
3 × 3 and 5 × 5 dilated convolutions to process the input feature map. 
This allows multi-scale information to be captured, enhancing overall 
feature representation. After the convolutions, a batch normalization 
(BN) layer normalizes each output. The feature maps from the different 
dilated convolutional layers are concatenated along the channel 
dimension [55]. These concatenated features are then fused through a 
re-parameterization module to generate a high-quality feature map, 
which is simplified into a single convolutional operation for efficient 
inference. The output feature map typically retains the input’s spatial 
dimensions but may vary in the number of channels. The DRB module 
combines large-kernel (k = 5, 7) convolutions with multiple dilated 
convolutional layers to capture both local fine details and widely 
distributed sparse features. This enables a comprehensive perception of 
the structural information in the input data [56]. During inference, the 
module is converted into a single nondilated convolutional layer, 
enhancing computational efficiency. 
(2) DWR–DRB module
The DWR–DRB module [53], designed to enhance CNN performance 
by leveraging multi-scale features and improving representation 
learning, is illustrated in Fig. 6. Initially, the input features, with 2c 
channels, undergo a 3 × 3 standard convolution combined with BN and 
ReLU activation. This step produces regional residual features, resulting 
in 3c channels. These features are then subjected to multi-scale fusion 
through several 3 × 3 dilated convolutions and dual parallel DRB 
modules, each with different dilation rates. The outputs of these mod­
ules are concatenated to form 4c channels. Following this, a 1 × 1 
convolution with BN integrates these multi-scale features back into 2c 
channels. These are then combined with the original input via a residual 
connection, which produces the final output feature map.
In the proposed framework, the DWR–DRB module is integrated with 
the last two C2f modules in the original YOLOv8n backbone. This 
combination effectively enhances multi-scale feature extraction and 
integration through standard and dilated convolutions within a residual 
learning framework. The DWR module processes input features to 
expand the receptive field and capture broader contextual information. 
Subsequently, the DRB module fuses and re-parameterizes these features 
at multiple scales, producing high-quality feature maps tailored for 
apple orchard detection. By integrating broader contextual information 
and multi-scale features, the enhanced network achieves higher 
detection accuracy and stability across complex scenarios [57]. This 
combined DWR–DRB design notably augments the YOLOv8n architec­
ture, enhancing its performance while preserving the original network’s 
efficiency and robustness.
2.4.3. GFPN module
Detecting apples in complex orchard environments presents signifi­
cant challenges, particularly due to variations in apple size, perspective, 
and background. While these issues have been partially addressed 
through traditional multi-scale feature fusion methods, such as FPN,

PAN, and bidirectional feature pyramid network (BiFPN) [58], these 
methods have limitations as well. FPN employs a top-down path to 
aggregate high-level semantic information, but it often loses crucial 
details needed for accurately detecting small objects. PAN introduces a 
bottom-up path to improve information flow and address scale varia­
tions, but it still primarily concentrates on feature scaling rather than a 
comprehensive integration of feature hierarchies. BiFPN dynamically 
adjusts feature weights through weighted fusion, which improves flex­
ibility but at the expense of high computational costs and challenges in 
selecting optimal feature weights. Consequently, these methods may 
struggle with significant variations in object size and complexity, which 
often lead to suboptimal performance in dynamic and cluttered envi­
ronments [59].
To address these limitations, Jiang et al. [60] introduced the GFPN, 
which significantly improves the robustness and accuracy of object 
detection through efficient cross-scale and cross-level feature fusion. 
GFPN adeptly manages features from both the same and adjacent levels, 
facilitating the transmission of high-level semantic information while 
preserving critical low-level spatial details. This holistic fusion approach 
enriches feature representation by balancing spatial scale consideration 
with effective information exchange between different feature levels. 
The GFPN module completely replaces the neck of the original 
YOLOv8n. It incorporates lightweight convolutional layers, the 
CSPstage submodule (spanning multiple stages), a combination of 3 × 3 
and 1 × 1 convolutions, and the re-parameterization convolution 
(RepConv) submodule. Through the integration of GFPN into the orig­
inal YOLOv8n, the network gains additional feature fusion paths, which

2.4.4. Shape IoU loss function for bounding box regression
The loss function plays a crucial role in the training of deep learning 
models as it quantifies the discrepancy between the model’s predicted 
outputs and the actual targets. In the domain of object detection, the 
precision of bounding box regression is particularly critical, with the 
model’s overall performance being heavily dependent on the design of 
the loss function. Typically, the loss function in object detection com­

complete intersection-over-union (CIoU) loss function, which assesses 
the alignment between the predicted and ground-truth boxes by

Despite its advantages, however, CIoU has several drawbacks,

slow convergence, and a complex optimization objective. These limita­

alternative loss functions such as distance IoU (DIoU), generalized IoU

training requirements, as well as the potential instability due to 
improper weight selection.

shapes; thereby, it significantly reduces localization errors caused by 
overlaps with elements such as leaves and branches, which enhances 
accuracy. Although SIoU is more dependent on extensive training data 
and may be less effective than some simplified loss functions in certain 
extreme cases, it excels in orchard environments. By addressing the 
vector angles between the predicted and ground-truth boxes through 
vertical or horizontal alignment, SIoU ensures effective model perfor­
mance in terms of accurately detecting and aligning bounding boxes.

several key performance metrics, namely P, R, F1 score, and mAP, which

P is the ratio of true positives (TP) to the sum of TP and false positives 
(TP + FP). It measures the model’s accuracy in distinguishing apples 
from other objects, such as leaves and branches. A high precision in­
dicates that the model can identify apples accurately under varying light 
conditions and occlusion by foliage.
R is the ratio of TP to the sum of TP and false negatives (TP + FN). It 
gauges the model’s effectiveness in detecting all actual apples, with a 
higher value reflecting strong detection capabilities even in challenging 
environments with dense foliage and occlusion.
F1 score is the harmonic mean of P and R, balancing these two 
metrics. It is especially useful for imbalanced datasets, where a high 
value indicates strong performance in detecting apples while mini­
mizing false detections.
mAP evaluates the model’s performance across multiple classes by 
assessing P and R at various IoU thresholds. In Eq. (24), C denotes the 
number of classes, T represents the number of IoU thresholds, k is the 
specific IoU threshold, P(k) indicates the value of P at the k-th threshold, 
and ΔR(k) reflects the change in R at the k-th threshold. mAP serves as a 
comprehensive global metric for multi-class detection, with mAP50 (IoU 
threshold of 0.5) measuring the model’s ability to predict objects with at 
least a 50 % overlap with the ground truth [38].
The accuracy of the proposed localization method was evaluated 
through target detection experiments, which measured the average 
error in normalized coordinates between the predicted and actual cen­
ters of the apple bounding box. Apple images were captured using the 
Intel RealSense D455 camera, with the actual center points manually 
annotated, as shown in Fig. 8. The predicted and true center points are 
denoted as (X, Y, Z) and (X0, Y0, Z0), respectively. This study focuses 
on developing and validating a new detection algorithm. While RGB-D 
cameras inherently produce noise and manufacturer-specified depth 
errors that could impact localization accuracy, these factors were not 
considered, as they fall outside the scope of this study. However, these 
potential errors were equally present during the validation of both the 
original and enhanced algorithms, ensuring a fair and consistent 
comparison.
To ensure rigorous performance assessment in real-world scenarios, 
the localization error was evaluated across all three spatial directions. 
This comprehensive approach ensures reliable apple localization, which 
is crucial for effective detection and harvesting in an orchard setting.

rections. This approach provides a detailed assessment of the system’s 
accuracy along each axis, ensuring that the localization process is robust 
and reliable for subsequent harvesting operations.

3.2. Ablation experiments
To validate the enhancements made to the YOLOv8n model, three 
major modifications were implemented: (D) integrating DWR–DRB 
modules into the last two C2f layers of the backbone, (G) employing 
GFPN as the neck network, and (S) utilizing SIoU as the loss function. 
Ablation experiments were conducted to evaluate the individual and 
combined effects of these modifications. Table 3 shows the performance 
changes relative to the original YOLOv8n model, based on validation set 
results during the training phase. These results provide critical insights 
for analyzing and optimizing the model’s architecture. Positive impacts 
are marked with red upward arrows, while negative effects are indicated 
with black downward arrows.
The results revealed that incorporating DWR–DRB modules led to 
significant improvements across all performance metrics, with P, R, F1 
score, and mAP50 increasing by 1.20 %, 0.56 %, 0.91 %, and 1.01 %, 
respectively. These gains are attributed to the expanded receptive field 
and more contextual information being captured, which together boost 
feature extraction. Additionally, replacing the traditional PAN–FPN 
with GFPN in the neck network resulted in notable performance en­
hancements: R, mAP50, P, and F1 score increased by 0.66 %, 0.79 %, 
0.27 %, and 0.44 %, respectively. The improved multi-scale feature 
fusion offered by GFPN contributes to a greater detection accuracy and 
robustness. Separately, utilizing SIoU as the loss function led to a 1.38 % 
increase in P and a slight decrease in R by 0.77 %; however, it improved 
F1 score by 0.41 % and mAP50 by 0.24 %. The strength of SIoU lies in its 
precise object localization capabilities, which enhance boundary 
handling, despite the minor reduction in R.
Combining the DWR–DRB modules with SIoU resulted in notable 
improvements, with P, R, F1 score, and mAP50 increasing by 1.22 %, 
0.84 %, 0.60 %, and 0.65 %, respectively. Further, integrating GFPN 
with SIoU also yielded significant gains, increasing P, R, F1 score, and 
mAP50 by 0.49 %, 0.37 %, 0.44 %, and 0.69 %, respectively. Addi­
tionally, combining the DWR–DRB modules with GFPN notably 
increased R and mAP50, by 0.54 % and 0.99 %, respectively. Ultimately, 
the model incorporating all these modifications achieved the highest 
performance improvements, with P, R, F1 score, and mAP50 increasing 
by 1.50 %, 0.81 %, 1.19 %, and 1.60 %, respectively. These results 
underscore the synergistic effect of these enhancements, demonstrating 
their substantial impact on model performance. This validation not only 
confirms the effectiveness of the proposed improvements but also offers 
valuable insights for future model optimization.

4. Discussion
With the growing demand for apple harvesting from orchards, 
traditional manual picking methods are becoming increasingly favor­
able due to challenges such as labor shortages, inefficiency, and the 
physically demanding nature of the work. Ground-based harvesting 
using robotic arms, one of the most advanced and thoroughly researched 
solutions, addresses these challenges by increasing load-handling ca­
pacity, ensuring stable operation across diverse terrains, and maintain­
ing high productivity, particularly in small-scale orchards. Developing 
efficient automated picking models not only alleviates labor demands 
but also provides a scientific foundation for streamlining robotic orchard 
management.
This study presents an enhanced YOLOv8n model, which integrates a 
DWR–DRB module, a GFPN module, and the SIoU loss function to 
optimize feature extraction, feature fusion, and localization accuracy. 
Ablation experiments confirmed the significant positive effects of these 
architectural improvements, illustrating their synergistic impact. 
Despite the complexities and challenges of agricultural environments, 
these enhancements significantly strengthened the model’s ability to 
capture and process multi-scale features, thereby improving its adapt­
ability across a wide range of detection scenarios. The enhanced 
YOLOv8n consistently outperformed state-of-the-art models, including 
YOLOv5, YOLOv6, the original YOLOv8n, and RT-DETR-L, especially in 
terms of robustness against complex backgrounds. Although the model 
showed slight underperformance on specific metrics in the test set such 
as an F1 score of 74.40 and an mAP50 of 81.68 compared to some 
existing models [15,66]. This discrepancy can largely be attributed to 
the use of a more comprehensive and complex dataset. Despite these 
minor shortcomings, the enhanced model demonstrated notable 
robustness and adaptability when deployed in a robotic harvesting 
system for simulated orchard detection. It effectively performed apple 
detection, localization, and counting, underscoring its potential for 
real-world outdoor applications. This study provides valuable insights 
into model improvement, with future efforts aimed at refining the 
dataset and optimizing the model to achieve superior performance 
across all metrics, ensuring its suitability and reliability for agricultural 
applications.
To capitalize on the generally smaller depth errors of cameras 
compared with manual measurements, an innovative method was 
implemented to calculate the absolute error between manually anno­
tated apple centers and predicted bounding box centers in the images, 
which is considerably simpler than the often cumbersome and error- 
prone manual measurement process. Despite these advancements, 
challenges related to manual annotation errors and performance in 
complex environments still impact the model’s accuracy and stability.

To assess the localization error, we utilized the average value from 300

The enhanced YOLOv8n model demonstrated remarkable adapt­
ability across various apple growth stages, FOV conditions, lighting 
environments, and occlusion scenarios. It achieved a high detection 
accuracy of 97.3 % for mature apples, surpassing the results reported by 
Wang et al. [68] and Ma et al. [24]. This level of accuracy is essential for 
making informed harvest decisions. However, the detection perfor­
mance for immature apples was less satisfactory, highlighting areas for 
potential improvements in feature extraction, data augmentation, and 
handling of complex environments. While the model excelled under 
natural and low-light conditions, its performance declined slightly under 
overexposed conditions and artificial lighting, which highlights the need 
for better processing of extreme lighting conditions. Additionally, 
despite improvements in severe occlusion scenarios, the persistence of 
false negatives under partial occlusion highlights the necessity for 
advanced occlusion-handling techniques or the use of synthetic data 
augmentation. Outdoor experiments in dynamic environments revealed 
that the enhanced YOLOv8n model outperformed the original model 
across various complex lighting conditions, including cloudy weather, 
front lighting, side lighting, back lighting, low light, and artificial 
lighting. The localization and counting errors were the smallest under 
cloudy weather and front lighting, with significant enhancements in 
low-light and artificial light conditions. However, factors such as 
shadows, reflections, and uneven lighting led to errors in boundary 
recognition and localization. To ensure maximum efficiency, it is rec­
ommended to perform apple harvesting under evenly lit conditions or to 
improve the lighting uniformity. The model’s average localization errors 
were 2.43 mm in the X direction, 3.70 mm in the Y direction, and 1.28 
mm in the Z direction, all of which meet the localization requirements 
for robotic arms and surpass the calculation errors reported in recent 
studies. Table 7 compares the performance of the proposed model with 
that of several recently developed apple localization models, high­
lighting its superior accuracy and reliability.
Despite the significant advancements achieved with the enhanced 
YOLOv8n model, certain limitations remain. The counting method 
employed in this study, while effective for guiding harvesting strategies 
and facilitating localization, did not achieve the precision of more 
advanced approaches [70,71]. This method was chosen for its simplicity 
and ease of integration into real-world harvesting scenarios, with future 
efforts focused on improving its accuracy and adaptability for more 
complex and dynamic environments. Additionally, the outdoor experi­
ments conducted with simulated fruit trees and artificial apples do not 
fully replicate the complexities of real orchards, which present more 
intricate backgrounds, uneven lighting conditions, dynamic occlusions,

model in real orchard environments, incorporating data fusion from 
multiple depth cameras to enhance localization accuracy. Additionally, 
efforts should be concentrated on improving the model’s ability to 
detect and differentiate tree branches, a critical feature for collision 
avoidance in robotic harvesting, as well as refining its capability to 
accurately assess apple sizes, which plays a key role in yield estimation 
and selective harvesting [72,73]. While this study focused on distant 
apple images and green apples, extending the model’s applicability to 
include the classification of apples at various stages of maturity and the 
identification of defective apples will be crucial. Furthermore, a 
comprehensive assessment of the model’s size, computational demands, 
and detection speed is essential to ensure its accuracy and efficiency are 
suitable for large-scale agricultural applications.
The findings of this study have significant implications for precision 
agriculture. The model’s strong performance under complex conditions 
underscores its vast potential for applications in orchards and broader 
agricultural scenarios. The results also emphasize the importance of 
continuous innovation and technological optimization to tackle chal­
lenges posed by extreme environments and difficult-to-detect targets. 
Future research should continue the exploration of these technologies, 
considering the integration of the enhanced YOLOv8n with other 
detection methods to develop more robust hybrid models, which could 
improve the effectiveness of robotics in apple harvesting and thereby 
drive progress in precision agriculture.
The lack of pose estimation represents a significant limitation, as 
spatial and directional information is critical for precise apple grasping, 
trajectory planning, and collision avoidance in robotic-arm-based har­
vesting systems. Without this capability, handling varying apple orien­
tations, occlusions, and complex orchard canopies becomes challenging, 
which reduces both accuracy and operational efficiency. Future work 
will focus on integrating advanced pose estimation techniques, such as 
RGB-D cameras, LiDAR, or multi-view stereo vision, to capture detailed 
3D information. By combining pose estimation with detection, locali­
zation, and dynamic path planning in a unified framework, the system’s 
adaptability will be significantly enhanced, enabling more efficient and 
reliable autonomous harvesting in complex orchard environments.
5. Conclusions
This study introduced an enhanced YOLOv8n model, specifically 
optimized for apple detection, localization, and counting within com­
plex orchard environments, with the goal of advancing robotic har­
vesting automation. Compared to the original YOLOv8n model, the 
enhanced version achieved substantial improvements: P increased by 6 
% to 81.43 %, R by 1.42 % to 68.48 %, F1 score by 1.28 % to 74.40 %, 
and mAP50 by 1.61 % to 81.68 %, all while maintaining consistent 
model parameters, computational demands, and detection speed. The 
model underwent rigorous testing in both static and dynamic orchard 
environments, delivering significant performance gains, particularly in 
handling complex backgrounds and extreme lighting conditions. 
Localization errors were markedly reduced, with average errors of only 
2.43 mm, 3.70 mm, and 1.28 mm along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes, respec­
tively, resulting in reductions of 19.27 %, 12.67 %, and 23.05 % 
compared to the original YOLOv8n model. Furthermore, the model’ 
counting accuracy improved to 69.39 %, reflecting a 2.42 % increase.
In summary, the enhanced YOLOv8n model exhibited outstanding 
performance in challenging orchard environments, substantially 
reducing operational errors while improving the efficiency and reli­
ability of automated harvesting systems. Future research will prioritize 
evaluating the model’s computational efficiency and detection speed, 
further optimizing it for real-time harvesting. Moreover, efforts will be 
made to enhance robotic arm path planning and gripper technology to 
ensure scalability for large-scale agricultural production. With ongoing 
advancements, the enhanced YOLOv8n model holds the potential to 
revolutionize precision agriculture and orchard automation, driving 
greater efficiency and fostering smarter, more sustainable farming 
practices.
