Annotation Cost for Natural Orchard Scenes
Xiaofeng Xie

Abstract
Accurate multi-level grape counting in natural orchard images provides useful visual infor-
mation for image-based crop-load characterization and vineyard management. However,
cluster overlap, occlusion, boundary adhesion, and illumination variation complicate berry
localization and cluster-wise counting. Existing detection- and density-regression-based
methods remain susceptible to missed detections, false positives, and cross-cluster mis-
assignment, whereas approaches relying on pixel- or instance-level supervision typically
require costly annotations. To address these challenges, this paper proposes UniGC, a
unified framework for multi-level grape counting. At the berry level, UniGC employs CSD-
P2PNet, a P2PNet-based point-regression branch equipped with a Condition-Structure
Decoupled Frequency Enhancement Block (CSD-Block), to improve the localization and
counting of image-visible berries under complex illumination and occlusion using only
berry point annotations. At the cluster level, a You Only Look Once (YOLO) detector pre-
dicts grape-cluster bounding boxes, which are used as prompts for the Segment Anything
Model (SAM) to generate cluster instance masks. We further propose Boundary-Depth
Guided Mask-Point Assignment (BDMPA), which assigns each predicted berry point to at
most one grape-cluster instance and thereby produces cluster-wise berry counts. Selected
key experiments on the mixed dataset organized from WGISD and GBISC were repeated
using three random seeds. CSD-P2PNet achieved an MAE of 23.4407 ± 0.1577, an RMSE of
32.8898 ± 1.2682, and a point-localization F1 score of 0.8374 ± 0.0097. YOLO12-S achieved
an F1 score of 0.8433 ± 0.0056 and an AP50 of 0.8789 ± 0.0080 for grape-cluster detection.
For BpGC counting, UniGC obtained a matched-cluster MRD of 0.1288 ± 0.0043, a 1 −FVU
of 0.8589 ± 0.0098, and an all-cluster penalized mean relative deviation (ACP-MRD) of
0.5062 ± 0.0124. These results demonstrate that UniGC achieves competitive multi-level
grape counting performance while requiring only point and bounding-box annotations
for training.
Keywords: orchard intelligent counting; grape cluster detection; point regression; SAM
segmentation; UniGC framework; condition-structure decoupling; mask-point assignment
1. Introduction
In natural orchard images, grape cluster-level detection and counting, image-visible
berry-level counting, and berries-per-grape-cluster (BpGC) counting provide complemen-
tary visual information for image-based crop-load characterization, cultivation manage-
ment, and precision operations. Previous studies have applied computer vision techniques

to grape yield estimation, harvesting localization, cluster-level recognition, and berry phe-
notypic analysis, providing a feasible way to reduce reliance on manual counting and
improve field management efficiency [1–8]. Compared with whole-image or cluster-level
counting alone, multi-level counting provides more structured information, including the
spatial distribution of grape clusters, the number of image-visible berries, and the visible
berry load within individual clusters. Such information is therefore more consistent with
practical needs in fruit thinning, crop-load regulation, and precision management [9–12].
Automatically obtaining these multi-level visual measurements using computer vision
has therefore become an important research direction in intelligent grape perception and
precision management.
Existing studies on grape detection and counting can be broadly divided into three cat-
egories according to task level and technical approach. The first category focuses on grape
cluster detection or segmentation, usually using object detection, semantic segmentation,
or instance segmentation models to obtain cluster-level geometric information for tasks
such as cluster counting, harvesting localization, or yield estimation [13–24]. The second
category focuses on berry-level detection, segmentation, or counting, including instance
segmentation and small-object detection methods, as well as point-supervised, density-
map-based, or semi-supervised counting methods, to support quantitative assessment in
densely distributed berry scenarios [7,11,12,25–29]. The third category further attempts to
model grape cluster detection, grape berry counting, and BpGC counting as interrelated
subtasks [9–12,30]. Overall, existing studies have laid an important foundation for multi-
level visual analysis of grapes, but most methods still focus on single-level tasks or do not
sufficiently address consistent associations among results at different levels. Therefore, es-
tablishing a reliable and efficient unified framework for multi-level grape counting requires
further investigation.
Specifically, in natural orchard images, grape-cluster overlap, severe occlusion, bound-
ary adhesion, and complex illumination variations jointly affect cluster-level detection,
image-visible berry-level counting, and BpGC counting [31]. First, complex illumination
variations can alter local berry texture, edge responses, and contrast distributions, making
it difficult for detection- or density-regression-based methods to distinguish berry-related
structures from background disturbances and thereby increasing missed and false berry
detections. Second, severe occlusion, inter-cluster overlap, and boundary adhesion can
weaken the separability of grape-cluster regions, making it difficult for cluster-level detec-
tion or segmentation results to provide a reliable geometric basis for subsequent counting
and assignment. Furthermore, when berry-point localization results are assigned to spe-
cific grape clusters, localization errors and regional boundary uncertainties can propagate
through the task pipeline, increasing the risk of cross-cluster misassignment. If pixel-level
segmentation or more fine-grained instance-level supervision is used to alleviate these
problems, the annotation burden can increase substantially, limiting the scalable application
of such methods in real agricultural scenarios.
To address the above problems, this paper proposes UniGC, a unified framework for
multi-level grape counting. UniGC follows a unified pipeline that combines berry-level
point-regression counting, cluster-level detection and mask generation, and berry point-
to-cluster assignment-based counting. It jointly performs image-visible berry-level count-
ing, grape cluster-level detection and counting, and BpGC counting, while the trainable
branches require only berry point annotations and grape-cluster bounding-box annota-
tions. By using predicted grape-cluster bounding boxes as prompts for mask generation,
UniGC maintains a unified multi-level inference process without requiring grape-cluster
instance-mask annotations for task-specific training.

In the berry-level branch, P2PNet is used as the point-regression counting basis [32],
and the Condition-Structure Decoupled Frequency Enhancement Block (CSD-Block) is
introduced. This module models intermediate features from a frequency-inspired per-
spective. Through learnable low-pass filtering and residual decomposition, the module
promotes the spatial-domain disentanglement of slowly varying condition-related compo-
nents from residual structure-sensitive components. It then performs condition-decoupled
enhancement and controlled recoupling on the residual structural features, thereby improv-
ing the robustness of image-visible berry-point localization under complex illumination
and occlusion.
In the cluster-level branch, You Only Look Once (YOLO) is used to predict grape-
cluster bounding boxes, which serve as prompts for the Segment Anything Model (SAM)
to generate the corresponding cluster-level instance masks and provide geometric priors
for subsequent assignment inference [33]. On this basis, the Boundary-Depth Guided
Mask-Point Assignment (BDMPA) is further proposed to establish a unique point-to-cluster
assignment for each predicted berry point. In addition, at the data level, the Wine Grape
Instance Segmentation Dataset (WGISD) and the Sichuan Grape Berry Instance Segmen-
tation and Counting Dataset (GBISC) [13,27] are uniformly organized and integrated to
construct a data organization scheme suitable for multi-level grape detection and counting
tasks. Experimental results show that, under the evaluated mixed-dataset setting, UniGC
achieves competitive performance in image-visible berry counting, grape-cluster detection,
and BpGC counting, while its trainable branches use only berry point annotations and
grape-cluster bounding-box annotations.

2. Related Work
2.1. Grape Cluster-Level Detection and Segmentation
Grape cluster-level detection and segmentation aim to obtain the locations, counts,
and spatial regions of grape clusters, providing key visual information for yield esti-
mation, harvesting localization, and field management. Santos et al. [13] proposed the
WGISD dataset and used deep neural networks for grape cluster detection, segmentation,
and tracking, providing an important data resource for grape cluster-level visual research.
Subsequently, the YOLO series of detectors has been widely used for grape cluster recogni-
tion in natural scenes [17–24]. Zhang et al. [14] achieved real-time grape cluster detection
in complex natural scenes using YOLOv5s, whereas Zhang et al. [2] proposed YOLOv5-
GAP for grape cluster recognition and picking-point localization under rachis occlusion.

In addition to detection methods, Huang et al. [16] achieved grape cluster detection and
instance segmentation in orchard environments based on an improved Mask R-CNN.
The above studies provide grape-cluster candidate regions and cluster-level spatial
priors, but their outputs are mainly limited to the cluster level, making it difficult to directly
perform BpGC counting or describe berry assignment relationships among adjacent clusters.
For BpGC counting, cluster-level detection boxes or instance masks need to be further
associated with berry-level localization results. Especially under grape cluster overlap,
boundary adhesion, and local occlusion, relying solely on cluster-level region information
makes it difficult to obtain stable intra-cluster counting results.
2.2. Grape Berry-Level Detection, Segmentation, and Counting
Grape berries are characterized by small size, large numbers, dense distributions,
and frequent mutual occlusion. Therefore, berry-level studies usually adopt approaches
such as instance segmentation, semantic segmentation, density-map regression, point-
supervised counting, and semi-supervised learning [4–6,11,12,29]. Coviello et al. [34]
transferred the idea of density estimation from crowd counting to field grape berry counting
and proposed GBCNet for yield estimation. Zabawa et al. [35] used semantic segmentation
and connected-component analysis to achieve grape berry detection and counting. Du
and Liu [25] proposed AS-SwinT, which performs instance segmentation and counting
of berries in individual grape clusters before thinning through Swin Transformer, feature
fusion, and Soft-NMS. Li et al. [26] proposed CDMENet, introducing a density mutual-
exclusion auxiliary task to alleviate dense occlusion and annotation-cost problems under
semi-supervised conditions. Yang et al. [27] proposed Mask-GK, which uses mask Gaussian
kernels to generate probability maps and combines them with watershed post-processing to
achieve field grape berry segmentation and counting, while also releasing the GBISC dataset.
Parr et al. [1] used smartphone RGB-D data for grape yield estimation, demonstrating
the application potential of low-cost acquisition devices in berry analysis. Hao et al. [36]
proposed WGMG-Net for postharvest grape quality assessment, preserving edge details
under dense occlusion through discrete wavelet transform and simultaneously performing
berry instance segmentation, counting, and compactness grading.
These methods improve berry-level visual analysis from different perspectives,
but most studies mainly output berry counts for whole images, local regions, or single-
cluster images. For multi-level grape counting, the berry-level branch must provide not
only counts but also stable coordinates for intra-cluster assignment. Point-based counting
frameworks such as P2PNet show that directly predicting object locations and counts
based on point supervision can avoid intermediate errors introduced by density maps or
pseudo-box construction, providing a useful reference for low-cost berry localization [32].
However, complex illumination, shadows, highlights, and occlusions in natural orchards
can interfere with berry edges and local peak responses; therefore, point-based localization
still requires stronger fine-grained structural representation.
2.3. BpGC Counting and Multi-Level Grape Counting
BpGC counting requires the simultaneous acquisition of grape-cluster regions, berry
locations, and their correspondence, making it more complex than grape cluster detection
or berry-level counting alone [4,11,12,30]. Yang et al. [9] proposed PMGDC, defining grape
cluster detection, grape berry counting, and BpGC counting as three related subtasks. It
predicts grape-cluster and berry probability maps using a neural network and applies
multi-stage post-processing for joint inference. Du et al. [10] proposed MVDNet, which
adopts a dual-branch network to perform berry density-map regression and grape cluster
segmentation simultaneously and combines post-processing algorithms for intra-cluster

berry counting. Related studies indicate that multi-level grape counting requires the
integration of cluster-level and berry-level information and the establishment of stable
associations between them.
Existing multi-level methods still face two key problems. First, probability-map-based
and density-map-based methods usually require post-processing to obtain object locations,
cluster-level regions, or intra-cluster counts, and they are susceptible to response drift
and regional boundary errors under occlusion, overlap, and boundary adhesion. Second,
intra-cluster counting based on segmented regions usually assumes reliable geometric
containment between berry points and grape-cluster regions. However, natural orchard
images often contain locally undersized masks, boundary jitter, multiple overlapping
masks, and berry points outside mask boundaries; therefore, simple in-box or in-mask rules
can easily cause cross-cluster misassignment. General-purpose segmentation foundation
models such as SAM provide a new technical basis for prompt-based instance mask gener-
ation [33], and their applications in grape cluster structure and berry trait analysis have
also shown potential [3]. Therefore, stable assignment of berry point localization results to
cluster-level instance masks remains a key problem in BpGC counting in natural orchards.
3. Materials and Methods
3.1. Overall Framework
UniGC is designed for three interrelated subtasks with distinct outputs: berry-level
counting, grape cluster-level detection and counting, and BpGC counting. As shown in
Figure 1, given an input RGB image I ∈RHI×WI×3, the overall pipeline consists of three
connected modules. First, the berry-level branch adopts CSD-P2PNet, a P2PNet-based
point-supervised counting branch enhanced with the CSD-Block, to predict berry points
for berry localization and whole-image berry counting. Second, the cluster-level branch
uses a YOLO detector to predict grape-cluster detection boxes, which are then fed into
SAM as box prompts to generate the corresponding grape-cluster instance masks. Finally,
the BDMPA establishes assignments according to the spatial relationships between berry
points and grape cluster instance masks, thereby outputting BpGC counting results.

3.2. CSD-P2PNet-Based Berry-Level Counting Branch
In natural orchard scenes, grape berry counting is highly sensitive to fine-grained
structural cues such as local edges, texture changes, peak responses, and occlusion con-
tours. However, disturbances such as strong illumination, shadows, uneven exposure,
and highlight reflections can introduce slowly varying feature biases or local response shifts,
which may interfere with the representation of berry-related geometric structures. If these
condition-related variations are mixed with structure-sensitive responses in intermediate
features, the model may become less stable in distinguishing true berry structures from
background or illumination-induced disturbances.
To alleviate this problem, we introduce the CSD-Block into an intermediate feature
layer of P2PNet, as shown in Figure 2. This module models intermediate features from a
frequency-inspired perspective without applying an explicit Fourier transform. Instead,
through learnable low-pass filtering and residual decomposition, it encourages the ex-
traction of slowly varying condition-related components and residual structure-sensitive
components in the spatial domain. The module then applies de-conditioned enhancement
to the residual structural features and uses a condition-guided controlled recoupling mech-
anism to reintegrate structural information, thereby improving the robustness of berry
point localization under complex illumination and occlusion.

3.2.1. Explicit Decoupling of Condition and Structural Components
Let the input feature map be X ∈RB×C×H×W, where B, C, H, and W denote the
batch size, number of channels, feature map height, and feature map width, respectively.

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7500

The output of CSD-Block has the same tensor shape as its input; therefore, the block can
serve as a plug-and-play enhancement unit in the intermediate layers of P2PNet.
The module first uses a channel-wise learnable low-pass operator to extract a condition-
related component as Ccond = L(X). Because the operator is constrained to perform
spatial smoothing, Ccond is encouraged to capture slowly varying feature patterns that
are associated with imaging conditions, such as illumination changes, exposure varia-
tions, and smooth background responses. The residual component is then obtained as
S0 = X −Ccond. Compared with the smoothed component, S0 is expected to retain more
structure-sensitive responses related to berry localization, including local contrast changes,
edge-like responses, texture variations, and occlusion contours.
The low-pass operator adopts depthwise convolution. Let the low-pass kernel size be
k × k. For each channel, the convolution kernel weights are obtained by applying softmax
normalization to the kernel parameters θc, namely Wc = Softmax(θc) for c = 1, 2, . . . , C.
Thus, any position (u, v) within the kernel satisfies Wc(u, v) ≥0 and ∑u,v Wc(u, v) = 1.
This parameterization makes the convolution kernel naturally satisfy the constraints of
non-negativity and unit-sum weights, thereby providing stable smoothing properties and
helping extract slowly varying imaging-condition information from intermediate features.
3.2.2. De-Conditioned Structural Enhancement
Although S0 separates high-frequency structures to some extent, shadows and local
uneven exposure may still affect the amplitude of structural responses. To reduce this
condition-induced influence, CSD-Block applies Instance Normalization (IN) without
affine parameters to S0, yielding the de-conditioned structural feature ˆS. This operation
can reduce mean drift and variance fluctuation caused by imaging-condition variations,
allowing subsequent convolutions to focus more on the geometric structure itself.
On this basis, the module uses multi-dilation depthwise convolutions to extract struc-
tural details. Let the set of dilation rates be D = {d1, d2, . . . , dM}; then the structural

intra-cluster berry counting.
3.4. Boundary-Depth Guided Mask-Point Assignment and BpGC Counting
3.4.1. Assignment Modeling Between Berry Points and Grape Cluster Masks
After obtaining the predicted berry point set P and the grape-cluster instance mask set
M, UniGC uses BDMPA to assign each predicted berry point to at most one grape cluster in-
stance. These assignments define a many-to-one point-to-cluster mapping. BDMPA follows
the design principle of mandatory internal assignment and cautious external adsorption:
for points located inside masks, unique disambiguation is preferentially completed among
internal candidate instances; for points not covered by any mask, adsorption-based assign-
ment is allowed only when they are located within the relative boundary neighborhood
of an instance. This design alleviates point-to-cluster misassignment caused by adjacent
grape-cluster overlap, boundary adhesion, locally undersized masks, and boundary jitter.
Let the predicted point set be P = {pi}N
i=1, with pi = (xi, yi), and let the instance mask
set be M = {Mk}K
k=1, with Mk ⊂Ω. Here, Ωdenotes the image pixel domain, and N and
K denote the number of predicted berry points and the number of grape cluster instances,
respectively. The objective of BDMPA is to output an assignment label ai ∈{−1, 1, 2, . . . , K}
for each predicted point, where ai = k indicates that point pi is assigned to the k-th grape
cluster instance, and ai = −1 indicates that the point is unassigned.
This assignment problem is difficult for two main reasons. On the one hand, adjacent
grape clusters in natural orchard scenes often exhibit occlusion, adhesion, and local overlap,
making it difficult to resolve assignment ambiguity in regions covered by multiple masks
based solely on whether a point falls inside a mask. On the other hand, instance masks

3.5. Dataset and Annotation Organization
The experiments in this study use a mixed grape dataset constructed from WGISD
and GBISC. WGISD was collected from the vineyard of Guaspari Winery in São Paulo
State, Brazil, with images acquired in April 2017 and April 2018, and covers five wine
grape cultivars: Chardonnay, Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sauvignon Blanc,
and Syrah [13]. GBISC was collected in July 2023 from vineyards in Longquanyi District
and Shuangliu District, Chengdu, Sichuan Province, China, and covers three grape cultivars:
Kyoho, Shine Muscat, and Summer Black [27]. After annotation unification and validity
filtering, the organized dataset contains 450 RGB images, 6152 grape-cluster bounding
boxes, and 237,696 berry center points.
The dataset was randomly divided at the image level into 360 training images and
90 validation images. The training split contains 240 WGISD images and 120 GBISC images,
while the validation split contains 60 WGISD images and 30 GBISC images. For model
training, the organized dataset provides berry center-point annotations for berry-level
counting and grape-cluster bounding-box annotations for cluster-level detection.
For BpGC evaluation, point-to-cluster correspondence labels were additionally anno-
tated for the validation split, indicating which grape cluster each annotated berry point
belongs to. These correspondence labels cover 1258 ground-truth grape clusters and
46,292 berry center points in the validation split and are used only for BpGC evaluation.

To quantify the annotation effort associated with the supervision formats used in this
study, three annotators participated in a timed annotation study. Each annotator labeled
15 randomly selected WGISD images and 15 GBISC images, resulting in 90 annotated
images in total. The mean operation times were approximately 0.8 s per berry center
point, 1.4 s per grape-cluster bounding box, 2.6 s per berry instance mask, and 10.6 s per
grape-cluster instance mask. Based on the numbers of berries and clusters in the complete
450-image dataset, the average annotation time was estimated to be 7.36 min per image
for berry center points and grape-cluster bounding boxes, compared with 25.30 min per
image for dual instance masks. Thus, dual instance-mask annotation required approxi-
mately 3.44 times as much time as point-and-box annotation. These estimates quantify
annotation time rather than monetary cost, and the point-to-cluster correspondence labels
used for BpGC evaluation were not included in the training supervision. In addition to
the measured time difference, UniGC requires simpler supervision formats: its trainable
branches rely only on berry center points and grape-cluster bounding boxes, without pixel-
level instance masks. In regions with overlapping clusters, touching berries, or occlusion,
initial masks generated using interactive segmentation tools generally require repeated
inspection, boundary correction, separation of touching instances, and removal of mask
overlaps. In comparison, center points and bounding boxes are more direct to create,
inspect, and revise, facilitating the organization of large-scale grape image datasets.
4. Experiments and Results
4.1. Experimental Setup
All experiments in this study were conducted on the same server. The hardware
platform was configured with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8481C CPU and an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 4090D GPU with 24 GB of memory. During UniGC training, the cluster-level
detection branch used YOLO12-S [37] by default and was trained for 200 epochs with a
batch size of 8, an input image size of 1024 × 1024 pixels, SGD as the optimizer, an initial
learning rate of 0.01, a momentum of 0.937, and a weight decay of 0.0005. The SAM-based
mask generation module was used only as a prompt-driven inference module: the detection
boxes predicted by YOLO are fed into the pretrained SAM to generate grape-cluster
instance masks, and no SAM training or fine-tuning is performed. The berry-level counting
branch uses the proposed CSD-P2PNet, which follows the training protocol of P2PNet [32].
Specifically, CSD-Block is inserted into the intermediate feature layer of P2PNet, while the
original point-regression training objective and prediction head are retained. This branch
was trained for 500 epochs, with a batch size of 4, Adam as the optimizer, and a learning
rate of 1.0 × 10−4. To improve training robustness, YOLO12-S was trained using the
official settings and data augmentation strategies, including HSV perturbation, translation,
scaling, horizontal flipping, Mosaic, RandAugment, and random erasing. CSD-P2PNet
followed the data augmentation settings of P2PNet, including random scaling, random
cropping, and random horizontal flipping. During inference and evaluation, the YOLO
confidence threshold is set to 0.25, and the relative adsorption threshold of BDMPA is set to
ρ = 0.03; in BpGC evaluation, intersection over union (IoU) is set to 0.6 as the matching
threshold between ground-truth (GT) grape clusters and predicted grape clusters. To assess

4.4. Ablation Experiments
This section presents ablation experiments and sensitivity analyses for the key modules
and the main reported configuration of UniGC, sequentially examining the effects of CSD-

threshold ρ on the experimental results.
4.4.1. Component Ablation of CSD-Block and Gains for the Berry-Level Backbone
To validate the contribution of CSD-Block to the berry-level counting branch and
analyze the roles of its internal components, this study uses P2PNet [32] as the baseline
and inserts CSD-Block or its ablated variants after the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)
output features and before the regression and classification heads. In addition to the
original P2PNet, this study defines five controlled variants: Plain DWConv Block retains
only standard depthwise convolution enhancement to exclude the effect introduced by
additional convolution modules; CSD without Decoupling removes the explicit decoupling
between the low-frequency condition component and the structural residual component;
CSD without Instance Normalization (IN) removes Instance Normalization on the structural
component; CSD without Multi-Dilation retains only a single dilation-rate branch; and
CSD without Condition Re-coupling removes Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)
and spatial-gate-based condition recoupling. All variants are compared under the same
training settings.
As shown in Table 7, the full CSD-Block achieved the lowest MAE and RMSE among
all evaluated variants. Relative to the original P2PNet [32], it reduced MAE from 27.7
to 23.3 and RMSE from 37.7 to 33.2, corresponding to absolute reductions of 4.4 and 4.5,
respectively. By comparison, the Plain DWConv Block achieved an MAE of 27.0 and an
RMSE of 37.6, remaining close to the original P2PNet. This comparison indicates that the
improvement cannot be attributed solely to the addition of convolutional enhancement.
The component ablations further clarify the contribution of each design element.
Removing condition–structure decoupling increased MAE and RMSE to 25.2 and 35.7,
respectively, supporting the role of explicit component separation in reducing interference
from smooth background responses and broader contextual variations. Removing Instance
Normalization resulted in an MAE of 25.2 and an RMSE of 34.0, indicating that normaliza-
tion of the structural residual contributes to stable feature enhancement. Retaining only a
single dilation branch increased the errors to 24.2 and 33.4, demonstrating the benefit of
aggregating structural information across multiple receptive fields. Removing condition

4.4.5. Ablation Analysis of BDMPA Point-Cluster Assignment Strategies
To validate the roles of internal boundary-depth disambiguation and external ad-
sorption in BDMPA, this study compares six point-cluster assignment strategies under
fixed settings of YOLO12-S [37], SAM3 [46], and IoU = 0.6. All strategies use the same
predicted berry points, grape-cluster detection boxes, and SAM-generated masks; therefore,
the comparison focuses on the effect of the assignment strategy itself on BpGC counting.
The compared strategies are defined as follows. Box-only assigns a point to a YOLO
detection box if the point falls inside the box; when multiple boxes contain the same point,
the box with the highest detection confidence is selected, and points outside all boxes are
discarded. Mask-only assigns a point to a SAM mask if the point falls inside the mask; when
multiple masks contain the same point, the mask associated with the highest-confidence
detection box is selected, and uncovered points are discarded. Nearest-mask assigns each
point to the nearest SAM mask according to the Euclidean distance to the mask boundary,
without using the ρ constraint. Mask + centroid tie-break assigns only mask-covered
points and resolves overlapping-mask ambiguity by selecting the mask whose centroid
is closest to the point. BDMPA without external adsorption uses normalized internal
boundary-depth disambiguation for mask-covered points, while points outside all masks
are discarded. Full BDMPA further introduces ρ-constrained external adsorption after
internal boundary-depth disambiguation.
As shown in Table 12, Full BDMPA achieved the lowest MRD and ACP-MRD, with val-
ues of 0.1336 and 0.4977, respectively, together with the highest 1 −FVU of 0.8557. Box-only
provided useful coarse spatial constraints but remained limited by rectangular boundaries,
whereas Mask-only and Nearest-mask produced larger MRD and ACP-MRD values, indicat-
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16157500

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7500
24 of 33
ing that mask containment alone or unconstrained nearest-mask assignment is insufficient
for reliable point-to-cluster association.
Replacing centroid-based ambiguity resolution with normalized internal boundary
depth reduced MRD from 0.1451 to 0.1424 and ACP-MRD from 0.5068 to 0.5046. Introduc-
ing ρ-constrained external adsorption further reduced MRD and ACP-MRD to 0.1336 and
0.4977, respectively, while increasing 1 −FVU from 0.8420 to 0.8557. Relative to BDMPA
without external adsorption, Full BDMPA reduced MRD by 6.18% and ACP-MRD by 1.37%.
These results support the complementary roles of internal boundary-depth disambiguation
and constrained external adsorption in improving both matched-cluster counting and
overall BpGC performance.

5. Discussion
This study proposes UniGC, a unified framework that integrates whole-image count-
ing of image-visible berries, grape-cluster detection and counting, and BpGC counting
within a common inference pipeline. The framework combines a point-based berry branch
derived from P2PNet [32], a YOLO12-S grape-cluster detector [37], box-prompted cluster-
mask generation with SAM3 [46], and BDMPA-based point-to-cluster assignment. Unlike
single-task approaches that provide only image-level counts or cluster locations, UniGC
explicitly links image-visible berry-point localization, grape-cluster instance geometry,
and cluster-wise berry counting. Its trainable branches require berry center-point anno-
tations and grape-cluster bounding-box annotations. By producing associated outputs at
the berry, cluster, and within-cluster levels, UniGC provides structured information on
image-visible berry load. Compared with isolated image-level counts, these outputs may
provide a more informative basis for crop-load characterization, identification of clusters
with atypical visible berry loads, and cluster-specific thinning or management decisions.
Existing studies on grape cluster detection or instance segmentation can provide
cluster-level geometric priors [13], but they usually cannot directly provide cluster-wise
counts of image-visible berries; although berry-level density-map, probability-map, or seg-
mentation methods can improve local counting accuracy, they still require additional
post-processing to convert responses into instances or intra-cluster counts [26,27,35].
Yang et al. [9] proposed PMGDC, which models grape clusters, berries, and BpGC as
related tasks and provides an important basis for unified counting; however, probability
maps and multi-stage post-processing may still lead to misassignment under adjacent-
cluster overlap, boundary adhesion, and response drift. Du et al. [10] proposed MVDNet,
which jointly estimates intra-cluster berry counts through density-map regression and
cluster-level segmentation, and also shows strong potential; however, its segmentation
branch relies on polygon- or pixel-level mask supervision, which is more fine-grained than
the point-and-box supervision adopted in this study, and its main evaluation metrics differ
from the MRD and 1 −FVU used in this study, making a fully equivalent direct comparison
difficult. Within the UniGC framework, berry predictions are explicitly represented as
coordinate points, while the SAM framework proposed by Kirillov et al. [33] generates
cluster-level masks from box prompts to provide instance-level geometric boundaries.
BDMPA subsequently establishes point-to-cluster assignments by integrating these two
forms of information. In this way, UniGC explicitly links berry-point localization and
cluster membership estimation within the unified multi-level counting framework.
The repeated-run results support the principal design choices. CSD-P2PNet, built on
P2PNet [32], achieved an MAE of 23.4407 ± 0.1577, an RMSE of 32.8898 ± 1.2682, and an F1
score of 0.8374 ± 0.0097 for image-visible berry-point localization. YOLO12-S [37] achieved
an F1 score of 0.8433 ± 0.0056 and an AP50 of 0.8789 ± 0.0080 for grape-cluster detec-
tion. For BpGC counting, UniGC obtained a matched-cluster MRD of 0.1288 ± 0.0043,
a matched-cluster 1 −FVU of 0.8589 ± 0.0098, and an all-cluster penalized ACP-MRD
of 0.5062 ± 0.0124. Relative to PMGDC [9], UniGC reduced the matched-cluster MRD
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16157500

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7500
28 of 33
and ACP-MRD by 20.15% and 2.43%, respectively, while increasing the matched-cluster
1 −FVU by 3.91%. These results support the complementary contributions of image-visible
berry-point localization, grape-cluster geometry, and point-to-cluster assignment to BpGC
performance within the evaluated unified framework. From an agronomic perspective,
the combined outputs of whole-image visible berry load, cluster locations, and per-cluster
visible berry counts provide a more informative image-based basis for crop-load characteri-
zation than any single output alone. Although these image-derived outputs do not directly
estimate severely occluded or invisible berries, cluster mass, or field-scale yield, they pro-
vide a feasible visual-information basis for subsequent integration with complementary
phenotypic traits, multi-view or depth information, and calibration models toward more
comprehensive crop-load and yield estimation.
The contribution of BDMPA is mainly reflected in its handling of boundary uncertainty.
Simple in-box or in-mask rules assume reliable geometric boundaries, but natural orchard
images often contain undersized masks, boundary jitter, multiple overlapping masks,
and berry points located outside boundaries. BDMPA adopts the rule of “mandatory
internal assignment and cautious external adsorption”: points covered by multiple masks
are disambiguated using normalized internal boundary depth, whereas external points
are adsorbed only within the relative distance threshold. The sensitivity analysis shows
that when ρ increases from 0 to 0.03, MRD decreases from 0.1424 to 0.1336, ACP-MRD
decreases from 0.5046 to 0.4977, and 1 −FVU increases from 0.8420 to 0.8557. When ρ
exceeds 0.03, MRD and ACP-MRD no longer improve consistently, while 1 −FVU changes
only marginally. This indicates that limited outside-boundary adsorption can recover near-
boundary berry points that are excluded by locally incomplete masks, whereas an overly
wide adsorption range relaxes the geometric constraint and may increase the possibility of
incorrect assignment.
Although UniGC achieves competitive results, several limitations remain. First,
the unified modular formulation remains sensitive to inter-stage error propagation.
The single-stage oracle diagnostics in Table 14 show that replacing grape-cluster detection
and prompt geometry produced the largest reduction in ACP-MRD (0.3773), whereas
replacing berry-point localization or cluster masks yielded smaller reductions of 0.0279 and
0.0204, respectively. These results identify grape-cluster detection completeness and prompt
geometry as the principal bottleneck of the current pipeline, indicating that upstream lo-
calization and geometric accuracy should be prioritized. Second, the source-stratified
evaluation showed broadly consistent comparative trends across WGISD and GBISC, al-
though the magnitude of the gains varied between the two sources. Because both sources
contributed to the training and evaluation splits, these results do not constitute an inde-
pendent cross-domain evaluation. Furthermore, we did not identify an additional publicly
available grape dataset that simultaneously provides the berry-point annotations, grape-
cluster annotations, and point-to-cluster correspondence labels required by the current
training and BpGC evaluation protocol. Therefore, independent external BpGC evaluation
under a directly compatible protocol remains unavailable. Environmental variations may
affect different stages of the unified framework in distinct but related ways. Changes in
cultivar appearance, berry size and color, cluster compactness, growth stage, canopy struc-
ture, and background composition may alter local berry textures and cluster boundaries.
Variations in acquisition distance, viewing angle, image resolution, sensor characteristics,
season, and region may further affect berry-point localization, detection-box complete-
ness, and prompt geometry. Illumination changes, including shadows, highlights, strong
backlighting, and nighttime artificial illumination, may introduce local contrast shifts and
boundary ambiguities, thereby influencing point prediction, mask generation, and subse-
quent point-to-cluster assignment. These errors can propagate through the unified pipeline
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16157500

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7500
29 of 33
and affect the final BpGC results. Related cross-site visual inspection research has simi-
larly shown that model performance may degrade under unseen acquisition conditions
and that domain adaptation can mitigate such distribution shifts [52]. Third, the current
model mainly counts image-visible berry points and does not directly estimate severely
occluded or invisible berries or jointly model yield-related traits such as berry size, berry
mass, cluster compactness, and cluster weight. Nevertheless, its whole-image visible
berry counts, cluster locations, and per-cluster visible berry counts provide a structured
basis for subsequent integration with multi-view imagery, depth information, physical
measurements, and calibration models. The study by Parr et al. [1] further demonstrates
the potential of combining low-cost RGB-D acquisition with complementary phenotypic
information for crop-load and yield estimation. Fourth, the complete UniGC pipeline
remains computationally intensive. On the evaluated RTX 4090D server, the application-
level runtime was 1009.629 ms per image, corresponding to 0.990 FPS, while the peak
allocated and reserved GPU memory reached 8533.2 MiB and 17,554.0 MiB, respectively.
The current configuration is therefore more suitable for offline or server-side analysis than
for real-time onboard inference. Device-level deployment experiments were not conducted
because representative mobile-robot, unmanned-aerial-vehicle, and edge-computing plat-
forms and the corresponding system-integration conditions were unavailable in the present
experimental environment.
Our future work will proceed in the following four directions. First, end-to-end or
weakly coupled multi-task networks will be explored under comparable point-and-box
supervision to promote representation sharing among grape-cluster detection, berry-point
localization, mask generation, and point-to-cluster assignment and thereby reduce inter-
stage error propagation. Second, we will further construct and annotate a large-scale,
multi-scene BpGC dataset containing berry points, grape-cluster annotations, and point-to-
cluster correspondence labels. The dataset will progressively cover additional vineyards,
cultivars, growth stages, cluster structures, acquisition distances, viewing angles, illumi-
nation conditions, and imaging devices. This dataset will enable independent external
evaluation and vineyard-, cultivar-, and acquisition-separated testing, while also support-
ing further investigation of domain adaptation and few-shot learning for unseen vineyard
environments. Third, multi-view imagery, RGB-D sensing, physical berry and cluster
measurements, and calibration models will be incorporated to extend UniGC from image-
visible counting toward hidden-berry estimation and complementary yield-trait analysis.
The cluster locations, visible berry load, and BpGC outputs will also be connected to
fruit-thinning decisions, picking-point prediction, and yield-estimation workflows. Fourth,
lightweight mask generators, reusable image embeddings, and parallel or vectorized im-
plementations of BDMPA will be investigated to reduce computational overhead. When
suitable mobile-robot, unmanned-aerial-vehicle, or edge-computing platforms and the
required system-integration conditions become available, device-level experiments will be
conducted to evaluate inference latency, memory consumption, energy efficiency, and oper-
ational stability in practical vineyard environments.
6. Conclusions
This paper proposes UniGC, a unified framework for multi-level grape counting in
natural orchard images. The framework integrates grape-cluster detection and counting,
image-visible berry localization and counting, and BpGC counting into a unified pipeline.
First, CSD-P2PNet incorporating CSD-Block is used to perform berry-point localization and
whole-image visible-berry counting. Second, YOLO predicts grape-cluster bounding boxes,
which are used as prompts for SAM to generate the corresponding cluster instance masks
as geometric priors. Finally, BDMPA, which requires no additional trainable parameters,
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16157500

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7500
30 of 33
assigns each predicted berry point to at most one grape-cluster instance and produces
per-cluster visible berry counts.
The experimental results show that UniGC achieves stable and competitive multi-level
counting performance on the mixed dataset organized from WGISD and GBISC, while
its trainable branches use only berry-point annotations and grape-cluster bounding-box
annotations. For whole-image berry counting and point localization, CSD-P2PNet achieves
favorable performance compared with the evaluated methods. For grape-cluster detection
and BpGC counting, UniGC demonstrates clear and consistent overall improvements over
the representative baseline PMGDC in most evaluated metrics, particularly in grape-cluster
localization quality and intra-cluster counting accuracy. Overall, UniGC provides a method-
ological reference for multi-level grape counting under point-and-box supervision and may
inform the development of related fruit-counting and visual phenotyping methods. Future
work will focus on developing end-to-end modules for multi-level counting, constructing
large-scale multi-scene BpGC datasets, conducting independent cross-domain evaluation,
integrating complementary phenotypic information, and improving computational effi-
ciency to support practical applications.
