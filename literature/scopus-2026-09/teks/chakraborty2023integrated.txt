Abstract—Oranges hold significant sway in the global food 
market, making the monitoring of their production a matter of 
utmost importance. Keeping track of the production procedure 
is the most important part of having a great yield of oranges. 
Through the integration of cloud computing, IoT, machine 
learning, and advanced image analysis techniques, we aim to 
streamline orange orchard management. Machine learning and 
computer vision have brought revolutionary changes to the 
agricultural sector, particularly in orchard monitoring. This 
paper introduces an integrated framework for orchard 
monitoring, focusing on real-time imaging of oranges, cloud-
based storage, and the application of machine learning 
algorithms for automated monitoring and yield notifications to 
farmers. Additionally, we have conducted a comparative 
analysis of various machine learning models to evaluate their 
capacity to detect and count overlapped oranges within the 
dataset. Our proposed framework encompasses image capture, 
cloud storage, and machine learning analysis, providing farmers 
with invaluable insights into orange production. The 
comparative analysis delves into the merits and limitations of 
different machine learning models, including the K-means and 
Distance Least Square, Hough Transform, and YOLOv3, 
addressing the challenge of detecting overlapped oranges. 
Keywords—Fruit Detection, Fruit Counting, Smart Farming, 
IoT, Machine Learning, Image Analysis.

II. LITERATURE SURVEY 
Previously, several works have been done in this field. 
Different researchers proposed different methods. However, 
this topic has been of greater importance to date. Many works 
are still going on to improve the segmentation of different 
fruits in complex environments, like illumination, contrast, 
etc. Researchers have also tried to overcome the issues of 
overlapping and occlusion. Different researchers approached 
it in different ways, like the geometric method, edge detection 
method, Hough transform, region-based convolutional neural 
network, deep learning, etc. 
Murillo-Bracamontes et al. published a research paper 
about fruit image segmentation using the Hough transform in 
2012 [1]. They converted the original image into grayscale at 
first and then performed the Hough transform on that image. 
They performed this on images of cluster grapes in a vineyard 
with a solid background. Feng et al. came up with the idea of 
separating adjacent apples in an image using machine vision 
and chain code information [2]. Their proposed method 
consists of the following steps: extraction of the valid region 
from the image; acquisition of chain code information; 
calculation of chain code difference; selection of concavity 
points; and matching concavity points, which leads to separate 
regions. Lastly, they calculate the centroid of each region. By 
separating the centroids, they separate the apples. This method 
has been tested on 100 apple images, and the successful 
separation rate has been recorded at more than 91.2%. Xue et 
al. proposed a method to detect overlapped images in orchard 
scenes [3]. They have used an improved version of the K-
means and the Distance Least Square Technique in their 
method of identification. At first, K-Means is used to identify 
the potential presence of apples in the images. Then, using 
Canny’s edge detection algorithm, the contour is detected. 
Lastly, the Distance Least Square Circle Fitting Technique is

like the positive attention mask (PAM-Mask) and MaskIoU

Mirhaji et al. published a paper on the topic of fruit 
detection and load estimation of an orange orchard using deep 
learning models [6]. They have used different YOLO (You 
Only Look Once) models for detecting oranges. They have 
also estimated the yield in the orchard. They have taken 
images in different conditions, like cloudy images, sunny 
images, nighttime images, etc. The proposed method has a few 
steps: acquiring images, annotation, image augmentation, 
evaluation of the model yield estimation, training model, 
model evaluation, and yield map. They have performed it on 
three models of YOLO, namely YOLOv2, YOLOv3, and 
YOLOv4. YOLOv2, YOLOv3, and YOLOv4 have an F1 
score of 74.99%, 87.11%, and 92%, respectively. Li et al. 
published a paper on this problem [7]. The research paper was 
about detecting green peppers quickly and accurately in 
complex backgrounds using an improved YOLOv4-tiny 
model. The AP value of the green peppers reached 95.11% by 
using the proposed model of Li et al. He et al. proposed a 
method of Cascade-SORT for counting fruits [8]. It is a robust 
counting approach based on multiple features of cascading. 
The proposed method is divided into three segments: 
detection, data association, and trajectory prediction. They 
have used YOLOv3 as a detector. They have also provided a 
comparative analysis between CascadeSORT and the SORT 
algorithm. Liu et al. proposed a method of segmenting green 
fruits in obscured environments [9]. This is a DLNet double-
layer occlusion segmentation model. This research work was 
done on unripe persimmon and green apple images. Feature 
extraction was done by the RS-RFP detection network. Lei et 
al. introduced a multi-module convolutional neural network 
for Bayberry segmentation in a complex environment [10]. 
They collected 378 images of Bayberry, among which 300 
were randomly chosen as a train set, while the rest of the 
images were kept as the test set. The proposed method consists 
of the following steps: data acquisition, data complexity 
analysis, data augmentation, overall scheme, backbone 
network (mostly based on CNN models), image correction 
module, shape completion module, model construction, and 
evaluation. Hence, it can be said that CNN [11] has addressed 
this lots of agricultural problem very efficiently. 
III. PROPOSED FRAMEWORK 
A. Overview 
Here, we are proposing a framework for monitoring 
orange orchards using different state-of-the-art technologies 
like computer vision, machine learning, cloud computing, and 
IoT. Fig. 1 depicts the complete framework for orchard 
monitoring. The overview of the proposed framework has 
been explained in this section. Firstly, images of the oranges 
are taken by high-resolution cameras placed in the orchard in 
front of every tree. Cameras are placed in such a way that we 
can cover each tree completely from all angles. In the next 
step, the captured images are sent to cloud storage for further 
analysis. This can be achieved through a variety of wireless or 
wired network technologies such as Wi-Fi, ZigBee, Bluetooth, 
or Ethernet. The images are taken from the cloud and machine 
learning algorithms are applied to those images. After that, the 
result of the images like the count of oranges is sent to the 
user. This will help the user to keep track of the production of 
the oranges. This process is repeated with newly captured 
images after a certain time limit (like every 5-10 minutes). 
 
Fig. 1. Proposed framework for automated orange orchard monitoring 
B. Image capturing & sending to the cloud 
 
In the image-capturing and sending process in orange 
orchards, specialized cameras equipped with high-resolution 
sensors capture detailed visuals of the orchard. These images, 
featuring various spectral bands, are then processed to extract 
valuable information about crop health and environmental 
factors. The data is swiftly transmitted to the cloud via secure 
networks, where sophisticated algorithms analyze it. This 
seamless integration of imaging technology and cloud 
computing allows for real-time monitoring, enabling farmers 
to make informed decisions for optimal orchard management 
and increased agricultural productivity. 
C. Orange detection & counting 
 
The most important part of this framework is to detect the 
orange in its actual shape. The perfect detection of oranges 
leads to accurate counting. We have explored different 
techniques for addressing the challenges of on-tree fruit 
detection. The techniques are k-means and distance least 
square, Hough transform, and Deep Learning (YOLOv3). 
D. Further Analysis 
 
After the successful detection and counting of oranges, the 
framework allows for in-depth analysis to derive valuable 
insights. Trend analysis of orange production over time 
enables the identification of seasonal patterns and variations. 
Additionally, the data can be utilized to pinpoint areas within 
the orchard with higher or lower yields, facilitating targeted 
interventions.

IV. COMPARATIVE STUDY ON DETECTION AND COUNTING 
A. K-means and distance least square 
This section delineates the methodology employed for the 
automated detection and counting of oranges in a series of 
images, ensuring accuracy and robustness. The process 
initiates with image segmentation through the conversion of 
the RGB color space to LAB, separating color information 
from the brightness. The reshaped 2D array of pixels 
undergoes K-Means clustering (k=3) to identify clusters 
corresponding to oranges, stems, and non-relevant regions. 
Cluster centers are then computed to ascertain characteristic 
colors. 
Subsequently, the orange regions are extracted through the 
creation of a binary mask based on cluster assignments. This 
mask, highlighting orange regions, is applied to the original 
image using bitwise operations. To enhance accuracy, 
morphological operations, specifically opening and closing 
with an elliptical kernel (5, 5), are applied for denoising the 
segmented orange regions. 
Further processing involves the conversion of the denoised 
orange image to grayscale, followed by Gaussian blur 
application for noise reduction. The Hough Circle Transform 
[12] is employed for orange detection in the blurred grayscale 
image, with parameters carefully tuned. Detected circles are 
visually represented on the original image using green 
contours. 
For the processing of multiple images, the sequential 
application of this methodology on a series of images is 
executed. The results, including the processed image and the 
count of detected oranges, are visually presented for each 
image. This comprehensive methodology aims to automate 
accurate orange detection and counting across diverse images, 
holding promise for applications in quality control, 
agricultural assessments, and domains requiring precise 
orange quantification. 
B. Hough Transform 
This section details the methodology employed for the 
detection and counting of oranges in a series of images. The 
process encompasses two primary phases: pre-processing and 
orange detection. 
In the pre-processing phase, images are first converted 
from RGB to HSV color space, separating color information 
from brightness. To identify orange regions, a specific HSV 
range is defined, forming the basis for a mask highlighting 
these regions. Gaussian blur is then applied to the mask to 
reduce noise and enhance circle detection reliability. 
The orange detection phase employs the Hough Circle 
Transform from the OpenCV library, with fine-tuned 
parameters for optimal circle detection. Additionally, an 
outlier removal mechanism is introduced to enhance accuracy. 
Circles deviating significantly from the expected size are 
identified and removed based on a threshold established from 
the average radius of detected circles. 
This comprehensive methodology aims to develop a 
robust and accurate automated system for orange circle 
detection and counting. By processing a series of images, 
valuable insights are gained, applicable in diverse domains 
such as agriculture, manufacturing, and quality control. 
C. YOLOv3 
This section focuses on the utilization of YOLOv3 for 
real-time object detection, particularly in identifying and 
counting oranges within a series of images. To configure and 
load the YOLOv3 model, class labels from the COCO dataset 
were used, providing essential context for object recognition. 
The model architecture was specified through a configuration 
file, and pre-trained model weights were loaded, enabling the 
neural network to detect various objects, including oranges. 
Input images were loaded, and their dimensions were 
determined for subsequent processing. 
The forward pass and object detection involved creating a 
blob from the input image, resizing it to 416x416 pixels, and 
scaling it to the range [0, 1]. This blob was then input into the 
YOLO model, generating layer outputs interpreted to identify 
objects. Objects with confidence scores surpassing a specified 
threshold, specifically, those labeled as 'orange,' were 
considered for further analysis. 
Bounding boxes were calculated around detected orange 
objects, utilizing predicted locations and sizes. The confidence 
scores of detected oranges were evaluated, retaining only 
those exceeding a minimum threshold. Non-maximum 
suppression (NMS) was applied to the set of detected 
bounding boxes, ensuring consideration of only the most 
relevant oranges. 
For visualization, bounding boxes were annotated on the 
input image, outlined in orange to highlight the detected 
oranges. Confidence scores were displayed alongside the 
respective bounding boxes, providing a quantitative measure 
of confidence. The annotated images were then displayed to 
visualize detection results. 
In processing multiple images, the YOLO-based 
methodology was sequentially applied to a set of images 
containing oranges. The annotated images with detection 
results were displayed, and the number of detected oranges in 
each image was recorded and visually presented. This 
comprehensive approach using YOLOv3 aims to provide 
accurate and efficient orange detection, with applications in 
diverse domains. 
By implementing this comprehensive methodology, we 
aimed to achieve automated and accurate orange detection and 
counting within a diverse range of images. This approach has 
significant applications in industries requiring precise object 
quantification, such as agriculture and quality control. The 
described methodology provides a detailed step-by-step 
process for the automated detection and counting of oranges 
in images using the YOLO object detection framework.

vision, cloud computing, and IoT has the potential to 
revolutionize how farmers manage their orchards, optimize 
yield predictions, and enhance overall resource management. 
By leveraging this technology, farmers will gain immediate
