A B S T R A C T
Oil palm fruit, a high-demand and economically valuable crop, faces grading challenges in Malaysia due to 
labour-intensive methods, resulting in unharvested ripe fruits and yield losses. Extensive research has been 
conducted on outdoor palm fruit classifiers, but most high-accuracy research in this field relies heavily on large 
image datasets. This creates a trade-off between employing more sophisticated deep-learning approaches that 
require extensive data and utilising traditional techniques that have lower data requirements. Therefore, the aim 
is to propose an outdoor image-based fresh fruit bunch (FFB) classification system, emphasising pre-processing 
technique and feature extraction techniques suitable for limited datasets. To achieve this, the FFB was localised 
within the original images using the YOLOv4-Tiny algorithm. Subsequently, a salient segmentation method 
utilising superpixel-based Fast Fuzzy C-means (FFCM) clustering is employed to remove the background from the 
images. Next, colour moment and opposite colour local binary pattern (OCLBP) features are extracted from the 
segmented images to capture important information for classification. Finally, the extracted features are fed into 
a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) classifier, which enables the system to predict five classes: damaged, empty, 
unripe, ripe, and overripe. The developed system demonstrated a commendable performance in accurately 
classifying fruit bunches, achieving an accuracy of 93.68 %. In conclusion, the proposed system effectively ad­
dresses the issue of unripe fruit harvesting and contributes to the advancement of state-of-the-art methods in 
classifying outdoor FFB images.
1. Introduction
The palm oil industry is a major contributor to Malaysia’s economy, 
which contributed to 14.48 billion USD of exports in June 2022 [1] and 
the production expected to increase by 3 % in 2023 [2]. The traditional 
manual grading process relied on human vision, which was slow and 
subjective, requiring experienced staff for grading and sorting [3]. This 
approach also involved multiple stages of ripeness identification, lead­
ing to increased sorting time.
The sustainability of harvesting process might be impacted by the 
decreasing of human labour due to the slow entry process of migrant 
workers caused by the Covid-19 pandemic [4]. This has led to a potential 
loss in palm oil production and a significant impact on the industry [5]. 
To ensure sustainable profitability in the palm oil industry, the har­
vesting process must be mechanised to increase productivity [6]. 
Implementing an automated grading system using machine-vision 
technology would greatly enhance the sorting process, improve effi­
ciency, and provide standardised results [7].
The FFB grading requires the observation of external bunch’s colour 
as per Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) guideline. The FFB ripeness 
could be categorised into four groups: unripe, underripe, ripe, and 
overripe [3]. The purplish-black indicating unripe bunches and

2. Related works
With the increasing outdoor FFB grading research, there are gaps 
that should be investigated. Recent applications have predominantly 
relied on deep learning methods, which typically demand a substantial 
amount of training data to achieve higher levels of system accuracy. 
Robi et al. [15] took the initiative to classify FFB on the tree with various 
outdoor lighting using YOLOv4 with 77.20 % mAP while Lai et al. [17] 
achieved 87.9 % mAP with YOLOv4 classification. Most of the latest 
implementations of FFB grading use the YOLO algorithm, however, the 
accuracy is still limited and improvements should be pursued. Junos 
et al. [16] developed a system that achieved peak performance with an 
mAP of 98.91 % after training on 5000 images. However, their system 
was tested solely on three classes (ripe, underripe, and overripe).
While the deep learning method has been preferred for the recent 
application, the data limitation is also a concern for an outdoor dataset. 
Conversely, handcrafted feature extraction techniques with appropriate 
segmentation methods have demonstrated good performance under 
outdoor lighting conditions, even with limited training data, as 
demonstrated in studies by Septiarini et al. [9]. In Table 1, the com­
parison of recent implementation [21] has been outlined, where the 
deep learning method dominated most of recent techniques, while less 
amount of dataset reduce the performance of the deep learning system.
Another challenge in FFB classification stems from the limitation of 
the training dataset. The lack of outdoor datasets with consistent out­
door conditions poses difficulties in obtaining data, which could reflect 
the variations in sunlight illumination caused by shadows and cloud 
movements. This led to few standardised dataset, which makes it chal­
lenging to test data across different systems. Due to those challenges, the 
semi-handcrafted FFB grading techniques were proposed in this research 
to improve the grading system using limited dataset.
Salient object detection is a rising trend in image segmentation for 
outdoor scenes [27] to identify the most prominent object within an 
image [28]. The Fast Fuzzy C-means (FFCM) represents an advancement 
of the FCM algorithm, focusing on the histogram of the grey image 
rather than individual image pixels [29]. FFCM has demonstrated its 
superiority by offering fast, computationally efficient segmentation re­
sults [29,30]. It also been experimented by Siddesha et al. [31] for FFB 
segmentation where FCM shows better performance than K-means 
clustering despite some over-segmentation areas. However, the study 
did not specify the colour space utilised in the clustering process.
Other than that, the texture features could outperform colour fea­
tures in the changing illumination condition [32], but the imple­
mentation on FFB images shows that texture features did not make a 
huge difference in outdoor FFB images [33,34]. This highlights the need 
to explore the application of texture features in outdoor FFB classifica­
tion for different classes of fruit such as damaged and empty FFB. There 
is also a gap in the implementation of a hybrid approach combining 
colour and texture, as most scholars have focused solely on grayscale 
texture analysis [22]. The hybrid technique of colour and texture fea­
tures has remarkable reliability in addressing some of these outdoor 
image challenges as suggested by Humeau-Heurtier et al. [35]. Exam­
ples of such hybrid features include Opposite Colour Local Binary 
Pattern (OCLBP) [36] and Colour Co-occurrence Matrix (CCM) [37], 
which could blend colour and texture descriptors to provide enhanced 
classification capabilities.
In this study, the objective is to develop a system to classify outdoor 
FFB images according to five classes using machine learning and engi­
neered techniques, which consist of the two main processes: segmen­
tation and features extraction. The improved superpixel-based FCM 
segmentation technique was proposed to obtain the salient fruit image 
with less computational time. Other than that, the hybrid of colour- 
texture technique using OCLBP features also being explored to cater 
the illumination variations in outdoor FFB images.

affects the white balance in the image, with warm-toned images 
potentially leading to the misclassification of fruits as overripe bunches.
4. Methodology
A series of experiments were conducted to successfully differentiate 
at least three classes of FFB ripeness: unripe, ripe and overripe and two 
FFB quality: damaged and empty. The handcrafted segmentation process 
was implemented where the FFB were localised from a complex back­
ground, then the remaining background were removed to produce a 
salient FFB image. The texture-based image features was applied in 
additional to colour features to analyse the empty sockets of the FFB 
before being trained for the deep learning classifier. There are training 
and detection stage where training stage involves the data augmentation 
and feature selection process. The training and detection process were 
described in Fig. 2 and the experiment were conducted using MATLAB 
R2023b with a specification of Intel Core i5–1135G7 2.40 GHz and 
8.00GB RAM.
4.1. Segmentation
Two stages of segmentation were performed in this research to 
obtain a salient image of FFB without losing empty socket characteristics 
of the fruit. Firstly, the FFB images were localised using YOLOv4-Tiny to 
identify the fruit bunches’ locations and eliminate most of the tree and 
ground pixels. Then, the localised images were cropped and segmented 
using the salient segmentation technique which is superpixel-based Fast 
Fuzzy C-means (FFCM).
4.1.1. FFB localisation
The first step for the image localisation process involves manually 
annotating the images using the ImageLabeler function in MATLAB 
R2023b. Bounding boxes labelled as ‘FFB’ were drawn around the fruit 
bunches, regardless of their ripeness class, including their spikes and 
stalks. Loose fruits were excluded from the annotation area. Fig. 3 il­
lustrates the image annotation process.
Following that, 371 training images were augmented and resized to 
640 × 640 for YOLOv4-Tiny training. It was performed by adjusting 
Hue-Saturation-Value (HSV) components and contrast of the image 
using sigmoid function [40] before being trained in YOLOv4-Tiny sys­
tem. The images were randomly reflected and translated besides being 
added with synthetic shadow [41] and rain masks [42] to mimic the 
outdoor conditions. These augmentation process were applied not only 
to the images but also to the corresponding bounding boxes, ensuring 
consistent transformations across both elements. This resulted in six-fold 
of the training data as shown in Fig. 4.
In this study, the YOLOv4-tiny algorithm [43] was employed for the 
purpose of FFB localisation due to its lightweight system for faster 
processing speed. It represents lightweight version of YOLOv4, charac­
terised by a reduced number of anchor boxes compared to its counter­
part. The YOLOv4-tiny’s training is based on 29 convolutional layers 
(Fig. 5) with the activation function known as Leaky-ReLu (Rectified 
Linear Unit) and 2 × 2 Max Pooling to down-sample the feature maps 
while preserving the important information. Furthermore, the 
YOLOv4-tiny system works effectively with augmented datasets that 
simulate real-world conditions, such as lighting variations and occlu­
sions, enhancing its ability to generalize to diverse scenarios [44]. The 
system was trained using the hyperparameter value as stated in Table 3. 
As this system was trained using a tiny version of YOLOv4, the mini 
batch size of 16 was set and 30 epoch was determined by trial and error 
to achieve lower training loss.
4.1.2. Salient segmentation
The cropped image from FFB localisation process underwent an 
image enhancement using White Patch Retinex technique to improve 
the white balance before the next process. It assumes that 3 % of the 
brightest R, G and B pixels represents the light source [45]. Then, the 
superpixel operation was accomplished by employing simple linear 
iterative clustering (SLIC) [46] to group pixels based on their similar 
intensity values. This was performed to reduce the computational size of 
the clustering algorithm.
Following that, the FFCM algorithm [47] was employed to partition 
the image components based on the colour channels. Prior to clustering, 
the images were converted to the CIELAB, HSV, and YCbCr colour 
spaces. The clustering process was performed by randomly assigning the 
cluster centroids and updating the membership values for each pixel 
based on the distances to the cluster centres and the fuzziness parameter.

4.2. Feature extraction
Both colour and texture features of the segmented images were 
extracted during this stage. The extraction of colour moment was 
extracted together with the opponent colour local binary pattern 
(OCLBP). The first to third moments were calculated and 12 colour 
channels could be analysed from four colour spaces (RGB, HSV, CIELAB 
and YCbCr), therefore, 36 colour features could be extracted from an 
image. The additional of 14 features from combined channels were also 
computed.
The OCLBP [36] was applied based on the LBP technique [49], as 
shown in Eqs. (3) and (4). It was performed by calculating the possible 
combination of the three channels and compares the Ic with its neigh­
bours from other colour channel. For each colour channel, the calcula­
tion of channel permutations was carried out. The CIELAB colour space 
was chosen for OCLBP extraction due to the a∗channel that incorporates 
a spectrum of FFB ripeness colours. The OCLBP extraction aids in 
improving the identification of overripe and empty fruits by estimating 
the presence of empty sockets within an FFB.

In this phase, a total of 56 colour and texture features were extracted. 
A feature ranking approach utilising the Chi-Square (χ2) assessment was 
employed to determine the degree of dependency between the feature 
and categorical variables. It was proven as a reliable method in text 
detection [50] and facial recognition [51]. An evaluation was performed 
by considering an array of features alongside their respective FFB class 
labels. Through an analysis of feature scores, the top 27 features were 
identified for selection, effectively enhancing computational efficiency.
4.3. Classification
The features were classified using multilayer perceptron (MLP) 
neural network. Within the hidden layers, the Rectified Linear Unit 
(ReLU) activation function was applied following batch normalisation, 
while the Softmax function was used in the output layer to calculate the 
probability of the possible outcome. The choice of employing a basic 
MLP classifier stems from its suitability for evaluating the proposed pre- 
processing methodology. Additionally, MLP is a well-established and 
dependable classifier, with prior successful applications in outdoor FFB 
classification research [52]. The architecture of the network encom­
passes an input layer, an output layer, and hidden layers. Within the 
hidden layers, the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function is 
applied following batch normalisation, while the Softmax function is 
used in the output layer to calculate the probability of the possible 
outcome.

For a comprehensive evaluation of the system, the classification re­

5. Result and discussion
The performance evaluation was conducted at each stage of the 
process, including localisation, segmentation, feature extraction, and 
classification, as previously outlined. The evaluation parameters were 
used to evaluate the proposed techniques and the application was made 
based on suitability to each stage of methodology.
5.1. FFB localisation
These results were compared with those obtained through with no 
data augmentation. The result in Table 4 shows that the FFB localisation 
system could successfully locate most of the FFB images with >99 % 
precision. Despite there was a drop of recall percentage in the proposed 
system, the IoU and mAP gave good results shows that there is less error 
being made by the system. The low recall value indicates that it detect 
less true positive which is suitable for removing background, but need 
some improvements.
In Fig. 7, the ground truths for five of the training images are 
depicted by the blue boxes, both for images taken on the ground and 
those captured on the tree. Additionally, the yellow boxes represent the 
localisation results acquired. It is noticeable that a majority of the yellow 
boxes are contained within the blue boxes, signifying the high precision 
achieved. Nevertheless, there were instances where the edges of unripe 
fruits were not accurately localised and some ground elements were 
misclassified as overripe spikes.
5.2. Salient FFB segmentation
The efficacy of the superpixel and FFCM segmentation techniques 
was evaluated to determine their ability to produce salient images. 
Cropped images from previous stage were input into the superpixel 
generation, colour enhancement, FFCM clustering and morphological 
operations, aimed at achieving a salient image segmentation.
Table 5 presents the result for image segmentation for damaged (D), 
empty (E), overripe (O), ripe (R) and unripe (U) classes. The segmen­
tation process accurately identifies an average of 98 % of the pixels that 
belong to the damaged and empty FFB based on the precision value. It 
also demonstrates a high level of reliability in recognising the actual 
background, achieving an average specificity rate of 97 % for those 
classes. It could be concluded that the segmentation method works 
across all types of FFB classes with least accuracy in unripe classes. This 
indicates that the proposed approach effectively removes unwanted 
background elements from the FFB images of the damaged, empty, ripe 
and overripe bunches.
The moderate recall and F1-score values show that the segmentation 
process is performing reasonably well but has not achieved a high level 
of accuracy or recall. The relatively low IoU value suggests that the 
segmented areas overlap with the ground truth to a limited extent. This

5.3. Feature extraction
27 features were selected based on importance score ranking, 
comprising 25 colour features and 2 texture features. It could be 
observed that L-a∗and a∗-b∗OCLBP features have higher importance 
score than other texture features.
5.4. Classification
The entire system underwent training employing the MLP architec­
ture, resulting in an accuracy of 93.68 %. The system’s performance was 
also being compared to the technique that discards the superpixel-based 
FFCM segmentation. Notably, the accuracy dropped to 77.89 %, along 
with a decline in other key parameters. This reduction can be attributed 
to the persistence of background noise in the images, even though object 
localisation was performed. Another performance comparison was 
conducted by excluding texture features and utilising only colour fea­
tures. The results show a 3 % reduction in accuracy, particularly in the 
classification of damaged, overripe, and unripe FFB. This reduction 
underscores the significance of texture features, especially since over­
ripe bunches share similar colours with empty bunches, and damaged 
bunches exhibit colour similarities with unripe ones, as determined 
through our data analysis. The proposed system exhibits higher preci­
sion and specificity when compared to using colour features alone. This 
suggests that the system is effective in minimising false positives, 
aligning with its primary goal of reducing misclassifications and asso­
ciated losses in FFB classification. The result for ablation study was 
shown in Table 6.
This study aimed to tackle two main issue: First, the challenge of 
segmenting outdoor images. Typically, scholars have applied manual 
cropping [52] and masking [53] to identify fruit locations, ensuring 
proper clustering. By using YOLOv4-Tiny for automatic fruit local­
isation, this study reduced the need for manual processing and aimed to 
develop a real-time system for future applications. In addition, this study 
achieved salient image of the FFB from the analysis of the colour spaces 
in order to preserve the shape and texture of the fruit for further anal­
ysis. Second, this study pursue to employ texture analysis and compare 
the classification performance of the system with and without texture 
features. Due to the limited research on utilising texture features for 
outdoor FFB classification, where most previous studies conducted 
texture analysis in controlled environments rather than in real-world 
outdoor settings, [7,54–56] this study provides a valuable comparison. 
The results shown in Table 6 indicate that incorporating texture fea­
tures, alongside colour features, which are major contributors to feature 
analysis, significantly improved accuracy.
Furthermore, a comparative analysis between the proposed system 
and existing works that share a similar dataset was conducted. These 
studies were also performed outdoors, allowing for a reliable evaluation 
of the proposed system’s performance under varying outdoor lighting 
conditions. Ghazalli et al. [34] captured FFB images using a smartphone 
camera and utilised K-means clustering for image segmentation. For 
feature extraction, they applied the bag-of-visual-words approach in 
addition to colour features, and the classification was performed using 
an SVM classifier. The experiment conducted by Ghazalli et al. using 
only colour features achieved an accuracy of 57 %, highlighting the 
importance of preprocessing steps in improving system performance. In 
contrast, the proposed system, when tested with colour features alone, 
achieved an accuracy of over 90 %, as shown in Table 6, demonstrating 
that the inclusion of additional preprocessing techniques significantly 
enhances classification accuracy.
Next, the proposed system’s performance was benchmarked against 
direct classification using deep learning, as seen in the work by Khamis 
et al. [12], which implemented the YOLOv3 algorithm for FFB classifi­
cation. The direct classification using direct YOLOv4-Tiny and CNN also 
being included into the comparison. The dataset used in the proposed 
study consisted of 466 images, the largest among the three studies. 
Ghazalli et al. analyzed 400 images, while Khamis et al. used only 299. 
The proposed method’s dataset also incorporated a wider range of FFB 
classes, including unripe, ripe, overripe, damaged, and empty FFB, 
making it more comprehensive compared to the other two studies, 
which primarily focused on ripeness levels.
The proposed method achieved the highest accuracy of 0.9368 
compared to 0.7600 by Khamis et al. and 0.7000 by Ghazalli et al. The 
increase in accuracy can be attributed to the combination of YOLOv4- 
Tiny for localisation, FFCM for segmentation and the use of advanced 
colour and texture features for classification, which provided a more 
robust systemIt has been demonstrated that YOLOv4-Tiny outperforms 
YOLOv3 in direct classification tasks, with a notable accuracy 
improvement of 6.1 %. This indicates that YOLOv4-Tiny’s architecture is 
more efficient and effective for this specific application, confirming its 
superiority over the previous version [57]. Table 7 summarised these 
comparisons, demonstrating that the proposed system achieved the 
highest performance when compared to other systems.

6. Conclusion
This study aims to enhance palm oil production in Malaysia and 
minimise losses caused by harvesting unripe fruit. This aligns with the 
government’s efforts to promote sustainability in the palm oil industry 
through the Malaysian Sustainable Palm Oil (MSPO) certification 
scheme [58]. The accurate identification of fruit ripeness in the plan­
tation can reduce storage and sorting time, thereby maintaining low 
Free Fatty Acid (FFA) levels [59]. This, in turn, is expected to narrow the 
yield gap and enhance the sustainability of palm oil production. This 
work presents the FFB grading using custom image segmentation and a 
hybrid of colour and texture features. The object localisation using 
YOLOv4-Tiny 
and 
image 
segmentation 
technique 
using 
superpixel-based FFCM have been applied to automatically segment the 
image into a salient image with no or less background, while preserving 
most of the texture features. Then, the colour moment and OCLBP fea­
tures were extracted and selected based on its suitability for images with 
different challenges of outdoor conditions. To validate the efficacy of the 
proposed system, the training and testing was conducted using MLP 
architecture where the average accuracy of 93.68 % was achieved. The 
limitation of this framework is lack of on-tree images for damaged and 
overripe classes. Therefore, further works should be done by expanding 
the number of damaged, empty, and overripe samples, ensuring that the 
classes are evenly represented. The acquisition process also should be 
timed just before the harvesting stage to capture a higher number of 
overripe and damaged FFB still on the tree. Other than that, imple­
menting a real-time system into a smartphone application will facilitate 
immediate data collection and analysis. To further assess the effective­
ness of the developed model, perform k-fold cross-validation, which will 
provide an estimate of its performance on new, unseen data.
