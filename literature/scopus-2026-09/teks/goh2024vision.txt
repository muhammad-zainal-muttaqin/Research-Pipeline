Abstract— The accurate classification of fresh fruit bunch 
ripeness is crucial for optimizing oil quality and yield in the 
palm oil industry. Traditional manual inspection methods are 
labor-intensive, subjective, and prone to errors, motivating the 
exploration of automated solutions. This paper examined the 
potential of vision language models, including LLaVA 1.5, Yi-
VL, and PaliGemma, to automate and enhance FFB ripeness 
assessment. The models were evaluated on their ability to 
classify ripeness stages and the accuracy of generated 
descriptive text using metrics like BLEU and ROUGE scores. 
Yi-VL achieved the highest descriptive accuracy with a 
ROUGE-L score of 93.14. However, it processes 0.18 samples 
per 
second, which is slower than PaliGemma (0.53 
samples/second). PaliGemma is 194.44% more efficient in 
samples/second than Yi-VL, making it better suited for real-
time applications despite its lower accuracy (ROUGE-L: 26.15). 
LLaVA 1.5 offers a balance between accuracy (ROUGE-L: 
82.16) and efficiency (0.22 samples/second). This research 
highlighted the trade-offs between different VLMs for FFB 
ripeness 
assessment, 
demonstrating 
their 
potential 
to 
revolutionize the agriculture industry. Future work may focus 
on optimizing model performance and deploying these 
technologies in real-world scenarios.

I. INTRODUCTION 
The classification of oil palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) 
ripeness is a pivotal process within the palm oil industry. This 
process is known to have significant implications for both the 
quality and yield of the extracted oil. Traditionally, this 
assessment has been heavily reliant on manual inspection 
methods, which are not only labor-intensive but also prone to 
inconsistencies due to their subjective nature. The inherent 
challenges in accurately determining FFB ripeness using 
manual inspection often lead to suboptimal harvesting 
schedules and potential losses due to premature or overripe 
bunches [1]. To overcome these challenges, improvement in 
the efficiency and reliability of FFB ripeness assessment are 
relatively crucial. An automated solutions that leverage 
computer vision and deep learning have been increasingly 
turned to by the research community. Recent advancements in 
Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly Vision 
Language Models (VLMs) designed to process and interpret 
visual data, have opened up new avenues for FFB analysis. 
These models, trained on vast datasets of images and text, 
have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding 
visual content and generating human-like text descriptions. 
VLMs, as a subset of LLMs, have been successfully applied 
in various visual questions answering (VQA) applications, 
demonstrating their potential for providing a deeper 
understanding of image content [2].

A. LLaVA 1.5 
LLaVA 1.5 represents a significant advancement in 
multimodal models, specifically designed to bridge the gap 
between visual and textual understanding. The architecture is 
designed to effectively process and integrate information from 
both image and text modalities as illustrated in Fig. 2. This 
enables a wide range of applications, including the 
classification and inference of FFB ripeness. A frozen 
Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training (CLIP) Vision 
Transformer with 14 layers (ViT-L/14) model is employed as 
the visual encoder, serving as the model's eyes to extract 
meaningful features from input images of FFBs [4]. These 
features capture essential visual information relevant to 
ripeness assessment, such as the color distribution of the fruits 
that indicate the ripeness of FFB. A pre-trained CLIP model 
is utilized due to its ability to learn rich representations that 
connect visual concepts (e.g., colors associated with ripeness) 
with textual descriptions (e.g., "ripe," "unripe," "overripe"). 
The ViT-L/14 variant is known for its ability to capture long-
range dependencies in images, which is crucial for 
understanding the spatial relationships between fruits within 
the bunch and their overall ripeness pattern. The visual 
encoder is frozen during training, preserving its ability to 
extract high-quality visual features while making the overall 
model more efficient to train. 
The LLM acts as the model's brain, responsible for 
processing text input, understanding language nuances, and 
generating coherent and contextually relevant text output 
pertaining to FFB ripeness. LLaVA 1.5 offers flexibility in 
choosing the LLM, with two options available: Vicuna-7B 
and Vicuna-13B [5]. Vicuna models are derived from Large 
Language Model Meta AI (LLaMA), a family of LLMs 
developed by Meta AI. The choice between 7B and 13B 
variants depends on the desired trade-off between model size 
and performance. Similar to the visual encoder, the LLM is 
also frozen during training, allowing LLaVA 1.5 to leverage 
the knowledge and capabilities acquired by the pre-trained 
LLM during its extensive language training on vast datasets, 
including descriptions of fruit ripeness and agricultural terms, 
while focusing the training process on adapting the model for 
multimodal tasks specifically to FFB analysis. The projection 
layer serves as a bridge between the visual and textual 
modalities, translating the complex visual features of FFBs 
extracted by the encoder into a format that the LLM can 
understand and process to generate ripeness classifications 
and descriptive inferences.  In LLaVA 1.5, the projection 
layer is implemented as a two-layer multi-layer perceptron 
(MLP). This MLP architecture allows for non-linear 
transformations of the visual features, enhancing the model's 
capacity to learn complex relationships between the visual 
appearance of FFBs and the corresponding textual 
descriptions of their ripeness stages. This is the only trainable 
component in LLaVA 1.5, and it is fine-tuned on a dataset of 
image-text pairs using instruction tuning.  
It acts as a bridge between the frozen visual encoder and 
the frozen LLM, transforming the visual features into a format 
compatible with the LLM's embedding space. This layer is 
implemented as a two-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP),

architectural foundation with LLaVA 1.5. Both models 
employ a frozen visual encoder based on CLIP ViT to extract 
visual features from input images, followed by a frozen LLM 
for processing textual information and generating outputs. A 
trainable projection layer connects these two components, 
aligning the visual and textual representations to facilitate 
multimodal understanding. However, Yi-VL distinguishes

C. PaliGemma 
PaliGemma [7], an open-source VLM developed by 
Google AI, presents a novel approach to bridging the gap 
between visual and textual understanding. Its architecture, 
while rooted in the foundational principles of VLMs, 
incorporates distinctive elements that enhance its efficiency, 
adaptability, and performance. It is able to perform tasks such 
as image captioning, VQA, and notably FFB ripeness 
assessment. At the heart of PaliGemma's visual processing 
lies Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training (SigLIP), 
a visual encoder that sets itself apart from other models like 
CLIP. While both models share the goal of learning 
representations that connect images and text, SigLIP adopts a 
different approach by employing a sigmoid loss function 
during training. Unlike CLIP's reliance on a softmax cross-
entropy 
loss, 
SigLIP's 
sigmoid 
loss 
function 
is 
computationally simpler. This translates to a more efficient 
model that requires less computational resources to process 
visual information, a crucial advantage for real-time 
applications like FFB ripeness assessment, where rapid 
analysis is often required.  
Despite its computational efficiency, SigLIP rivals CLIP 
in its ability to understand the relationship between images 
and text. This is achieved through contrastive learning, a 
training methodology that encourages the model to learn 
representations that bring visually similar images and their 
corresponding textual descriptions closer together in the 
embedding space. In the context of FFB ripeness assessment, 
this means that SigLIP can effectively identify and describe 
the subtle visual cues that indicate a fruit bunch's ripeness 
stage, such as color variations, texture changes, and the 
presence or absence of specific features. PaliGemma's text 
decoding capabilities are powered by Gemma, a lightweight 
decoder-only language model designed for efficient text 
generation. Unlike larger language models, which can be 
computationally 
expensive, 
Gemma's 
streamlined 
architecture allows it to generate text quickly and with 
minimal resource consumption. This efficiency is particularly 
valuable for real-time FFB ripeness assessment, where 
prompt feedback is essential for informed decision-making. 
Furthermore, Gemma is a highly adaptable language model, 
allowing for easy fine-tuning on specific tasks or domains.  
This adaptability is crucial for FFB ripeness assessment, 
as it enables the model to be tailored to the specific vocabulary 
and language patterns used in the palm oil industry. By fine-
tuning Gemma on a dataset of FFB images and descriptions, 
the model can learn to generate accurate and informative 
descriptions that are relevant to the specific context of FFB 
ripeness assessment. PaliGemma's ability to integrate visual 
and textual information stems from its use of a shared 
embedding space. Both SigLIP and Gemma are designed to 
project visual and textual information into this shared space. 
This shared representation allows the model to understand the 
relationship between images and text, enabling it to accurately 
connect the visual appearance of FFBs with the corresponding 
textual descriptions of their ripeness stages. 
III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY 
The flowchart shown in Fig. 3 outlines the process of 
assessing the ripeness of FFBs using fine-tuned language 
models. The fine-tuned LLaVA 1.5, Yi-VL, and PaliGemma 
models were evaluated on a dedicated test set of 100 FFB 
images captured manually from the orchard, evenly split 
between ripe and unripe samples. Each image was annotated 
with bounding boxes around the FFBs and corresponding 
captions describing their ripeness stages. This approach 
facilitated supervised learning, enabling the models to 
associate visual features with textual descriptions and learn to 
accurately classify and describe FFB ripeness. To 
accommodate limited data and computational resources, the 
models were fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation [8]. A 
parameter-efficient technique that significantly reduces the 
number of trainable parameters and memory requirements, 
making it feasible to fine-tune large models on a workstation 
with an RTX 3080 Ti GPU. The dataset followed an 8:1:1 split

BLEU and ROUGE are the score metrics used to evaluate the 
quality of text generated by models. BLEU calculates -gram  
overlap between the generated text and reference text, with a 
brevity penalty to account for short translations. Higher 
BLEU scores indicate greater similarity to the reference text. 
ROUGE encompasses ROUGE-N (N=1 or 2), measuring -
gram overlap, and ROUGE-L, measuring the LCS, with 
precision and recall used to calculate scores. Higher ROUGE 
scores, particularly ROUGE-L, suggest more informative and 
comprehensive generated text. A high ROUGE score, 
particularly ROUGE-L, suggested the model's ability to 
generate informative, comprehensive, and well-structured 
descriptions of FFBs. BLEU and ROUGE scores range from 
0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better performance. In 
the context of natural language generation, scores above 80 
are often considered excellent, demonstrating a high degree of 
similarity between the generated text and the reference text. 
Scores between 60 and 80 are generally considered good, 
while scores below 60 may indicate areas for improvement.

V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
In conclusion, this paper demonstrates the significant 
potential of VLMs for automating and enhancing FFB 
ripeness assessment in the palm oil industry. The comparative 
analysis of LLaVA 1.5, Yi-VL, and PaliGemma reveals a 
diverse landscape of strengths and weaknesses. Yi-VL, with 
its superior descriptive accuracy attributed to its enhanced 
visual encoder and specialized language model, emerges as 
the most promising model for generating comprehensive and 
nuanced descriptions of FFB ripeness. However, its 
computational demands underscore the need for further 
research into optimization techniques, such as model 
compression or quantization, to enhance its practical 
feasibility in real-world scenarios. PaliGemma's exceptional 
computational efficiency positions it as a compelling option 
for time-sensitive applications where rapid FFB ripeness 
assessment is critical. Nevertheless, its lower descriptive 
accuracy 
compared 
to 
Yi-VL 
necessitates 
further 
investigation into enhancing its descriptive capabilities while 
maintaining its speed advantage. LLaVA 1.5, offering a 
balanced compromise between accuracy and efficiency, 
presents a versatile tool for FFB ripeness assessment, 
particularly in scenarios where both aspects are valued. The 
findings of this research not only contribute to the 
advancement of VLM research but also pave the way for 
significant advancements in the palm oil industry. By 
adopting VLM-based FFB ripeness assessment tools, 
stakeholders can streamline harvesting operations, improve 
yield prediction accuracy, and enhance the overall 
sustainability and efficiency of palm oil production.

informed decisions, ultimately leading to a more sustainable
