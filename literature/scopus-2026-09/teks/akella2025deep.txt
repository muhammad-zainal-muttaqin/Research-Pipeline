Abstract—Accurate detection and classification of coconuts
according to their ripeness stages are essential for optimizing
agricultural harvesting practices, reducing manual labor, and
increasing
economic
efficiency.
The
ripeness
of
coconuts
significantly
influences
their
commercial
value,
nutritional
content,
and
suitability
for
various
products
such
as
coconut water, coconut milk, and oil extraction. However,
manual inspection for ripeness determination is labor-intensive,
subjective,
and
prone
to
inaccuracies.
To
address
these
challenges,
this
paper
proposes
a
deep
learning-based
methodology employing YOLOv11 integrated with the Kalman
Filter-Hungarian Algorithm, YOLOv8, and a custom-designed
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to accurately identify
coconuts at three distinct ripeness stages: immature, mid-ripe,
and fully ripe. An open-source coconut dataset was utilized
and enhanced through data augmentation techniques, including
rotation,
scaling,
and
color
adjustments,
to
improve
the
robustness and generalization capabilities of the models across
varying environmental and lighting conditions. The models were
trained and rigorously evaluated using standard object detection
metrics,
specifically
mean
Average
Precision
(mAP50
and
mAP50:95). Furthermore, the proposed approach was extended
to facilitate automated counting of coconuts using different
tracking methodologies, demonstrating practical applicability
and potential integration into real-time agricultural monitoring
systems. Experimental results illustrate that the proposed deep
learning models effectively differentiate coconut ripeness stages,
providing a significant advancement for precision agriculture and
automation in coconut farming.
Index Terms—Deep Learning, Object Detection, YOLOv8,
Kalman Filter, Coconut Ripeness Classification

Accurate

in

Traditional methods for estimating yield, which typically
rely on manual sampling and destructive measurements, are
labor-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to significant error.
In recent years, advances in computer vision (CV) have
enabled the development of non-destructive, real-time yield
estimation systems that offer greater accuracy [1]. Early
approaches to fruit yield estimation primarily used handcrafted
features—such as color, texture, and shape—to segment
fruit from the background. For example, color thresholding
techniques were applied to canopy images to detect apples
and grapes. However, these methods often struggled under
varying illumination conditions, limiting their practical use in
real-world environments [2].
The advent of deep learning has significantly transformed
the field of automated yield estimation. Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs) have shown remarkable ability to learn
discriminative features directly from raw images, overcoming
many of the limitations associated with traditional methods.
For
instance,
Bargoti
and
Underwood
[3]
successfully
employed CNN-based segmentation to achieve high-precision
fruit detection in orchards, while Gongal et al. [4] combined
stereo vision with deep learning to enhance apple crop-load
estimation. These studies demonstrate that robust feature
learning and multi-view imaging can substantially improve
detection accuracy, even under challenging conditions such as
occlusions and variable lighting [5].
Despite these advances, many existing systems remain
constrained
by
their
reliance
on
controlled
imaging
environments and often require extensive post-processing to
correct for issues like double-counting in sequential frames.
Additionally, most current methods focus on estimating fruit

II. RELATED WORK
Recent advances in fruit detection and maturity estimation
have increasingly shifted from traditional image processing
techniques to deep learning-based approaches. Early machine
vision
systems
relied
on
manually
engineered
features
such as color, shape, and texture for fruit segmentation
and classification, but were often limited by variations in
illumination and background complexity [2]. To overcome
these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to deep
learning methods.
YOLO-based models have been particularly influential in
advancing fruit ripeness detection. Xiao et al. [8] introduced
a YOLOv8-based framework for fruit ripeness identification,
demonstrating improved accuracy and robustness across
varying environmental conditions. Similarly, Yang et al. [5]
enhanced YOLOv8 by integrating it with a lightweight
Transformer module (LW-Swin Transformer), significantly
improving strawberry ripeness detection under occlusions and
partial visibility.
Lightweight adaptations of YOLO architectures have also
been proposed for specific agricultural applications. Chen et
al. [9] developed YOLOv8-CML, a streamlined model that
improves the detection of color-changing melon ripeness while
reducing computational complexity, making it suitable for
real-time agricultural monitoring. Likewise, Zhang et al. [10]
proposed an improved YOLOv8-based maturity recognition
algorithm for Xinhui citrus, incorporating multiscale feature
fusion to boost precision under complex field conditions.
Beyond vision-only approaches, Ouhda et al. [2] explored
combining YOLO detection with K-means clustering for
maturity analysis of date fruits, achieving higher accuracy
compared to purely vision-based methods. Additionally, Wang
et al. [7] utilized UAV-based imagery combined with deep
learning to detect litchi fruit maturity, addressing challenges
associated with wide-area agricultural monitoring.
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have also played a
major role in fruit classification. Han and Yi [11] proposed
a deep CNN model specifically for palm fruit maturity
classification, achieving strong generalization performance
even with smaller datasets. Similarly, Caladcad and Piedad
[11] and Sattar [12] explored acoustic signal-based deep
learning approaches for coconut maturity estimation, offering
non-visual methods that complement traditional imaging
techniques.
Ma
et
al.
[1]
provided
a
comprehensive
review
of
state-of-the-art deep learning techniques for fruit maturity
detection, outlining challenges such as dataset imbalance,
model overfitting, and real-time deployment issues. Additional
studies by Li et al. [6] and Khan et al. [13] have focused
on fruit yield mapping and palm fruit ripeness detection,
respectively, using deep learning models that integrate both
visual and spatial information.
Other recent innovations include Nguyen and Tran’s
[14] Fruit-Net system, a CNN-based multi-fruit recognition
framework, and Abdul Rahman et al.’s [15] intelligent
system for predicting orange fruit ripeness, combining image
processing and deep neural networks for better harvesting
decision support.
Building upon these advancements, our approach leverages
YOLOv8
architectures
combined
with
robust
tracking
algorithms like the Kalman filter and the Hungarian algorithm
to achieve accurate fruit detection, ripeness classification, and
yield estimation in dynamic agricultural environments.

A. Data Collection
The dataset utilized in this study was obtained from
an open-source repository, ensuring both accessibility and
reproducibility. It consists of high-resolution images of
coconuts captured in their natural environment, annotated with
bounding boxes indicating their location and ripeness stage
(Stage 1, Stage 2, and Stage 3). The annotations followed
standard formats compatible with object detection frameworks
such as YOLO [13].
B. Preprocessing and Augmentation
To enhance the flexibility and generalizability of the
models, comprehensive preprocessing and data augmentation
techniques were applied to the raw dataset. These steps are
critical for addressing challenges such as class imbalance,
variations in illumination, and environmental noise [16]. The
preprocessing steps included:
1) Data Cleaning: Low-quality or duplicate images, which
could negatively impact model training, were removed.
Additionally, annotation accuracy was carefully verified to
ensure consistency throughout the dataset [14].
2) Data Augmentation:
Augmentation techniques were
applied to the original images to expand the dataset and
introduce variability, thereby improving the model’s ability
to generalize to unseen scenarios. Figure 1(a) illustrates the
original image and various augmented samples. The following
augmentation techniques were employed: Images were rotated
at random angles to simulate different viewpoints as shown
in Fig. 1(e). Horizontal and vertical flips were performed to
account for orientation changes as shown in Fig. 1(f). Blurring
was applied to a subset of images to simulate sensor noise and
varying focus conditions as shown in Fig. 1(c). In Fig. 1(b)
and Fig. 1(d), random noise was added to simulate real-world
sensor imperfections, and four different images were combined
into a single image to expose the model to varied object
sizes and contexts. Random regions of images were masked to
encourage the model to learn from incomplete object patterns
[10].

IV. TRAINING METHODS
In this section, the training methodologies employed
for object detection and ripeness classification of coconuts
are presented. Three distinct models were developed and
evaluated: YOLOv11 integrated with a Kalman filter and
the Hungarian algorithm, a custom-designed Convolutional
Neural Network (CNN), and YOLOv8 utilizing transfer
learning. Each model was trained with tailored configurations
concerning optimization strategies, learning schedules, loss
functions, and regularization techniques to maximize detection
accuracy and generalization performance. The following
subsections detail the specific training setups, hyperparameter
choices, and enhancements incorporated for each model.

B. YOLOv8
The YOLOv8 model, recognized for its efficiency in
object detection tasks, was trained using transfer learning
from pre-trained weights on the COCO dataset. Training
employed the Adam optimizer alongside a dynamic learning
rate scheduler to adjust the learning rate in response to
validation performance. A batch size of 16 images per batch
was selected to maintain consistent gradient updates. The
model was trained for 25 epochs, employing both standard
and advanced data augmentation techniques, including mosaic
and mixup, to enhance generalization. For bounding box
regression, the Complete Intersection over Union (CIoU) loss
was utilized, while focal loss was adopted for classification
tasks. The YOLOv8 model exhibited rapid convergence and
demonstrated robust performance across all ripeness stages of
coconuts. Throughout the training process, all models were
systematically monitored, and the best-performing checkpoints
were selected based on validation metrics for subsequent
evaluation.
C. YOLOv11 with Kalman Filter and Hungarian Algorithm
The YOLOv11 model, integrated with a Kalman filter
and
the
Hungarian
algorithm,
was
trained
to
enhance
object detection accuracy and tracking stability. The training
configuration employed Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)
as the optimizer, with an initial learning rate of 0.001
and a momentum coefficient of 0.9. A batch size of
16 images per batch was utilized to balance memory
consumption and training stability. The model was trained
for 100 epochs, with checkpoints implemented to preserve
the best-performing weights based on validation loss. A
composite loss function comprising localization, confidence,
and
classification
components
was
adopted,
specifically
optimized for the detection of coconuts at various stages of
ripeness. The incorporation of the Kalman filter contributed
to improved temporal consistency in tracking, while the
Hungarian
algorithm
facilitated
effective
association
of
detections across frames [17].

of detections in each frame is summed to estimate the
number of coconuts present as shown in Fig. 4. Although
computationally efficient, this method can overestimate counts
when the same object appears in multiple consecutive frames.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this study, a deep learning-based framework for coconut
ripeness classification and yield estimation was proposed,
leveraging the strengths of YOLOv11 with Kalman Filtering
and
the
Hungarian
Algorithm,
a
custom
Convolutional
Neural Network (CNN), and YOLOv8. By systematically
applying
data
augmentation
techniques
and
optimizing
model
architectures,
the
proposed
models
demonstrated
robust performance under varying environmental conditions.
Among the evaluated models, YOLOv8 achieved the highest
accuracy and generalization capabilities, making it the most
suitable candidate for real-world deployment. Furthermore,
the integration of tracking algorithms significantly improved
counting accuracy, reducing duplication errors across frames.
This work provides a practical solution for automating
coconut harvesting and yield estimation, which can enhance
efficiency in agricultural operations. Future work will focus on
expanding the dataset, incorporating geospatial mapping for
yield distribution analysis, and exploring lightweight models
for real-time deployment on mobile and edge devices.
