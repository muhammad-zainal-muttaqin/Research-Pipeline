Accurate fruit detection in cluttered orchard environments remains challenging due to visually similar back-
ground elements such as foliage and specular highlights, which lead to high false positive rates in RGB-based de-
tectors. In addition, conventional RGB-D fusion methods often degrade under real-world depth noise and domain
shifts. To address these limitations, this study introduces GeoSphere-DETR, a geometry-aware extension of RT-
DETR that leverages robust depth-derived cues for reliable fruit detection. A real-world RGB-D citrus dataset
comprising 2200 images and 102,719 annotated instances was collected across multiple orchards and acquisition
days, with a strict cross-ﬁeld and cross-day evaluation protocol to assess generalization. An efﬁcient annotation
pipeline combining a foundation model (Grounding DINO) with human veriﬁcation was developed to reduce la-
beling effort while maintaining annotation quality. GeoSphere-DETR integrates two key components. First, a
neck-level geometry-gated fusion module incorporates curvature-based geometric cues (bumpiness and
Laplacian-based convexity) into multi-scale visual features via learnable spatial gates, enabling selective use of
reliable depth information. Second, a query-level geometry-aware recalibration head re-scores detection conﬁ-
dence based on geometry-informed conﬁdence adjustment, suppressing geometrically inconsistent false posi-
tives. Experimental results on the held-out test set show that GeoSphere-DETR achieves a mAP50 of 0.863 and
3.286 FPPI, outperforming RGB-only RT-DETR (0.786 mAP50, 7.240 FPPI) and YOLOv11m (0.715 mAP50, 8.735
FPPI), corresponding to a 9.8% improvement in mAP50 and a 54.6% reduction in FPPI over RT-DETR. Ablation
studies conﬁrm the effectiveness of curvature-based cues and gated multimodal fusion. Overall, this work intro-
duces a dual-level geometry-aware detection framework that combines feature-level reliability modeling with
object-level shape consistency, providing a principled approach for reducing false positives in RGB-D agricultural
perception systems and building a perception foundation for robotic harvesting, yield estimation, and precision
orchard management. The dataset and code are publicly available at citrus-fruit-rgbd (https://huggingface.co/
datasets/ariel0818/citrus-fruit-rgbd) and GeoSphere-DETR (https://github.com/Ariel0818/Geosphere-DETR),
respectively.

1. Introduction
Citrus is one of the most widely cultivated fruit crops globally,
with production exceeding 150 million tons annually and signiﬁcant
economic
importance
across
subtropical
and
tropical
regions
(Mukhametzyanov et al., 2024). Accurate and timely fruit detection in
orchards is a foundational capability for a broad range of precision agri-
culture applications, including yield estimation (Vijayakumar et al.,
2023), precision spraying (Partel et al., 2021), and robotic harvesting
(Abeyrathna et al., 2023). Reliable fruit localization at the instance
level is a prerequisite for downstream yield prediction pipelines,
where per-image fruit counts serve as inputs to forecasting models
that support harvest planning and resource allocation (Dorj et al.,
2017). For growers, improved fruit detection can reduce manual scout-
ing effort and support more efﬁcient orchard operations. Despite its
practical importance, robust fruit detection in outdoor orchard environ-
ments remains an open problem due to the visual complexity of
cluttered canopy scenes (Tang et al., 2023).
In citrus orchards, fruits frequently share similar colour and texture
characteristics with surrounding foliage, and specular highlights on
leaves can produce appearance cues that closely mimic fruit surfaces.
Combined with frequent occlusions and strong illumination variations
across different times of day and ﬁeld conditions, these factors cause
RGB-based detectors to generate excessive fruit-like false positives,

which is a challenge that is particularly pronounced when fruits are
small or partially occluded. Recent advances in deep learning-based ob-
ject detection, including YOLO-style models (Redmon et al., 2016),
R-CNN variants (Girshick et al., 2014), and transformer-based detectors
such as DETR (Carion et al., 2020) and RT-DETR (Zhao et al., 2024), have
demonstrated strong performance on general-purpose benchmarks.
However, most existing detectors are RGB-only, and primarily rely on
appearance cues, which can be insufﬁcient in orchards where fruits
are easily confused with background clutter such as specular leaf high-
lights and branch textures (Xiao et al., 2023). Moreover, they mainly op-
timize standard accuracy metrics such as mAP, while explicit analysis
and suppression of false-positive behavior under cluttered foliage and
specular artifacts remain limited (Singh et al., 2021).

RGB-D sensing, which combines appearance and depth information,
offers a natural avenue for addressing this limitation, as geometric cues
can disambiguate fruit-like distractors that are visually plausible but
geometrically inconsistent with a spherical fruit shape (Lin et al.,
2020). RGB-D and multi-modal fusion approaches have been explored
in agricultural perception for tasks such as tree height estimation
(Zhou et al., 2025), canopy reconstruction (Zheng et al., 2026), and
row-level mapping (Su et al., 2024). However, naive depth fusion strat-
egies, such as early channel concatenation of raw depth with RGB, are
fragile in practice: stereo depth in outdoor orchard scenes frequently
contains missing values (Riegler et al., 2017), boundary noise
(Petrović and Xydeas, 2003), and misalignment artifacts (Einecke
et al., 2014), and performance degrades substantially under cross-ﬁeld
and cross-day domain shifts (Cao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2023).
Moreover, existing fusion approaches rarely integrate object-level geo-
metric consistency explicitly, leaving an important gap in ﬁne-grained
false positive suppression at the detection query level.
A further practical challenge in developing orchard fruit detectors is
the high cost of instance-level annotation. Fruits in cluttered canopy
images are small, occluded, and visually ambiguous, making manual
labeling labor-intensive and prone to inconsistency. Foundation
model-based detectors, such as Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2024),
have recently demonstrated strong zero-shot object proposal capabili-
ties across diverse visual domains, offering a promising mechanism for
bootstrapping agricultural annotations at reduced cost while retaining
human veriﬁcation for quality control.
This study introduces a geometry-aware detection framework,
GeoSphere-DETR, to address two key challenges in orchard fruit detec-
tion: the susceptibility of RGB-based detectors to fruit-like false posi-
tives, and the fragility of existing RGB-D fusion strategies under
real-world depth noise and domain shifts. Speciﬁcally, GeoSphere-
DETR is developed as an extension of RT-DETR tailored to RGB-D citrus
fruit detection in cluttered orchard environments. The contributions of
this study are fourfold and are positioned relative to current gaps in lit-
erature. First, a neck-level geometry-gated fusion module is proposed to
enable selective integration of depth-derived curvature cues into multi-
scale visual features. Unlike conventional early or attention-based
fusion approaches (Sun et al., 2022) that treat depth as uniformly reli-
able, the proposed gating mechanism explicitly models spatial reliabil-
ity, allowing robust use of geometry under missing or noisy depth
conditions. Second, a geometry-aware recalibration head is introduced
to enforce object-level conﬁdence adjustment. In contrast to existing
multi-modal detectors that rely solely on feature-level fusion, this mod-
ule operates at the detection query level and recalibrates conﬁdence
scores based on geometric context encoded in the ﬁnal decoder embed-
dings, providing an additional mechanism for suppressing false posi-
tives arising from foliage clutter and specular artifacts. Third, a
foundation model-assisted annotation pipeline is developed, leveraging
Grounding DINO for zero-shot proposal generation followed by human
veriﬁcation, substantially reducing labeling cost while maintaining
high-quality instance-level labels in complex orchard scenes. Finally, a
real-world RGB-D citrus dataset comprising 2200 annotated images
and over 100,000 instances is constructed, with a strict cross-ﬁeld and
cross-day evaluation protocol designed to assess generalization under
realistic deployment conditions. Extensive ablation and robustness ex-
periments are conducted to systematically analyze the role of geometry
in improving detection reliability.
Compared to existing RGB-D fruit detection methods, the proposed
approach advances the state of the art by jointly addressing geometry
reliability at the feature level and shape consistency at the object level,
rather than relying on direct depth fusion alone. This dual-level design
provides a principled framework for reducing false positives in unstruc-
tured agricultural environments.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Dataset and annotation pipeline
2.1.1. Data collection
As shown in Fig. 1, RGB-D data were collected at two commercial cit-
rus orchards (Fields A & B) in Florida using the Agrosense v2 mobile
sensing platform (Liu et al., 2026). The Agrosense v2 sensing system
was mounted on a truck traveling at approximately 8 km/h between
tree rows. Agrosense v2 includes two ZED X stereo cameras (StereoLabs
Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) with a 2.2 mm lens, mounted at a height of
1.7 m above the ground and oriented laterally toward the tree canopy.
At a typical stand-off distance of 2–4 m, the camera ﬁeld of view spans
approximately 5.7–11.4 m in width and 3.4–6.8 m in height, allowing
the full tree canopy to be captured in a single frame. Synchronized
RGB and depth image pairs were acquired at 15 FPS at a resolution of
1920 × 1200 pixels using the SDK-provided calibration parameters. All
data were collected under natural illumination, encompassing both
sunny and cloudy conditions and a mixture of front-lit and back-lit sce-
narios to capture diverse lighting variations. Stereo depth quality is in-
herently variable under these outdoor conditions, with missing values
and boundary noise near occlusions and leaf edges. This variability di-
rectly motivates the reliability-aware design of the proposed fusion
module.
Field A, with lower maintenance intensity (864 trees), served as the
source for training and validation data, collected on December 8, 2025.
Frames were sampled from 12 rows in total: the ﬁrst 10 rows were des-
ignated for training and the remaining 2 rows for validation, ensuring
that no row overlap exists between the two subsets. Field B, a better-
maintained commercial orchard (855 trees), contributed 2 rows to the
held-out test set, with data collected on November 19, 2025. Both ﬁelds
are planted with Hamlin orange (Citrus sinensis), an early-season variety
typically harvested from October through December in Florida, with an
in-row tree spacing of approximately 3.5–4.0 m and a row spacing of
approximately 7.0–7.5 m. The two orchards differ in canopy density
and management condition, and data were collected on different
dates during the active harvest window, capturing fruits across a
range of maturity stages from partially green to fully orange, enabling
a strict cross-ﬁeld, cross-day evaluation protocol that reﬂects realistic
deployment variability.
2.1.2. Frame sampling and annotation pipeline
From the continuous RGB-D video streams, frames were uniformly
sampled to reduce temporal redundancy while maintaining consistent
spatial coverage across rows. Fig. 2 illustrates the complete annotation
pipeline. For the train/validation ﬁeld, 150 frames per row were sam-
pled from the 10 training rows and 100 frames per row from the 2 val-
idation rows, yielding 1500 training and 200 validation images
respectively. For the test ﬁeld, 250 frames per row were sampled from
2 held-out rows of Field B, yielding 500 test images. Sampling was per-
formed prior to annotation, and train/validation/test assignment was
determined solely by row and ﬁeld identity, and no row appears in
more than one split. The dataset contains two annotation categories:
fruit-on-tree and fruit-on-ground, with a total of 102,719 annotated in-
stances across 2200 images, summarized in Table 1. The fruit-on-tree

in this benchmark is compounded by degraded depth quality at test
time.
Depth maps from the ZED X stereo camera are spatially aligned with
the RGB frames using the SDK-provided intrinsic and extrinsic calibra-
tion. From each aligned depth map, two compact geometry cues: sur-
face bumpiness and Laplacian-based convexity, are derived on the
same image grid to construct the two-channel geometry maps used
by the proposed geometry branch, detailed in Section 2.3. The full
dataset is publicly available at citrus-fruit-rgbd.
2.2. Overall framework
The proposed framework addresses false positive suppression at
two complementary levels: a neck-level geometry-gated fusion mod-
ule that selectively incorporates depth-derived curvature cues into
multi-scale features, and a query-level geometry-aware recalibration
head that enforces object-level conﬁdence adjustment at inference
time. Together, these modules provide a dual-level defense against
fruit-like distractors arising from foliage clutter and specular artifacts.
RT-DETR builds on the DETR framework by adopting an end-to-end
query-based formulation with global attention, allowing each object
query to aggregate context from the entire image rather than relying
on dense local predictions, which is particularly suited to orchard
scenes with heavy clutter and occlusion (Wu et al., 2025; Zhang,
2025). Its real-time inference capability further makes it a practical
choice for ﬁeld deployment. However, existing RT-DETR-based fruit
detectors remain predominantly appearance-driven and rarely incor-
porate explicit geometric priors or false-positive suppression mecha-
nisms, leaving an important gap for geometry-aware detection in
cluttered orchard environments. RT-DETR is well suited to address
this gap: its query-based global attention mechanism is particularly ro-
bust to heavy clutter and occlusion, while its modular decoder

As shown in Fig. 3, GeoSphere-DETR is built on top of RT-DETR as the

feature fusion, transformer-based detection, and shape-consistency re-
scoring. The RGB image is processed by the RT-DETR backbone and
neck to extract multi-scale features. In parallel, geometry cues derived
from depth (e.g., surface bumpiness and Laplacian-based convexity
cues) are encoded by a lightweight geometry branch. Instead of early
concatenation, a neck-level geometry-gated fusion injects geometry
into intermediate feature maps via a learnable gating mechanism, en-
abling adaptive use of geometric information under noisy and missing
depth values.
The fused features are fed into the RT-DETR decoder to produce ob-
ject queries with classiﬁcation logits and bounding box predictions. A
query-level geometry-aware recalibration head predicts a scalar
shape-consistency score for each query using the ﬁnal decoder embed-
dings. During inference, we adjust classiﬁcation logits with the
predicted conﬁdence adjustment scores. This reﬁnes detection conﬁ-
dence without modifying box regression, suppressing geometrically in-
consistent false positives and improving precision, especially for small
and occluded fruits.

2.3. Geometry representation
Geometry cues are designed based on a key observation from the
dataset: citrus fruits exhibit a consistent spherical and convex shape,
while most background structures in orchard scenes, such as leaves,
branches, and soil, are locally ﬂat, planar, or elongated. Raw stereo
depth in outdoor orchard scenes is unsuitable as a direct geometry
input: it contains large-scale range variation correlated with back-
ground distance, missing values due to stereo failures, and boundary
noise near occlusions. To encode a compact shape prior toward local
convex structure, we instead derive two depth-based cues: surface
bumpiness and Laplacian-based convexity, which emphasize locally
protruding, spherical regions while suppressing planar and edge-like
background structures. Fig. 4 presents examples of the RGB image,
depth, bumpiness, and Laplacian-based convexity.

2.4. Neck-level geometry-gated fusion
The geometry-gated fusion module serves as an active depth quality
control mechanism. Rather than blindly injecting all available geometry
into the visual features, it learns to selectively amplify reliable depth sig-
nals and suppress contributions from noisy or missing regions on a per-
location, per-scale basis. Directly concatenating depth or geometry with
RGB at the input level is often unstable in orchard scenes, where stereo
depth contains noise, missing values, and local misalignment under oc-
clusions. Unlike early fusion approaches (Sun et al., 2022; Rong et al.,
2023) that concatenate depth with RGB at the input level, or additive fu-
sion that treats geometry as uniformly reliable, the proposed gating
mechanism learns where geometry is trustworthy on a per-location,
per-scale basis. To address this, we introduce a neck-level geometry-
gated fusion module that injects geometry into multi-scale features in
an adaptive manner, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

distractors, such as leaf highlights, specular reﬂections, and textured
background regions that are visually plausible but lack the spherical ge-

conﬁdence adjustment score for each detection query and uses it to
re-score classiﬁcation conﬁdence at inference time, thereby suppressing
detections that are visually plausible but geometrically inconsistent
with true fruit instances.

To systematically evaluate the contribution of each proposed com-

the fusion module while keeping the backbone, neck, and training con-

contribution of the fusion strategy itself and ensures a controlled, fair
comparison. Speciﬁcally, the two-channel geometry map G = [B, C] is

added to the neck feature maps via channel-wise attention weighting.

explicit spatial reliability modeling. This conﬁguration serves as a direct
comparison point for evaluating whether the proposed spatial gating
mechanism provides beneﬁts beyond standard attention-based fusion.

Introducing naive depth concatenation (E2) improves detection ac-
curacy over the RGB-only baseline (E1), conﬁrming that depth carries
complementary discriminative cues beyond appearance. However, the
reliability of this gain is limited. As demonstrated in Section 3.3, E2 is
the most sensitive to depth corruption among all fusion variants,
degrading rapidly under both dropout and Gaussian noise. This instabil-
ity is physically intuitive: in outdoor orchard scenes, stereo depth is in-
herently unreliable near leaf boundaries and occluded regions, precisely
the locations where fruit-like false positives tend to arise. Injecting raw
depth unconditionally into the shared representation means that
corrupted geometry propagates through the network unﬁltered,
degrading the features it was meant to improve. The neck-level
attention fusion baseline (E2b), inspired by NT-FFN (Sun et al., 2022),
demonstrates that attention-based feature recalibration provides
meaningful improvement over naive early fusion, reducing FPPI from
6.716 to 5.927 and FP rate from 0.205 to 0.197. However, the gap be-
tween E2b and E3 (FPPI 5.927 vs. 4.596) reveals that global channel at-
tention is insufﬁcient to address the spatial heterogeneity of outdoor
depth reliability. Attention-based fusion recalibrates which channels
to emphasize but does not model where in the spatial feature map the
geometry signal is trustworthy.
Geometry-Gated Fusion (E3) addresses this by learning to selec-
tively trust geometry on a per-location basis. Rather than treating
depth-derived cues as uniformly reliable, the gating mechanism sup-
presses geometry contributions where depth is noisy or inconsistent
and ampliﬁes them where local convex structure is conﬁdently de-
tected. This selective modulation translates into substantially stronger
FP suppression compared to E2, with FPPI dropping from 6.716 to
4.596 and FP rate falling from 0.205 to 0.161. The key insight is that in
cluttered orchard scenes, the value of geometry lies not in its average
signal but in its reliable signal, and gating is the mechanism that sepa-
rates the two. The Sphere-Consistency re-scoring (E4) provides a com-
plementary and distinct form of FP suppression. While E3 operates at
the feature level and ﬁlters regions where geometry is globally unreli-
able, it remains limited against false positives where the depth signal
is locally ambiguous. For example, partially occluded leaves whose
depth values fall within the range of nearby fruits. The geometry-
aware recalibration head addresses this residual failure mode by operat-
ing at the query level, re-scoring each detection based on whether its
ﬁnal decoder embedding is consistent with a spherical shape prior.
This late-stage calibration captures geometric plausibility that feature-
level gating cannot, explaining why E4 achieves further FPPI reduction
on top of E3 (3.286 vs. 4.596) despite sharing the same fused feature
representation. Together, E3 and E4 address FP suppression at two com-
plementary levels of the detection pipeline.
Per-class results reveal a clear asymmetry between the two detec-
tion categories.
On fruit-on-tree, the geometry-aware modules deliver the most pro-
nounced gains, with FPPI falling substantially from E1 to E4, reﬂecting
the strong alignment between the spherical shape prior and canopy
fruits embedded in leaf clutter. The fruit-on-ground class, however, re-
mains consistently more challenging across all methods. Three
compounding factors explain this gap. First, severe class imbalance in
9

the training set (Table 1) limits the model's ability to learn robust
ground-fruit representations. Second, fallen fruit shares visual and tex-
tural similarity with soil and leaf litter, making appearance-based dis-
crimination inherently harder. Third, and most critically for the
geometry modules, ground fruit frequently exhibits shape degradation
due to physical damage and desiccation. Deformed or desiccated fruit
no longer conforms to the spherical prior that both E3 and E4 are de-
signed to exploit. This means the geometry-based gains are structurally
limited for this class, and the smaller improvements on fruit-on-ground
reﬂect the boundary of the spherical shape assumption rather than a
shortcoming of the proposed method. Beyond false positive suppres-
sion, false negatives represent a complementary limitation. The domi-
nant sources of missed detections include heavily occluded fruits
where neither RGB appearance nor depth geometry provides sufﬁcient
signal, small or distant instances that fall below the effective resolution
of the geometry cues, and fruit-on-ground instances where shape defor-
mation reduces convexity responses. In addition, the gating mechanism
may suppress geometry contributions in stereo-unreliable regions such
as canopy boundaries, which can inadvertently increase false negatives
when some fruits are located precisely at such a boundary. These failure
modes are consistent with the per-class results in Table 5, where fruit-
on-ground consistently shows lower mAP50 and mAP50:95 across all
methods. Beyond class-level differences, the effectiveness of the spher-
ical prior is also inﬂuenced by instance-level factors that affect both cat-
egories. Partially occluded fruits present incomplete depth proﬁles to
the stereo camera, reducing the convexity response and weakening
the geometric signal available to the gating and recalibration modules.
Overlapping fruit clusters produce merged depth surfaces that deviate
from the single-sphere assumption, potentially causing the gating mod-
ule to underweight geometry in high-density canopy regions. These
instance-level failure modes are consistent with the observation that
geometry-based gains are most pronounced in sparse canopy condi-
tions, where individual fruit instances are more spatially separated,
and their spherical geometry is more completely captured by the

From the result, both naive additive and concatenation fusion per-
form worse than the RGB-only baseline in terms of FPPI (9.582 and
8.864 vs. 7.240 for E1 (in Table 5)), despite having access to additional
geometric information. This counter-intuitive result highlights a funda-
mental risk of unconditional fusion: when noisy or missing depth is
injected directly into the shared feature representation, it does not
merely fail to help, but it actively introduces spurious signals that the
network cannot distinguish from meaningful geometry, leading to
more false positives than if depth had been ignored entirely. These ﬁnd-
ings highlight that the value of geometric information in outdoor or-
chard scenes is contingent on how reliably it can be incorporated, not
simply on whether it is present. Gated fusion resolves this by condition-
ing geometry injection on predicted spatial reliability, substantially re-
ducing FPPI from 8.864 to 4.596 compared to naive concatenation
while also improving mAP50:95. The contrast between gated and
naive fusion isolates the contribution of the gating mechanism itself:
the geometry cues are identical across all three fusion variants, and
the performance gap is attributable entirely to whether the network
learns to selectively trust them.
The geometry-aware recalibration head ablation reveals two distinct
but complementary roles for query-level shape consistency. In train-
only mode, the sphere loss improves both mAP50:95 and FPPI without
any modiﬁcation at inference, suggesting that the auxiliary supervision
encourages the decoder queries to internalize a stronger geometric
shape prior during training. The sphere loss effectively regularizes the
ﬁnal query representations toward fruit-consistent geometry, produc-
ing more discriminative embeddings even when the score itself is not
used at test time. Enabling inference-time re-scoring further improves
performance, conﬁrming that the predicted sphere consistency score
carries useful signal beyond what is already captured in the classiﬁca-
tion logits. Together, the two modes show that the geometry-aware re-
calibration head contributes at two levels: shaping the learned
representation during training and providing an explicit calibration sig-
nal at inference.
3.3. Sensitivity to missing and noisy geometry
Fig. 9 evaluates robustness under two test-time stress conditions:
depth dropout, where geometry pixels are randomly masked at increas-
ing ratios, and additive Gaussian noise at increasing sigma levels. The
RGB-only baseline (E1) remains ﬂat across all corruption severities by
construction, serving as a stable reference that isolates the effect of ge-
ometry corruption on the fusion variants. E2 exhibits the steepest deg-
radation under both conditions. At mask ratio 0.7, mAP50:95 drops to
0.553 while FPPI increases to 10.2. Most notably, at high corruption se-
verities, E2's mAP50:95 falls below the RGB-only baseline, indicating
that corrupted depth actively hurts performance relative to using no
depth at all. Just as unconditional fusion of clean-but-noisy depth intro-
duces spurious signals under normal conditions, it becomes increasingly
harmful as corruption severity grows, with the network unable to dis-
tinguish degraded geometry from meaningful signal.
E3 and E4 degrade substantially more gracefully across both corrup-
tion types. The performance gap between gated and naive fusion
widens as severity increases, suggesting that the gating mechanism pro-
vides the most beneﬁt precisely when depth quality is poorest. This be-
havior provides implicit but quantitative evidence that the gating
module has learned to model the spatial reliability of geometric infor-
mation: rather than applying depth cues uniformly, it suppresses geom-
etry contributions in regions where depth is corrupted or absent and
preserves them where depth remains coherent. A module that treated
geometry uniformly would be expected to degrade at least as severely
as E2 under the same corruption conditions. The observed stability of
E3 and E4 conﬁrms that selective reliability modeling is the operative
mechanism. This is the most representative of real cross-ﬁeld deploy-
ment conditions, where the test ﬁeld exhibits systematically lower
depth coverage than the training ﬁeld. This asymmetry is encouraging
from a practical standpoint: the proposed method is most robust
when robustness matters most.
E4 consistently maintains the lowest FPPI across all severity levels,
conﬁrming that query-level sphere consistency provides an additional
layer of resilience beyond feature-level gating. Even as depth corruption
degrades the geometry features fed into the gating module, the
geometry-aware recalibration head continues to suppress geometri-
cally implausible detections based on the ﬁnal decoder embeddings,
acting as a second line of defense when the upstream geometry signal
is compromised.
4. Conclusion and future work
GeoSphere-DETR was introduced as a geometry-aware extension of
RT-DETR to address the persistent false positive challenge in fruit detec-
tion under cluttered orchard conditions. The framework advances be-
yond existing RGB-D approaches by jointly modeling geometry
reliability at the feature level and shape plausibility at the object level.
This is achieved through two complementary modules: a neck-level
geometry-gated
fusion
module
that
adaptively
incorporates
depth-derived curvature cues into multi-scale visual features, and a
query-level geometry-aware recalibration head that re-scores detection
conﬁdence based on a learned spherical shape prior. Under a strict
cross-ﬁeld, cross-day evaluation protocol, GeoSphere-DETR improves
mAP50:95 from 0.562 to 0.642 and reduces FPPI by 54.6% compared
to the RGB-only RT-DETR baseline. In contrast to conventional early fu-
sion strategies, which degrade under noisy or incomplete depth, the
proposed approach demonstrates substantially improved robustness,
maintaining stable performance under simulated depth dropout and
noise. These results highlight that the effectiveness of RGB-D sensing
in outdoor agricultural environments depends not only on the availabil-
ity of depth information, but critically on how its reliability and geomet-
ric relevance are modeled.
The key contribution lies in establishing a dual-level geometry-
aware detection paradigm, where conditional feature fusion and
12

query-level geometric validation operate in a complementary manner
to suppress fruit-like false positives. This design departs from prior
work that relies on direct or unconditional depth fusion, providing a
more principled and robust framework for multi-modal perception in
unstructured ﬁeld conditions. However, several limitations remain.
The evaluation is conducted across two orchards and one cultivar
(Hamlin orange) under Florida growing conditions. Generalization to
other citrus varieties, growing regions, or sensor conﬁgurations has
not been validated and remains an important direction for future
work. The robustness experiments employ random depth masking
and Gaussian noise as controlled stress tests. While the test set itself
contains real structured stereo failures, explicitly simulating structured
dropout patterns such as boundary-concentrated or specular-region
depth loss remains a direction for future work. Per-lighting-condition
performance breakdown was not collected, and the contribution of
lighting variation to the observed cross-ﬁeld domain gap therefore re-
mains unquantiﬁed. Detection performance is still inﬂuenced by stereo
depth quality and RGB-D alignment, and severe degradation in depth
coverage cannot be fully mitigated. In addition, the effectiveness of the
geometric prior is reduced for fruit-on-ground instances, where shape
deformation violates the spherical assumption. More broadly, the cur-
rent formulation is tailored to spherical objects, and its applicability to
crops with diverse morphologies requires further investigation. The
query-level recalibration head is supervised by detection-level binary
labels derived from Hungarian matching rather than explicit geometric
annotations such as sphere-ﬁtting residuals. While ablation results con-
ﬁrm its effectiveness in reducing FPPI, future work could investigate in-
corporating explicit 3D shape supervision to more directly enforce
spherical shape consistency at the query level. While the proposed
framework is evaluated against representative RGB-D fusion strategies,
broader comparison across heterogeneous architectures such as late fu-
sion pipelines and RGB-D YOLO variants remains a direction for future
work, as these approaches require substantially different training infra-
structure and are architecturally incompatible with the single-stream
RT-DETR decoder used in this study. Finally, while the proposed method
demonstrates strong false positive suppression, missing fruit detection
and absolute fruit count accuracy relative to physical ground truth
were not evaluated, as the dataset does not include manual per-tree
fruit counts. This represents an important direction for future work, par-
ticularly for yield estimation applications.

Future work will pursue three directions. First, integrating depth
completion or denoising as a preprocessing stage could reduce the
method's sensitivity to stereo quality degradation and RGB-D misalign-
ment, broadening its applicability to scenes with severe depth dropout
or sensor noise. Second, replacing the ﬁxed spherical prior with a learn-
able or crop-speciﬁc shape prior would generalize the consistency head
beyond citrus to fruits and vegetables with more complex morphol-
ogies. Third, validating the proposed detector as a perception module
within a robotic harvesting pipeline, where detection outputs feed di-
rectly into pick-point estimation and manipulation planning, would es-
tablish its practical utility for integrated orchard automation and
provide a more operationally grounded evaluation of its false positive
suppression capability.
