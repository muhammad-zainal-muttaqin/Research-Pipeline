Te detection and classifcation of oil palm fruit bunches are critical for optimizing palm oil yield and quality. Traditional methods
relying on human visual inspection are prone to errors and inconsistencies. To address these challenges, this study proposes an
optimized You Only Look Once Version 7 (YOLOv7) algorithm for real-time detection and classifcation of oil palm fruit
bunches. By leveraging a comprehensive dataset and implementing strategic fne-tuning techniques, we signifcantly improved the
model’s performance. Te optimized model achieved a classifcation accuracy of 92.55% and a mean average precision (mAP) of
95.08%, demonstrating its efectiveness in real-world applications. Te model was integrated into web application, allowing
farmers and processing facilities to assess the ripeness of palm fruit in real time, thereby improving decision-making and
operational efciency. Tis research highlights the potential of advanced deep learning models in agricultural applications,
promising a transformative impact on the palm oil industry by ensuring consistent quality assessments and optimizing production
processes.

1. Introduction
Oil palm is currently recognized as a major agricultural crop
in Southeast Asian countries such as Indonesia, Malaysia,
and Tailand [1, 2]. It is a major economic crop in Tailand,
particularly in the southern peninsular region where it
dominates agricultural production [3]. Te country’s annual
palm oil production reaches an impressive 11–13 million
tons, underscoring its critical role in Tailand’s economy.
While the optimal oil yield from palm fruit bunches typically
ranges from 16% to 18%, some smallholders harvest pre-
maturely, resulting in lower yields of 14%–15%. Tis vari-
ability in harvesting practices leads to signifcant quality
fuctuations in the palm bunches purchased by processing
facilities, both large and small. Such quality variations have
a substantial impact on the overall palm oil production and,
consequently, on the industry’s economic performance.
Te quality assessment of palm fruit bunches is critically
dependent on their ripeness level, categorized as raw, half
ripe, or ripe [4]. Both small and large palm-processing fa-
cilities grapple with the challenge of accurately classifying
ripeness levels, given the immense volume of palm bunches
they handle and the absence of immediate quality evaluation
technology. Historically, harvesters have relied on manual
techniques for assessing palm bunch quality, including vi-
sual color inspection and counting fallen fruits beneath the

Image processing employs algorithms to analyze images,
encompassing tasks like enhancement, feature extraction,
segmentation, and object detection [4]. Tis technology
ofers signifcant advantages over human assessment, in-
cluding time savings, cost reduction, and minimized bias. In
the context of oil palm fruit ripeness classifcation, image-

visual assessment, leveraging computational power for more
efcient and consistent analysis. For palm bunch evaluation,

(YOLOv2) and YOLOv3 [11], in an efort to identify the

However, while the YOLO-based model has been developed
to ofer higher classifcation performance, few researchers
have applied it specifcally to classify the ripeness levels of oil
palm fruit bunches. Tis task is challenging due to the
similarity of fruits within the same bunch and the varying
ripeness levels of individual fruits in a single bunch. In this
research, You Only Look Once Version 7 (YOLOv7) was
selected because it ofers a good balance between detection
accuracy and processing speed, making it highly suitable for
real-time agricultural applications involving large volumes
of data. Compared with its predecessors, YOLOv7 in-
troduces architectural improvements that enhance learning
capacity and reduce inference time. YOLOv7 was utilized to
classify palm fruits in real time, accessible to farmers via
a website. In addition, purchasing factories can use this
model to verify ripeness levels and check for irregularities in
the palm cuttings supplied by each customer.

Lai et al. [26] developed a YOLOv4-based real-time
detection system for identifying ripe oil palm fresh fruit
bunches. Tis system utilizes an RGB camera (Intel Real-
Sense D435) to capture images of oil palm trees, which are
then processed by a Single Board Computer (Nvidia Jetson
NX) running a YOLOv4 inference model. Te trained model
detects the target objects, records their coordinates, and
transmits positional information to the robotic harvesting
mechanism within the Robot Operating System (ROS). Te
model achieved a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 87.9%
and operated at a speed of 21 frames per second. Despite its
efectiveness, the training dataset required numerous iter-
ations, and the system was restricted to detecting only ripe
oil palm fresh fruit bunches, excluding other maturity stages
such as unripe or half-ripe fruit. Building on this, Suharjito
et al. [27] employed the YOLOv4 model to detect the
ripeness of oil palm fruit bunches, classifying them into
categories such as unripe, under-ripe, ripe, overripe, empty
bunch, and abnormal fresh fruit bunch. Tey fne-tuned the
hyperparameters for the training data, resulting in the ef-
fective detection of oil palm fruit bunches. However, their
study did not mention augmenting the dataset to increase
the number of images in the oil palm fruit dataset.
Lai et al. [28] proposed an enhanced YOLOv7 model
optimized for feld environments. Teir research modifed
the original YOLOv7 architecture by incorporating several
key improvements. First, they integrated the Simplifed
Attention Module (SimAM) into the backbone network to
enhance feature extraction capabilities and minimize in-
terference from complex backgrounds, all without in-
creasing the number of network parameters. SimAM can be
embedded at any position within the model. Next, they
improved the multipath convolution (MPConv) structure to
reduce feature loss during down sampling.
In addition, they enhanced the soft Nonmaximum
Suppression (NMS) algorithm to improve detection accu-
racy, especially when pineapples are occluded or over-
lapping. Tese enhancements signifcantly boosted the
detection accuracy and speed of the YOLOv7 model in
complex feld environments for various maturity stages of
pineapples. Similarly, Gu et al. [29] improved the YOLOv7-
Tiny version for detecting citrus fruits in complex envi-
ronments. Tey replaced ELAN with Efcient Layer Ag-
gregation Network with Depth-wise Convolutions (ELAN-
DW) in the backbone network to reduce the number of
model parameters. In the neck network, they used Channel
Attention (CA) to replace Convolution Batch-norm Lea-
kyReLU (CBL), enhancing the model’s feature extraction
ability. In addition, they integrated a Dynamic Head into the
head network to improve the model’s ability to detect citrus
fruits at diferent scales by fusing multilayer features. Tis
improved model, named YOLO-DCA, outperforms the
original YOLOv7-Tiny in terms of lightweight design and
can be deployed on embedded devices with limited com-
putational resources. YOLO-DCA accurately detects citrus
fruits in complex environments across various devices.
Chen et al. [30] developed a multiscale lightweight model
based on YOLOv7 by incorporating the Convolutional Block
Attention Module (CBAM) into the backbone network. Tis
attention module enhances detection accuracy by enabling
the network to focus on important features while sup-
pressing unnecessary ones. To address the issue of large
parameter sizes in traditional feature extraction, the re-
searchers introduced GhostConv to replace conventional
convolutional layers in the Convolution-BatchNorm-Swish
(CBS) module, promoting a more lightweight model. Ad-
ditionally, the head network utilizes four Reparametrized
Convolution (RepConv) modules to reparametrize the
network. Te resulting Citrus-YOLOv7 model demonstrated
excellent performance, achieving a mAP of 97.29%, an in-
ference time of 69.38 ms, and a parameter size of 24.26 M.
Further enhancements to the YOLOv7 architecture were
made by Hu et al. [31], who integrated CBAM and CA
attention mechanisms to improve feature extraction and
apple detection in orchards. Tey also combined multiobject
tracking with the SURF algorithm to track apples across
video frames. Te improved YOLOv7-CA achieved a mAP
of 91.3% and has a model size of 12.1M. Tis cascaded
multiobject tracking method with SURF proved more ac-
curate than other similar tracking approaches.

3. Materials and Methods
Tis research proposes an enhanced object detection model
for classifying the ripeness levels of oil palm fruit bunches
using the YOLOv7 architecture. Te methodology is
structured into four major components: dataset preparation,
model training and fne-tuning through layer freezing,
evaluation of the model, and implementing it in a web
application, as shown in Figure 1.
Te process begins with the acquisition of a custom-
labeled image dataset, captured under feld conditions and
classifed into three ripeness categories: raw, half ripe, and
ripe. Te dataset is augmented to increase robustness and
then split into training, validation, and testing sets in a 70:20:
10 ratio. YOLOv7, known for its balance of speed and ac-
curacy, is chosen for its suitability in real-time agricultural
applications.
Te key innovation in this study lies in the strategic fne-
tuning technique using network layer freezing, a method
under the transfer learning paradigm. Instead of training the
model from scratch or fully retraining a pretrained YOLOv7
model, we systematically freeze a portion of the network
layers to retain generalized features from the original model,
while adapting the remaining layers to our specifc appli-
cation domain: oil palm fruit ripeness classifcation. Te
rationale is that early layers in a convolutional network
typically learn low-level features like edges and textures,
which are transferable across image domains, while deeper
layers capture task-specifc patterns.
To determine the optimal confguration, we evaluated
multiple freezing fractions—50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and
90%—across
the
model
architecture.
Tese
specifc
thresholds were selected to investigate the gradual efect of
diferent freezing levels on model performance. Te selected
values span from moderate (50%) to aggressive (90%)
freezing, aligning with strategies reported in transfer
learning literature. For instance, training only the last layers
of a pretrained DenseNet-161 network resulted in a high
performance [32]. Similarly, retraining only the last three
layers of a deep model has been shown to achieve optimal
results while preserving the knowledge captured in earlier
layers [33]. Moreover, experiments involving YOLOv7 and
YOLOv8 models with 50%–75% frozen layers yielded the
best performance across various agricultural datasets, such
as apple and blueberry detection [34]. Te rationale is based
on the understanding that early layers in convolutional
networks extract generic features, while deeper layers learn
task-specifc patterns. By progressively freezing larger por-
tions of the model from the backbone forward, we aimed to
identify the best trade-of between leveraging pretrained
knowledge and adapting to the oil palm fruit dataset. Each
version of the model was evaluated using mAP@0.5, pre-
cision, recall, F1-score, and model size in megabytes (MB).
3.1. Dataset Preparation. Te dataset was collected from
Taksin Palm Company with a focus on grading the ripeness
levels of oil palm fruit bunches into three categories: raw,
half ripe, and ripe. All images were captured under natural
sunny feld conditions, resulting in a total of 427 images
comprising 146 raw, 157 half, and 124 ripe samples, as
shown in Table 2. Te dataset was then divided into training,
validation, and testing sets using a 70:20:10 ratio, corre-
sponding to 297, 84, and 46 images, respectively. To improve
the learning capability and robustness of the model, various
data augmentation techniques including grayscale conver-
sion, horizontal fipping, and noise injection were applied
exclusively to the training set, expanding it to 891 images.
Table 3 summarizes the fnal number of images used in each
phase of model development.

and the half-ripe stage, where the bunch is a mix of red and
black. In YOLOv7, the model relies on features such as color,
texture, and spatial patterns to make these distinctions. Te
model learns to detect and classify the diferent stages of

Te process began with capturing images of oil palm
fruit bunches and labeling their classes using the original
YOLOv7 network structure, as shown in Figure 2. Te
network is primarily composed of the backbone, neck, and
head. Starting with the input image, it goes to the backbone
for feature extraction. Te backbone includes the ELAN
module, which enhances feature extraction while main-
taining computational efciency, and then sends the
extracted features to the neck. Te SPPCSPC module
concatenates feature maps from diferent pooling sizes,
providing multiscale features for better object detection.
Moving to the head, it is responsible for the fnal output of
detected objects, using RepConv to optimize the detection
and reduce the model’s computational complexity.
Each image’s object classes were defned using Robofow,
a software tool for annotating images with bounding boxes.
Tis step was time-consuming, as it required fltering out
irrelevant elements to ensure accurate input for model
training. Before training, a YOLOv7 fle (.txt) was exported,
containing [class id, x, y, w, h], where x and y are the co-
ordinates of the box, w is the width, and h is the height. Te
annotated results for each class were stored in separate fles,
as illustrated in Figure 3.
After training the initial model, the fne-tuning process
begins by selecting the best-performing model. Te network
layers will be incrementally frozen from the head to the tail,
with 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% of the layers being
frozen, as shown in Figure 4. Each model, with a diferent
percentage of frozen layers, will then be trained for 100
epochs. Post training, each model will be tested using a test
dataset, and the results will be recorded. Finally, these results
will be compared to determine which fne-tuned model
achieves the highest performance.
Te augmentation technique proposed in this paper will
split the images into 70, 20, and 10 for training, validation,
and testing, respectively. Te data will then be trained with
the YOLOv7 algorithm for 100, 300, and 500 epochs, re-
spectively. Once the results from each epoch of training are
received, the models will be compared to determine which
one has the best performance. Te best model will then be
used for fne-tuning, and the models that were fne-tuned.

5. Implementation
Tis section will be divided into two parts. Te frst part
covers model training and choosing the model that achieves
the best performance. Te second part focuses on integrating
the model into a web application by creating a YOLO al-
gorithm service that uses the trained model to receive images
and return results to the server and web client application,
respectively.
In the previous section, the model was trained using
YOLOv7 and fne-tuned with varying percentages of frozen
network layers. Tese models were then compared to
identify the best performer. After selecting the optimal
model, the next step involved integrating it into a web
application. As shown in Figure 5, the chosen model will be
incorporated into the development process to create
a YOLO algorithm service. Tis will be done by designing an
API using Flask, a Python framework, to receive image data
from the web client. Te torch library will be imported to
load the model and utilize it for data detection. Once
processed, the data will be formatted into a JSON object to
facilitate easy use by both the web client and backend server.
To ensure fast detection, the machine running Flask requires
a GPU. Te specifcations include a 12th Gen Intel(R)
Core(TM) i5-12400 2.50 GHz CPU, 32 GB RAM, 1 TB disk,
and an Nvidia Geforce GTX 3070 TI GPU. Te JSON object
data will then be sent to the web client to render the results
and to the backend server to save the data in the database.
Te web client dashboard will also retrieve and display the
detected images from the database history. Vue, a JavaScript
framework, will be used on the web client side, while NestJS,
another JavaScript framework, will be used on the backend
server side. PostgreSQL is chosen for the database because it
can store data as JSON objects directly, unlike MySQL,
which requires conversion from JSON to string frst.
Figure 6 shows a use case diagram of the system and
functions within the web application. In the client section,
there are two roles: customer and administrator. Both can
upload images of palm bunches to check the ripeness level
and count the number of palm bunches detected. Te ad-
ministrator has additional capabilities, including viewing the
history of palm bunch image uploads from customers and
generating monthly reports on the number of palm fruit
bunches received at each ripeness level. In addition, the
administrator can manage customer accounts.
Examples of the user interface of a web application
integrated with the model and backend are shown in Fig-
ures 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11. Figure 7 displays preview images
uploaded through the web application. Upon clicking the
upload button, the results will show the detected palm
bunches, including the original image, the detected image,
class probabilities, and the count of palm bunches, as shown
in Figure 8. Te history of palm bunch detection results is
stored in the database and displayed on the web application,
accessible only to administrators. Tey can view the history
and see detailed images from each upload, including the total
number of palm bunches at each ripeness level and the
overall count, as shown in Figure 9.
Figures 10 and 11 will display a bar chart and a line chart,
respectively, showing the counting of palm bunches. Te bar
chart will display the number of oil palm fruit bunches
detected at various ripeness levels over a range of months for
each customer. Similarly, the line chart will show the trend of
detected ripeness levels of oil palm fruit over the same
period. Te beneft of these graphs is that the owner can
predict the quantity and quality of oil palm fruit from each
supplier, aiding in future sales planning within the industry.

 . Conclusion
Tis study successfully optimized the YOLOv7 algorithm
for the detection and ripeness classifcation of oil palm
fruit bunches. By utilizing a comprehensive dataset and
employing strategic fne-tuning methods, we achieved
signifcant improvements in model accuracy and per-
formance. Te optimized model demonstrated a classif-
cation accuracy of 77.42% and a mAP of 87.60%,
showcasing its efectiveness in real-world applications.
Te integration of this model into a web application allows
for real-time assessment of oil palm fruit ripeness, pro-
viding a practical tool for both farmers and processing
facilities. Tis system not only enhances the efciency and
accuracy of quality assessments but also addresses the
limitations and subjectivity associated with traditional
manual inspections. Te fndings of this research highlight
the potential of deep learning models like YOLOv7 in
agricultural
applications,
particularly
in
improving
decision-making and resource management in the palm
oil industry. Te implementation of such advanced
technologies promises to transform quality assessment
practices, ultimately contributing to better management
