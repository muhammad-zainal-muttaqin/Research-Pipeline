A B S T R A C T  
Accurate estimation of tomato cluster yields is critical to the advancement of intelligent and unmanned green­
houses, guiding horticultural management and adjusting sales and marketing strategies. However, due to the 
complex natural environment and tracking stability, there are still considerable challenges for automated yield 
estimation to be deployed in practice. Therefore, this paper presents an improved tomato cluster counting 
method that combines object detection, multiple object tracking, and specific tracking region counting. To 
reduce background tomato misidentification, we proposed the YOLOv5-4D that fuses RGB images and depth 
images as input. Next, we adopted ByteTrack to track tomato clusters in continuous frames and designed a 
specific tracking region counting method to overcome the problem of tracked tomato cluster ID shift. In the test 
set, the improved YOLOv5-4D had a detection accuracy of 97.9 % and a mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.748. Field exper­
iments showed that the counting method achieved a statistical average counting accuracy of 95.1 % and the 
integrated algorithm ran at more than 40 FPS, enabling stable real-time yield estimation.   
1. Introduction 
Crop yield information is a critical role in the management of crop 
production, guiding agricultural planting practices and assisting farmers 
in fruit market strategy layout such as selling their crops earlier (Alwis 
et al., 2022; Koirala et al., 2019a; Maheswari et al., 2021). Stakeholders 
across the agricultural supply chain can gain from this reduction in 
production process uncertainty by forecasting crop yield. Worldwide 
production of tomatoes had reached 187 million tons by 2020, with 
China accounting for more than 30 % of that total (Sun et al., 2022). 
With the spread of the smart greenhouse tomato growing model in 
China, total tomato production and unit yield will continue to rise in the 
future. This means, in contrast to the declining labour force in agricul­
tural production, a growing amount of labour is required to manually 
count tomato yields. Therefore, it is significant and urgent to realize the 
automatic counting of tomatoes in greenhouses by inspection robots 
(Saleem et al., 2021). 
Tomatoes are grown at a high density in production greenhouses, 
with small spacing between tomatoes on adjacent ridges, as shown in 
Fig. 1. When the camera acquires images at the current ridge, tomato 
clusters growing at the opposite ridge can appear in the field of view. 
Except for differences in pixel area between tomatoes, the tomatoes on 
both the opposite ridge and current ridge have similar colour and 
morphological characteristics, which complicates the task of dis­
tinguishing between foreground and background tomatoes. In our pre­
vious work, we also encountered the problem of background tomato 
misidentification (Rong et al., 2021), which is a challenge in the tomato 
counting task, over-estimating yields. Another issue is that the unripe 
tomatoes are similar in colour to the green background, which allows 
the detector to miss some tomatoes and affects counting accuracy. 
Further, variations in fruit colour, shape, or brightness can be encoun­
tered over different plants of the same tomato crop, and over images of 
the same tomato cluster from different perspectives (Afonso et al., 2020; 
Bac et al., 2017). These mentioned problems will affect the accuracy of 
yield estimation in terms of repeated counts, over-counting, etc. 
In recent years, many studies have been done on fruit identification 
and counting (Liu et al., 2018a; Wang et al., 2022a; Zheng et al., 2022). 
Compared with the traditional image processing methods based on 
manual feature extraction (Lin et al., 2019; Rakun et al., 2011; Yama­
moto et al., 2014), object detection algorithms based on deep

detecting crops (Liu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2018b; Tian et al., 2019). 
Many of these works in fruit detection focused on the application of 
mainstream visual algorithms based on deep learning in agricultural 
scenarios or adjusting the partial structure of the network to improve the 
detection performance for specific crops (Saleem et al., 2021; Wang 
et al., 2022b). An improved YOLOv3 detector with a backbone network 
of DenseNet was proposed, which could be used to detect apples at 
different growth stages in orchards, including those apples under oc­
clusion and overlap conditions (Tian et al., 2019). Hu et al. (2019) used 
the Faster R-CNN model to realize fast and accurate detection of ripe 
tomatoes on the ridges. First, Faster RCNN was deployed to locate to­
matoes, and then the Gaussian density function of H and S in the HSV 
colour space was used to segment the tomato regions for removing 
background pixels. The detection accuracy of separate, adjacent, over­
lapped and shaded tomatoes were 95.5 %, 93.8 %, 78.4 % and 81.9 %, 
respectively. Liu et al. (2020) proposed an improved tomato detection 
model based on YOLOv3 with an accuracy of 94.75 % and a recall of 
93.09 %, which replaced the traditional rectangular bounding box with 
a circular bounding box for better tomato localization. To locate the 
tomato shearing point more precisely, Yu et al. (2020) proposed a novel 
object detector called rotated YOLO (R-YOLO). Except for the output of 
the bounding box corresponding to the strawberry, R-YOLO additionally 
provided an angle to make the bounding box fit the target better. In the 
field test, R-YOLO could achieve a precision of 97.17 % and a recall of 
95.81 %. In contrast to laboratory conditions, the equipment used in 
actual agricultural production had to face hardware limitations. In an 
attempt to achieve real-time recognition for the stem/ calyx of apples 
under CPU conditions, Wang et al. (2022c) optimized the YOLOv5 
model by using detection head searching, layer pruning, and channel 
pruning. The model parameters and weights volumes decreased by 
about 71 % while the detection performance decreased slightly. 
Based on crop identification research, some scholars have further 
studied crop counting to estimate crop yield in future periods. Liu et al. 
(2018a) proposed a fruit-counting method that combined deep seg­
mentation, frame-to-frame tracking, and 3D localization. The FCN 
model was first trained to segment fruit pixels from video frame images, 
and subsequently, the Hungarian algorithm was used to track fruits 
across frames. In order to correct outliers and double-counted fruit 
tracks, they combined tracking results with the Structure from Motion 
(SFM) algorithm. As compared to previous tracking algorithms, Deep­
SORT has been proven to be a capable tracker, and Parico and Ahamed 
(2021) used the method to count pear fruit. The counting task was 
divided into two parts. The first part was to recognize the pears using 
YOLOv4, and later to track the fruits between multiple frames using the 
DeepSORT algorithm. Similarly, Lyu et al. (2022) and Ge et al. (2022) 
also made some progress in yield estimation for green citrus and tomato 
using the DeepSORT algorithm. Furthermore, Zhang et al. (2022) pro­
posed an improved DeepSORT algorithm, called OrangeSort, to track 
and count citrus fruit. This method tracked citrus based on motion 
displacement estimation, which effectively alleviated the problem of 
repeated counting of obscured fruit. 
Most of the aforementioned fruit counting was implemented based 
on multiple object tracking (MOT) algorithms, which generally include a 
detection module and a tracking module. Earlier, we discussed the 
challenge of misidentifying background tomatoes, which can lead to 
inflated yield estimates. Although object detection algorithms based on 
deep convolutional networks (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020; Ge et al., 2021) 
have been able to easily perform the task of detecting tomato clusters 
and fruits, the lack of depth information can make it possible to detect 
tomatoes growing on the opposite side as well. One way to reduce the 
background interface is to perform image acquisition at night (Afonso 
et al., 2020; Koirala et al., 2019b; Liang et al., 2020). 
In this paper, a method for detecting and counting tomato clusters in 
the production greenhouse was presented. In the detection task, the 
improved YOLOv5 was proposed to increase the detection accuracy by 
fusing RGB images and depth images. In the counting task, the tomato 
clusters across multiple frames were tracked using the ByteTrack mul­
tiple object tracking algorithm. Finally, a specific tracking region 
counting method was designed to reduce the effect of repeated counting 
of the same tomato cluster due to the ID switch. After reviewing the 
challenges and progress of fruit detection and counting, the rest of this 
paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents the image acquisition 
and dataset creation, the improved YOLOv5 based on RGB-D fusion, and 
the tomato cluster tracking and counting method; Section 3 shows the 
evaluation of the improved YOLOv5, provides the results and discussion 
of the tracking and counting methods; finally, Section5 concludes the 
research work.

(1) Only foreground tomatoes were marked without doing anything

which were defined as those more than 0.8 m away from the 
camera (corresponding to a value greater than 140 on the depth

(2) As the field of view of the camera was not sufficient to cover the 
entire tomato growing area, we labelled only tomato clusters 
with more than four fruits that could be observed in the image. 
2.2. Improved YOLOv5 detector 
As one of the most widespread single-stage object detection algo­
rithms, YOLO has been commonly employed in industry and agriculture 
with its outstanding performance and fast detection speed after several 
version iterations and module upgrades (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020; 
Redmon et al., 2016; Redmon and Farhadi, 2017; Redmon and Farhadi, 
2018). YOLOv5 was released on the GitHub website by Ultralytics LLC 
(https://github.com/ultralytics/yolov5) and provided four different 
scale versions (S, M, L and X, in which different network depths and 
widths were set), improving and upgrading on YOLOv3. Compared to 
YOLOv3 and YOLOv4, YOLOv5 uses various data enhancement tech­
niques and has a clean network structure design. Additionally, it was 
fully written in the PyTorch framework rather than using the Darknet 
framework, resulting in better accessibility and easy deployment. For 
these reasons, we chose YOLOv5 as the benchmark for improvements 
and performance comparison. 
In this paper, we apply multi-modal (RGB image and Depth Image) 
data fusion to optimize the input of YOLOv5, with the goal of reducing 
false detection of background tomatoes and improving the recall of 
immature tomatoes. The original structure of YOLOv5 is given in Fig. 4, 
with the difference that only the RGB image is used as input. Like the 
current state-of-the-art object detection algorithms, YOLOv5 is made of 
the backbone network, the neck network and the detection head. 
The backbone network of YOLOv5 mainly includes Focus module, 
CBS module, SPP module and C3 module. In deep learning, the back­
bone network is used to extract features at different scales of the image, 
including low-level features (textures, boundaries, etc.) extracted at 
shallower layers, and high-level semantic features extracted at deeper 
layers. Firstly, the Focus module performs a slicing operation on the 
input image (see Fig. 5(a)), which expands the input channel to four 
times its original channel while reducing the width and height of the 
image to half its original size. In the case of no information loss, the 
Focus module performs two-fold downsampling, which helps reduce the 
number of model parameters and speed up inference. The basis module 
for other modules in YOLOv5 is the CBS module (see Fig. 5(b)), which 
combines the 2D convolution (Conv), batch normalization (BN), and 
SiLU activation function (SiLU). As shown in Fig. 5(c), it’s the Spatial 
Pyramid Pooling (SPP) module. The SPP module is typically used to 
solve problems of the non-uniform size of input images and enormous 
variations in object sizes in images by employing kernels of various sizes 
for performing maximum pooling on feature maps. The C3 module is 
crucial for building the backbone of YOLOv5, as given in Fig. 5(d). It’s 
composed of multiple CBS modules to form residual connections,

After image features are extracted by the backbone network, the 
neck network is then applied to connect the backbone and the detection 
head. This step is to extract information for fusion on feature maps at 
different scales aiming to increase feature reuse. In this model, the neck 
uses a combination of Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and Pixel Ag­
gregation Network (PAN), integrating the regular top-down FPN with 
the bottom-up PAN to fuse the extracted semantic features with fine- 
grained positional features. Finally, the detection head performs the 
prediction on three different scales of feature maps for detecting target 
objects of various sizes, which outputs vectors containing information 
about the probability of the target object categories, the confidence level 
and the locations of the bounding boxes. 
In contrast to the original YOLOv5, the input information of the 
object detector is added in this paper. As shown in Fig. 4, the 3-channel 
RGB image (1280 × 720 × 3) and the single-channel depth image 
(1280 × 720 × 1) are fused into 4-channel input data (1280 × 720 × 4) 
using the Concat module. The addition of the depth image can increase 
more features (the distance information), which is beneficial for the 
detector to learn the difference between the target tomatoes and the 
background tomatoes, hence lowering the rate of tomato misidentifi­
cation. Simultaneously, the available depth features effectively distin­
guish tomatoes in the foreground and background and increase the 
saliency of green tomatoes, which can reduce the missed detection of 
immature tomatoes. In this paper, only the first layer of the network is 
modified, and the increased model parameters are negligible compared 
to those of the whole network, which hardly affects the inference speed 
of the model. In the training process, we can still train a new model using 
pre-trained weights, and only need to load the parameters of the first 
convolutional layer using random initialization. 
2.3. Tomato cluster tracking and counting 
Multiple object tracking, abbreviated as MOT, allows detecting and 
assigning IDs to multiple objects in a video (consecutive frames) for 
trajectory tracking without knowing the number of targets in advance. 
Most of the current MOT algorithms are based on the Tracking-by- 
Detection strategy. After the original consecutive frames are given, an 
object detector is run to obtain the bounding box of the object. Then, 
different features (visual and motion features) are computed for each 
detected object. After that, the similarity is computed for the neigh­
bouring frame objects. Finally, each object is associated and assigned an 
ID. 
Compared to the pedestrian tracking task, the feature variations 
between different tomatoes are not substantial. In contrast, the same 
tomato cluster from different perspectives presents diverse appearance 
characteristics, as shown in Fig. 6. Moreover, computing appearance 
similarity using the Re-Identification features in the DeepSort has been 
proven to be a time-consuming task. Therefore, considering both the 
similarity of tomato appearance and real-time detection, the ByteTrack 
multi-object tracking algorithm (Zhang et al., 2021) is used to track and 
count the tomato cluster objects detected by YOLOv5 in this paper. 
The ByteTrack method is simple and efficient, using only Kalman 
filtering to predict trajectories of the current frame in the next frame. 
Then, the IoU between the predicted boxes and the detected boxes is 
used as the similarity between the two matches, and the matching is 
completed by the Hungarian algorithm. The specific workflow of the 
ByteTrack data association method is shown in Fig. 7. First, the

When using ByteTrack for tomato cluster tracking, there are still

prone to shift when the blocked tomato cluster reappears in view. 
Therefore, the total number of tomato clusters is not directly counted

3.1. Evaluation of the improved YOLOv5-4D 
3.1.1. Model training 
In the detection model evaluation experiments, the native YOLOv5 
and the improved YOLOv5 based on RGB-D fusion (YOLOv5-4D) were 
trained and evaluated respectively, and the effect of depth information 
(distance information) on model performance improvement was illus­
trated by analyzing the evaluation metrics of several models. 
In the model training, the image input size was set to 640, and the 
maximum batch size was only set to 3 due to the limitation of GPU 
memory capacity. Referring to the experience, it was difficult to 
converge the training data if the batch size was too small, and eventu­
ally, the model would be under-fitted. Thus, all models undertook a 
back-propagation after accumulating the batch size a specified number 
of times (32 times). Furthermore, the initial learning rate and the cycle 
learning rate were set to 0.01 and 0.2 (the learning rate decayed 
sequentially after 200, 1000, and 1500 iterations), and an SGD optimizer 
with 0.937 momenta and 0.0005 wt decay was adopted. 
A total of 574 sets of aligned RGB images and depth images were 
manually labelled and divided into training, validation, and test sets in 
the ratio of 6:2:2. In the data preprocessing, data augmentation methods 
such as scaling, panning, rotation, colour transformation, and Mosaic 
were used in YOLOv5, which significantly increased the number of 
training samples and improved the model generalization ability. The 
improved YOLOv5-4D also inherited the data augmentation methods of 
the original YOLOv5. It is required to distinguish if a geometric trans­
formation was done on the RGB image, a corresponding operation was 
also performed on the depth image so that the two images remained 
pixel-aligned. In contrast, when a colour transformation is done on the 
colour image, no colour change was made to the depth image, to ensure 
that the distance features are accurate. 
Considering the effects of loading pre-trained weights on detection 
results, we trained each model twice separately, once from scratch and 
the other time using pre-trained weights. All pre-trained weights were 
downloaded from the official website and obtained from training on the 
COCO dataset by the original authors. We trained each model for 2000 
epochs and evaluated the model on the validation set after an epoch was 
completed. The principal metrics for evaluation included precision, 
recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95. The precision indicates the ratio 
of the correct detection results to the total detection results, and the 
recall is defined as the proportion of the correct detection results in all 
true results, as expressed in Equations (1) and (2). Compared with the 
precision and the recall, the mean average precision (mAP) can provide 
a more balanced reflection of the global detection performance of the 
model. The mAP is the average value of each category (c) of AP, and the 
AP is defined as the area under the precision-recall curve (Zheng et al., 
2015). The mAP@0.5 represents the mAP value with the intersection- 
over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.5. The mAP@0.5:0.95 indicates the 
average mAP on different IoU thresholds, moving from 0.5 to 0.95 in 
intervals of 0.05.

3.1.2. Performance on the test dataset 
To further demonstrate the superiority of improved YOLOv5 based 
on RGB-D fusion in tomato cluster detection, we compared the accuracy, 
recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95 of each trained model on the test 
set. To further bolster the credibility of our results, we introduced two 
popular object detection algorithms, YOLOX-s and CenterNet, in our 
comparison study on the tomato cluster dataset. As compared to 
YOLOv5, which employs an anchor-based approach, both anchor-free 
YOLOX-s and CenterNet present a greater challenge in terms of 
training, necessitating a greater number of epochs to achieve conver­
gence. In light of the many training sessions, we deemed it appropriate 
to conduct training for 12,000 epochs for both YOLOX-s and CenterNet, 
in order to ensure robust and accurate results. 
Table 1 presented the test results of different models trained from 
scratch. Compared with the native YOLOv5, the improved YOLOv5-4D 
significantly improved precision and recall, with a 2.1 % to 3.2 % in­
crease in accuracy and a 1.9 % to 3.6 % increase in recall. Meanwhile, 
the mAP@0.5 and the mAP@0.5:0.95 also increased by 4.0 % to 4.6 % 
and 2.8 % to 5.3 % respectively. In contrast, the results of YOLOX-s were 
much lower than the other models. Further analysis of the model’s 
predictions through visualization indicated that the YOLOX-s model 
frequently misidentified background regions as foreground targets, 
resulting in a high number of false positive detections. This also led to a 
similar reduction in recall as the model fought against the dataset. And it 
could be seen that the mAP@0.5 of both CenterNet and YOLOv5m may 
converge towards similar levels. However, the detection speed of Cen­
terNet was significantly slower in comparison to other models. These 
were sufficient to prove that the method of improving the model by 
fusing RGB images and depth images was effective. In addition, we 
separately assessed the models trained from scratch and the models 
trained using pre-trained weights (see Table 1 and Table 2) and found 
that loading the pre-trained weights for training could obtain better 
results (the mAP@0.5:0.95 of YOLOv5-4D could be increased to 0.769), 
which matched the findings of other scholars (Duong et al., 2020). 
Further, test results using different scale versions of YOLOv5-4D did not 
show significant metric variations, suggesting that using the smallest 
version (YOLOv5s-4D) was enough to obtain excellent performance on 
the tomato cluster dataset in edge devices, instead enabling significant 
computational time savings.

3.3. Discussion 
The current work showed that the entire tomato cluster detection 
and counting algorithm could work well in the greenhouse. In the to­
mato detection task, the improved YOLOv5-4D can significantly in­
crease detection accuracy and recall metrics. In this paper, we only tried 
to build a simple multi-model fusion model, and future attempts can be 
made to fuse in the Neck module or to additionally add NIR images. 
Also, the input used for model training and testing consisted of RGB 
images and corresponding depth images, thus increasing the cost of 
reacquiring the data. Therefore, we need to re-perform the complete 
data acquisition in future work, which increases the cost of re-acquiring 
data. 
When tracking each identified tomato cluster using the MOT algo­
rithm such as ByteTrack and DeepSORT, the ID shift can lead to inac­
curate counting if the total number of tomato clusters is counted directly 
by the ID number. Therefore, we handle the tracking and counting tasks 
separately and propose a specific tracking region counting method to 
strictly limit the repeated counting behaviour. Experiments have 
demonstrated the effectiveness of this method. In the experiments, there 
were still a few missed detections, and if a tomato cluster was missed in 
the middle counting region, it would result in an undercount. Another 
remaining issue was the shift of IDs after tomato clusters entered the 
counting area, which led to repeated counting. These are the reasons 
why there is still a 4.9 % counting error and also are issues that need to 
be improved in the future. 
Although we have been able to identify and localize tomato clusters, 
we only detect a mixture of all tomato clusters with different maturities. 
Before it is truly implemented to guide farmers’ production activities, 
we need to further count tomato clusters of different maturity levels 
separately (at least distinguishing between ripe and unripe tomatoes). 
Through the collection and analysis of data on tomato ripeness, farmers 
can optimize their harvesting and processing operations. By gaining 
insights into the expected quantity of tomatoes that will be available for 
harvest on any given day, they can more effectively allocate their re­
sources and streamline their production processes. In addition, suppliers 
can leverage this data to enhance their transportation and distribution 
logistics by proactively scheduling the necessary resources to move large 
quantities of produce to market. However, the maturity classification of 
tomato clusters is a daunting challenge, as either the tomato fruit tip or 
light can interfere with the accuracy of ripeness detection. Only when 
such problems are solved can the inspection robot better serve farmers 
and genuinely automate yield estimation. 
4. Conclusion 
This paper presents the improvements in detection, tracking, and 
counting methods to achieve tomato cluster yield estimation in a 
greenhouse scenario. To reduce background tomato misidentification, 
we proposed the improved YOLOv5-4D based on RGB-D fusion, which 
achieved an accuracy of 97.9 % and a mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.748. We 
adopted the ByteTrack MOT method to track tomato clusters and 
designed a specific tracking region counting method to overcome the 
problem of tracked tomato cluster ID shift in consecutive greenhouse 
scenarios, which effectively reduced the duplicate counts. Field test re­
sults showed that the statistical average counting accuracy of this 
method reached 95.1 % and the integrated algorithm ran faster than 40 
FPS, enabling stable and real-time yield estimation. Experiments clarify 
the effectiveness of machine counting tomato clusters. In the future, 
after further classification of tomato maturity, the yield prediction 
technology will have a positive impact on both the supply chain and the 
market.
