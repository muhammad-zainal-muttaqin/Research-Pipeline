a b s t r a c t
Accurate fruit counting is a critical component of yield estimation and harvest planning in oil palm plantations, 
yet manual counting remains labor-intensive and unreliable due to dense clustering, occlusion, and illumination 
variability under real ﬁeld conditions. To address this challenge, this study introduces a density-aware bench-
mark dataset comprising 539 high-resolution RGB images of oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) collected in 
natural plantation environments in East Kalimantan, Indonesia. The dataset includes instance-level bounding-
box annotations and captures substantial variability in fruit density, maturity stages, viewing angles, and lighting 
conditions. Three state-of-the-art deep learning architectures: YOLOv5, YOLOv8, and Mask R-CNN, were com-
paratively evaluated under a standardized image-level dataset split and common evaluation protocol. Beyond 
conventional detection metrics, including Precision, Recall, and mAP, a density-level counting analysis was con-
ducted using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across low-, medium-, and high-density fruit scenarios. Experimental 
results demonstrate that YOLOv5 achieved the most balanced overall performance, attaining a Precision of 0.963, 
Recall of 0.946, F1-score of 0.954, and the lowest global counting error, with an MAE of 4.96 fruits per image. 
YOLOv8 showed competitive detection capability with stable convergence behavior, while Mask R-CNN provided 
a complementary instance-based comparison but exhibited increased counting variability in high-density con-
ditions. The ﬁndings conﬁrm that lightweight one-stage detectors, particularly YOLOv5, oﬀer a robust trade-oﬀ 
between detection accuracy and counting stability for practical deployment in large-scale oil palm plantations. 
The proposed density-aware benchmark and evaluation framework establish a reproducible foundation for scal-
able, AI-driven yield estimation in precision palm oil agriculture.
1.  Introduction
Oil palm (Elaeis guineensis Jacq.) plays a pivotal role in the global 
vegetable oil industry, with Indonesia standing as the largest producer 
worldwide. The sector contributes substantially to national GDP and 
export revenues, reaching approximately 3.27% of Indonesia’s GDP in 
2019 [1,2]. Fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) serve as the primary raw ma-
terial for crude palm oil, which is widely utilized in food, cosmet-
ics, biodiesel, and detergent industries [3,4]. In this context, accurate 
estimation of fruit quantity per bunch is essential for yield forecast-
ing, harvest scheduling, and eﬃcient resource allocation in precision 
agriculture [5,6]. Unlike ground-level crops such as apples or straw-
berries, oil palm fruits grow in compact, high-altitude bunches, often
obscured by fronds and overlapping structures [7,8]. These visual com-

2.  Related works
The development of robust automated fruit counting systems is cen-
tral to modern precision agriculture, serving as the primary mecha-
nism for empirical yield forecasting and harvest optimization [18]. Un-
like classiﬁcation or detection alone, fruit counting requires resolving 
densely packed and partially occluded instances, which represents a 
technical challenge demanding both high recall and precise localization 
[29]. Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of deep learning 
for counting fruits in orchard environments. For example, Su et al.[19] 
achieved a MAE of 1.8 fruits per image for juvenile abalone counting 
using an improved SSD architecture, emphasizing the importance of in-
tegrating counting-speciﬁc metrics alongside standard detection bench-
marks. In apple orchards, Maheswari et al.[16] compared DeepLabv3+ 
and YOLO variants and found that lightweight models like YOLOv8 of-
fer the best trade-oﬀ between accuracy and inference speed for edge 
deployment. Similarly, Crespo et al.[7] reported that Mask R-CNN re-
duced strawberry counting error by 18% compared to bounding-box 
methods by leveraging pixel-level segmentation to disentangle overlap-
ping fruits. These ﬁndings highlight a key dichotomy in agricultural 
counting, wherein detection-based approaches prioritize speed while 
segmentation-based methods favor accuracy in clustered scenes.
YOLO architectures have become dominant in real-time agricultural 
vision due to their single-stage design and eﬃciency. Nan et al.[11] in-
troduced WGB-YOLO, a lightweight variant for pitaya detection, achiev-
ing 89% on mAP50 at 42 FPS on edge GPUs. YOLOv8 improves gradient 
ﬂow and multi-scale feature fusion via CSPDarknet and PANet, making 
it particularly suitable for small-object detection in cluttered agricul-
tural scenes [20]. In contrast, Mask R-CNN is widely used as an instance 
segmentation architecture because it can generate object-level masks in 
addition to bounding boxes. In dense agricultural scenes, this capability 
may help separate overlapping objects, although its computational cost 
is generally higher than one-stage detectors [7]. Although computation-
ally heavier, Mask R-CNN has been successfully adapted to agricultural 
contexts through optimization techniques such as TensorRT as in Crespo 
et al.[7] and model pruning as in Maheswari et al.[16]. These architec-
tural advancements underscore the importance of selecting models that 
align with the speciﬁc challenges of dense fruit counting, particularly 
in complex environments like oil palm plantations where occlusion and 
variability are prevalent.
Most computer vision research in oil palm has focused on ripeness 
classiﬁcation and disease detection, not fruit counting [10]. Septiarini 
et al.[13] used contour-based segmentation to classify ripeness stages 
but did not count individual fruits. Josdaan et al.[17] employed 
YOLOv8 for ripeness detection with 92% accuracy, yet their dataset 
lacked instance-level annotations. For disease, Kent et al.[15] used UAV 
imagery and ResNet to detect basal stem rot, while Hamdani et al.[14] 
applied color histograms for leaf disease classiﬁcation. However, none 
of these studies addressed quantitative yield estimation through direct 
fruit counting. Critically, no publicly available dataset exists for oil palm 
fruit counting. Existing resources such as datasets for oil palm disease 
or ripeness do not provide bounding boxes or masks at the fruit level. 
This gap limits reproducibility and benchmarking, hindering progress 
in automated yield estimation [5,6].
Recent literature emphasizes the need for multi-metric evaluation in 
agricultural computer vision. While mAP and Precision or Recall remain 
standard, studies increasingly incorporate counting accuracy (MAE) and 
inference eﬃciency (FPS) to assess real-world deployability [19]. For in-
stance, Suharjito et al.[9] evaluated fruit detectors not only on detection 
performance but also on robustness to occlusion and lighting variation 
factors highly relevant to oil palm plantations. This evaluation frame-
work aligns with the operational needs of plantation managers, who re-
quire systems that are not only accurate but also fast, lightweight, and 
robust under ﬁeld conditions.
3.  Material and methods
The methodological workﬂow of this study comprises ﬁve main 
stages: data acquisition, preprocessing and augmentation, ground-truth 
labeling, model development, and evaluation. The overall process fol-
lows a structured pipeline to ensure the reproducibility and robustness 
of the proposed fruit counting framework. The ﬁrst stage, data acquisi-
tion, involved capturing high-resolution RGB images of oil palm fresh 
fruit bunches (FFBs) under natural ﬁeld conditions. Images were col-
lected from multiple viewpoints including top-down, side, and oblique 
angles to capture variations in bunch orientation, lighting, and occlu-
sion. These diverse acquisition conditions represent the real-world com-
plexity of plantation environments to ensure that the dataset is suitable 
for both controlled and operational settings. The workﬂow of the pro-
posed methodology can be seen in Fig. 1.
In the preprocessing stage, each image was resized to 640 × 640 pixels 
to standardize the input size for model training. There was no sliding-
window patch extraction or overlapping patch-based splitting was used 
in the ﬁnal experimental pipeline. Various augmentation techniques 
were applied to enhance the model’s ability to generalize across environ-
mental conditions. These included random horizontal ﬂipping, Contrast-
Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), rotation, and ran-
dom brightness-contrast adjustment. This augmentation step eﬀectively 
simulates illumination diﬀerences, camera angle variations, and occlu-
sion commonly encountered during data collection. The third stage, 
ground-truth generation, focused on manual annotation of visible in-
dividual fruit instances within each bunch. Human annotators marked 
individual fruits using bounding boxes, and the number of annotated 
boxes in each image was used as the reference fruit count. This dataset 
served as the foundation for training and validating the detection and 
counting models. Next, in the model development phase, the annotated 
dataset was split into training, validation/testing subsets.
The training data were used to optimize the model parameters, while 
the validation set guided early stopping and learning-rate adjustments. 
The model architecture consisted of a backbone convolutional network 
that extracted multiscale feature maps, followed by a region proposal 
network and fully connected layers for object classiﬁcation, bounding 
box regression, and mask prediction. The framework was trained to pre-
dict the number of detected fruits per bunch by integrating both spatial

3.1.  Data acquisition
The dataset was collected in Kutai Timur Regency, East Kalimantan, 
Indonesia (0.5607◦N, 117.4245◦E) during the early 2025 harvest sea-
son. A total of 539 RGB images of oil palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) 
were captured using a Xiaomi 12 Pro-smartphone (50 MP, f/1.9 aper-
ture) under natural daylight, without artiﬁcial lighting or ﬂash. Fruit 
bunches on-tree images were photographed from distances of 1.0–1.5 m 
and multiple angles (top-down, side, oblique) to maximize variability in 
occlusion and background.
The images exhibit diverse maturity stages (reddish orange to deep 
black), fruit densities, and levels of ﬁber occlusion, conditions represen-
tative of real plantation environments [13]. This variability is critical 
for developing models that generalize across operational scenarios Fig. 2 
shows representative examples of oil palm bunches with diﬀerent visual 
characteristics.

3.3.  Models architecture
This subsection introduces the deep learning architectures evaluated 
in this study. Both one-stage object detectors and a two-stage instance-
based model were selected to analyze the trade-oﬀs between detection 
accuracy, counting robustness, and computational eﬃciency in dense 
fruit scenarios. Two state-of-the-art architectures were evaluated:
1. YOLOv5s [21] and YOLOv8n [20]: Single-stage object detectors op-
timized for speed and accuracy. Uses CSPDarknet backbone, PANet 
neck, and decoupled detection head. Selected for edge deployment 
feasibility.
2. Mask R-CNN (ResNet-50 + FPN) [22]: A two-stage instance-based 
model that performs object localization and can generate instance 
masks. Included as a complementary architecture to examine detec-
tion and counting behavior in dense and occluded fruit clusters.
The three architectures were selected to represent two diﬀerent 
methodological families for oil palm fruit counting. YOLOv5s and 
YOLOv8n represent lightweight one-stage object detectors that are suit-
able for real-time deployment, while Mask R-CNN represents a two-stage 
instance segmentation approach that can provide pixel-level object sep-
aration in dense and occluded fruit clusters. Therefore, the comparison 
designed as an architecture-level benchmark between detection-based 
and segmentation-based approaches.
This design allows the study to assess whether lightweight object 
detectors or a two-stage instance-based architecture are more suitable 
for practical oil palm fruit detection and counting under natural ﬁeld 
conditions. Therefore, YOLOv5, YOLOv8, and Mask R-CNN were com-
pared using common detection and counting metrics. Mask R-CNN was 
included to provide an instance-based comparison because its architec-
ture can separate object instances in dense scenes, but the main reported 
outcomes remain detection accuracy and counting error.
All models were implemented using the PyTorch framework and 
trained on the same dataset split to ensure a fair comparison. The ar-
chitectures were chosen based on their proven performance in agricul-
tural vision tasks and their complementary strengths in detection and 
segmentation, providing a comprehensive evaluation of approaches for 
oil palm fruit counting under ﬁeld conditions.
3.3.1.  YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 architecture
YOLOv5 is a widely adopted single-stage object detection architec-
ture known for its balance between accuracy and inference speed. It 
utilizes a CSPDarknet-based backbone for feature extraction, which en-
hances gradient ﬂow while reducing computational redundancy. The 
neck incorporates a Path Aggregation Network (PAN) structure that fa-
cilitates multi-scale feature fusion, improving the model’s ability to de-
tect objects of varying sizes. The detection head employs a decoupled 
structure that separates classiﬁcation and localization tasks, combined 
with distributional focal loss to enhance bounding box regression ac-
curacy [21]. YOLOv5 was selected for its real-time detection capabil-
ity, making it suitable for deployment on edge devices such as smart-
phones used in oil palm plantations. The overall network architecture 
of YOLOv5, including the backbone, neck, and detection head, is illus-
trated in Fig. 3.

3.3.2.  Mask R-CNN architecture
While bounding box detection is eﬀective for most scenarios, occlu-
sion and dense clustering in oil palm bunches may result in underesti-
mation when fruits overlap. To examine this issue, Mask R-CNN was in-
cluded as a complementary two-stage instance-based architecture. Mask 
R-CNN extends Faster R-CNN by adding an additional branch for in-
stance mask prediction while also producing object classiﬁcation and 
bounding box localization outputs [22].
In the context of this study, Mask R-CNN was evaluated primarily 
using detection and counting metrics to maintain comparability with 
YOLOv5 and YOLOv8. Its inclusion was intended to assess whether an 
instance-based architecture could improve robustness in dense fruit re-
gions. Therefore, the comparison focuses on Precision, Recall, F1-score, 
mAP where applicable, and MAE, rather than positioning this work as a 
standalone image segmentation study.
The segmentation masks provided by Mask R-CNN allow for a more 
precise delineation of individual fruits, even in cases where multiple 
fruits overlap within a bunch. Such capability is particularly valuable 
for oil palm fruit estimation, where bunches typically exhibit dense 
packing and partial occlusion by ﬁbers. Previous agricultural studies 
have shown that Mask R-CNN achieves superior performance in tasks

3.3.3.  Network training
The training of the deep learning models was performed using 
Google Colaboratory equipped with an NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB 
VRAM). The runtime environment utilized Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.0, 
CUDA 11.8, and cuDNN 8.7, ensuring compatibility with current deep 
learning frameworks. Model training and evaluation were executed 
within the Colab Pro+ environment, which provided approximately 51 
GB of system RAM and 100 GB of temporary storage. This conﬁguration 
enabled eﬃcient parallel processing and reproducibility across multi-
ple experimental runs, ensuring reproducibility and compatibility with 
modern deep learning frameworks.
The network weights were initialized from the pre-trained model to 
leverage transfer learning, as commonly practiced in agricultural object 
detection Nan et al.[11]. Images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels and 
trained for 100 epochs, following the standardized comparison protocol 
used in this study [3].
The optimizer was Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with a mo-
mentum of 0.937 and a weight decay of 5e-4, following best practices 
in YOLO optimization [20]. The learning rate was initialized at 0.01 
and adjusted dynamically using a cosine annealing scheduler. Data aug-
mentation techniques included random ﬂipping, mosaic augmentation, 
brightness adjustments (Â±20%), and random rotations (Â±10◦).
Early stopping was implemented with a patience of 10 epochs to 
prevent overﬁtting, and the best model weights were saved based on 
validation mAP. The same training protocol was applied to all three 
architectures to ensure a fair comparison of their performance under 
identical conditions. The hyperparameter settings for each model are 
summarized in Table 2.

3.4.  Standarized comparison protocol
To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison among the evalu-
ated architectures, a standardized experimental protocol was applied. 
Although YOLOv5s, YOLOv8n, and Mask R-CNN have diﬀerent archi-
tectural characteristics and architecture-speciﬁc training requirements, 
all models were evaluated using the same core experimental conditions. 
These included the same image-level dataset split, identical input resolu-
tion of 64 × 640 pixels, the same number of training epochs, pre-trained 
initialization, identical validation/testing subset, and the same evalua-
tion metrics.
The comparison was designed as a practical architecture-level bench-
mark rather than a full hyperparameter ablation study. YOLOv5s and 
YOLOv8n were included to represent lightweight one-stage object de-
tectors suitable for real-time deployment, whereas Mask R-CNN was in-
cluded to represent a two-stage instance segmentation model that may 
provide advantages in dense and occluded fruit regions. Therefore, the 
standardized protocol focused on maintaining consistency in dataset us-
age, input conﬁguration, training duration, and evaluation criteria while

each image was ﬁrst computed as the absolute diﬀerence between the 
predicted count and the manually annotated ground-truth count. Boot-
strap samples were then generated by resampling the image-level errors 
with replacement, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap 
MAE distribution were used as the lower and upper bounds of the 95% 
CI.

conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test because the counting er-
rors were paired across the same image set and were not assumed to fol-
low a normal distribution. A signiﬁcance level of (p < 0.05) was used to 
determine whether the diﬀerence between two models was statistically 
signiﬁcant.

To complement the quantitative evaluation metrics, a confusion ma-

trative manner to highlight the relationship between correctly detected 
instances and missed detections, rather than to represent true negative 
background classiﬁcations. This visualization helps to better understand 
the detection reliability of the models on the validation set.
Confusion matrices illustrate the instance-level detection perfor-
mance for the oil palm fruit class. As shown in Fig. 12a, YOLOv8n cor-
rectly detects approximately 95% of fruit instances, with around 5% 
missed detections. Fig. 12b indicates that YOLOv5s achieves a higher 
true positive rate of approximately 98%, reducing the missed detection 
rate to around 2%. This conﬁrms the stronger recall performance of 
YOLOv5s observed in Table 6.

93% of fruit instances, with approximately 7% missed detections. Al-
though the detection performance remains competitive, the slightly 
higher false detection and missed detection rates explain its lower F1-
score compared to YOLOv5s. It is important to note that the absence 
of true negative values in the matrices reﬂects the instance-level na-
ture of object detection tasks, where background regions are not ex-
plicitly treated as independent classiﬁcation samples. Therefore, preci-
sion, recall, and F1-score remain the primary performance indicators 
for comparative evaluation. Overall, the quantitative results conﬁrming 
that YOLOv5s achieves the most balanced detection performance among 
the evaluated models.

4.6.  Limitations, future work, and real-world application
Although this study provides a benchmark dataset and compara-
tive evaluation for oil palm fruit detection and counting under natural 
ﬁeld conditions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the 
dataset was collected from a speciﬁc plantation area in East Kaliman-
tan, Indonesia. Although the images include variations in lighting, fruit 
density, maturity stage, viewing angle, and occlusion level, the dataset 
may not fully represent broader variations across diﬀerent plantation 
regions, cultivars, seasonal conditions, camera devices, and manage-
ment practices. Therefore, future work should expand the dataset across 
multiple geographic locations and acquisition periods to improve model 
generalization.
Second, the current evaluation was conducted using an image-level 
validation/testing subset derived from the available annotated dataset. 
While this setup provides a consistent basis for comparing YOLOv5, 
YOLOv8, and Mask R-CNN, a larger independent external test set would 
further strengthen the assessment of model robustness. Future studies 
should include cross-site validation or external testing using images col-
lected from diﬀerent plantations to better evaluate the transferability of 
the proposed approach.
Third, the counting results were derived directly from valid detec-
tion outputs after conﬁdence ﬁltering and Non-Maximum Suppression. 
This approach is practical and straightforward, but counting errors may 
still occur in dense bunches where fruits are highly overlapping, par-
tially hidden by ﬁbers, or visually merged with neighboring fruits. Fu-
ture work may incorporate density-aware post-processing, instance re-
ﬁnement, or hybrid detection-segmentation strategies to reduce under-
counting and overcounting in highly clustered regions.

5.  Conclusion
This study presented a comprehensive evaluation of three modern 
deep learning architectures YOLOv5s, YOLOv8n, and Mask R-CNN for 
automated oil palm fruit detection and counting using a newly de-
veloped annotated dataset consisting of 539 ﬁeld-acquired RGB im-
ages with instance-level labels. The dataset contributes to bridging 
the gap in agricultural computer vision research by providing struc-
tured annotations for dense fruit bunch detection. Experimental re-
sults demonstrate that YOLOv5s achieved the most balanced overall 
performance across detection and counting metrics. It obtained the 
highest Precision (0.963), Recall (0.946), and F1-score (0.954), while 
also exhibiting the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) across vary-
ing fruit density levels. YOLOv8n showed competitive performance 
with stable convergence behavior, though slightly lower recall com-
pared to YOLOv5s. Mask R-CNN delivered reliable detection capabil-
ity but demonstrated increased counting variability in high-density
scenarios.
The ﬁndings conﬁrm that lightweight one-stage detectors, particu-
larly YOLOv5s, are highly suitable for practical oil palm fruit detection 
and counting applications, especially in large-scale plantation environ-
ments. Their balance between detection robustness and counting sta-
bility makes them strong candidates for ﬁeld deployment using drone 
or mobile-based platforms. Future work will focus on expanding the 
dataset across multiple geographic regions, improving performance un-
der severe occlusion conditions, and optimizing model deployment for 
embedded edge devices. This research establishes a solid foundation for 
scalable, AI-driven yield estimation systems in precision palm oil agri-
culture.
