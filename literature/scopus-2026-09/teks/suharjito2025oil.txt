This article presents a dataset of oil palm Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs) images from commercial 
plantations in Central Kalimantan, Indonesia, focusing on five maturity stages: Unripe, Underripe, Ripe, 
Flower, and Abnormal. The data collection involved smartphone video recordings of unharvested trees 
from multiple angles under varying conditions. Video frames were extracted and expertly annotated 
using Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT), with annotations exported in Common Objects in 
Context (COCO) format suitable for object detection tasks. It has 10,207 images in its training set, 
2,896 in the validation set, and 1,400 in the test set, which are supplemented using data augmentation 
to handle class imbalance and increase variation. These images have real-world complications arising 
from partial visibility, low contrast, occlusion, and blurriness. It forms the basis that will support the 
development of deep learning models for detection and classification of FFB, particularly for monitoring 
of harvest times, yield prediction, and optimization of resources in plantation operations.
Background & Summary
Manual approaches to counting palm oil fruits are inherently slow, costly, and lack scalability, underscoring 
the need for automated system1. The complexity arises from the diverse shapes and ripeness stages of palm oil 
FFBs, making precise visual detection challenging. Advancements in artificial intelligence, particularly machine 
vision-based technologies, offer a more efficient alternative to manual counting2,3. However, existing datasets 
for FFB detection on plantation sites are limited due to the lack of comprehensive training data4,5. Creating a 
plantation dataset is crucial for monitoring harvest times, predicting dates, optimizing resource management, 
and minimizing waste in harvest operations6. The necessity arises from the complexities of palm oil cultivation, 
with FFBs located at various heights, resulting in images of varying detail and quality due to distance constraints 
and occlusions. Many studies focus on harvested fruits; however, datasets captured in plantation environments 
often contain a limited number of images and are less effective in capturing the challenging conditions where 
fruits are frequently occluded, clustered, or overlapping. This novel dataset supports our research on developing 
deep learning models for detection and counting in plantations7, serving as the main dataset for training and 
validation to verify the possibility of automatically counting and identifying FFBs in plantation environments.
This dataset comprises images of oil palm FFBs captured directly taken from the trees in plantations located 
in Central Kalimantan, Indonesia. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the dataset focuses on categorizing oil palm fruits 
into five maturity stages: Unripe (fruits in the initial stage), Underripe (fruits in the transitional stage), Ripe 
(fruits ready for harvesting), Flower (representing the flowering stage), and Abnormal (fruit with atypical char-
acteristics due to potential disease or irregularity). Oppositely, many datasets focus either on harvested fruits, 
few numbers of classes, or single FFB per photo, as noted in Table 1, this dataset captures FFBs in their natu-
ral unharvested state, within the plantation environment. This approach, while exposing challenges of occlu-
sions, conditions all going well, deliberately mimics conditions that practitioners face in field conditions rather

Methods
The creation of this dataset involved several stages, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Initially, video data of oil palm FFBs 
was collected from commercial oil palm plantations in Central Kalimantan province, Indonesia, in 2023. Using 
a smartphone video camera, footage was captured under various conditions, including different tree heights and 
recording times. The data collection process entailed recording videos by rotating around each tree to capture 
all fruit images from multiple angles.
The captured videos were then extracted frame by frame into images using the CVAT (Computer Vision 
Annotation Tool) application. This facilitated the identification and annotation of fruit objects, which were 
categorized into five classes of ripeness levels: unripe, underripe, ripe, flower, and abnormal. Bounding boxes 
were drawn around each fruit and oil palm flower in the images. An expert, seasoned in quality control, 
defined the fruit ripeness levels to ensure accurate labeling and annotation. The annotation results were 
exported in COCO format, making the dataset suitable for processing using the YOLO (You Only Look Once) 
method.
Next, the dataset was split into three subsets with a ratio of 70% training data, 20% validation data, and 10% 
testing data. To address class imbalance and enhance data variation, data augmentation techniques were applied 
using the Albumentations library8. The augmentation methods included random cropping (80% probability), 
horizontal and vertical flips (50% probability), 90° rotations, brightness and contrast adjustments with a limit of 
20 (50% probability), addition of Gaussian noise, RGB shift, and HSV shift (50% probability), both with a shift 
limit value of 20. Data balancing was achieved through over-sampling and under-sampling methods to equalize 
the number of objects across classes.
Data Records
The dataset for this study was gathered from commercial oil palm plantations in Central Kalimantan Province, 
Indonesia, by recording videos at 30 frames per second inside the plantation9. The data used in this study were 
acquired with a smartphone to take video recordings of FFB on oil palm trees. The trees selected were 1–3 meters 
in height, and the recording process involved circling each tree to capture all visible FFB. Sample frames from 
these videos can be seen in Fig. 3. In total, 440 videos were collected in this study, where the durations are rang-
ing from 8 seconds to 1 minute and 31 seconds. The recording resolution is 320 × 640 pixels (which is in portrait 
orientation). All those videos are stored in the MP4 format.

Initially, the dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets with a 70:20:10 ratio. However, through 
augmentation techniques, the dataset’s size increased significantly, resulting in the distribution shown in Table 2. 
The “Unripe” class has the most substantial representation, with 4,990 images in the training set, 1,699 in the 
validation set, and 764 in the test set. This is primarily due to the nature of the dataset, which focuses on FFBs 
in the plantation; naturally, there are more unripe fruits since most ripe ones are harvested promptly, leaving 
a larger number of unripe fruits to be documented. Conversely, the “Abnormal” class has the fewest images, 
reflecting its lower occurrence and the specific nature of these instances. Overall, the dataset consists of 10,207 
training images, 2,896 validation images, and 1,400 test images, providing a well-rounded and extensive set of 
data to train, validate, and test object detection models10.
The dataset presents numerous challenges for the detection and counting of FFBs due to complexities inher-
ent in real-world conditions. These challenges in Fig. 4 include partial visibility, where objects are only partially 
visible within the image frame (refer to a); low contrast, resulting from lighting conditions that cause classes to 
resemble one another (refer to b); occlusion, where objects are blocked by other objects (refer to c); and blurri-
ness, which reduces perceptual clarity (refer to d). These complexities mirror the actual conditions found in oil 
palm plantations, emphasizing the need for sophisticated models capable of accurately recognizing and classify-
ing FFBs in their natural state on the trees.

Technical Validation
Before deploying the models, the effectiveness of image augmentation used in this dataset needs to be evalu-
ated. While augmentation techniques were applied, there still remained significant class imbalances, especially 
between the “Unripe” and “Abnormal” classes. This is because FFBs in these developmental stages are inherently 
uncommon in plantations, hence setting a limit to how much augmentation can be done without risking over-
fitting of the models on repetitive, slightly modified versions of a few images. Ablation study in comparing the 
model trained with and without augmentations to assess its impacts despite the class disparities was presented 
in Table 3. The results confirmed the benefits of augmentation, evidenced by improvements in both mAP_0.5 
and mAP_0.5:0.95 scores.

Ultimately, the dataset was validated by the development of a palm oil FFB detection and counting model 
using YOLOv8, which was then implemented on a smartphone. Technical validation was performed by training 
YOLOv8n and YOLOv8s models with identical core hyperparameters for fair comparison, as shown in Table 4. 
Both models were configured to run for a maximum of 100 epochs, with batch sizes of 64 for YOLOv8n and 16 
for YOLOv8s. The input dimensions were set to 640 × 640 pixels with 3 input channels (RGB images). The learn-
ing rate was 0.01, using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer with a momentum of 0.9. Eight workers 
were used for data loading, and a random seed of 10 ensured reproducibility.
The training performance of the YOLOv8n model demonstrated smooth convergence across all monitored 
metrics (see Fig. 5). The training box loss, class loss, and distribution focal loss (DFL) consistently declined, indi-
cating effective learning as the number of epochs increased. Validation losses followed a similar trend, starting 
slightly higher due to differences in data complexity between training and validation sets. As can be seen from 
Table 5, YOLOv8n achieved a precision and recall of 0.725 and 0.711, respectively, at the final epoch, while its 
mAP scores show an overall improvement with minor variabilities.
On the other hand, the YOLOv8s model, in Fig. 6, had a similar learning trend but was automatically stopped 
at epoch 87 since there was no visible improvement for 50 epochs. While the training losses had a smooth, 
downwards trend, the validation losses were quite erratic, with box loss and DFL loss being especially erratic in 
terms of fluctuations in the validation data. In spite of that, all metrics for the YOLOv8s model were marginally 
better, where precision and recall achieved 0.738 and 0.719, respectively, while mAP_0.5 was 0.745, edging out 
the 0.735 for YOLOv8n. However, the consistent mAP_0.5:0.95 results obtained in both models show that the 
high precision of YOLOv8s did not help improve performance across the entire scale of IoU thresholds.

The other important aspect of technical validation involves model efficiency, referring to the use of the 
GPU and the size of the model. YOLOv8n has a GPU usage of 9.6GB, thus being much lightweight in contrast 
to YOLOv8s, which used 4.5GB of GPU memory. On the other hand, YOLOv8s had a much lower value for 
frames per second (FPS). Specifically, YOLOv8s ran in 36 FPS compared to YOLOv8n’s 90 FPS. On the opposite 
side, YOLOv8n has a smaller size at 6MB, whereas the YOLOv8s size is at 21MB. These metrics suggested a 
tradeoff between the size of the model and the processing speed. In particular, the higher GPU memory usage 
of YOLOv8n during training, despite its smaller model size, can be largely explained by the larger batch size

Lastly, Fig. 7 shows the application of the YOLOv8 model in a mobile-based e-census prototype specifically 
designed for FFBs detection and classification. This real-world implementation serves as a necessary validation 
mechanism to assert the model’s performance outside laboratory settings and into practical applications. This 
prototype can detect and classify the various maturity stages of FFBs directly at the site. The interface in Fig. 7 
gives real-time feedback on the recognized objects, showing the classification confidence for each FFB with its 
respective class label (e.g., ripe, underripe, unripe, flower, or abnormal). The ability of the system to process 
images very fast, with measured response times in milliseconds, emphasizes the efficiency of the model in prac-
tical scenarios. In the left part of Fig. 7, there are quite a number of FFBs detected and classified, together with 
confidence scores for each detected instance. Whereas, in the right part of Fig. 7, an example with fewer detected 
FFBs is shown, and the results are summarized in a pop-up interface. This proves that the system can adapt to 
changing conditions, such as, the number of visible FFBs or differences in image quality.
Usage Notes
This dataset has the following limitations:
	 1.	 This dataset was collected on oil palm trees with a height of between 1–3 meters so that this dataset does 
not represent every image of oil palm FFB in plantations that have tree heights above it.
	 2.	 This dataset was collected using a smartphone that has resolution limitations so that the right application 
for this dataset is an application that uses data input from a smartphone or similar embedded applications.
Code availability
The software used in this study includes CVAT (https://www.cvat.ai) for converting video data into frames and 
performing annotation, including labeling and creating bounding boxes for each object. Albumentations (https://
albumentations.ai/) was employed for image augmentation to enhance the training dataset. Additionally, the 
Ultralytics repository (https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics) was utilized for implementing YOLOv8 and 
customizing its configuration to suit the specific requirements of the research.
