Abstract—The accurate estimation of crop yield is crucial
in modern agriculture; however, traditional methods, such as
manual counting of grape clusters, often result in errors exceed-
ing 20% and are impractical for large-scale monitoring under
variable environmental and climatic conditions. While object
detection techniques have been successfully implemented in real-
world agricultural scenarios, the integration and performance of
multi-object tracking (MOT) algorithms in such dynamic and
unstructured environments remains underexplored. This article
provides a comparative evaluation of state-of-the-art object detec-
tion and tracking methodologies for use in vineyard conditions.
Here, two detection methods—YOLOv12 and RF-DETR—were
compared in combination with two tracking algorithms—BoT-
SORT and ByteTrack—to generate four distinct configurations
aimed at the automatic counting of grape clusters. Experimental
results on a video dataset recorded in agroindustrial vineyards
showed that the combination of YOLOv12 with BoT-SORT
achieved the highest average relative counting accuracy, with
84.31%. These findings confirm the potential of AI-based tech-
niques for precision agriculture, offering greater accuracy in yield
estimation, optimized resource management, and a substantial
reduction in reliance on manual labor.

I. INTRODUCTION
In modern agriculture, efficient management and monitor-
ing of agricultural resources are fundamental for optimizing
performance and ensuring sustainable practices. In viticulture,
accurate grape cluster counting directly influences production
prediction, harvest scheduling, and resource allocation. How-
ever, conventional fruit counting methods depend on human
experience and availability, with error rates exceeding 20%
under adverse conditions, generating production cost overruns
[1].
Advances in computer vision, particularly deep learning,
have improved agricultural automation by overcoming manual
counting limitations. YOLO architectures have demonstrated
92% accuracy in grape cluster identification under controlled
conditions using RGB images [2].
While object detection provides accurate identification of
grape clusters in individual frames, counting across time or
video sequences requires tracking each instance to avoid dupli-
cates or omissions. Therefore, to achieve reliable counting in
dynamic agricultural environments, it is essential to integrate
object detection with multi-object tracking (MOT) algorithms
such as BoT-SORT and ByteTrack, which address these issues
by maintaining object identities across frames, enabling more
accurate counting even in dense vineyard canopies [3].
Integrated real-time detection and tracking approaches show
potential for automating grape cluster counting. AgriSORT
implements the tracking-by-detection paradigm achieving
66.13% MOTA in real vineyard environments [4]. In [5],
YOLOv3 integrated with depth sensors achieves 95.7% ac-
curacy and 22 FPS in simulated environments using spatial
positioning logic for cluster detection. In [6], a hybrid Swin-
Transformer-YOLOv5 architecture obtains 97.4% mAP in
luminance variability scenarios, but without tracking modules,
limiting utility in dynamic tasks.
Despite these advances, key limitations persist. System
performance decreases under abrupt lighting changes, severe
occlusion, or dense overlap. Studies such as [7] show 15%
detection accuracy drops in poor lighting, highlighting the
need for robust models in unstructured outdoor conditions.
Furthermore, systematic comparative analyses of modern de-
tector–tracker combinations under these challenging condi-
tions remain underexplored.
This work presents a comparison of YOLOv12 and RF-
DETR architectures integrated with BoT-SORT and ByteTrack
algorithms for grape cluster counting. Building on established
individual algorithm performance, this research examines how
modern detection-tracking combinations perform under ad-
verse field conditions including partial occlusions, motion blur,
and high object density. The results offer empirical evidence
on the behavior of these integrated detector-tracker systems in
challenging agricultural environments.

II. MATERIALS AND METHODS
A. Data Acquisition and Detection Dataset
Data were collected in a vineyard during the final maturation
stage of the Allison grape variety. Images and videos were
captured under natural conditions to ensure the relevance of
the study in real agroindustrial scenarios. A total of 880 images
with a resolution of 4080 × 3072 pixels were collected, which
served as the basis for model training and validation.
The dataset was split into 80% for training (704 images),
10% for validation (88 images), and 10% for testing (88
images). Bounding boxes for grape clusters were generated
semi-automatically using the GroundingDINO model [8] and
manually verified to ensure labeling accuracy. To enhance
model robustness and prevent overfitting, data augmentation
techniques—such as random horizontal flips and color ad-
justments—were applied exclusively to the training set. As
a result, the training set was expanded to 2112 images.
The complete process for generating the grape cluster
detection dataset is illustrated in Figure 1.

B. Detection Algorithms Configuration
The selection of YOLOv12 and RF-DETR was based on
their complementary characteristics. YOLOv12 is recognized
for its high inference speed and ease of implementation,
making it suitable for real-time applications on devices with
limited computational resources. On the other hand, RF-
DETR employs a transformer-based architecture that is robust
in complex environments. This combination allowed for a
comparative analysis between convolutional neural network
(CNN)-based and transformer-based object detection models
under standardized conditions.
1) YOLOv12: Introduces an attention-centered architec-
ture that departs from the traditional CNN-based approaches
used in previous YOLO models, while preserving the real-
time inference speed essential for many applications. This
model achieves high accuracy in object detection through
novel methodological innovations in attention mechanisms
and overall network architecture, while maintaining real-time
performance [9].
In this study, the YOLOv12l (large) variant was employed
with a total of 60 epochs for training, a batch size of 16, the
SGD optimizer with momentum, and a learning rate of 10−4.
The loss function used was binary cross-entropy (BCE) for
classification and objectness tasks, and Complete IoU (CIoU)
loss for bounding box regression. The respective configuration
can be observed in Table I.

2) RF-DETR: It is based on the DETR (DEtection TRans-

long-range dependencies and models global context, making
it advantageous for scenarios with occlusions or overlapping

C. Tracking Algorithms Configuration
To ensure temporal consistency in object trajectories, the
detection outputs from YOLOv12 and RF-DETR are coupled
with two multi-object tracking (MOT) algorithms: ByteTrack
and BoT-SORT. These trackers were selected based on their
proven effectiveness in handling challenges such as occlusions,
variable motion, and identity preservation in video sequences.
A high-level overview of the input–output relationship in the
detection and tracking process is shown in Figure 2.

1) ByteTrack: It introduces a dual-threshold association
mechanism that improves object recovery under occlusion
by incorporating low-confidence detections during the data
association process [12]. It uses high-confidence detections
for primary matching and low-confidence detections (between
0.1 and 0.5) to recover targets that have been temporarily oc-
cluded. Additionally, it implements a Kalman filter for motion
prediction and employs a cost matrix based on Intersection
over Union (IoU) to optimize trajectory assignment. Five dis-
tinct parameter configurations were evaluated for experimental
validation, which are summarized in Table III.

It is based on traditional tracking-by-

the experimental combinations summarized in Table V. This

III. RESULTS
A. Training Performance Comparison
Figures 4 and 5 show the performance of the YOLOv12 and
RF-DETR models, respectively, in terms of mean Average Pre-
cision at IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 (mAP@[.50:.95])
across 60 epochs. These curves highlight the convergence
behavior and learning dynamics of each detection architecture.
Fig. 4: YOLOv12 detection performance across 60 epochs,
measured by mAP@[.50:.95]. The model gradually improves,
reaching 0.73 at convergence.
YOLOv12 demonstrates a steady performance trajectory,
starting with relatively low mAP values near 0.25 and steadily
improving throughout the first 30 epochs. After epoch 35,
mAP stabilizes with fluctuations between 0.70 and 0.73.
This suggests effective learning and generalization as training
progresses.
Fig. 5: RF-DETR detection performance across 60 epochs,
measured by mAP@[.50:.95]. The model starts strong with
high mAP values around 0.71 and remains stable throughout.
In contrast, RF-DETR starts with a high mAP value of 0.71
from the first epoch, showing minimal variation over time.
The curve remains relatively flat, fluctuating slightly between
0.72 and 0.74. This suggests that RF-DETR benefits from
strong pretraining or architectural advantages, enabling early
convergence. However, the lack of further improvement may
indicate limited responsiveness to continued optimization.
Overall, YOLOv12 exhibits a more typical learning curve
with progressive gains, while RF-DETR delivers immedi-
ate but plateaued performance. These trends align with
downstream results in object counting and tracking, where
YOLOv12 showed greater adaptability across diverse scenar-
ios.

consistency and adaptability. These outcomes likely stem from
compounded issues, including overcounting at the detection

IV. CONCLUSIONS
This study evaluated object counting performance across ten
videos using four detector-tracker combinations. YOLOv12
with BoT-SORT achieved the lowest MRE (15.69%), corre-
sponding to the highest relative counting accuracy of 84.31%,
along with the lowest MAE (17.16) and RMSE (20.80),
making it the most consistent pairing overall. Among the
five configurations tested, parameter configuration 3 yielded
the best results, with an MAE of 12.10, MRE of 10.89%,
and RMSE of 14.84, highlighting the importance of careful
parameter tuning.
In contrast, RF-DETR exhibited a tendency to overestimate
counts, with this behavior more pronounced when paired with
ByteTrack, reducing utility in precise-count tasks.
The results emphasize that effective model-tracker pairing is
critical, as sophisticated detectors alone do not guarantee accu-
rate counts. For high-accuracy counting tasks, well-calibrated
systems like YOLOv12 with BoT-SORT may outperform more
complex alternatives.
These findings suggest broader applications where this
perception model could support crop monitoring through inte-
gration with autonomous navigation platforms [14], though ex-
tension to different crop morphologies would require dedicated
recalibration, as demonstrated by the specialized parameter
optimization needed for citrus-specific detection systems [15].
Future work should explore deep learning-based tracking
algorithms such as pseudo-labelling approaches [16] and en-
hanced association methods like StrongSORT [17] that could
potentially offer improved accuracy and robustness compared
to the traditional tracking methods evaluated in this study.
