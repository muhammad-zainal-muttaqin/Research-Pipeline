Eﬀective yield prediction is crucial for farmers and the agricultural sector. It allows producers to enhance 
control over their operations and better align with market supply and demand. With the emergence of Artificial 
Intelligence (AI), various Machine Learning (ML) models have been developed to predict crop yield. In this 
study, we conducted a systematic literature review to examine the ML models used for predicting tomato yield, 
the features associated with the most eﬀective models, and the challenges faced by users. We retrieved 1,486 
scientific papers from six electronic databases. Following the PRISMA guidelines, we included 57 studies in our 
analysis. The results showed that 66.67% of the models achieving the best performance in predicting or estimating 
tomato yield are Deep Learning (DL) models, with neural networks accounting for 42.11% of these. Speciﬁcally, 
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Support Vector Regression (SVR) are the 
models most commonly used, demonstrating strong performance when considering factors such as climate, soil 
conditions, plant growth, fertilization, and irrigation. Additionally, when using computed vegetation indices from 
image data, Random Forest Regression (RFR) is frequently applied with notable success. The YOLO-Tomato and 
R-CNN methods are commonly used for detecting tomato fruits prior to yield estimation. Furthermore, DeepSort 
and linear regression are the predominant methods employed for counting and estimating tomato yield. For 
future research, it is important to conduct a comparative analysis of models such as LSTM, ANN, SVR, and RFR 
speciﬁcally for predicting tomato yield using data from Africa.
1. Introduction
Agriculture is the backbone of the economy in many countries [1]. 
Tomatoes are among the most widely grown vegetables worldwide [2]. 
It is a staple crop in many African countries and contributes to farm­
ers’ income and the local economy. In Togo, for example, tomato is the 
ﬁrst fruit vegetable. It represents 39.9% of the total vegetable produc­
tion in the country during the 2021-2022 campaign, and it is also one 
of the most consumed foodstuffs [3]. It is rich in vitamins, minerals, an­
tioxidants, and fiber, helping to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease 
and obesity. However, tomato production is often threatened by disease, 
pest attacks, climate variability, etc., causing signiﬁcant losses. More­
over, tomato growers cannot optimize their income because they often 
lack accurate crop yield forecasts, which limits their ability to anticipate 
available quantities and adapt their marketing and pricing strategies to

2. ML architecture
Machine learning (ML) techniques excel in extracting features that 
may be implicit to human experts, making decisions without explicit 
programming, and improving their performance automatically through 
accumulated experience [19]. ML methods are generally classified into 
four main frameworks: supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, and 
reinforcement learning [20].
2.1. Supervised learning techniques
Supervised learning maps inputs (𝑋) to outputs (𝑌) using labeled 
data, enabling predictions and classifications. The most commonly used 
form is linear regression [21]. Regression techniques are used primarily 
to infer causal relationships and make predictions or forecasts. Support 
Vector Machines (SVM), another supervised technique, aims to find the 
best hyperplanes that separate data classes by maximizing margins. A 
variant SVR is used for regression tasks. Then, decision trees (DT), a 
representative of logical learning methods, use tree structures to build 
regression or classification models. The result is a tree with decision 
nodes (features) and leaf nodes (outputs). Artificial neural networks 
(ANN) can approximate a diverse range of functions. A single-layer per­
ceptron is typically employed for linear regression, whereas complex 
Deep Neural Networks (DNN) can approximate non-linear functions due 
to their multiple layers. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are also 
Deep Neural Networks that learn from sequences and histories. In ad­
dition, Ensemble Learning (EL) combines multiple models strategically 
to solve a particular problem, with the primary goal of achieving better 
predictive performance than any individual model. Standard ensemble 
methods include random forests and Gradient Boosting (GB) [22]. Fur­
thermore, Hybrid Machine Learning (HML) models combine diﬀerent 
learning techniques to solve complex problems more eﬀectively.
2.2. Unsupervised learning techniques
Unsupervised learning is the process of finding previously unknown 
patterns based on unlabeled data sets. Principal component analysis 
(PCA) [23] and clustering analysis [24] are two popular techniques. 
The two main objectives of clustering are the search for potentially 
hidden structures or patterns in the data sets and the appropriate clas­
sification of the raw data. Clustering involves dividing data objects into 
disjoint clusters according to a similarity metric so that objects within 
the cluster are comparable and objects outside the cluster have mini­
mal similarities. K-means clustering is one of the most widely used and 
straightforward clustering methods. PCA is a statistical method used to 
reduce the dimensionality of a dataset while retaining as much informa­
tion as possible by projecting the initial variables onto new dimensions 
called principal components. This allows the underlying structures of 
the data to be identified, the relationships between variables and indi­
viduals to be visualized, and the interpretation of these relationships to 
be facilitated.
2.3. Semi-supervised learning techniques
Semi-supervised learning combines a signiﬁcant amount of unla­
beled data with a small amount of labeled data [25]. It is beneficial 
when data labeling is expensive or complex, but many unlabeled data 
is available. This approach improves model accuracy by exploiting the 
unlabeled data’s underlying structure to better generalize predictions.

3. Methodology
3.1. Review protocol
A review protocol is established before starting a systematic review 
to provide a roadmap for the study. The research questions are initially 
formulated, and relevant databases are identified to select pertinent 
studies. Next, a search strategy is implemented based on specific key­
words within the chosen databases. We apply a set of exclusion criteria 
to filter and evaluate the relevant research. Details regarding the num­
ber of studies identified, screened, and included or excluded at each 
stage are documented. After gathering all relevant information from the 
selected papers, the extracted data are synthesized to address the re­
search questions. This process constitutes the results obtained in our 
review. The protocol is shown in Fig. 1.
3.2. Research questions
This systematic literature review (SLR) aims to collect information 
on published research on machine learning (ML) and tomato yield pre­
diction. The studies have been analyzed from various perspectives to 
fully understand the topic. Ten research questions (RQs) have been for­
mulated as part of this SLR. 
RQ1: What is the objective behind the use of ML models? 
RQ2: What are the groups and the source of data used? 
RQ3: What is the country-wise distribution of the self made-data? 
RQ4: What features have been used in the literature for the prediction 
of tomato yield using machine learning? 
RQ5: What models were used? 
RQ6: What is the relationship between the models used and the region 
in which the study was conducted? 
RQ7: What are the best models in terms of performance? 
RQ8: What are the evaluation metrics? 
RQ9: What are the best and most commonly used models? 
RQ10: What are the gaps and perspectives?
3.3. Description of the search strategy
The search was performed in six databases. The scientific papers 
were searched using Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar from 
the Research4life platform, Science Direct, Wiley, and Springer link. 
The following keywords were used: ``Artificial intelligence'', ``Machine 
learning'', ``Deep learning'', ``Tomato yield'', ``Tomato yield prediction'', 
“Tomato yield estimation'', and ``Tomato yield forecasting''. To avoid 
missing relevant studies, the search string is as follows: ((``Artificial in­
telligence'' OR ``Machine learning'' OR ``Deep learning'') AND (``Tomato 
yield'' OR ``Tomato yield prediction'' OR ``Tomato yield estimation'' OR 
“Tomato yield forecasting'')).
3.4. Study selection
To improve the transparency and scientific merit of the SLR, we 
used the Preferred Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 
guidelines [27]. After executing the search string, 1435 studies were 
retrieved. In the study, 165 papers are identified from Web of Science, 
321 from Scopus, 829 from Google Scholar, 46 from Science Direct, 12 
from Wiley, and 62 from Springer Link. The Mendeley software was 
used to manage the identified papers. The articles were examined and 
classified according to the exclusion criteria to determine the parame­
ters of the systematic review and exclude irrelevant studies. These are

the exclusion criteria displayed in the case: (i) publication is not related 
to the agricultural sector and yield prediction or estimation or forecast­
ing combined with machine learning, (ii) publication is not written in 
English or French, and (iii) conference abstract or editorial.
The first screening procedure was applied by excluding duplicates. 
Mendeley showed 319 duplicate papers. By screening the titles and the 
abstracts, 767 and 271 papers were respectively excluded. After the full 
content screening, 21 irrelevant papers were excluded. Among the irrel­
evant papers, some have discussed predicting tomato yield using other 
methods that are not ML, others discussed the prediction of yield using 
ML techniques but on other crops. Others have addressed the use of ML 
for other tasks like monitoring the growth of the tomato plant, and the 
price of a tomato. Finally, 57 papers were included in the study. Seven 
of the 57 papers were not free of charge, so we used only their abstracts. 
The data that was collected on these papers concerns the name of the au­

3.5. The literature synthesis and statistics analysis
The literature synthesis is divided into three sections. The first sec­
tion provides a broad analysis of descriptive statistics. The second sec­
tion addresses the research questions and their corresponding answers. 
We calculated the frequencies based on the occurrence of each response. 
We employed the chi-square test to determine whether there is a signif­
icant association between the learning techniques used and the region 
where the study was conducted. This test is based on the chi-square 
statistic, which measures the diﬀerence between observed and expected 
frequencies in a contingency table. The chi-square test has some limi­
tations; speciﬁcally, the expected frequency for each cell in the contin­
gency table must be equal to or greater than 5 [28]. In contrast, the 
Fisher exact test does not impose specific conditions on the expected 
(theoretical) frequencies [29]. Like the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact 
test is also applied to a contingency table, but the p-value for Fisher’s 
exact test is computed based on the hypergeometric distribution.
The study provided valuable insights through the analysis of a com­
bination of variables. The findings were presented using bar charts, pie 
charts, and tables. Data analysis was conducted in a Jupyter Notebook 
environment using Python 3.9.12 within Anaconda. The visualizations 
were created from data frame synthesis utilizing the Matplotlib, NumPy, 
and Pandas libraries. Additionally, we employed the ‘ggplot2’ package 
[30] from R software [31]. ArcMap was used to illustrate the geograph­
ical locations of the collection sites. Finally, the identified gaps were 
compiled and ranked according to their relevance, and suggestions for 
further research were provided.

4.1.2. RQ1: what is the objective behind the use of the ML techniques?
Based on the included papers, the objectives were divided into two 
large groups: yield prediction and yield estimation. Table 1 presents the 
distribution of papers according to the study’s objectives. Thirty-six pa­
pers (63.15%) of the 57 worked on tomato yield prediction, while 21 
papers (36.85%) worked on tomato yield estimation. Yield prediction 
involves forecasting future agricultural production using statistical or 
machine learning models. Its primary purpose is to assist in decision­
making prior to harvesting, including resource planning, logistics, and 
trade. In contrast, yield estimation is about assessing current or past 
production levels based on field observations or empirical models. Fur­
thermore, 73.68% of the papers (42 papers) expressed the yield by the 
weight of tomatoes per unit of area, while 19.3% of the papers (11 pa­
pers) expressed it by the number of tomato fruits, and the rest expressed 
the yield by the weight of tomato fruit or the tomato fruit mass per plant.
4.1.3. RQ2: what are the groups and the source of data used?
The research work selected for predicting tomato yield generally 
used seven (07) groups of data (Table 2): climatic data, image data, 
data on plant growth, soil property, fertilization, irrigation, and produc­
tivity. Climatic variables are the most used among the selected papers 
(66.67%) and most chosen as the essential factor in yield prediction. 
Besides the climatic variables, 42% of papers (24) used image data, 
while 29.82% of them (17 papers) used data linked to plant growth, 
and 10.52% (06 papers) used data related to soil properties. Moreover, 
6 papers used fertilization data, and 3 papers used irrigation and soil 
characteristics data. Other data groups, such as soil management, the 
cultivation technique applied, the maintenance of the crop, the variety 
of tomatoes grown, and the pest and disease data, are used in 5 papers.
The data sources (Fig. 5) are much more related to the areas in which 
the data used in the papers were collected. Indeed, 37 papers (61.7%) 
collected data inside the greenhouses, the environment with the best 
control of production factors, 18 (30%) in fields, 3 added features from 
weather stations for collecting climatic information of the study area, 
and 2 papers obtained their data online. The data are collected using 
various devices such as sensors, cameras, robots, drones, and other ar­
tifacts. Some data, like productivity data, was collected by humans.

4.1.5. RQ4: which features have been used in literature for tomato yield 
prediction using machine learning?
The features are the variables used in the ML models as inputs to 
predict tomato yield. In this study, many features were used (Table 3). 
Regarding climatic variables, we recorded several factors, including 
temperature, carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, vapor pressure deficit (VPD), 
radiation, transpiration, humidity, precipitation, plant evapotranspira­
tion, dew point, wind speed, and leaf wetness duration.
For features related to plant growth, papers examine aspects such 
as planting date, planting density, stem elongation, plant length (or 
height), plant width, leaf length, leaf width, flowering speed, number of 
leaves, truss height, leaf area, stem diameter, flowering truss, number 
of flowers, set truss, set speed, leaflet length and width, stem thickness 
(measured between the third and fourth nodes), height of the flower, 
and the Leaf Area Index (LAI).
The properties of soil include various parameters such as soil texture, 
pH, electrical conductivity, organic matter content, and nutrient levels. 
When analyzing features extracted from images, we consider two main 
categories.
Firstly, we have image characteristics, which include image height, 
spectral bands, and dimensions of detected fruits (length and width), as 
well as canopy volume. Additional features include measurements in the 
Near-infrared (NIR), Red edge, and Short Wave Infrared (SWIR) regions, 
along with overall image shape, texture, color, and geometric attributes. 
Secondly, we utilize vegetation indices derived from images, such as the 
Normalized Diﬀerence Vegetation Index (NDVI), Perpendicular Vegeta­
tion Index (PVI), Green Chlorophyll Vegetation Index (GCVI), Normal­
ized Diﬀerence Red Edge Index (NDRE), and the Weighted Diﬀerence 
Vegetation Index (WDVI). In terms of fertilization data, we categorize it 
into organic and inorganic fertilizers (like NPK). Finally, we also gather 
data on irrigation practices.
Regarding the group of variables used (Table 3), 16 papers used only 
climatic data as input variables, and 14 papers used only the image fea­
tures to predict or estimate the tomato yield. On the other hand, 7 papers 
combined climate data and plant-growth data, 7 papers combined the 
VI with image features, and one article combined the VI with the cli­
matic data. Most of the papers (45) used only one or at most two groups 
of features for the prediction or estimation. While few papers (12) have 
combined more than two variable types to predict or estimate tomato 
yield. But there are not many of them. It should be remembered that for 
the most part, this is not due to variable selection, but the initial input 
variables were not diverse enough.

4.1.11. RQ10: what are the gaps and perspectives?
The ability to predict or estimate tomato yield is crucial for eﬀective 
management of experimental fields or agricultural farms. The research 
papers reviewed have signiﬁcantly contributed to this area by employ­
ing AI for tomato yield prediction. However, there are six important gaps 
in the current understanding. The first and most critical gap relates to 
the data used for training and testing models. A majority of the stud­
ies, speciﬁcally 61.7% were conducted in greenhouses. This approach is 
quite costly and limits the amount of data that can be gathered [36,76]. 
The second gap in research is the diﬃculty models face in providing 
a mechanistic explanation for the factors that influence tomato yield 
increases [32]. While there are model-agnostic interpretation methods 
available that can be applied to any model to explain these ``black-box'' 
approaches, they are not commonly utilized. The third gap pertains to 
the types of data used for prediction. Approximately 80% of the stud­
ies (45 out of 57) focused on predicting tomato yield primarily utilized 
a single data type or, at most, two types of data. This limitation means 
that the eﬀects of other variables on yield are often overlooked during 
the prediction process.
The fourth gap concerns the use of images for predicting tomato 
yield. About 50% of the papers that employed images for tomato de­
tection revealed a weakness in their models due to overlapping objects

4.2. Discussion
This study looks deeply into the classic ML models and the DL models 
which are used to predict or estimate the yield of tomatoes in diﬀerent 
environments. Most papers collected their data on greenhouses. This re­
veals that the greenhouse plays a crucial role in predicting tomato yield 
in the field of precision agriculture [79]. Many features are used only 
once and are of a large number. This limits the integral and participa­
tory analysis of these features in selecting the best features used. The 
statistical tests prove evidence of an association between the learning 
techniques used and the regions of study. Indeed, this study shows that 
when we are faced with climate data it is much more usual to use deep 
learning models, more precisely neural network models. This confirms 
the work of [80], which focused on neural network models for predict­
ing tomato yield. Furthermore, there are other potential factors such 
as data on the soil, plant growth, and data on the fertilization applied. 
With these data combined with climate data, neural networks are be­
coming even more common for tomato yield prediction. This confirms 
the work of [81] on crop yield prediction using machine learning, where 
he proved that the DL model such as LSTM [37] and ANN [69,70] are 
the most applied algorithms for yield prediction. Moreover, ML such 
as SVR [73] is also one of the most applied models when using these 
features as predictors. However, When it comes to vegetation indices 
calculated from images, which were used to estimate tomato yield, it 
is much more usual to use RF models. This is supported by [82] for 
whom RF are robust ensemble models that can be used for regression. 
Furthermore, when the estimation is based on the feature images that 
can perform the detection before estimating the yield, the most chosen 
algorithms for detection are YOLO-Tomato [83,84] with all the exten­
sions of improvement and version of YOLO [85--87]. Also, the R-CNN 
[88] with the extensions Mask R-CNN and Faster R-CNN [89,57], with 
then some regression like DeepSort [63] or Linear Regression [88] to es­
timate the tomato yield in the field. Beyond all of the above, the choice 
of model must be not only a function of the quality of the model but 
also other factors such as sample size.
5. Conclusion
This study presents selected publications that utilized various fea­
tures to predict or estimate tomato yield. Among these, climatic features 
are the most commonly used for tomato yield prediction. The analysis 
of results indicates a signiﬁcant relationship between the learning tech­
niques employed and the regions where the studies were conducted. The 
ﬁndings reveal that Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Ar­
tificial Neural Networks (ANN) are among the most frequently used and 
eﬀective models, given various input variables. Additionally, machine 
learning models such as Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Random 
Forest Regression (RFR) are often chosen as the best methods for pre­
dicting or estimating tomato yield. For tomato detection, models like 
YOLO-Tomato and R-CNN are primarily utilized, while regression mod­
els such as DeepSort and Linear Regression are used to estimate tomato 
yield. Therefore, it is important to conduct a comparative study of mod­
els like SVR, ANN, LSTM, and RFR, particularly using data collected in 
Africa to predict tomato yield eﬀectively.
