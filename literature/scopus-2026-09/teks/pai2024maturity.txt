Maturity and Yield Estimation of Tomatoes using RGB and
Multispectral Images
Chu-An Pai and Huei-Yung Lin
Abstract—Due to the rising cost of labor, smart agriculture is
regarded as the trend of future agricultural development. In this
work, we propose a system for maturity and yield estimation of
tomatoes using RGB and multispectral images. The tomatoes
are identified using object detection and tracking networks,
followed by ripeness classification into the categories “mature”,
“almost mature” and “immature”. In the experiment, the image
sequence is used to detect tomatoes, and assign an ID to each of
them. The principal component analysis is then performed using
multispectral indices including NDVI, GRRI and GNDVI. Finally,
tomato tracking and maturity classification are integrated, and
the number of tomatoes with various maturity in each group is
counted. Through this research, we can more effectively monitor
and manage tomato growth and improve agricultural production
efficiency.
I. INTRODUCTION
As the minimum wage continues to increase, the expense of
employing seasonal labor for harvesting has steadily climbed.
Moreover, this situation is exacerbated by the growing scarcity
of available workers, which results in the competition of labor
resources for farmers. When scheduling the workers to harvest,
farmers need to maximize the cost-performance by considering
the anticipated crop quantity or weight to be harvested. Thus, it
is necessary to estimate the maturity and yields of the crop on
a regular basis. To address this issue, image-based approaches
are commonly used. Computer vision has played a crucial role
in agriculture intelligence Its applications extend beyond field
monitoring, encompassing a range of techniques for inspection
and classification of the crop. By employing the learning-based
methods, several assessment factors such as size, color, shape,
defects, and other attributes could be used to evaluate both the
maturity and quality levels of crops.
Most of the existing methods for crop classification utilize
the reflection of different wavelengths by fruits as a primary
criterion. For instance, Guo et al. compared the wavelengths
emitted by strawberries at various stages of maturity to classify
their ripeness [1], while the blueberry ripeness was determined
by assessing the wavelength of infrared light reflected from the
fruit’s skin [2]. The quality assessment can be conducted based
on their changing appearances at different maturity stages, and
use conventional classification techniques. In [3], blackberries
and raspberries were categorized to 4 different maturity levels

at different stages, facilitating the classification and estimation
of maturity progress throughout the strawberry’s growth cycle
with transfer learning.
In addition to quality and maturity assessment, vision-based
techniques were also used to identify and locate ripe fruits for
yield prediction and automated harvesting. For the localization
of fruits, Liu et al. presented a multi-elliptical boundary model
in Cr-Cb coordinate to separate the citrus fruits and tree trunks
from background [5]. In a previous work [6], a robotic system
capable of autonomously harvesting cherry was developed. It
was equipped with thermal cameras to distinguish the infrared
light reflection between ripe cherries and green leaves in order
to identify the position of mature cherries more accurately. For
yield prediction, Niedbała [7] employed a comprehensive array
of factors as indicators of maturity. These included more than
30 multispectral indices, weather data spanning from the 121st
to the 181st day of each year, and the foraging density of bees.
Alternatively, DeLong et al. measured the chlorophyl in fruits
weekly to predict the optimal time to start fruit harvesting [8].
By leveraging smart agricultural technologies, farmers are able
to better manage orchards, take a timely action to prevent the
occurrence and spread of pests and diseases, and enhance fruit
quality and yield. Moreover, research on fruit yield prediction
can provide valuable insights to the government in formulating
better agricultural policies to promote agricultural production
and development.
This paper focuses on the maturity and yield predictions of
cherry tomatoes. Over entire harvesting seasons, three datasets
consisting of both RGB and multispectral images are collected
systematically. With a lateral camera movement, the tomatoes
on the shelves of a tomato field are photographed for counting.
For the identified tomatoes, their ripeness levels are classified
based on RGB primary colors and multispectral indices. This
study also analyzes the effectiveness of different classification
indices and their impacts on the classification results. Finally,
the quantities of tomatoes at each ripeness level in the image
sequences are tabulated. It provides invaluable information for
farmers in allocating labor resources during harvesting [9].

A. Classification Index
As crops progress through their growth stages, they reflect
light of different wavelengths [10]. By capturing reflected
light using multispectral cameras, various growth indicators
of crop, such as leaf area, chlorophyl content, vegetation
index, can be obtained. Based on the analysis of changes
in these indicators, the maturity of crops can be determined.
The indicators can be broadly classified using RGB images
and multispectral images. In this paper, NDVI (Normalized
Difference Vegetation Index) [11], GNDVI (Green NDVI),
and GRRI (Green-Red Ratio Index) are utilized as indicators
for maturity classification and predicting maturity time of
tomatoes. Three algorithms, SVM (Support Vector Machine),
KNN (K Nearest Neighbors), and ANN (Artificial Neural
Network) are employed for classification of tomato maturity.
1) NDVI: The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index is
an indicator used to measure land cover and vegetation growth.
It is calculated based on the ratio of infrared and visible light
reflectance obtained from remote sensing imagery [12]. The
range of NDVI values is from −1 to +1, where the negative
values represent non-vegetated areas (such as rocks, bare land,
water), while positive values indicate vegetated regions. In
general, the higher NDVI value indicates more vigorous and
healthier vegetation growth. NDVI is defined by

B. Classification Algorithm
After deriving the values of each tomato on various classifi-
cation indicators, the next stage is to utilize SVM, KNN, and
ANN algorithms to classify the ripeness level of each tomato.
Under different parameter settings, SVM, KNN and ANN may
be affected in terms of the error tolerance, generalization, and
accuracy. In addition to the variation in parameter settings, the
splitting of training and validation sets might also influence the
classification results differently. For parameter settings, SVM’s
C value ranges from 10 to 40 with a step of 2 for classification,
while the KNN’s K value ranges from 2 to 30, also with a step
size of 2 for classification. To ensure the classification stability,
we adopt cross-validation in all algorithms. This will allow all
training data to be trained and validated multiple times. Using
cross-validation, all training data have a chance to be used as
the testing set in rotation. For SVM, KNN, and ANN, we use
10-fold cross-validation for verification under each different
parameter setting to obtain the most suitable parameters.
Finally, with the derived parameters, we classify the testing
set to obtain the classification results. The detailed parameter

C. Tomato Detection, Tracking and Segmentation
To detect tomatoes in an image, we have utilized a detection
network combining YOLOv8 and Osnet [14]. Osnet is a CNN
model specifically designed for person re-identification, REID.
It employs depth-wise separable convolutions, and replaces the
original 3×3 convolutional layer with a combination of a 1×1
convolutional layer and a 3×3 separable convolutional layer. In
its Omni-Scale Residual Block, multiple bottleneck branches
consisting of different sets of Lite 3×3 convolutional layers are
combined to form the multi-scale bottleneck. All bottlenecks
are then aggregated using the Unified Aggregation Gate.
To track the individual tomatoes, we adopt StrongSORT and
Bytetrack as separate tracking networks. The one with superior
tracking performance is then selected as the tracking network
for tomato quantity estimation in final analysis. StrongSORT
[15] is improved over DeepSort [16]. The proposed AFLink,
an appearance-free linking model, converts short trajectories
into complete trajectories. It takes spatio-temporal information
of two trajectories as the input to predict their connectivity, and
provides confidence scores for the connections using MLP. In
Bytetrack [17], the low confidence issue caused by occlusion
in multi-object tracking is addressed. An association method is
proposed to retain and classify all bounding boxes into high-
score and low-score. The low-score ones are then linked by
Kalman filtering to predict the potential positions of targets in
the next frame. This can effectively filter out the background,
and resolve the problem of occlusion.
To remove the occluded branches, we refer to the work by
Sari and Adinugroho for tomato segmentation [18]. Collected
tomato images are first converted from the RGB to YUV color
space. Otsu thresholding [19] is then applied to the V-channel
for binary segmentation, removing the non-tomato regions and
specular reflections. Due to dense tomato growth and possible
occlusion by branches, leaves, and neighboring tomatoes, the
rectangular bounding boxes are segmented into ellipses. This
might include partially occluded or neighboring tomatoes, but
it minimizes the potential error in pixel calculations caused by
image segmentation.
It is required to compose NDVI, GNDVI and GRRI chan-
nels from multiple spectral images for analysis. However, the
direct overlay without processing would result in misalignment
and incorrect spectral values. To ensure the precise alignment
of the same tomato in different spectral images, preprocessing
is necessary for near-infrared, red, green, and red-edge images.
In our implementation, it is a 4-step process given as follows.
(1) the removal of lens distortion, (2) image warping to align
the positions of near-infrared, red, and infrared images, (3) the
alignment of images required for each multispectral index, and
(4) application of the formulas for each multispectral index to
the aligned images to obtain the corresponding TIFF images.
III. EXPERIMENTS
A. Data Collection and Ripeness Annotation
In the experiment we use a Parrot Sequoia+ camera for data
collection. It is commonly used in agriculture applications, and
composed of a multispectral sensor and a sunshine sensor. The
multispectral sensor captures images in multiple spectral bands
using five cameras on the sensor. In addition to RGB images,
it captures NIR, red, green, and red-edge images, providing a
more comprehensive vegetation image and data. The sunshine
sensor acquires the state of sunlight illumination. Its purpose
is to calibrate the multispectral images, and address variations
caused by differences in sunlight intensity, to ensure consistent
output images regardless of lighting conditions.
The captured tomato image sequences are transformed into
the IRIS dataset format [20]. In the data format, the color data
of each tomato in every frame, including the tomato ID, RGB
images, multispectral images and ripeness, are recorded. The
multispectral image data further includes NDVI, GNDVI, and
GRRI. Except for the tomato ID and ripeness, the calculation
for all other values involves averaging the pixel intensities of
all unmasked parts within each tomato bounding box. In each
dataset, all photographs of tomato image sequences, the IRIS
format, the MOT dataset format1 for the test image sequences,
and the weights trained on the our data using SVM, KNN and
ANN are included.
The grading of tomato ripeness is done on a weekly basis,
by comparing data captured at the same location but different
weeks to determine if the tomato is ripe and ready for harvest.
If a tomato is not harvested and appears green in the nth week,
remains unharvested and turns red in the (n + 1)th week, and
is harvested in the (n + 2)th week, then it will be labeled as
−2 (i.e., n−(n+2)) in the nth week, and labeled as −1 (i.e.,
n+1−(n+2)) in the n+1th week. In addition, tomatoes that
are more than three weeks away from ripening show minimal
changes in color and shape. Thus, they will all be labeled as
−3 (including three weeks). In simple terms, we use −1, −2,
−3 to indicate the tomato is ripe and ready for harvest, taking
one more week to harvest, and taking two or more weeks to
harvest, respectively.

B. Ripeness Classification
By employing the approach for annotating tomato ripeness
and utilizing the classification algorithms, the tomato ripeness
in the test image sequences is classified. In the experiments,
the classification accuracy is defined as the number of correct
classification for tomato bounding box in all frames after the
voting method divided by the total number of tomato bounding
boxes in all frames. It is important to note that tomato tracking
is not considered here, and all tomato bounding boxes in the
training and testing sets are generated using the ground truth.
The number of tomato bounding boxes in training and testing
sets for three datasets is tabulated in Table II.
In image sequences, the same tomato can appear in multiple
frames, potentially with the partial leaf obstruction or variation
in the detected position. It may lead to inconsistent recognition
results for the same tomato across frames. To address this, we
employ the voting approach from ensemble learning. Multiple
models are used to predict the target, and the most frequently
predicted category is the final prediction result. The ripeness
of the same tomato is determined by the majority of maturity
classification. Table III tabulates the classification results. The
accuracy is defined by the number of correctly classified over
the total number of tomato bounding boxes in all frames.
In the results, it is observed that, except for the 19th tomato
dataset, two other datasets showed better classification results
in RGB compared to multispectral images. To figure out the
superior performance of RGB classification, the contribution
of each indicator is analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 2. It shows
that green light consistently provides the highest contribution
to ripeness classification. The contributions of multispectral
indices, red light and blue light varied among the datasets. It
can therefore be inferred that better performance of the RGB
classification is primarily due to the effectiveness of the green
light component.

also examine the accuracy and fuzziness of the classification
algorithms on different maturity categories through the obser-
vation of the confusion matrix as shown in Fig. 3. It is found
that the accuracy is better between -1 (mature) and -2 (almost
mature), but much worse between -2 (almost mature) and -3
(immature). In the categories of almost mature and immature,
their skin colors are very similar, making it difficult to classify
based on the color.

D. Tomato Counting
The tomato counting process can be divided into two parts:
detection and tracking. Initially, YOLOv8 is trained using the
training set consisting of the RGB images captured by Parrot
Sequoia’s camera. The only class in the training set is tomato.
Subsequently, the trained YOLOv8 model weight is passed to
the tracking network. For the tracking network, the pre-trained
models from StrongSort and ByteTrack are utilized. YOLOv8
is responsible for detection, while StrongSort and ByteTrack
are responsible for assigning ID to the detection box. Finally,
the allocated IDs are used to derive the number of tomatoes.
The tracking results for three datasets are illustrated in Table
V. It shows that StrongSort has the best performance in terms
of MOTA and IDs, and is adopted for our yield estimation.
In the next experiment, we combine the tomato tracking and
tomato maturity assessment. The objective is to calculate the
number of tomatoes for each maturity level in the images, as
well as their distributions. Table VI shows the statistics of the
number of tomatoes with various maturity levels in all testing
videos. Fig. 4 illustrates the final output of the detection and
classification results. The tomatoes belonging to the “mature”,
“almost mature” and “immature” categories are represented by
red, orange and yellow bounding boxes, respectively.
IV. CONCLUSION
This work proposes a system to identify tomatoes and assess
their ripeness levels using both RGB and multispectral images.
Our approach consists of four parts: dataset establishment and
ripeness labeling, tomato position tracking and quantity assess-
ment, tomato ripeness classification, and statistical analysis of
quantity of each ripeness category. We first employ YOLOv8
and OSNet object detection techniques to detect the tomatoes,
followed by using StrongSort to assign appropriate IDs to all
tomato bounding boxes. In the ripeness classification, principal
component analysis is performed using various indices (NDVI,
GRRI, and GNDVI). Subsequently, SVM, KNN, and Neural
Network are applied separately to classify tomatoes into three
classes: “immature”, “almost mature”, and “mature”. Finally,
the quantity and location of tomato in each ripeness category
are obtained to assist farmers in planning harvesting routes
and determining the optimal number of workers.
