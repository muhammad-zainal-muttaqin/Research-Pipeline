A B S T R A C T  
Fruit load estimation is an essential step toward Precision Agriculture (PA) as it helps growers more accurately 
predict market planning, worker planning, purchase of appropriate equipment and so on. Reliable and accurate 
estimation of fruit yield in an orchard with hundreds of trees needs automatic methods. In recent years, Deep 
Learning (DL) has been studied widely and applied in various fields of agriculture. Accordingly, the YOLO 
detection models were applied to detect and count ripe Dezful native orange in an orchard in southwestern Iran. 
The models were adapted through transfer learning and trained by Google Colaboratory in the RGB images to 
detect and count orange fruits. Models performance and accuracy of yield estimation for an orchard with 1115 
trees were examined. The process was conducted in 3 steps, including training and testing the different versions 
of the YOLO models by creating an image dataset of orange trees in different illumination conditions, evaluating 
the models on 100 sample trees, and finally extracting the yield variation map of the orchard after detecting and 
counting the oranges on images taken from all the trees in the orchard. The precision, recall, F1-score and mAP of 
the YOLO-V4 as the best model for orange detection over the test images were 91.23%, 92.8%, 92%, and 90.8%, 
respectively. The overall performance of the models in nighttime and daytime imaging was not significantly 
different. The YOLO-V4 model was chosen to use for yield estimation in the orchard. The promising results show 
that the YOLO models can effectively provide researchers and agricultural activists with a simple and practical 
method for detecting and estimating the yield of orange fruits in an orchard. Significant differences were 
observed in yield estimation for two-side and four-side imaging. Accordingly, a combined imaging method 
including two-side and four-side imaging was proposed for thin and dense canopy, respectively. The map of fruit 
yield changes showed the spatial distribution of tree yield with a +9.19% error.   
1. Introduction 
In order to improve crop management practices and reduce the waste 
of resources, new technologies are a vital necessity in agriculture 
(Vasconez et al., 2020). Agricultural ecosystems are complex, multi­
variate, and unpredictable. Continuous monitoring and analysis are 
necessary to achieve PA goals (Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018). 
Image processing facilitates observation and monitoring of agricultural 
environments through satellites, airplanes, unmanned aerial vehicles 
(UAV), advanced cameras, and even low-cost RGB cameras. Therefore, 
image processing is an important research field in agriculture, and 
artificial intelligence (AI) methods for image classification, anomaly 
identification, object detection, etc., have been repeatedly studied in 
various agricultural applications (Vasconez et al., 2020). 
The fact that crop yield varies from place to place, even within a farm 
uniformly managed, has been the driving force behind developing the 
site-specific product management (SSM) concept or PA (Gupta and 
Ibaraki, 2015). So, yield monitoring is the first step in developing a PA 
system. Prediction of the fruit load at the end of the season helps with 
the detailed marketing plan and providing labor, facilities, and materials 
needed (Dorj et al., 2017). Accurate yield estimation, in addition to 
predicting labor and bin needs, supports product pre-sale plans and post- 
harvest operations such as packaging, storage, and distribution (Dorj 
et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2013). It also helps insurance companies

It is possible to estimate the fruit yield of trees directly and indirectly 
through crop simulation models, remote sensing, and computer vision. 
Fruit estimation models are influenced by environmental conditions and 
crop characteristics. Therefore, due to different variables, accurate 
modeling faces many complexities. Some crop simulation models are 
developed based on the relationship between fruit yield and the number 
of flowers, leaves, or shoots (Liu et al., 2017). A strong correlation has 
also been found between tree canopy size and citrus yield (R2 = 0.80). 
The correlation between actual citrus yield and model prediction was 
also significant in model validation on new trees. (R2 = 0.42) (Zaman 
et al., 2006). 
The use of computer vision technology in fruit packaging began 
widely in the 1970s. Nowadays, a wide range of computer vision ap­
plications is available in horticulture, including weed detection to guide 
targeted spraying, fruit load estimation, yield monitoring, and autono­
mous harvesting. However, efforts and studies are underway to address 
the impact of environmental complexities such as changes in lighting 
conditions (Gupta and Ibaraki, 2015). Satellite and aerial Remote 
Sensing (RS) platforms facilitate larger-scale observation using satellites 
and aerial wide-view images. Because, in general, these technologies are 
more compatible with row crops than fruit trees, more studies have been 
conducted on row crops. Very high resolution satellite imagery and 
many experiments are needed to identify the correlation of canopy 
reflectance characteristics of individual tree and yield parameters by 
vegetation indices. 
Typically, due to the availability of commercial grain yield moni­
toring devices, many changes have been made in machine vision to 
monitor grain crops. Recently, however, due to advances in computers, 
cameras (RGB camera, 3D camera, thermal camera, and spectral cam­
era) and image processing techniques, a wide range of non-destructive 
methods has been developed to estimate crop yields based on fruit 
counting (Gongal et al., 2016). Usually a computer vision algorithm for 
identifying and counting citrus on the trees is developed by using image 
processing techniques such as converting color space, morphological 
operations, segmentation, and watershed transform. A computer vision 
system was proposed by (Liu et al., 2017) to estimate grape yield in the 
early stages based on shoot detection. The shoot detection framework 
included image processing, feature extraction, unsupervised feature 
selection, and unsupervised learning as the final classification step. A 
computer vision-based system was applied by (Payne et al., 2014) to 
estimate mango yield based on counting the number of fruits in daytime 
RGB images of individual trees. These images were taken from one and 
four sides of the trees three weeks before the fruit was harvested. 
Studies mentioned above have applied conventional image process­
ing techniques based on hand-crafted features that do not have high 
performance in practical applications. Object detection models are 
currently being developed to overcome obstacles such as variations in 
viewpoints, poses, occlusions, lighting conditions, backgrounds, and 
object sizes. Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) or Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CNNs), as one result of these efforts, act quite differently from 
conventional techniques. The deeper architecture of DNNs compared to 
shallow networks, enabling them to learn more complex features. They 
also use robust training algorithms that allow them to learn valuable 
patterns in input data without the need for manual feature design (Zhao 
et al., 2019). By using data augmentation techniques, the number of 
images required for training can be reduced (Gao et al., 2020). 
After introducing DNNs and the evolution of models such as R-CNN, 
Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, and YOLO, more accurate and real-time 
object detection became possible (Zhao et al., 2019). The You Only

proposed to compress the size and reduce the complexity and number of 
model parameters. The test results showed that the mAP (mean average

directions (Wu et al., 2020). To minimize light-related problems in 
imaging, some researchers have studied nighttime imaging with artifi­
cial illumination, imaging under a shade structure, or in a tunnel 
structure. Uniform artificial light at night reduces variation in lighting 
conditions and intense directional shadows in the orchards (Koirala 
et al., 2019; Payne et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2013). Compared to the light 
changes experienced during the day due to the angle of the sun and the 
weather conditions, it is possible to provide constant lighting conditions 
for nighttime imaging (Payne et al., 2014). In this regard, YOLO-V3 was 
successfully applied for very accurate and robust detection of litchi fruits 
in the natural nighttime environment (Liang et al., 2020). 
Imaging from both sides is usually sufficient to estimate fruit loads on 
trees (Payne et al., 2014). To solve the problem of double counting, a 
machine vision system was built that moved through a tunnel-like 
platform over the row of trees and took images from both sides of the 
rows. Estimation accuracy in shooting mode from both sides increased 
from 58% to 82% compared to shooting from one side (Gongal et al., 
2016). Imaging from four sides is also effective, as long as there is 
minimal overlap between the images. There was a strong correlation 
between yield estimation with human counting and citrus counting al­
gorithm in RGB images taken from four sides of the citrus tree in 
daylight (R2 = 0.93) (Dorj et al., 2017). A computer-based system using 
a mast equipped with two stereo cameras was used to scan both sides of 
the row of trees at night under controlled artificial light. Then, a com­
puter vision algorithm identified and counted the green and red apples

Citrus is one of the most important agricultural products in the south 
and north of Iran. In 2018, citrus with an area of 257,310 ha and 
5,113,655 million tons, respectively, accounted for 10% in terms of area 
and 25% by weight of fruits produced in Iran (Ministry of Agriculture- 
Jahad, 2018). As mentioned earlier, early yield estimation can help to 
plan horticultural operations more accurately. The current traditional 
manual yield estimation is time-consuming, labor-intensive, inaccurate, 
and usually relies on previous yield history. Choosing and using the best 
model from many available models, especially considering the recent 
advances in computer vision and DL, and investigating the factors 
affecting its application in the natural environment in an orchard is a 
vital step in PA. To the best of our knowledge, high cost and lack of 
sufficient knowledge are the crucial reasons for less use of these tools 
and techniques. In addition, agricultural researchers may not have 
enough experienced in programming and computer technology. 
Accordingly, this study was conducted to apply and evaluate the YOLO- 
V2, V3 and V4 algorithms through transfer learning to detect and count 
ripe oranges of Dezful native species in images taken from an orchard in 
Dezful, Iran. The specific objectives of the study were:  
1. Applying YOLO-V2, V3 and V4 to count and detect fruits taken from 
a citrus orchard and choosing the best model version  
2. Investigating the effect of light level during the imaging on fruit 
detection performance (under sunlight, cloudy weather during the 
day or under artificial light at night)  
3. Comparing the accuracy of yield estimation under two-side and four- 
side imaging  
4. Creating a map of potential yield variation 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Image acquisition 
This study was conducted in 2018 on an orange orchard (cultivar: 
native orange of Dezful) with an area of 4.8 ha in Dezful, Khuzestan 
province, southwestern Iran. The orchard was planted in 2010 with a 
tree spacing of 6 m × 6 m. Its location is shown in Fig. 1. 
Two RGB cameras, Nikon COOLPIX S8100 and Pentax K10D, with a 
resolution of 3000 × 4000 and 2592 × 3872 pixels, respectively, were 
used in the auto mode (JPG format) to capture images. Most of the 
images were captured from a vertical distance of 4 m from the trees. To 
have various images for a robust orange detection model, the images 
were captured in different illumination conditions. The images were 
captured from four sides of tree rows one month before harvesting. To

2.2. Annotation 
The oranges in the images were annotated individually and as one 
class, using the “LabelImg” annotation tool in bounding box format 
(Tzutalin, 2016). Annotation was done very carefully so that all the 
oranges were annotated in the images. The annotation samples are 
depicted in Fig. 4. Due to the many oranges in the images and the 
manual process, annotating each image took an average of 10 min. The 
target at the edge of the images was not labeled. 
2.3. Image augmentation 
Data augmentation expands the variability of training data by arti­
ficially enlarging the dataset using label-preserving transformations and 
ensures robustness in the training phase. The process increases the 
network capability to generalize and reduces overfitting (Buslaev et al., 
2020). 
Rotating, re-scaling, and changing the color of the image are the 
most common augmentation techniques (Gao et al., 2020). In the cur­
rent study, the Albumentations tool was used to augment the dataset. It 
is a Python open-source library that provides many image transferring 
operations for a fast, flexible and user-friendly image augmentation 
(Buslaev et al., 2020). Fig. 5 shows the results of some transformations in 
the images. As a result, a dataset containing 1568 images was used to 
train and test the YOLO models. A total of 20% of these images were 
used to test the models. 
2.4. Evaluation of the model yield estimation 
To evaluate the accuracy of the number of oranges estimated by the 
models, 100 trees were randomly selected and were imaged from four 
sides. The number of fruits of these trees was also counted after harvest. 
To estimate the average weight of oranges, 20 oranges were randomly 
selected and weighed. This operation was repeated three times, and the 
average weight of oranges was considered as the approximate weight of 
the orange in the orchard.

YOLO, one of the most prominent object detection algorithms, was 
first introduced in 2016 (Redmon et al., 2016), and its fifth version is 
now available. The algorithm with its impressive specifications has 
improved the accuracy and speed of detecting and determining the co­
ordinates of objects. Many localization errors are produced in YOLO-V1 
especially in detecting small objects and objects in groups as this version 
of the model learns to predict boxes directly from the image data (Tian 
et al., 2019). To address this problem, the idea of the “anchor box” was 
introduced in YOLO-V2. The fully connected layer in the output layer of 
YOLO-V1 is replaced by a convolution layer in YOLO-V2. YOLO-V2 also 
introduces batch normalization, a high-resolution classifier, dimension 
clusters, direct location prediction, fine-grained features, multi-scale 
training, and other methods that greatly improve the detection accu­
racy and speed compared with YOLO-V1. The backbone feature 
extractor for YOLO-V2 is the Darknet-19 framework. YOLO-V3 is an 
improved version of YOLO-V2 and providing much faster detection. It 
uses multi-scale prediction to detect the final target. It also predicts 
bounding boxes on different scales, and multi-scale prediction, all of 
which make the YOLO-V3 more powerful than YOLO-V2 in detecting 
small targets. This version has 106 convolutional layers, which is much 
more than that of previous YOLO versions and is 3.0 and 3.8 times faster 
than SSD and RetinaNet, respectively YOLO-V3 does not use fully- 
connected and pooling layers but uses convolutional layers. This struc­
ture makes it possible to deal with images in different sizes. Predictions

training process continued as long as the mAP value and the average loss 
increased and decreased, respectively. The best performance of the 
models is achieved when the weights are gotten from an early stopping 
point. Weight-files were saved every 1000 iteration, and those with the 
highest mAP were used for detection.

2.8. Yield map 
Finally, after detecting and counting the number of fruits in each 
tree, using the Orange Detection Model, the yield of each tree was 
overlaid on a Google map image of the orchard using ArcGIS 10.8 
(Environmental Systems Research Institute. Redlands, CA, USA). The 
output was a map of fruit yield changes at different locations in the 
orchard. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Model evaluation 
Fig. 8 shows the examples of orange detection in daytime and 
nighttime images. In general, the oranges in close images were detected 
well. Similar results were obtained from a model developed in the real, 
outdoor orchard environment to detect oranges for a harvesting robot 
working with RGB-D images (Lin et al., 2019). It is proved that the closer 
the image is taken, the better the performance of the detector in an 
orchard environment. It is easier to see fruits in nighttime images and 
the background fruits don’t disturb the process. The achieving scores of 
precision, recall, F1-score and mAP, and processing speed of the test set 
images are shown in Fig. 9 and Fig. 10 for YOLO-V2, V3 and V4. The 
recall and precision mean that, for example, for YOLO-V4, 92.23% of the 
ground truth oranges were detected, and 91.84% of the detections were 
orange in nighttime images. All models achieved good detection results 
on the test images. The accuracy and speed of the YOLO-V4 were greater 
than that of the other two versions of YOLO. Therefore, YOLO-V4 can 
provide better real-time detection of oranges in the natural 
environment. 
Performance metrics in almost all daytime and nighttime images are 
high enough, confirming the high robustness of the trained models in 
different illumination conditions. False positive values in daytime im­
ages were more than that of in nighttime images. This mainly because 
the reflection of light during the day makes it difficult to distinguish the 
leaves from the orange. In contrast, false negative values in nighttime 
images were more than that of daytime images because artificial light 
could not fully penetrate the canopy, and some oranges weren’t detected 
by the model. In daytime, light is scattered throughout the tree, and 
most of the oranges are visible, so true positives in daytime images are 
more than that of nighttime images. Overall, the number of detections in 
daytime, whether false or true, is more than that of in nighttime. In 
nighttime, the precision is more than that of in daytime, whereas the 
recall is less than that of in daytime. It has already been confirmed that 
intense sunlight has a destructive effect on imaging (Payne et al., 2014; 
Wang et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 9, F1-Score is generally slightly 
improved in nighttime images, indicating that nighttime imaging under 
artificial illumination can address the potential light problems caused by 
changing lighting conditions. It has already been confirmed that the 
trained YOLO algorithms on images taken at daytime can successfully 
detect objects in images taken at nighttime (Wang et al., 2018). This is 
mainly because nighttime imaging under controlled artificial illumina­
tion can reduce the light changes that are common under natural light. 
In nighttime imaging under artificial illumination, lighting conditions 
must be kept constant. A machine vision imaging system over the rows 
was introduced by (Gongal et al., 2016), which can control light con­
ditions in a tunnel structure. The system can be used during both day­
time and nighttime, but because of the large and dense canopy of orange 
trees, the platform may not be used in the citrus orchard. 
For comparison, some results of performance metrics of similar 
studies are given in Table 4. In all DL studies, the model is accepted if F1- 
score > 0.8. Although some studies have used a new design or a deeper 
architecture of the available models, in the present study, the original 
YOLO versions were used to demonstrate the simple and fast methods of 
using the accurate deep models in agriculture. The achieved results are 
significant in comparison with the result of other complex and time- 
consuming models. Sometimes, results can be improved by increasing 
the size of the input images (Koirala et al., 2019).

3.2. Types and sources of errors 
Most errors occurred in the form of false positives rather than false 
negatives. The false positive errors were mainly associated with 
yellowish foliage and poor lighting, whereas false negatives were mainly 
associated with orange clusters, small oranges and orange occlusion.

As an example of a false negative (Fig. 13, top), the model detected 2 
oranges (yellow boxes at the top left image) as an orange, and as an 
example of a false positive, a yellow leaf is detected as an orange 
(Fig. 13, bottom). 
3.3. Detection of the background oranges 
When imaging a tree, the presence of oranges from other trees in the 
background is unavoidable. The YOLO orange detection models can 
recognize the background oranges properly. In fact, in model training, 
the areas that were not annotated in manual annotation will be labeled 
as the background by default. So, during training, the inputs include the 
background and the oranges.

due to the more significant overlap of images in four-side imaging. Also, 
the error rate of estimating fruit yield in two-side imaging is higher than 
that of four-side imaging. In citrus orchards, most oranges usually 
appear on the outer layer of the canopy. Thus, the repetition of oranges 
in images taken from different angles is the main reason for over­
estimating in four-side imaging. Based on Fig. 12, only 3%, 7% and 13% 
of oranges were not detected by the YOLO-V4, V3 and V2 models,

aging is that, unlike four-side imaging, not all oranges are visible in tow- 
side imaging. 
Based on observations, four-side imaging in large and dense canopies 
leads to a better estimation rather than two-side imaging, but in orange 
trees with thin canopy, four-side imaging may lead to overestimation. 
The canopy with an area of almost less than 6 m2 was considered a thin 
canopy (Fig. 16). Accordingly, a combined imaging method including 
two and four-side imaging is proposed for thin and dense canopies, 
respectively. Fig. 15 and Fig. 16 show a tree with a dense canopy in four 
directions and a tree with a thin canopy in two opposite directions, 
respectively.

for underestimation. In the four-side imaging, the error rates were 
decreased, and overestimation occurred. Combined imaging had the

because of double counting, but the errors were less than four-side im­

in two-side imaging, because of the increasing double counting rate of 
overlap images in four sides of a tree, it increased overestimation.

and the error was equal to 202 kg for 100 trees.

(62%), the model overestimated the number of oranges.

4. Conclusion 
Different versions of the YOLO model (V2, V3 and V4) were applied 
to detect ripe oranges in RGB images taken from an orchard in Dezful, 
Iran. The transfer learning technique was used to train the models to 
detect and count orange fruits on Google Colaboratory. Therefore, 
instead of developing a new model, a simple and straightforward model 
is available for agricultural researchers without the need for compli­
cated programming and professional hardware. Almost all performance 
metrics in all studied model versions were acceptable in both daytime 
and nighttime images. The best mAP and F1-score on the test images set, 
90.88% and 92%, respectively, were obtained by YOLO-V4, one of the 
most up-to-date version of the YOLO series. Statistical analyses showed a 
good correlation between models estimation and ground truth oranges 
in the test set images, and YOLO-V4, V3 and V2 had −8.55%, −15.87%
