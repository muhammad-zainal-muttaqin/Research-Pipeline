ABSTRACT
Multi‐object tracking (MOT) is a fundamental problem in computer vision that involves tracing the trajectories of foreground
targets throughout a video sequence while establishing correspondences for identical objects across frames. With the
advancement of deep learning techniques, methods based on deep learning have significantly improved accuracy and efficiency
in MOT. This paper reviews several recent deep learning‐based MOT methods and categorises them into three main groups:
detection‐based, single‐object tracking (SOT)‐based, and segmentation‐based methods, according to their core technologies.
Additionally, this paper discusses the metrics and datasets used for evaluating MOT performance, the challenges faced in the
field, and future directions for research.

Introduction
Multi‐object tracking (MOT) is the task of forming the trajectories
of foreground objects in video sequences while establishing the
correspondence of identical objects across frames; it has played an
essential role in many real‐world applications, for example,
intelligent transportation [1, 2], video surveillance [3], and
human–computer interaction [4]. With the development of deep
learning (DL) technologies, DL‐based MOT methods have made
significant progress in accuracy and efficiency. We have explored
the recent literature on deep learning‐based Multiple Object
Tracking (MOT) to contribute to its further development. Among
them, some reviews focus specifically on data association. For
instance, the work byRakai et al. [5] emphasises the task of data
association using uniquely defined similarity functions and relies
solely on filters. Another study [6], surveys data association
methods where instances are determined based on parameters
derived from a set of observations and a pool of potential
hypotheses. Other reviews [7, 8] primarily discuss tracking
methods linked to detection. For example [8], Pal et al. analyse
existing object detection and tracking techniques that utilise deep
learning networks. Additionally, some reviews [9, 10] offer a
unified representation of MOT algorithms, outlining the methods
and related procedures without comparisons details.
This paper highlights the core technologies integrated into Multi‐
Object Tracking (MOT), which differs from previous surveys. As
illustrated in Figure 1, we categorise these technologies into three
main approaches: detection‐based, single‐object tracking (SOT)‐
based, and segmentation‐based. Additionally, we examine opti-
misation strategies, including framework structure, feature
extraction, and data association techniques.
We have provided a summary of metrics and datasets relevant to
MOT methods' performance evaluation and an overview of the
challenges and future directions within this field. We aim to

The Essential Technologies of MOT
2.1 |
Object Detection Methods
Object detection performance is crucial for MOT because it
accurately identifies potential target candidates for tracking
throughout the video sequence. With advancements in object
detection, as illustrated in Figure 2, detectors can be categorised
into several approaches, including two‐stage, one‐stage, anchor‐
free and transformer methods.
Among them, two‐stage object detection methods consist of
candidate generation and classification process. In this cate-
gory, Faster R‐CNN [11] and Cascade R‐CNN [12] are the
typical two‐stage detectors to reveal the potential for objects
detecting. Despite the pretty performance of these two‐stage
methods, methods' complexity, speed, and inaccuracy may be
the limitations.
Following, one‐stage object detection methods has been devel-
oped to achieve higher detection speeds while maintaining
better accuracy by eliminating the region proposal step. In this
paradigm, YOLO series—such as YOLOv1 [13], YOLOv2 [13],
YOLOv3 [14], and YOLOv4 [15]—as well as RetinaNet [16], all
show performance improvements. Though these advancements,
anchor‐based methods still encounter challenges related to box
sizes, aspect ratios, imbalanced positive and negative samples,
and non‐object information obscured by poorly fitting boxes.
To address these issues, anchor‐free object detection methods
have emerged. For instance, CornerNet [17] approaches object

2.2 |
Single Object Tracking Methods
Single Object Tracking (SOT) focuses on the movement of a
single object over time within a video sequence. In the context
of MOT, SOT plays a crucial role as it helps compensate for
detection errors by providing more accurate tracking of
appearance and motion information. SOT methods with Sia-
mese structure are mainly integrated to provide localisation and
identification information among SOT‐based MOT methods.
Like SiameseFC [21], SiameseRPN [22], which is the improved
version of SiameseFC by introducing Region Proposal Network
(RPN), and RASNet [23] which employs the attention mecha-
nism models into the Siamese network. The development of
SOT is shown in Figure 3.
2.3 |
Segmentation Methods
Moreover, image segmentation, which includes semantic and
instance segmentation, involves categorising the pixels of an
image into different object regions. This process is essential in
multi‐object tracking (MOT) as it effectively distinguishes
adjacent objects at the pixel level, allowing for precise identifi-
cation of object boundaries. Figure 4 illustrates the progression
of segmentation methods. Among these segmentation‐based
methodsMaskRCNN [24], an instance segmentation technique,
serves as the primary segmentation branch integrated in MOT
to achieve improved tracking results.

Multiple‐Object Tracking
This section categorises the recent MOT methods into detection‐
based, SOT‐based, and segmentation‐based methods according
to the primary method employed.
3.1 |
Tracking‐By‐Detection
Tracking‐by‐Detection (TBD) is a standard paradigm for MOT
that involving object detection and data association. The
detection phase identifies individual objects in each frame,
while the association phase connects these detection results
over time to create a continuous track for each object. This
concept is illustrated in Figure 5.
In this section, we provide an overview of existing MOT
methods based on the type of backbone detector used in TBD.
We categorise these methods in three perspectives: two‐stage,
one‐stage, and anchor‐free. The relevant TBD approaches are
shown in Table 1.
3.1.1 |
Tracking‐By‐Two‐Stage Detection
In the Tracking‐by‐Detection (TBD) paradigm, the SORT [25]
method is a classic approach for constructing object trajectories
by analysing relationships between frames through the inter-
section over union (IoU) of detected and predicted positions.
While SORT is known for its efficiency and speed, occlusions
can negatively affect its performance, leading to a high rate of
ID switches. To overcome these challenges, DeepSORT [26]
introduces a cascade matching scheme that enhances tracking
performance in crowded environment and reduce ID switches
in real‐time scenarios. As indicated in Table 2, DeepSORT
significantly lowers the rate of ID switches (IDSW) and im-
proves the Multi‐object tracking accuracy (MOTA) compared to
SORT, demonstrating the effectiveness of its cascade matching
approach in dense scenes. However, DeepSORT relies on deep
learning models and a Kalman filter, which demands consid-
erable computational resources and storage capacity.

3.1.3 |
Tracking‐By‐Anchor‐Free Detection
With the introduction and advancement of anchor‐free detectors,
MOT can also benefit from the advantages of this detection
paradigm, including better adaptability to diverse target sizes,
shapes, and aspect ratios and more vital generalisation ability
without prior knowledge or manual feature engineering.
Regarding bounding box size, C‐BIoU sets a proportion to expand
the size of the box of detection used in the matching stage, thereby
increasing the chances of adequately matching [35]. In fact,
regardless of whether traditional IoU or C‐BIoU is used, there are
still limitations in matching bounding boxes, particularly in cases
of overlap or no intersection. Additionally, OC‐SORT [37] at-
tempts to recover the lost trajectories by optimising the Kalman
filter in occlusion and non‐linear motion scenarios. However,
methods such as OC‐SORT, which do not incorporate appearance
information, miss the additional cues provided by appearance
features during data association. For optimisation purposes, Deep
OC‐SORT [38] proposes dynamic visual appearance and camera
motion compensation with adaptive weighting factors, building
on the advantages of both motion and appearance information.
Similarly, GHOST [39] incorporates a scheme for calculating
appearance distance, offering a more accurate description of
appearance similarity while also considering target motion tra-
jectories to enhance the performance of MOT. BoT‐SORT [36]
strengthens the correlation between detection and trajectory by
integrating motion and appearance information. Additionally,
StrongSORT [34] enhances its tracking capabilities with modules
such as the appearance‐free linking model (AFLink), which im-
proves appearance features, and the Gaussian Smoothed Inter-
polation algorithm (GSI), which is beneficial for maintaining
trajectory continuity without compromising authenticity. As
illustrated in Figure 6a, these methods improve the association
success rate in multi‐object tracking by optimising the association
process. However, they often overlook or discard low‐quality
targets, which can negatively affect tracking accuracy. For this,
ByteTrack [33] enhances the accuracy of MOT by incorporating
low‐scoring detections into the data association process. This
method improves data association accuracy compared to other
approaches that only associate high‐scoring detection boxes.
Nevertheless, ByteTrack also has its limitations, as its simplistic
motion model and restricted ability to extract and match object
appearance features may result in tracking failures or identity
switches.
The tracking‐by‐detection paradigm could take advantage of ob-
ject detection's superiority to obtain more detailed information
about targets and assist in effectively tracking occlusion, small
objects, or blurred scenes. However, in this two‐stage paradigm,
the detect‐first‐then‐track execution logic would result in over‐
reliance on the detector's ability and increase the computational
complexity.
3.2 |
Joint Detection and Tracking
With the rapid advancement of multi‐task learning in deep
learning, there is growing interest in one‐shot multi‐object
tracking (MOT) research. The core idea of this paradigm is to
perform object detection and re‐identification embedding simul-
taneously within a unified architecture as illustrated in Figure 7.
Additionally, depending on the backbone detector used, recent
Joint Detection and Tracing (JDT) methods can be categorised
into three perspectives: two‐stage, one‐stage, and anchor‐free.
The related JDT paradigms are summarised in Table 3.
3.2.1 |
Joint Two‐Stage Detection and Tracking

Detection‐Tracking (JDT) paradigm to enhance the performance
of Multi‐Object Tracking (MOT) through mutual feedback be-
tween detection and tracking [40]. Following this paradigm,
SiamMOT demonstrates the positive impact of accurately esti-
mating instance motion between two frames on MOT [41].
Similarly, Tobias et al. highlight the significance of motion in-
formation and develop quasi‐dense tracking (QDTrack) to
address the challenges of accurate tracking in dense scenes with
significant appearance changes, relying solely on appearance in-
formation [42]. Additionally, ReleationTrack tackles the issue of

3.2.2 |
Joint One‐Stage Detection With Tracking
With the introduction and optimisation of the one‐stage de-
tectors, they are also employed in the JDT paradigm to improve
MOT performance further.
Joint Detection and Embedding (JDE) [45] is established to
integrate object detection and feature embedding in a shared
model that processes the detector and feature embedding simul-
taneously. The advantage of JDE lies in its attempt to balance
tracking speed and accuracy. It is capable of simultaneously
outputting detection boxes and embeddings, although it still re-
quires subsequent matching steps to meet the accuracy demands
of data association. Building on the concept of JDE, CSTrack [46]
introduces a cross‐correlation network (CCN) to address the issue
of over‐competition between detection and re‐identification
(ReID) branches. It employs an attention mechanism to
enhance the interaction between these two branches. In contrast,
a scale‐aware attention network (SAAN) helps prevent semantic
misalignment and improve association capability, resulting in
superior multi‐object tracking (MOT) performance. Nevertheless,
relying solely on single‐frame detection for predicting candidate
bounding boxes can be unreliable in complex real‐world sce-
narios. Issues such as occlusion, motion blur, and background
clutter can degrade visual features, potentially causing the de-
tector to mistake targets for background. Consequently, the
temporal consistency of the corresponding trajectory segment
may be compromised. To address this, CSTrackV2 [47] aims to
recover misclassified targets by utilising temporal cues for sec-
ondary inspection. This approach effectively restores missed
targets and produces smoother track trajectories. Additionally,
RetinaTrack [48] focuses on feature representation and leverages
post‐feature pyramid network (FPN) prediction layers to obtain
instance‐level features for each target. Moreover, the continuous
matching resolves identity switches that may occur due to oc-
clusion. Compared to the methods mentioned above, which
implement detection and embedding within a unified framework
but still depend on two‐stage tracking for detection and data as-
sociation, the Chained‐Tracker [49] achieves a complete end‐to‐
end implementation of Joint Detection and Tracking (JDT).
It transforms data association into a regression task that pairs
detections across two frames. Figure 8 illustrates the differences
between the Chained‐Tracker and traditional Multi‐Object
Tracking (MOT) algorithms.

Joint Anchor‐Free Detection With Tracking
Due to the promising performance of anchor‐free detectors in
mitigating anchor confusion, tracking with these detectors has
been explored to address the computational complexity and
inefficiency associated with the Joint Detection and Tracking
(JDT) paradigm.
For instance, following the JDE approach as illustrated in
Figure 9, FairMOT [50] combines the anchor‐free detector
CenterNet [54] and Re‐Identification (Re‐ID) into two homo-
geneous branches, which helps reduce excessive competition
between them and strike a better balance. FairMOT offers ad-
vantages in real‐time performance and high accuracy, making it
well‐suited for complex scenarios. However, it has notable
limitations, particularly its high demands for model training
and extensive dataset requirements. Following, UTM [44] takes
a
different
approach
by
emphasising
enhanced
identity
discrimination. UTM improves re‐identification feature repre-
sentation and opts not to rely on a shared network for detection
and re‐identification. This enhancement increases stability and
accuracy in challenging situations like dense crowds, occlu-
sions, and high target similarity. However, UTM's limitations
stem from its focus on target detection and Re‐ID feature
extraction within individual frames, lacking a dedicated long‐
term association mechanism. Therefore, Cetintas et al. [51]
introduced a dual strategy that incorporates both short‐term
tracking and long‐term association to address these chal-
lenges.
In
this
approach,
short‐term
associations
enable
continuous tracking across frames, while long‐term associations
ensure accurate re‐identification of targets after occlusions or
prolonged absences. This integrated design significantly im-
proves tracking continuity, particularly in complex scenarios
involving extended occlusions and disappearances.
In contrast to the aforementioned methods, building upon the
principles of CenterNet [54], which represents objects as points,
CenterTrack [52] formulates the tracking as a point detection
and offset prediction problem. It represents object positions
through centre point detection and utilises network‐predicted

Detection‐Based MOT Enhanced by
Transformers
Due to the strong modelling capabilities and excellent scalability
of the Transformer, many methods have opted to use Trans-
formers to enhance error control and improve MOT accuracy.
Unlike the methods analysis above, this section focuses on the
Transformer‐enhanced MOT paradigm, listed in Table 4.
Figure 10 presents the basic framework of Transformer‐assisted
methods for object tracking.
TransTrack [57], which utilises the query‐key mechanism, is a
pioneering Transformer‐based method that shows great promise
for
Multi‐Object
Tracking
(MOT).
However,
it
requires
significant training time and computational resources, which
limits its real‐time performance. Similarly, ContrasTR [62] en-
hances the object‐level embeddings of DETR by applying an
instance‐level contrastive loss and a refined sampling strategy.
Building on Deformable DETR, Zeng et al. extend it for MOT by
implicitly linking data and iteratively updating predicted tra-
jectories using track queries and a tracklet‐aware label assign-
ment (TALA) [63]. TrackFormer [58] employs a Transformer
encoder‐decoder framework to manage MOT in crowded
scenes, addressing issues related to trajectory initialisation,
identification, and formation. MOTRv2 [60] enhances MOTR by
incorporating Deformable DETR. In contrast, methods like
TransTrack, TrackFormer, and ContrasTR leverage the flexi-
bility of Transformers and have fewer domain‐specific priors,
resulting in significant improvements in tracking performance.
These approaches have yielded promising outcomes in complex
scenarios such as DanceTrack as illustrated in Table 5. How-
ever, on traditional pedestrian tracking datasets, they have not
surpassed existing state‐of‐the‐art tracking‐by‐detection algo-
rithms, such as ByteTrack and OC‐SORT. This limitation is
largely due to challenges like target density, as demonstrated in
Figure 6b.
In contrast to these structural optimisations, MeMOT [59] em-
phasises the importance of temporal history by utilising a
memory aggregation module. This module encodes object in-
formation from previous embeddings, facilitating long‐term
data association through attention mechanisms. Similarly,
MeMOTR [61] introduces a memory module that stabilises ob-
ject features over time and incorporates a memory‐attention
layer for enhanced object representation. These algorithms
have highlighted the critical role of temporal information in
multi‐object tracking scenarios. Despite these advancements,
challenges persist, such as data limitations and overfitting,
which hinder the full potential of the models. Developing
improved joint training approaches may provide a potential
solution to these issues.

Joint Single Object Tracking and Multiple
Object Tracking
To overcome the limitations of Multi‐Object Tracking (MOT)
regarding detection quality and to bridge the gap between Single
Object Tracking (SOT) and MOT, MOT incorporates SOT
methodologies to obtain more accurate information regarding
the tracked appearance and motion, thereby compensating for
detection errors [64–70]. The integrated SOT and MOT para-
digm is outlined in Table 6, and the fundamental framework of
SOT‐based methods for MOT is illustrated in Figure 11.
For example, DMAN [64] utilises a Bi‐LSTM network for end‐to‐
end feature extraction and matching the observation box with
historical target trajectory features. It incorporates a modified
ECO‐based single object tracking (SOT) module within the
network. The main focus of this approach is on the response
map information, which includes details for both target local-
isation and classification. With the introduction of the Siamese
network framework, FAMNet [65] integrates SOT into its
candidate generation and target management processes. In the
candidate generation module, FAMNet employs SOT to create
virtual candidates that complement the real candidates pro-
duced by the detector, addressing potential omissions. More-
over, during target management, these virtual candidates can
replace poorly matched real candidates from the detector,
helping to maintain the continuity of the target's trajectory. The
inherent presence of the Siamese structure provides localisation
and recognition information necessary for the SOT task.
To address the issue of missed detections caused by occlusion,
Feng et al. [66] leverage SOT to capture short‐term cues in
combination with long‐term cues from ReID to enhance the
robustness and accuracy of multi‐object tracking (MOT). The
algorithm employs the SiamRPN single object tracker, which
has strong localisation capabilities and essentially performs
additional detection, leading to improved tracking accuracy.
Similarly, for the occlusion problem, STAM [71] introduces a
spatiotemporal attention mechanism to tackle occlusion and
drift issues caused by interaction between templates. Addition-
ally, STAM also reduces computational complexity by sharing
computations across multiple targets. However, the model's
localisation output operates independently from the detection
module, necessitating each single‐object tracker to process a
significant volume of background data, which leads to repetitive
computations and considerable computational overhead. To

Multi‐Object Segmentation and Tracking
The assessment of segmentation reveals that MOT can leverage
the enhanced precision of segmentation to define target
boundaries at the pixel level. Compared to detection‐based MOT
methods, segmentation‐based MOT demonstrates significant
performance improvements, particularly in scenarios with
crowded and occluded objects, by addressing issues such as
excessive non‐target information in larger bounding boxes and
incomplete data in smaller ones. The basic framework for
segmentation‐based methods in MOT is illustrated in Figure 12;
and the related segmentation‐based MOT paradigm is sum-
marised in Table 7.
Voigtlaender et al. [72] propose a new approach called multi‐
object tracking and segmentation (MOTS), which extends
traditional MOT to include the instance segmentation tracking
task. To establish a baseline for this new task, they introduce
Track‐RCNN, an extension of Mask R‐CNN [24], incorporating
3D convolutions to capture temporal information and an asso-
ciation head to link object identities over time. Additionally,
they present two new datasets derived from the KITTI [78] and
MOTChallenge
[79]
datasets
for
training
and
evaluating
methods that address the MOTS task. Although this discovery is
promising, a significant drawback is the reliance on pixel‐level
annotations, which can be highly time‐consuming and costly
to generate. Therefore, MOTSNet [73] leverages optical flow for
automatic dataset annotation to overcome this issue. This work
addresses the challenge of insufficient MOTS training data
without the need for extensive manual annotation efforts.
Furthermore, MOTSNet incorporates a mask pooling layer to
integrate detailed features from the masks into the association
module of the tracking framework, resulting in a significant
improvement in tracking performance. However, the large
receptive fields of traditional 2D and 3D convolutions can
hinder the learning of discriminative instance embeddings.
Deep convolutional features may blur the distinction between
foreground and background regions and confuse the foreground
regions of the target instances with those of neighbouring in-
stances. Consequently, while current MOTS methods utilise
advanced segmentation backbones to extract image features,
they fail to learn discriminative instance embeddings, which are
crucial for robust instance association, ultimately leading to
limited performance.
Considering the limitations of 2D and 3D convolutions in
learning discriminative instance features, PointTrack [76] treats
2D image pixels as unordered 2D point clouds and learns
instance embeddings using a point cloud processing approach.
Furthermore, PointTrackerþþ [77] enhances PointTrack in
three key areas. First, it adapts a semantic segmentation decoder
trained with focal loss during the instance segmentation stage,
improving the selection quality. Second, it introduces a data
augmentation strategy that enhances segmentation performance

Metrics
There are metrics available to fairly evaluate the performance of
the MOT method, including:
 Frames Per Second (FPS): This speed metric measures the
number of frames tracked per second by the multi‐object
tracker. A higher value indicates a closer approximation
to the real‐time performance of the method
 False Positives (FP): Hypothetical bounding boxes in a
video sequence that does not align with the true trajectory
are termed false positives. In essence, they are erroneously
predicted to be true instances when they are, in fact, false.
FP represents the count of false positives observed across
the entire video sequence.
 False Negatives (FN): Bounding boxes in a video sequence
that correspond to the true trajectory but fail to match with
hypothetical bounding boxes are termed false negatives. In
simpler terms, they are instances that are true but are
predicted to be false. FN represents the count of false
negatives observed across the entire video sequence.
 Mostly Tracked tracks (MT): The number of tracked tra-
jectories in which each target is correctly tracked in at least
80% of the video frames during the tracking process.
 Mostly Lost tracks (ML): The number of tracked trajec-
tories in which each target is correctly tracked in at most
20% of the video frames during the tracking process.
 Fragments (Frag): When an accurate tracking trajectory is
interrupted for some reason and then accurately tracked
again later, it is called a tracking fragment. Thus, frag
represents the total number of fragments in the video
sequence.
 Recall (Recall): The ratios of correctly matched detection to
ground‐truth detection are used to measure the detector's
performance.
 ID Switches (IDSW): Refers to the number of identity
switches observed during the tracking process. In a video
sequence, IDSW indicates the frequency with which the
identity of a tracked object is exchanged.
 Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA), as depicted in
Equation (1), reflects the accuracy of MOT. Moreover,
MOTA is regarded as the most crucial precision indicator in
MOT algorithms, with 1 representing optimal performance.
Thus, a higher value signifies better tracking accuracy.

Discussion and Future Work
This paper reviews recent methods in MOT, focussing on their
techniques and notable features. Our description categorises
MOT into three groups based on core technologies: detection‐
based, SOT‐based, and segmentation‐based. Each of these
technologies contributes to improving MOT's performance. To
advance research in this field, the paper also highlights several
factors that influence MOT's tracking performance.
 The first factor is the dependency on object candi-
dates: In MOT, Object detection serves as the primary
foundation by providing the candidates to be tracked. SOT
and segmentation technologies also address inaccuracies in
object detection results, which can significantly impact
tracking performance. Together, these elements contribute
to the formation of tracking trajectories. These technology

 The second factor is the crowded scene: In such envi-

occlusion of objects by other targets, and variations in

tracking of target trajectories. Additionally, real‐time per-
formance and accuracy must be considered for an effective

 The final factor is the training data: The quality and
quantity of annotated video data are crucial for achieving
high‐quality tracking results, especially given the complexity
of real‐world scenes and the large number of parameters in
deep learning‐based methods. Several benchmark datasets
for MOT have beendeveloped in recentyears, particularly for
applications like autonomous driving and intelligent sur-
veillance. Notable examples include the MOT Challenge and
the KITTI datasets. However, due to the widespread use of
MOT and concerns related to privacy and security, there is
still a scarcity of MOT datasets, and annotating them remains
challenging.
Based on the discussed methods and evaluation results, several
future research directions are presented that would benefit the
field of MOT:
 Large‐Scale Video Datasets with Dense Annotations:
As of the submission date of this paper, several MOT
benchmark datasets have been developed across various
domains, including autonomous driving [78, 80, 81], aerial
views [85, 105, 106], crowded indoor and outdoor scenes
[102], animal tracking [93], and satellite tracking [95, 96].
However, there is a pressing need for a dataset that features
large‐scale data and long‐term temporal correlations to
enhance the performance of MOT methods.
 Scene Understanding Integration: In MOT, scene un-

and scene structure. This information is particularly valu-

where

frequently

Thus,

 Multi‐class,

and
3D
associated Multi‐object Tracking: Firstly, most existing
MOT algorithms [25, 26, 45, 49] primarily focus on
following a single type of object, such as a ‘car’ or ‘pedes-
trian’. However, tracking multiple categories of objects is
often necessary in practical applications. As a result, multi‐
class multi‐object tracking (MCOT) is expected to be a
significant direction for future development.
Secondly, multi‐camera object tracking (MCMT) effectively
overcomes the limitations of a single camera's field of view. This
task commonly involves overlapping and non‐overlapping sce-
narios within multi‐camera systems. Multiple cameras capture
the same scene in an overlapping scenario, necessitating data
fusion from these various sources [109, 110]. In contrast, a non‐
overlapping scenario utilises a multi‐camera network to record
different scenes, which introduces challenges related to cross‐
camera data association [111, 112]. Both scenarios contribute
to the complexity of MOT research.
Thirdly, information about tracked objects can be obtained from
multiple sensors in practical applications. The effective fusion of
various sensory modalities is crucial for accurately tracking
multiple objects and estimating their state information, including
position, velocity, and orientation. However, inconsistencies
among different modalities and observation noise limit the
robustness and reliability of tracking systems, highlighting the
need for further enhancements.
Finally, unlike the 2D scenes primarily addressed in MOT [33,
50], representing tracked objects in 3D environments holds
great potential for achieving more accurate results [113–115].
This approach can be particularly beneficial in resolving oc-
clusion issues. For instance, CAMO‐MOT [116] introduces an
innovative camera‐LiDAR fusion framework for 3D MOT based
on Combined Appearance‐Motion Optimisation. This frame-
work utilises camera and LiDAR data, significantly reducing
tracking failures caused by occlusions and false detections.
Therefore, further exploration of MOT in 3D scenes is essential.
 Language‐Guided Multi‐Object Tracking: The integra-
tion of language guidance in Multi‐Object Tracking (MOT)
presents an innovative avenue for enhancing the efficiency
and effectiveness of tracking systems. In language‐guided
MOT, the fusion of linguistic information with visual data
or the joint optimisation with object tracking is crucial.
First, the system must understand the input language de-
scriptions, which typically involve the attributes of the
objects (e.g., ‘red car’) or their behaviours (e.g., ‘driving
along the road’). This linguistic information is then inte-
grated with visual data through multimodal fusion models
to improve the accuracy of object recognition and tracking.
Unlike traditional bounding box or mask annotations, language‐
guided MOT presents a promising and friendly way to reduce
the burden of extensive annotations while maintaining tracking
systems' efficiency. However, discrepancies between language
and visual modalities will negatively impact tracking systems'
accuracy and reliability, and this needs further scheme optimi-
sations in modality alignment.

by analysing algorithmic contributions and presenting quanti-
