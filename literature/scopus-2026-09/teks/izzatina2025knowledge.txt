Abstract— Accurate classification of oil palm fresh fruit 
bunch (FFB) ripeness is essential to ensure palm oil quality and 
enhance production efficiency. Conventional manual grading is 
labor-intensive and prone to inconsistency, while many 
automated approaches rely on computationally heavy deep 
learning models that are impractical for resource-constrained 
environments. To address this, we propose a knowledge 
distillation-based framework for ordinal ripeness classification, 
where ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, and ResNet101 serve as 
teacher models and MobileNetV2 acts as the lightweight student 
network. The framework enables the student model to inherit 
discriminative features from deeper networks while preserving 
efficiency, making it well-suited for real-time deployment. 
Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method, 
derived from the distillation process where ResNet50 acts as the 
teacher model and MobileNetV2 as the student model, achieves 
95.64% ± 0.63%% accuracy, outperforming standalone 
MobileNetV2 while reducing parameter size by 91% compared 
to ResNet101. These findings highlight the potential of the 
proposed method to support sustainable palm oil production 
through reliable, low-cost, and scalable ripeness assessment in 
field conditions. 
Keywords—Oil palm classification, lightweight deep learning, 
knowledge distillation, ordinal classification, computer vision. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Indonesia is the world’s largest palm oil producer, 
supplying over 54% of global exports with 47 million tonnes 
in 2023 [1]. The sector contributes 4.5% to national GDP, 
employs over 16.2 million workers, and accounts for more 
than half of global exports [2]. Harvest quality is critical, as 
only ripe fresh fruit bunches (FFB) yield high-quality oil, 
while unripe bunches have lower oil content and overripe ones 
rapidly develop free fatty acids that degrade oil quality [3], 
[4]. Most plantations still rely on manual ripeness assessment 
based on visual inspection and loose fruit counts [4], a process 
that is labor-intensive and risky, particularly in difficult terrain 
and with taller palms [5]. These challenges emphasizes the 
urgent need for automated solutions to enhance accuracy, 
efficiency, and worker safety. 
Recent studies demonstrate the promise of automated FFB 
classification using computer vision [6]. Suharjito et al. 
achieved 98.7% accuracy with modified YOLOv4 [7], while 
lightweight models such as EfficientNetB0 reached ~89% 
accuracy for mobile deployment [8]. These works highlight 
the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost in 
practical field systems. 
Deep learning has also shown to be a powerful approach 
for grading and classification tasks, as its architectures can 
automatically extract complex hierarchical features from raw 
data, yielding high accuracy and robustness [9]. However, as 
networks grow deeper and more complex, accuracy tends to 
improve, but so do requirements for memory and processing 
power. This trade-off is especially critical in practical settings 
such as agricultural harvest assessment, where systems need 
to run efficiently on limited hardware and still provide 
accurate results. 
This study employs knowledge distillation to balance 
model accuracy with computational efficiency. Building on 
Hinton et al.’s extension of Caruana’s model compression 
concept, this approach allows compact student networks to 
learn from larger teacher networks by imitating their 
probability distributions rather than relying only on hard 
classification labels [10]. Applied to FFB ripeness 
classification, knowledge distillation bridges the gap between 
deep learning performance and practical agricultural 
deployment constraints. 
II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
A. Knowledge Distillation Framework  
Model compression has advanced significantly since 
Caruana’s early work, culminating in Hinton et al.’s 
framework [10]. The key idea is to transfer knowledge from 
large teacher networks to smaller student models using soft 
target distributions instead of hard labels. Teacher models 
produce 
probability 
distributions 
rich 
in 
inter-class 
relationships, enabling students to learn beyond categorical 
outputs. 
Knowledge distillation uses a composite loss balancing 
teacher prediction matching with ground-truth accuracy. Soft 
targets are generated via temperature-scaled softmax, where 
the temperature parameter smooths distributions and captures 
teacher uncertainty [10]. Surveys by Gou et al. confirm its 
central role in efficient computer vision, with applications in 
classification, detection, and segmentation [11]. 
Response-based distillation is most effective for 
classification, while feature-based approaches excel in dense 
prediction tasks [12]. Unlike prior applications, this study 
targets oil palm ripeness assessment, where computational 
efficiency is critical. Deploying large models on resource-
constrained plantation equipment is impractical and costly, 
hindering timely ripeness feedback essential for harvesting 
premium-grade fruit bunches [7]. Studies show that complex 
real-world datasets benefit greatly from distillation, achieving 
higher accuracy than direct training while maintaining the

B. Deep Learning Approaches for FFB Classification 
Knowledge distillation has emerged in agriculture for 
developing efficient computer vision models, with hybrid 
methods achieving up to 75% lower computational costs 
without sacrificing accuracy [14]. In FFB ripeness 
classification, Suharjito et al. attained 98.7% accuracy using 
modified YOLOv4 [7], while lightweight CNNs like 
EfficientNetB0 balanced speed and accuracy (89.3% at 96 ms) 
[8]; similar progress was reported with EfficientDet-Lite [15] 
and DenseNet, which outperformed AlexNet by 8.5% in 
accuracy and 8% in F1 score [16]. Reviews highlight 
computer vision as the most effective non-destructive ripeness 
assessment method (57–98.7% accuracy) [6], with recent 
ordinal regression models such as GNOOR enhancing 
reliability by enforcing label order and neighbour consistency 
[7]. 
C. Teacher and Student Architectures 
ResNet architectures are strong teacher models due to 
residual connections that prevent vanishing gradients and 
enable deep feature learning for effective knowledge transfer 
[18]. Their reliability, demonstrated in tasks like effluent 
dialysate abnormality detection [19], supports their use here, 
with variants from ResNet18–101 offering capacity–
efficiency flexibility. MobileNetV2, optimized for mobile 
deployment through depthwise separable convolutions and 
inverted residuals, achieves high efficiency and accuracy [20], 
[21], while moderate architectural differences between 
teacher and student models enhance generalization, making 
the ResNet–MobileNetV2 pair well-suited for knowledge 
distillation.

IV. EXPERIMENT 
A. Data & Setting 
The experimental evaluation employed a comprehensive 
dataset of FFB images captured on field conditions 
representative of Indonesian palm oil plantations. The dataset 
comprises 4,728 images sized 256×256 pixels distributed 
across five ripeness categories: immature (758 images, 16%), 
partially ripe (1,405, 30%), fully ripe (1,489 images, 31%), 
overripe (665 images, 14%), and decayed (411 images, 9%). 
The dataset distribution reflects the natural occurrence 
patterns observed in commercial plantation operations, 
consistent with recent annotated datasets developed for oil 
palm fruit bunch pile ripeness grading [18]. 
 
Fig. 2. Representative sample images from the oil palm fresh fruit bunch 
(FFB) dataset for each ripeness class: (a) Immature, (b) Partially Ripe, (c) 
Fully Ripe, (d) Overripe, (e) Decayed.  
Oil palm ripeness progresses through five stages defined 
by surface coloration and oil content: immature bunches 
exhibit dark purple-black coloration with negligible oil 
content; partially and fully ripe stages show increasing red 
coloration with corresponding oil accumulation, with fully 
ripe bunches (the commercial target) displaying maximum oil 
content and minimal free fatty acids [3], [4], [5]. Overripe and 
decayed stages feature brownish-red to black discoloration 
with accelerating free fatty acid development that 
progressively degrades oil quality and economic viability [3], 
[4]. 
The dataset partitioning strategy employed stratified 
random 
sampling 
to 
maintain 
representative 
class 
distributions across all data splits. The training set comprised 
70% of the total dataset (3,306 images), validation set 
contained 15% (704 images), and test set included 15% (718 
images). This split ratio was selected to ensure sufficient 
training data for complex model architectures while 
maintaining adequate validation and test sets for robust 
performance evaluation. The stratified sampling approach 
guaranteed that each ripeness category maintained its original 
distribution proportion across all three splits, preventing class 
imbalance issues that could compromise model training 
effectiveness and evaluation reliability. 
Data augmentation techniques addressed the challenges of 
limited agricultural datasets while maintaining realistic image 
characteristics 
essential 
for 
field 
deployment. 
The 
augmentation pipeline incorporated multiple transformations 
applied probabilistically during training to enhance dataset 
diversity without compromising visual authenticity. Applied 
transformations included random resized cropping with scale 
range 0.8-1.0 and aspect ratio range 0.75-1.33, random 
horizontal flipping with 50% probability, random rotation 
within ±15 degrees to simulate different viewing angles 
during harvesting, and color jittering with controlled 
brightness (±0.2), contrast (±0.2), saturation (±0.1), and hue 
(±0.05) adjustments. Additional augmentation techniques 
included random gaussian blur with kernel size 3×3 applied 
with 20% probability and random grayscale conversion with 
10% probability to enhance model robustness against varying 
image quality conditions. These augmentation strategies 
enhanced dataset diversity while preserving the visual

The experimental framework employed consistent training 
procedures across all model configurations to ensure reliable 
performance comparisons. Batch size was set to 16 across all

gradient updates, considering the computational constraints of 
the experimental hardware setup. All models employed 
identical loss functions, with categorical cross-entropy loss for 
standalone models and the composite knowledge distillation

performance comparisons across different initialization 
conditions, with final results reported as mean ± standard 
deviation across the three runs. 
B. Results & Discussion 
The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of 
knowledge distillation for FFB ripeness classification, with 
systematic performance improvements achieved through 
teacher-student training paradigms compared to standalone 
model implementations. The comprehensive evaluation 
reveals that knowledge distillation enables lightweight 
MobileNetV2 models to achieve performance levels 
comparable to substantially larger architectures while 
maintaining computational efficiency essential for field 
deployment scenarios.

The comparative analysis presented in the experimental 
results reveals significant insights regarding the effectiveness 
of different teacher-student combinations for knowledge 
transfer in FFB ripeness classification. The standalone 
MobileNetV2 model achieved 94.48% ± 0.57% accuracy with 
2.23M parameters, establishing a strong baseline performance 
considering its compact footprint suitable for mobile 
deployment. However, the knowledge distillation framework 
consistently improved upon this baseline across all teacher 
architectures evaluated, with improvements ranging from 
0.27% to 1.16% depending on the teacher model complexity 
and architectural characteristics. 
ResNet50 as teacher model demonstrated optimal 
knowledge transfer capabilities, enabling the MobileNetV2 
student to achieve the highest accuracy of 95.64% ± 0.63%. 
This superior performance can be attributed to ResNet50's 
optimal balance between representational capacity and 
training stability, providing sufficient architectural depth for 
capturing complex visual patterns associated with subtle 
ripeness variations while avoiding the potential overfitting 
tendencies observed in deeper architectures. The ResNet50-
distilled MobileNetV2 model achieved a weighted loss of 
0.09566, 
indicating 
superior 
convergence 
properties 
compared to other teacher-student combinations, while 
maintaining cross-entropy (0.1182) and mean squared error 
(0.0860) values that demonstrate robust learning of both 
categorical and ordinal relationships inherent in ripeness 
classification. 
The progression of knowledge transfer effectiveness 
across different ResNet teacher architectures reveals 
important insights about optimal teacher complexity for 
agricultural classification tasks. ResNet18, while providing 
meaningful knowledge transfer with a 0.27% improvement 
over standalone MobileNetV2 (achieving 94.75% ± 0.72%), 
demonstrates limited representational depth for capturing the 
subtle visual patterns that distinguish adjacent ripeness 
categories. The relatively modest improvement suggests that 
ResNet18's architectural capacity, while sufficient for basic 
feature extraction, lacks the sophisticated representational 
power necessary for encoding the complex knowledge 
required for optimal student training in this domain.

V. CONCLUSIONS 
This 
study 
investigated a knowledge distillation 
framework for efficient ordinal classification of oil palm fresh 
fruit bunch (FFB) ripeness levels, aiming to achieve high 
accuracy with minimal computational cost for real-world 
plantation deployment. The experimental results demonstrate 
that using ResNet50 as the teacher model enables the 
lightweight MobileNetV2 student network to achieve 95.64% 
classification accuracy with only 2.23M parameters, which 
represents 
a 
1.16% 
improvement 
over 
standalone 
MobileNetV2 and a 91% reduction in parameter size 
compared to ResNet101. The distilled MobileNetV2 model 
achieved performance comparable to more complex and 
contemporary lightweight architectures such as MobileViT-S 
(96.01% ± 0.40%) and ConvNeXt-Tiny (96.01% ± 0.35%), 
while significantly outperforming traditional deep networks 
including AlexNet (89.46% ± 0.35%), VGG16 (91.83% ± 
0.37%), and even standalone ResNet101 (93.18% ± 0.50%). 
These findings confirm that knowledge distillation enables 
compact models to reach accuracy levels close to state-of-the-
art networks, while maintaining far greater parameter 
efficiency and computational practicality for mobile and edge-
based agricultural systems. The results also highlight that 
knowledge distillation effectively transfers both categorical 
and ordinal relationships from deeper teacher networks to 
smaller student models, leading to reduced cross-entropy and 
mean-squared-error losses. This improved ability to capture 
the continuous progression of ripeness levels enhances 
prediction reliability in field conditions, which is an essential 
requirement for operational decision-making in palm oil 
harvesting.  The research is constrained by limited dataset 
diversity that may not fully represent varied geographical, 
seasonal, and cultivar conditions across different plantation 
environments. Additionally, the study focuses solely on 
visual-based knowledge distillation without exploring 
integration with complementary sensing modalities that could 
enhance classification robustness. Future research should 
expand dataset diversity to encompass broader geographical 
and seasonal variations while developing multi-modal 
knowledge distillation frameworks that integrate visual 
features with complementary sensing modalities such as near-
infrared spectroscopy or chemical sensors. Investigation of 
adaptive knowledge distillation strategies that dynamically 
adjust 
teacher-student 
relationships 
based 
on 
local 
environmental conditions could further enhance practical 
applicability and deployment effectiveness across diverse 
agricultural settings.
