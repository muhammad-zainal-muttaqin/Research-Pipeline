Abstract—Palm oil Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFBs) ripeness de-
tection is the key to precision agriculture, allowing ideal harvest-
ing as well as post-harvesting handling. Multiple deep-learning
methods for palm oil FFB ripeness detection were reviewed in
the literature. However, the current research challenge is training
the deep-learning models using images collected from plantations
directly, and hence, detecting palm oil FFBs in real-time with
reasonably sound detection accuracies. Hence, this study explored
three You Only Look Once (YOLO) algorithm versions to address
this challenge: YOLOv5, YOLOv6, and YOLOv7. Notably, no
prior research has utilized at least three YOLO versions to
recognize the ripeness levels of palm oil FFBs in real-time. This
research aims to identify the most effective algorithm among
these YOLO versions for this specific task. The methods involved
creating a model utilizing a custom dataset as well as conducting
training and testing. A custom dataset of 2000 images was
obtained from a palm oil plantation in the Selangor region
of Malaysia to train and test models. The dataset was trained
with YOLOv5s (small), YOLOv5m (medium), YOLOv6s (small),
YOLOv6m (medium), and YOLOv7 models. The YOLOv5s
model proved the most effective for real-time detection of palm oil
FFBs, with a 98.5% F1-score and a solid 35.76 Frames per Second
(FPS). Finally, performance analysis showed that the YOLOv5s
model outperformed existing methods, offering a dependable
solution for palm oil FFB ripening detection in real-time scenarios
right at the locations of palm oil plantations.
Index Terms—object detection, palm oil, fresh fruit bunch,
YOLOv5, YOLOv6, YOLOv7
I. INTRODUCTION
The world ranks Malaysia as one of its largest palm oil
producing nations. Palm oil industry plays a major role in
Malaysia’s gross domestic product (GDP) while its devel-
opment continues to rise. The process of maximizing oil
extraction begins with proper selection of fresh fruit bunches
for harvesting. Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) provides
the guidelines for completing this process [1]. The FFBs are
now picked up by humans and graded according to the color
of the surface of the fruit and the number of loose fruits on the
ground as an illustration of how well the fruits have ripened.
However, excessive reliance on human graders would lead to
such misclassification of the fruits as the position of the FFBs
on the trees, the height of the trees and lousy vision due
to the surrounding weather, lightning, wrong number of the
loose fruits on the ground and so on. Harvesting of unripe
FFBs as a result of such misclassifications would decrease the
profitability of palm oil plantation companies since they would
end up producing trashy palm oils [2].
The relationship between the oil content and fruit colour of
palm oil fruits was examined by Ghazali et al. The character-
istics of both the oil content and fruit colour of palm oil fruits
were positively correlated [3]. Fig. 1 shows the difference
between overripe FFB, ripe FFB, underripe FFB and unripe

YOLOv3, namely YOLO-P, to detect palm oil FFBs at various
stages of maturity, achieving an F1-score of 97.0% [7]. Lai et
al. used the YOLOv4 algorithm to detect ripe palm oil FFBs
in real-time, achieving an F1-score of 88.0% [8].

II. METHODOLOGY
A. About YOLO Algorithms
YOLO [10] represents a popular model for object detection
and image segmentation which Redmon et al. developed in
2015. YOLO treats object detection as a regression task which
generates spatially divided bounding boxes together with their
corresponding class probability predictions. YOLOv2 [11]
emerged in 2016 with new features that included batch normal-
ization and anchor boxes and dimension clustering. YOLOv3
[12] introduced multiple anchors and spatial pyramid pooling
in addition to a more efficient backbone network when it
launched in 2018. YOLOv4 [13] emerged in 2020 with Mosaic
data augmentation and a new anchor-free detection head with
an updated loss function as its key features. YOLOv5 [14]
delivered more performance upgrades alongside new capabil-
ities that included automatic format export and integrated ex-
periment tracking and hyperparameter optimization. YOLOv6
[15] received an open-source release from Meituan in 2022
and now powers autonomous delivery robots operated by the
company. YOLOv7 [16] added pose estimation capabilities to
the model through the use of the COCO keypoints dataset.
B. YOLOv5 Algorithm
YOLOv5 was distributed to the deep-learning community
in 2020 [17]. The YOLOv5 framework architecture can be
seen in Fig. 2. There are some similarities between YOLOv5
and its previous iterations. For instance, number one the
input, number two the neck, number three the backbone, as
well as number four the head are the four components that
make up its four-part architecture. Note that the four areas
stated above are those where YOLOv5 has made the most
progress. The input section uses Mosiac data augmentation. In
addition, random cropping, random scaling, as well as random
arrangement are examples of data augmentation techniques
that produce effective results [18]. DarkNet and the Cross-
Stage Partial Network (CSP- Net) [19] were combined by
YOLOv5 to create CSPDarknet, that acted as the backbone
of the network [20]. By incorporating gradient change in the
feature map via recurrent navigation in one relation map,
CSPNet resolves the re-entered gradient information for large-
scale backbones. It reduces model parameters as well as
FLOPS and ensures inference speed, accuracy, and model
size. Before carrying out inference regarding edge devices
having limited resources, both the speed and accuracy of the
palm oil FFB ripeness levels need to be determined at palm
oil plantation sites. The model’s size influences the model’s
inference performance on the cameras or other detection based
devices. Consequently, the YOLOv5 uses a Path Aggregation
Network (PANet), serving as the neck, to enhance data flow
[21]. The Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) topology is new
in PANet and has a bottom-up approach to enhance low-
level feature propagation. Since the feature grid is pooled to
all feature levels, it can be observed that adaptive feature
pooling guarantees that the following subnetwork receives
applicable information from every feature level. Moreover,

The YOLOv5s, as well as YOLOv5m models, were used in
this study. Both the YOLOv5s as well as YOLOv5m models
have the same neck, backbone, and head. The only elements
that differ are the width and depth. The model’s network
structure can be changed by adjusting these two variables.
Note that the depth multiple controls the model’s depth [23].
As an illustration, V5s and V5m have depths of 0.33 and
0.67, respectively. In other words, there are twice as many
bottlenecks in V5m as in V5s.
Width multiple controls the number with regard to convolu-
tion (conv) kernels [23]. V5m has a width of 0.75, while V5s
has a width of 0.50. This shows that V5s has two-thirds as
many conv cores as V5m. To test the accuracy of these two
models in actual situations, these two models were assessed
utilizing unknown images to determine their effectiveness in
real-life situations.
C. YOLOv6 Algorithm
Li et al. proposed YOLOv6 algorithm in the mid of 2022
year. Li et al. replaced the backbone and neck of the previous
YOLO version networks with the EfficientRep Backbone as
well as the Rep-PAN Neck to progress the YOLOv6 algorithm.
Here, the network head was then decoupled, which allowed
multiple features to be isolated from the final head. The ar-
chitecture with regard to the YOLOv6 framework is presented
in Fig. 3. The YOLOv6 architecture is comprised of three
sections: the Rep-PAN Neck, the EfficientRep Backbone, and
also the Efficient Decoupled Head. The overall performance
concerning the detection model depends on the backbone
network architecture [24].
The efficient re-parameterizable (Efficient-Rep) backbone
was proposed for the YOLOv6 backbone. In the training
Fig. 3. YOLOv6 Algorithm Architecture
phase, RepBlock became the primary support component
for the small-scale models concerning YOLOv6. In addition,
each RepBlock is translated into stacks of 3x3 conv lay-
ers, known as the RepConv, alongside the ReLU activation
function working during the inference stage. Li et al. also
observed that when the capacity of the models is extended,
the computational cost, as well as the parameter number
regarding the single-path plain network, enhances rapidly. The
YOLOv6 algorithm corresponds to a modified CSPStackRep.
Thus, the medium and large models are constructed with a
modified CSPStackRep. There are three 1x1 conv layers in
the CSPStackRep and also a pile of sub-blocks comprising
two RepVGG blocks [25] or RepConv having a residual
connection. A CSP connection was employed to improve
performance without adding much computational load. It has
a shorter perspective compared to CSPRepResStage [26] and
explores the question of speed/accuracy trade-off. Li et al.
used the reshaped PAN topology of YOLOv4 [13] as well as
YOLOv5 [14] as the base of YOLOv6 algorithm’s detection
neck. In addition, the CSPBlock of YOLOv5 was changed
with RepBlock as well as CSPStackRepBlock for the small
and large models of YOLOv6. Consequently, they modified
the depth and width according to the change. From now
on, Li et al. named Rep-PAN as the YOLOv6 neck. To
design a more effective decoupled head than the FCOS [27]
and the YOLOX [28], Li et al. suggested a hybrid-channel
strategy for the YOLOv6 head. Specifically, they reduced
the number of middle 3×3 conv layers to one single layer.
Correspondingly, the YOLOv6 head width was scaled by the
width multipliers of the neck as well as the backbone. This led
to reduced computational costs and reduced inference latency,
consequently [24].
The YOLOv6 algorithm has several models such as
YOLOv6l (large), YOLOv6m (medium), YOLOv6s (small), as
well as YOLOv6n (nano). In this study, the YOLOv6s, as well
as YOLOv6m models, were applied. Comparing the speed as
well as accuracy, the single-path structure is more effective in
the YOLOv6s model compared to the multiple-path structure.
Therefore, the base of the YOLOv6s model is a CSPStackRep
Block having a 1/2 channel coefficient. Here, the single-path
structure may run faster than the multipe-path structure, even
though it has more FLOPs and parameters because it is more
parallel and has fewer memory requirements. In the case
of the YOLOv6m model, multi-branch structures are more
accurate and faster than single-path structures. Therefore, the
YOLOv6m model is constructed with CSPStackRep Block,
where the channel coefficient is 2/3 [24]. Hence, these two

D. YOLOv7 Algorithm
In July 2022, YOLOV7 was made available to the deep-
learning community. Wang et al. used layer aggregation with
Extended Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (E-ELAN),
35% fewer network parameters, a trainable bag of freebies, as
well as model scaling concerning concatenation-based models
to develop the state-of-the-art YOLOV7 algorithm. Here, the
YOLOV7 architecture, later known as the E-ELAN, was
suggested by Wang et al. and is based on the ELAN. To
incrementally enhance the network’s learning capacity without
changing the initial gradient path, Wang et al. proposed the E-
ELAN architecture. This architecture makes use of shuffling,
expanding, as well as merging cardinality. The transition
layer’s architecture is left untouched by E-ELAN. Only the
computing block’s architectural design is changed. Wang et al.
used group conv to broaden the channel as well as the cardinal-
ity of computing blocks. Other than that, Wang et al. applied
the exact group conv as well as channel multiplier to each
of the computational blocks with regard to a computational
layer. The feature maps generated by each computing block
were then grouped into g groups as well as concatenated in line
with the initial group parameter g. At this time, each collection
regarding feature maps has a similar number of channels as the
initial architecture. To merge cardinality, Wang et al. inserted
g groups of feature maps. E-ELAN encouraged multiple com-
putational block groups to acquire additional, more diversified
features in addition to keeping the original ELAN design
architecture. Note that model scaling mainly modifies some
of the model’s characteristics and creates models at different
scales to account for different inference speeds. A method
for scaling models was created by Wang et al. of YOLOV7
that, when applied to concatenation-based models, only scales
the depth of a computational block. At the same time, the
transmission layer is still conducted with corresponding width
scaling. Here, the proposed compound scaling method put
forth by Wang et al. can preserve the YOLOV7 algorithm’s
best structure and the model’s original properties [29]. This
study utilized the YOLOV7 model. The model was evaluated
utilizing unknown images to determine the reliability with
regard to these models in real-life implementations.
E. Dataset
A custom dataset of 2000 images was obtained from a
palm oil plantation in Selangor, Malaysia, to train and test
models. The collected 2000 images were equally divided for
all four palm oil FFB classes, namely overripe FFB, ripe FFB,
underripe FFB and unripe FFB. The training and testing split
of the dataset was 80.0% and 20.0%, respectively.
Using the makesense.ai website, the images were labelled
accordingly after collecting the palm oil FFB image dataset.
Henceforth, the labelled images were converted into the YOLO
format text files. Five parameters make up the YOLO format
labelled text file: height, width, Ycenter coordinate, Xcenter
coordinate, as well as class [30].
F. Training & Evaluation Parameters
The training parameters utilised for all the models, namely
YOLOv5s, YOLOv5m, YOLOv6s, YOLOv6m and YOLOv7
models, are image size, batch number and number of epochs.
The image size, batch number and number of epochs for all
the models remained consistent at all times with values of 416,
16 and 100, respectively.

score, FPS, and lastly, Average Precision (AP) of all four
classes of palm oil FFBs were used to evaluate all the models
using an unseen image dataset. The precision (P) metric

IV. CONCLUSION
The work illustrated in this paper is the first example
of utilising at least three different versions of the YOLO
algorithm to detect palm oil FFBs in real-time. Although the
architecture of the models was left unchanged, it has been
demonstrated that the detection output is very promising when
using the training parameters selected. The YOLOv5s model
performed better than all the other YOLOv5, YOLOv6 and
YOLOv7 models and also better than previously established
models to detect palm oil FFBs in real-time with F1-score
and FPS values of 98.5% and 35.76 FPS, respectively. The
performance of the YOLOv5s model may be further enhanced
with improvements in the model architecture, training dataset,
as well as hyperparameter optimisation. For upcoming works,
the trained model would be applied to mobile devices, which
would facilitate human graders in precisely collecting only ripe
bunches as well as minimising waste from harvesting bunches
that are not ripe.
