Abstract— Morus Serrata is a nutritious and medicinally 
valuable fruit with notable ecological benefits. In the 
Philippines, it is widely cultivated by both farmers and hobbyists 
due to its zero-waste harvest process. Traditional methods for 
assessing mulberry ripeness are labor-intensive and time-
consuming. This study evaluates the effectiveness of an 
automated Jetson Nano-based system for multi-class detection 
and yield quantification of Morus serrata fruits, categorized into 
four stages: flowering, unripe, ripe, and overripe. The system 
incorporates a SONY IMX477 camera module for image 
acquisition. Performance is assessed using mean absolute error 
and correlation coefficient. Results show a strong alignment 
between predicted and actual fruit counts, with an overall 
correlation of 86.69 percent. The highest accuracy is observed in 
the flowering stage, while the unripe stage presents the most 
significant detection challenge. Despite its effectiveness, the 
system's performance is affected by the computational 
limitations of the Jetson Nano, especially during high-resolution 
processing. Future enhancements should focus on optimizing the 
processing pipeline, improving feature extraction, and utilizing 
more powerful hardware to achieve greater accuracy and real-
time reliability. 
Keywords— Yield Quantification, Morus Serrata, YOLOv8, 
Fruit Stages, Jetson Nano 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Mulberry cultivation has gained popularity among 
farmers due to its minimal waste production throughout 
harvesting, utilizing various parts of the tree, including roots, 
branches, leaves, and fruits [1]. Morus Serrata fruits develop 
in clustered formations, progressing through distinct stages 
from flowering to ripeness. These clusters appear at arbitrary 
spatial positions within the canopy, making manual 
harvesting labor-intensive [2]. Traditional and chemical 
methods for ripeness assessment are often invasive and can 
damage the fruit [3][4]. 
Fig. 1. Captured Morus Serrata Fruit Stages 
Mulberries undergo visible transformations during 
maturation, including changes in size and color, transitioning 
from green to darker shades depending on the variety [5]. Fig. 
1 illustrates the primary stages of Morus Serrata fruit 
development: flowering (inflorescence emergence), unripe 
(green fruit), ripe (dark fruit), and overripe (soft and fully 
matured). The fruit grows from 20% to 90% of its final size 
during maturation, becoming ready for harvest when it 
softens and reaches its characteristic color [6]. 
YOLO, or You Only Look Once, is a general computer 
vision model popularly used in multiple fields for automating 
tasks. Its black-box approach provides a beginner-friendly 
interface. Multiple studies demonstrate that the general model 
can adapt to physical constraints, including Small Object 
Detection (SOD) and physical deformities [7][8][9], as well 
as environmental challenges such as poor lighting conditions 
[10].  
Researchers have also applied YOLO and other CNN-
based models to real-time applications that assist visually 
impaired individuals with complex tasks [11]. Additionally, 
these models have proven effective in agricultural 
classification tasks, such as distinguishing fruit varieties and 
detecting crop attributes [12]. Labor shortages in fruit crop 
production have also driven advancements in automated 
agricultural systems, such as using YOLO-based methods to 
identify tree canopy parts and estimate crop yields, 
exemplifying its utility in addressing labor-intensive tasks in 
orchard operations [13]. 
Mulberry dense clustering on trees makes detection 
challenging, which has led to the application of computer 
vision (CV) models. For example, one study [14] improved 
YOLOv3 using DenseNet and CloU loss for better feature 
extraction for mulberry detection in complex environments. 
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) has also been applied 
for classification [15] and mulberry ripeness detection [16], 
with one study fine-tuning models through transfer learning 
for classification. Studies have also explored YOLOv8 for 
various 
agricultural 
applications, 
including 
seed 
identification [17], fruit quality control [18], and object 
detection in crops [19]. However, despite various studies on 
model improvements for Morus Serrata, none have focused 
on yield quantification using CV, leaving a gap in crop load 
estimation research. 
The objective of yield quantification of Morus Serrata fruit 
stages using YOLOv8-based identification is to address this 
research gap. The specific objectives are: 1) Develop a device 
with a Sony IMX477 camera to capture various fruit stages; 
2) Use image pre-processing techniques for precise training 
data, distinguishing ripe mulberries from leaves, branches, 
and the background; 3) Deploy YOLOv8 model to Jetson

II. METHODOLOGY 
A. Research Methodology 
This study employs an experimental methodology to 
develop a system and evaluate YOLOv8 to identify and 
quantify Morus Serrata fruit stages. The model is trained on 
novel image data captured by the researchers and assessed 
using mean average precision (mAP) and a confusion matrix 
to evaluate classification performance. The trained YOLOv8 
model is deployed on a Jetson Nano, where its effectiveness 
is further evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE) and the 
correlation coefficient (r) by comparing predicted and actual 
fruit counts. These metrics assess the model's accuracy in 
detecting and quantifying mulberry fruit stages under real-
world conditions. 
B. Conceptual Framework 
Fig. 2 presents the workflow for using image processing 
to identify and quantify various fruiting stages of Morus 
Serrata. The process begins with an input image of the tree, 
which undergoes pre-processing to enhance training and 
inference outcomes. Specifically, a 1920x1920 image 
captured with an IMX477 camera is resized to 3200x3200 
pixels and tiled into 640x640 segments, forming a 5x5 grid. 
Fig. 2. Conceptual Framework Diagram 
The YOLOv8 object detection model is then utilized to 
classify the fruits into four maturity stages: flowering, unripe, 
ripe, and overripe. The final output provides the identified 
fruits by maturity stage, with yield calculated for each class. 
This approach offers a comprehensive distribution of Morus 
Serrata fruit maturity, aiding in effective fruit quantification 
and monitoring. 
C. Hardware Setup 
Fig. 3 illustrates the system’s hardware architecture, with 
the Jetson Nano as the primary processing unit handling 
image acquisition and analysis. An IMX477 camera module 
captures high-resolution images, forming a dataset of 3,500 
images stored on a 1TB SD card alongside the system’s 
database. A touchscreen display provides classification and 
yield quantification. A key limitation of the system was the 
Jetson Nano's 5V 2A USB power supply, which was 
inadequate for high-resolution image processing [20].

The image is resized to 3200×3200 pixels to enhance the 
visibility of small fruits and improve annotation accuracy. 
This higher resolution facilitates a 5×5 tiling process, 
dividing each image into twenty-five 640×640 pixel tiles, 
which aligns with the standard input size required by the 
YOLOv8n model. This image tiling concludes the 
preparatory process, ensuring compliance with model 
specifications but also significantly improves small object 
detection by allowing finer spatial detail within each tile. This 
approach draws from prior work on dividing images for small 
object detection [21] and the efficacy of super-resolution in 
enhancing plant image detection [22]. The tiled images are 
then analyzed using the trained YOLOv8 model for yield 
quantification. Results, including detected fruit counts per 
stage, are displayed with an option to recapture, which 
redirects the user to the initial interface. 
E. Experimental Setup and Data Gathering Procedure 
Fig. 5. Experimental Setup of Jetson Nano with Morus Serrata Tree 
Fig. 5 shows the experimental setup using a Jetson Nano 
and a Morus Serrata tree for image-based analysis of fruiting 
stages. An opaque black curtain provided a uniform 
background, and a Sony IMX477 camera, housed in a 3D-
printed case and positioned at a maximum distance of 2.5 
meters, captured clear images of flowering, unripe, ripe, and 
overripe stages. The prototype system, powered by Jetson 
Nano, utilized the YOLOv8 model for image classification 
and yield analysis. Upon system initialization, images of the 
tree were captured and processed through a graphical user 
interface (GUI). The GUI offered options for users to analyze 
the captured image or retake it if necessary. The analyzed data 
included the classification of fruiting stages and the 
calculated yield ratio displayed within the interface. 
F. Model and Training Procedures 
The novel dataset used in this research was manually 
captured and consisted of four main classes: 'Flowering,' 
'Unripe,' 'Ripe,' and 'Overripe', having a total annotation of 
15,711. For the testing and validation phase, each stage had 
1178 samples, making a total of 4713 annotated by the 
researchers, while the training images consisted of 10998 
samples, 
adhering 
to 
a 
70% 
training 
and 
30% 
validation/testing ratio.  
Fig. 6 outlines the YOLOv8 training process. High-
resolution 1920x1920 images are captured using a Jetson 
Nano with an IMX477 camera (Step 1) and resized to 
3200x3200 for clarity (Step 2). These images are divided into 
25 smaller 640x640 tiles (Step 3), with non-annotated tiles 
removed (Step 4). The dataset is split into training, validation, 
and test sets using Roboflow and trained locally on an RTX 
3060 GPU for 300 epochs (Step 5). During early testing, a 
resolution higher than 1920×1920 was utilized; however, it 
compromised system stability. During intensive tasks, power 
spikes exceeded the supply limit, resulting in intermittent 
shutdowns. System stability improved when the capture 
parameters were reduced to a resolution of 1920×1920 pixels 
at 10 frames per second.

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
A total of 3,657 test annotations were conducted for 
training purposes, averaging 914 per category: flowering (F), 
unripe (U), ripe (R), and overripe (O). Of the 16,427 
annotations from the novel dataset captured using the Jetson 
Nano and IMX477 sensor, 12,710 were allocated for training. 
Testing and model validation were supported by Wilson’s 
Tree Bank, with all samples authenticated by a mulberry tree 
expert to ensure data reliability. The model, trained using 
Ultralytics YOLOv8 for 300 epochs, achieved an overall F1-
score of 72.2, with individual class scores of 71.3 (flowering), 
74.1 (unripe), 68.1 (ripe), and 76.3 (overripe). The confusion 
matrix indicated that the highest misclassifications occurred 
between the ripe and unripe stages. 
Table I presents the actual classification percentages for 
each test instance conducted throughout a 32-day testing 
period. During this time, the asynchronous ripening of 
Himalayan mulberries provided a natural progression to 
assess the model’s performance across all key fruiting stages. 
In the first week of testing, fruits were predominantly in the 
early

diversified, showing increases in unripe classifications and 
initial appearances of fully ripe fruits. The third week saw 
additional maturation, with a significant presence of ripe and 
overripe fruits, offering crucial data for validating later-stage 
detection. In the final week, the highest diversity of fruit 
maturity was observed, with all four stages—flowering, 
unripe, ripe, and overripe represented. The data collection 
ensured coverage of all the Morus Serrata fruit stages.

Shown in Table III is a comparative overview of recent 
deep learning-based systems for fruit detection and yield-
related assessment tasks. Similarly applied studies used 
various hardware platforms and models to address tasks 
ranging from fruit counting to growth stage characterization. 
Notably, the findings reported notable performance in terms 
of precision, recall, or accuracy; they often lack stage-specific 
yield quantification. In contrast, the proposed study leverages 
a custom-trained YOLOv8 model on Jetson Nano, achieving 
a high correlation coefficient (86.69%) between predicted and 
actual counts of Morus Serrata fruits across multiple 
developmental stages, potentially attributable to the ambient 
lighting condition and uniform background, along with 
enhanced pre-processing that included upscaling and slicing 
images to capture finer fruit details. 
IV. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
The yield quantification system, which integrates the 
Jetson Nano, IMX477 sensor, and YOLOv8, achieved an 
overall correlation coefficient of 86.69%, demonstrating 
strong predictive performance. The model performed best in 
detecting the flowering stage, with a correlation of 98.08% 
and a moderate mean absolute error (MAE) of 6.36. The 
overripe stage recorded the lowest MAE of 0.39, despite a 
lower correlation of 77.24%. In contrast, the unripe stage 
posed the greatest challenge, yielding the highest MAE of 
11.83 and a reduced correlation of 79.44%. Several factors 
influenced these results. Similar color characteristics across 
fruit stages, particularly between unripe and ripe fruits,
