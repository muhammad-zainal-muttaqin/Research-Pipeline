Abstract—Class imbalance is a major challenge in agricultural 
imaging, 
often 
leading 
classifiers 
to 
misclassify 
underrepresented but critical categories such as unripe or 
overripe fruit, potentially affecting yield estimation and harvest 
timing. Existing latent-space oversampling methods, such as 
Deep 
Synthetic 
Minority 
Oversampling 
Technique 
(DeepSMOTE), tend to degrade in performance when class 
embeddings overlap in the latent space, producing noisy and 
unreliable synthetic samples. We introduce SuperEncoder-
SMOTE (SE-SMOTE), a two-stage framework that first learns 
a highly separable, class-discriminative latent space via a 
supervised encoder, and then applies Synthetic Minority 
Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to generate high-quality 
minority samples. We validate our method on a novel long-tailed 
benchmark dataset for five-class oil palm fruit grading. At an 
extreme imbalance ratio of 100, SE-SMOTE achieves a macro-
averaged F1-score of 0.621, outperforming Expansive Over-
Sampling (EOS) (0.594) and DeepSMOTE (0.275) with relative 
improvements of 4.5% and 125.8%, respectively, thereby 
demonstrating 
superior 
robustness 
under 
severe 
class 
imbalance. These results highlight the importance of feature 
space engineering as a prerequisite for effective latent-space 
oversampling, offering a promising direction for tackling severe 
class imbalance in agricultural and other domain-specific 
imaging tasks. The SE-SMOTE code is publicly available at 
https://github.com/luckysantoso/se-smote.

I. INTRODUCTION  
High-quality data are the foundation of reliable machine 
learning models, yet agricultural image repositories are often 
high-dimensional, noisy, and strongly skewed toward 
majority classes. This problem is especially pronounced in the 
context of oil-palm fresh fruit bunch (FFB) grading. In 
practical harvesting operations, workers are primarily trained 
to collect bunches at the partially ripe stage to maximize oil 
yield. As a result, this class overwhelmingly dominates any 
collected dataset. In contrast, fully ripe bunches are usually 
the least represented, as they are deliberately left on the tree to 
create a time buffer and reduce the risk of overripening or 
decay if processing at the mill is delayed [1]. Although this 
harvesting practice is economically justified, it produces a 
severe class imbalance that causes convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs) to overpredict the majority class. This bias 
leads to misclassification of minority classes, suboptimal 
harvest scheduling, and ultimately financial losses for 
producers [2]. 
To address the challenge of learning from such skewed, 
high-dimensional data, one common strategy is to first 
transform the raw images into a more compact and 
discriminative 
feature 
representation. 
Classical 
dimensionality-reduction techniques such as principal 
component analysis (PCA) and linear discriminant analysis 
(LDA) mitigate the “curse of dimensionality”, but their linear 
projections cannot disentangle the complex color-texture cues 
[3][4] that separate immature from decayed fruit. While 
autoencoders (AEs) offer superior non-linear embeddings, a 
vanilla encoder tends to produce arbitrary latent codes that 
lack class separability [5]. Variational autoencoders (VAEs)  
attempt to regularize the latent space, but this constraint does 
not guarantee class separation and often results in overlapping 
latent clusters that blur inter-class margins, a problem that 
persists in imbalanced data contexts [6][7]. Thus, even 
advanced unsupervised methods for representation learning 
fail to produce a latent space that is inherently structured for 
clean classification or subsequent oversampling. 
Building on these latent representations, recent work has 
explored oversampling directly in the feature space. A 
prominent example is the Deep Synthetic Minority 
Oversampling 
Technique 
(DeepSMOTE) 
[8], 
which 
integrates an encoder and decoder architecture with the 
SMOTE methodology. However, a critical weakness persists: 
because the underlying latent space is not guaranteed to be 
separable; synthetic vectors generated between overlapping 
clusters can leak into neighboring classes and amplify label 
noise. In response, our work introduces a paradigm that 
prioritizes engineering the latent space itself before 
oversampling. At the core of our approach is the super-
encoder (SE) [9]. A specialized autoencoder architecture 
equipped with an important additional separator head. Unlike 
traditional autoencoders, it is trained with a cooperative loss 
function to explicitly learn a class-disentangled latent 
manifold. This process results in a structured latent space 
where different classes form distinct, well-separated clusters, 
directly mitigating the risk of generating overlapping samples.  
Based on this solid foundation, we propose SuperEncoder-
SMOTE (SE-SMOTE), a two-stage framework that combines 
a discriminative latent space structure with class-based 
oversampling. In this workflow, the pre-trained SE first 
transforms the imbalanced data into a class-disentangled 
latent space, after which SMOTE is safely applied by 
interpolating only between the nearest neighbors of the same 
class. The main contributions of this research include the 
introduction of this pipeline and the provision of the first long-
tail benchmark for five-class oil palm fruit maturity

II. RELATED WORK 
Research on oil palm fruit classification and methods for 
addressing dataset imbalance provides the foundation for this 
study. 
A. Vision-Based Oil Palm Fruit Classification 
Vision-based classification of fresh fruit bunch (FFB) 
maturity in oil palm has been extensively investigated, with 
many studies reporting high accuracy. Early research 
primarily relied on handcrafted color and texture descriptors 
combined with conventional classifiers such as Naïve Bayes, 
Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Artificial Neural 
Networks (ANN) [10]. More recent work has shifted toward 
deep 
learning 
approaches. 
For 
example, 
a 
custom 
convolutional neural network (CNN) has been deployed on 
mobile devices to enable on-site classification [11], DenseNet 
has been applied for improved feature extraction, and the 
YOLO architecture has been optimized for real-time fruit 
detection [12]. 
Despite these promising results, overall accuracy is a 
misleading metric for oil palm harvesting datasets that are 
naturally imbalanced [13][14]. With one maturity stage 
typically dominating, models tend to bias toward majority 
classes while neglecting rare but critical stages. This 
imbalance frames FFB grading as a unique challenge that 
requires balancing strategies beyond standard classification. It 
also necessitates evaluation metrics that better reflect 
performance across all classes, such as the macro-averaged F1 
score, along with dedicated strategies to mitigate skewed data 
distributions. 
B. Approaches to Handling Imbalanced Data 
Imbalanced learning strategies are generally categorized 
into three groups: data-level approaches, which manipulate 
the distribution of training samples; algorithm-level 
approaches, which modify the learning algorithm or loss 
function; and hybrid approaches, which combine both to 
leverage their complementary strengths. These strategies have 
been widely explored in computer vision tasks and 
increasingly applied to agricultural imagery, where minority 
classes are often underrepresented. 
At the data level, resampling seeks to rebalance class 
priors 
through 
undersampling 
or 
oversampling. 
Undersampling reduces bias but discards valuable data and 
weakens generalization, while oversampling generates new 
minority examples with SMOTE as a representative method 
that synthesizes minority samples by interpolating each 
instance with its k nearest minority neighbors to increase 
local minority density [15]. Classical pixel-based methods 
such as SMOTE [16] often yield unrealistic images and 
ignore spatial structure. To address this, oversampling has 
shifted into latent spaces, where embeddings preserve 
semantic context. VAEs synthesize samples from learned 
distributions [7], and GAN variants like GAMO [17] enhance 
minority representation through class conditioning or 
adversarial 
boundary 
refinement. 
More 
recently, 
DeepSMOTE integrates SMOTE interpolation into an 
encoder–decoder framework with a penalty-augmented loss, 
producing high-quality synthetic images without requiring a 
discriminator [8]. 
At the algorithm level, imbalance is addressed by 
modifying the training objective to emphasize minority 
classes during optimization. A widely used method is focal 
loss, which reshapes the standard cross entropy loss to down-
weight well-classified samples and concentrate training on 
hard, misclassified instances; this makes it particularly 
effective for object detection tasks where background pixels 
dominate foreground objects [18]. Another approach is the 
class balanced loss [19], which adjusts the loss contribution 
of each class according to the effective number of samples, 
thereby compensating for skewed distributions without 
discarding data. More recently, the label distribution aware 
margin (LDAM) loss introduces class-dependent margins, 
assigning larger margins to minority classes to encourage 
better separation from majority classes and reduce decision 
boundary bias [20]. Collectively, these methods demonstrate 
how tailoring the loss function can substantially improve 
classifier robustness under class imbalance, especially when 
resampling alone is insufficient. 
At the hybrid level, methods integrate resampling with 
tailored training objectives for stronger imbalance mitigation. 
Expansive Over-Sampling (EOS) exemplifies this by 
combining embedding-space augmentation with classifier 
fine-tuning, effectively narrowing the minority class 
generalization gap while remaining computationally efficient 
[21]. 
C. Supervised Latent Representation Learning 
Supervised latent representation learning seeks to 
overcome the limitations of unsupervised autoencoders, 
GAN-based generators, and linear projections, which often 
yield entangled or weakly discriminative embeddings that fail 
to capture subtle class boundaries in agricultural imagery. The 
super-encoder addresses this by coupling an encoder with a 
separator head that enforces label-based separation, while a 
decoder ensures reconstructability. A cooperative loss 
balances both objectives, producing compact, disentangled 
latent codes that are suitable for classification and sample 
generation [9]. In parallel, supervised contrastive learning 
extends contrastive learning into the fully supervised setting, 
pulling embeddings of the same class closer while pushing 
apart those of different classes, thereby improving inter-class 
margin separation and yielding stronger robustness compared 
to cross-entropy loss [22]. Together, these approaches 
demonstrate how supervision in the latent space enhances 
discriminability and reliability for downstream synthesis 
under imbalanced settings. 
III. METHODOLOGY 
The method we propose, SuperEncoder-SMOTE (SE-
SMOTE), is a hybrid framework designed to address class 
imbalance by first creating an optimally structured feature 
space before performing oversampling. This approach 
ensures that the generated synthetic samples have high 
validity and quality.

A. General Architecture 
The general architecture of SE-SMOTE, as illustrated in 
Fig. 1, consists of two main sequential stages followed by a 
final classification process. The first stage is Separable 
Feature Learning, where a super-encoder [9] model is trained 
to map input images into a latent space with clear class 
separability. The second stage is deep oversampling, where 
the trained Encoder from the first stage is used to transform 
the entire dataset, which is then balanced using the SMOTE 
algorithm. Finally, this balanced feature dataset is used to 
train a final classifier.

B. Architectural Components 
The SE-SMOTE framework is built on three main 
architectural components, each of which plays a crucial role 
in the workflow. At the core of our method is the super-
encoder [9], a generative model designed not only to 
reconstruct the input but also to organize its latent space. This 
component consists of three parts: the Encoder ( 𝐸), a 
convolutional network that compresses the input image 𝑥 
into a low-dimensional vector representation 𝑧 through a 
series of convolutional blocks; the Decoder ( 𝐷), a 
deconvolutional network that reconstructs the image 𝑥′ from 
the latent vector 𝑧; and the Separator (𝑆), a classification 
branch implemented as a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The 
Separator is connected to the latent vector 𝑧 and serves as an 
auxiliary task to enforce class separation during training. 
After the structured latent space is formed by the super-
encoder, the workflow continues with the SMOTE algorithm 
applied directly to the latent space. This mechanism works by 
selecting a latent vector from the minority class, 𝑧௜, and its 
nearest neighbor, 𝑧௝, to generate a new synthetic sample,

(1):

where 𝜆 is a random number in the range [0, 1]. This process 
is repeated until the class distribution becomes balanced. The 
final component in the pipeline is the classifier, a downstream 
classification model that is completely separate from the 
feature generation process. Its purpose is to take the clean and 
balanced feature dataset generated by SE-SMOTE and use it 
to perform the final prediction task. In this study, we used 
various traditional classification models, including support 
vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), Random 
Forest, XGBoost, and logistic regression. These models are 
chosen because they represent well-established, effective, 
and popular algorithms that have been extensively validated 
across diverse domains. By using them, we aim to 
demonstrate that the high-quality, class-separable features 
generated by SE-SMOTE can enhance performance even 
with relatively simple and traditional classifiers.

IV. EXPERIMENTS 
The experiments were conducted on a dataset of oil palm 
fruit images collected from a plantation in Kalimantan, 
Indonesia [14]. Each image was resized to 64×64 pixels and 
normalized to improve generalization. In addition, we applied 
a simple augmentation consisting of random horizontal 
flipping. The dataset comprises five distinct ripeness classes: 
Immature, Partially Ripe, Fully Ripe, Over Ripe, and 
Decayed, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

classes. 
To rigorously test robustness against imbalance, we 
organized the dataset into multiple scenarios with Imbalance 
Ratio (IR) of 10, 30, 50, 70, and 100, where IR denotes the 
ratio of samples in the majority to the minority class. The 
sample distribution is detailed in Table I. For all experiments, 
the data were split into 80% training and 20% testing using 
stratified partitioning, repeated under different random seeds 
for robustness.  
We compared SE-SMOTE against several baselines: 
training directly on the imbalanced data, SMOTE, VAE-
SMOTE, DeepSMOTE, and EOS. After feature generation 
and oversampling, the resulting balanced datasets were 
evaluated using five widely used classifiers: K-Nearest 
Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic 
Regression, XGBoost, and Random Forest. To ensure 
fairness, all classifiers were used with default parameters 
from the scikit-learn library.

The performance of all methods was evaluated using the 
macro-averaged F1-score. This metric was chosen for its 
superior ability to measure performance on imbalanced 
datasets. Unlike accuracy, which can provide a misleading 
picture by focusing only on the majority class, the F1-score 
calculates the harmonic mean of precision and recall. This 
provides a more balanced assessment of the model's ability to 
classify samples from both majority and minority classes. 
The use of macro average specifically ensures that each class 
is given equal weight in the final score calculation, allowing 
for accurate measurement of performance improvements in 
critical minority classes. 
The results in Table II show that SE-SMOTE consistently 
delivers substantial gains over traditional and generative 
baselines. DeepSMOTE, whose unsupervised encoder 
produces entangled latent spaces that limit oversampling 
effectiveness, achieves only 0.382 at IR = 10 and 0.275 at IR 
= 100. In contrast, SE-SMOTE more than doubles these 
scores, reaching 0.852 and 0.621, respectively. Against 
Expansive Oversampling (EOS), SE-SMOTE demonstrates 
competitive behavior: it slightly outperforms EOS at IR = 10, 
performs marginally worse at IR = 30–70, and regains 
superiority under the extreme imbalance of IR = 100 with a 
macro-averaged F1-score of 0.621 compared to 0.594. EOS 
remains a strong competitor because, like SE-SMOTE, it 
leverages supervised learning to enforce class separability. 
Overall, these results highlight a nuanced trade-off: EOS 
offers stability in mid-range imbalance, while SE-SMOTE 
provides broader robustness by combining supervised latent-
space structuring with oversampling, enabling it to surpass 
both unsupervised generative methods and EOS in the most 
challenging conditions.

V. CONCLUSION 
This study addressed the challenge of long-tailed class 
imbalance 
in 
agricultural 
image 
classification 
by 
introducing SE-SMOTE, a novel two-stage oversampling 
framework that first constructs a well-separated, class-
discriminative latent space via a super-encoder before 
synthesizing minority samples. Extensive experiments 
validated our hypothesis that feature-space engineering is 
critical for producing high-quality synthetic instances, with 
SE-SMOTE consistently outperforming baseline methods 
across varying imbalance factors and demonstrating 
notable robustness under extreme data skew. Quantitative 
gains were reinforced by qualitative analyses, which 
confirmed the super-encoder’s ability to yield structured 
and separable latent representations. This work advances 
latent-space oversampling by establishing feature-space 
engineering as a foundational step for effective sample 
generation. While results on the oil palm fruit grading task, 
a domain heavily dependent on subtle color and texture 
cues, are promising, validation is currently limited to a 
single agricultural imaging dataset. Future research should 
examine the generalizability of SE-SMOTE to diverse 
modalities such as medical imaging and structured tabular 
data, integrate advanced SMOTE variants, explore 
adaptive 
latent-space 
regularization, 
and 
assess 
computational trade-offs to optimize deployment in large-
scale, real-world scenarios.
