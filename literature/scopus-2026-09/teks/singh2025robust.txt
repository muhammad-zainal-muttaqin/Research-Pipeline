Abstract
Background  To address the challenge of real-time plant monitoring in greenhouse environments, this industry-
driven research focuses on developing an autonomous quadrotor UAV system specifically designed for monitoring 
strawberry plants. Traditional methods for greenhouse monitoring are labor-intensive and lack scalability, particularly 
in precision agriculture applications.
Method  This research introduces a mature strawberry detection model specifically designed for greenhouse 
environments. The proposed YOLOv9-GLEAN approach enables the identification of small mature strawberries 
through an onboard camera mounted on the quadrotor. Additionally, a hybrid trajectory tracking controller for 
the quadrotor is developed and tested in both simulated and real-world conditions. The UAV navigates through 
the greenhouse using predetermined waypoints, operating as a semi-autonomous system for navigation while 
maintaining full autonomy in mature strawberry detection tasks. The system incorporates an integrated onboard 
vision platform that utilizes an innovative YOLOv9-GLEAN-based algorithm to perform real-time and offline detection 
and counting of mature strawberries.
Results  The YOLOv9-GLEAN model achieves high detection accuracy, as confirmed by evaluation metrics such as 
precision, recall, and F1-score. The proposed hybrid (PID+LQR) controller demonstrates superior tracking performance 
compared to other conventional controllers. The integrated control and perception system proves effective in both 
simulated and real-world greenhouse environments.
Discussion  The research validates the efficacy of deep learning models, with YOLOv9-GLEAN showing exceptional 
performance in enabling rapid, precise, and automated detection of ripe strawberries through quadrotor deployment 
in greenhouse environments. Such agricultural monitoring technologies represent a substantial advancement 
beyond conventional manual inspection approaches, empowering farmers and greenhouse operators to execute 
well-informed, time-sensitive management decisions that minimize crop losses and optimize production yields. This 
investigation underscores the revolutionary impact that deep learning technologies can have within greenhouse 
agriculture.

Introduction
Greenhouse cultivation has become a crucial solution for 
tackling the challenges of modern agriculture, including 
limited cultivated land, unpredictable climatic condi­
tions, and the increasing demand for high-quality crop 
production. Monitoring in greenhouses is essential for 
multiple reasons. It facilitates the early detection of stress 
caused by diseases, pests, or unfavorable environmen­
tal conditions, enabling prompt interventions to reduce 
yield losses. Additionally, it aids in identifying the opti­
mal time for harvesting fruits, ensuring better quality and 
productivity [56, 61].
The accelerated advancement of artificial intelligence 
in recent years has driven significant progress in deep 
learning technologies. Deep learning enables machines to 
emulate human cognitive processes including vision, and 
classification to address sophisticated pattern recognition 
problems, leading to successful applications in rapid agri­
cultural crop and fruit identification.
Traditional computer vision approaches for fruit detec­
tion in agricultural settings face several significant limi­
tations. Classical methods such as template matching, 
feature-based detection (SIFT, SURF), and color-based 
segmentation techniques struggle with the inherent vari­
ability present in natural environments [1]. These conven­
tional approaches are particularly challenged by varying 
lighting conditions, occlusion from leaves and branches, 
diverse fruit orientations, and the complex background 
textures commonly found in greenhouse settings [52]. 
Furthermore, traditional methods often require exten­
sive manual feature engineering and threshold tuning 
for different environmental conditions, making them 
impractical for real-world deployment where adaptabil­
ity and robustness are essential [30]. Additionally, these 
approaches typically exhibit slow processing speeds when 
dealing with high-resolution imagery, making real-time 
applications computationally prohibitive [1].
Given these limitations, deep learning-based object 
detection frameworks, particularly the You Only Look 
Once (YOLO) architecture, present compelling advan­
tages for agricultural monitoring applications [45]. 
YOLO was specifically selected for this study due to its 
unique single-stage detection approach that simultane­
ously predicts bounding boxes and class probabilities, 
enabling real-time processing capabilities essential for 
UAV-based monitoring [62]. Unlike two-stage detectors 
such as R-CNN and Fast R-CNN that require separate 
region proposal and classification steps, YOLO’s unified 
architecture provides the optimal balance between detec­
tion accuracy and computational efficiency required for 
deployment on resource-constrained UAV platforms. 
Moreover, YOLO’s end-to-end learning capability elimi­
nates the need for manual feature engineering while 
demonstrating superior generalization across varying 
environmental conditions commonly encountered in 
greenhouse agriculture.

Drone-based solutions for crop monitoring are widely 
popular and highly reliable in the agricultural sector, due 
to their advanced computing capabilities and onboard 
sensors [26, 47]. In recent years, unmanned aerial vehi­
cles (UAVs) have been extensively utilized in the agricul­
tural sector [16]. The multi-spectral camera is mounted 
on the hexacopter to monitor the plant growth through 
ortho-images of the plants [7]. UAV based citrus trees 
disease detection (Huanglongbing) was proposed in [19]. 
The drones are used to detect the weeds in the open field 
crops using deep learning approach [46]. UAVs are uti­
lized for monitoring and managing diseases in strawberry 
crops [13, 33, 49, 77].
This study focuses on the design and simulation of a 
flight controller for UAVs, specifically tailored for plant 
monitoring within greenhouse environment. The Flight 
Control System (FCS) serves as the brain of a quadrotor 
UAV, with the responsibility of maintaining the desired 
attitude and position during flight. Factors such as wind 
disturbances, coupled nonlinear dynamics, and model­
ing uncertainties pose significant challenges for control 
researchers in developing effective flight control sys­
tems [38]. Adaptive control is well-suited for addressing 
uncertainties arising from the operating environment 
[44]. Model reference adaptive control for quadrotor to 
tackle uncertainties and external disturbance is proposed 
in [34]. The Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) con­
troller is the most commonly used control method in 
UAVs for real-time applications. It is a linear controller 
known for its simplicity and ease of tuning [42]. Romero 
et al. linearized the quadrotor model and proposed a PID 
controller for stabilization [50]. The authors showed that

Currently, no research has specifically explored the use 
of UAVs for strawberry (Fragaria spp.) plant monitoring 
in greenhouse environments for simulation and real-
world scenario. This paper aims to address this gap by 
proposing both a simulation and real time based inves­
tigation of a quadrotor flight controller designed for pre­
cise navigation within greenhouse settings and detecting 
mature strawberries. The plant monitoring tasks include 
fruit maturity classification, disease detection, yield esti­
mation, and growth assessment. The primary focus of 
this research is to develop a deep learning-based model 
for detecting mature strawberries, along with a hybrid 
flight controller for a quadrotor within greenhouse. The 
system is designed to effectively detect and monitor 
strawberry plants while accurately following a predefined 
trajectory within a greenhouse environment. The novelty 
of the work is that the quadrotor follows the predefined 
waypoints for monitoring the strawberry plants when 
GPS is not working in the greenhouse due to Polyethyl­
ene (PE) Film roof sheets. Hence, the aim of this study 
is to design and develop a quadrotor hybrid controller 
with capability to detect mature strawberries for plant 
monitoring in the greenhouse settings with the following 
major contributions:

Proposed a novel deep learning based YOLOv9-
GLEAN detection model tailored for strawberry 
detection from quadrotor-captured videos in both

art comparison.

Related work
Recent research in deep learning has focused on devel­
oping more powerful system architectures and learning 
methods, such as CNNs, Transformers, Perceivers, and 
Mambas [67, 68, 78]. The You Only Look Once (YOLO) 
series [5, 20, 63] has emerged as a leading approach in 
real-time object detection. These models commonly rely 
on core computational units such as Cross Stage Par­
tial Network (CSPNet) [64], Efficient Layer Aggregation 
Network (ELAN) [65], or their respective variants. A 
comprehensive methodology for labeling, training, and 
detecting tomato flowers tailored for robotic pollina­
tion was proposed by Singh et al [60]. Transfer learning 
techniques were employed using YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 
models for the detection of tomato flowers in the context 
of pollination. A multi-level deep information feature 
fusion extraction network (DFN-PSAN) was proposed by 
Dai et al. [15] for accurate plant disease classification in 
natural field environments. The DFN-PSAN model was 
built upon the YOLOv5 Backbone and Neck network 
as its base structure (DFN), and a pyramidal squeezed 
attention (PSA) mechanism was integrated to enhance 
classification performance. An improved YOLOv5-based 
model was proposed for detecting and grading sprouted 
potatoes in complex scenarios [14]. To enhance feature 
representation and mitigate the issue of feature simi­
larity loss during the fusion process, the Conv layers in 
the C3 module were replaced with CrossConv. Recently, 
real-time detectors such as RT-DETR [74], based on the 
DETR framework [10], have been introduced. However, 
adaptation of the DETR series to new domains has been 
found to be challenging without the use of domain-spe­
cific pre-trained models. As a result, the YOLO series has

been widely adopted as the most commonly used real-
time object detector.
CNN-based models
CNN-based object detection frameworks have been 
widely utilized in precision agriculture for identifying 
and monitoring fruit crops such as strawberries. Early 
studies applied standard Convolutional Neural Networks 
(CNNs) like VGGNet and AlexNet for fruit classification 
tasks, demonstrating the effectiveness of deep feature 
extraction in distinguishing strawberries from back­
ground clutter [58, 73]. To achieve better localization, 
Region-based CNN (R-CNN) and its successors—Fast 
R-CNN and Faster R-CNN—were explored for detect­
ing strawberries in both open-field and greenhouse set­
tings. These two-stage detectors offered high detection 
accuracy by combining region proposal mechanisms with 
CNN feature extraction, although their inference speed 
was limited for real-time deployment [21, 48].
Further improvements were made through the inte­
gration of Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD), which 
simplified the detection pipeline by eliminating the need 
for region proposals, enabling faster processing while 
maintaining reasonable accuracy in moderately cluttered 
scenes [32]. Studies applying SSD for strawberry detec­
tion have shown that it performs well under conditions 
with moderate occlusion and varying illumination, often 
encountered in greenhouse environments. Moreover, 
hybrid approaches integrating pre-trained CNN back­
bones (e.g., VGG16 or ResNet) with custom detection 
heads have also been explored to enhance feature gen­
eralization across diverse growing conditions [52, 72]. 
Overall, CNN-based detectors, especially those using 
R-CNN and SSD architectures, remain a strong founda­
tion for automated strawberry plant monitoring, particu­
larly in controlled environments where real-time speed is 
not a strict constraint.
YOLO and transformer-based models
Accurate and real-time detection of strawberries in com­
plex agricultural environments has been widely addressed 
using deep learning, particularly through CNN-based 
architectures like the YOLO series and more recently, 
transformer-based models. The YOLO (You Only Look 
Once) family of models has been extensively applied due 
to its speed and efficiency. Early versions like YOLOv3 
and YOLOv4 were successfully used to detect ripe straw­
berries in field and greenhouse conditions, demonstrat­
ing robustness against partial occlusion and variable 
lighting [57]. Subsequent models such as YOLOv5 and 
YOLOv8 introduced architectural enhancements includ­
ing CSPNet and PANet, leading to improved detection 
accuracy and reduced inference time. These models have 
been deployed on robotic platforms for tasks such as fruit 
counting, flower detection, and autonomous harvesting 
[2, 3, 8, 60].
In parallel, transformer-based object detectors have 
emerged as a powerful alternative, offering superior capa­
bility in modeling global relationships across an image. 
DETR (DEtection TRansformer) introduced a fully atten­
tion-based detection framework that eliminates the need 
for region proposals or anchor boxes [10]. However, due 
to limitations in convergence speed, improved variants 
such as Deformable DETR and Conditional DETR have 
been adopted in agricultural settings. For instance, Zheng 
et al. [76] used a Swin Transformer backbone for multi-
scale strawberry detection in greenhouses, outperform­
ing traditional CNNs in densely packed scenes. Similarly, 
Lu et al. [31] integrated Vision Transformers (ViT) into 
a multimodal framework that combined RGB and depth 
data for precise fruit localization.
Overall, YOLO-based detectors remain the preferred 
choice for real-time applications due to their lightweight 
architecture, while transformer-based models offer 
improved accuracy in complex scenarios and are gaining 
traction in precision agriculture.
This study introduces an efficient deep learning-based 
framework for real-time strawberry identification, lever­
aging recent advancements in computer vision and 
smart agriculture. The proposed system, named Mature 
Strawberry Detection (MSD), incorporates the YOLOv9-
GLEAN model to achieve high-speed and accurate object 
detection. The trained MSD model was deployed on a 
quadrotor equipped with a camera, aiming to bridge the 
gap between controlled laboratory research and practical 
field applications. This configuration enables automated, 
contactless, and real-time monitoring of strawberry 
plants within a greenhouse environment. The use of a 
quadrotor enhances flexibility and precision in scanning 
mature strawberry plants, facilitating accurate detec­
tion and timely harvesting. By combining deep learning 
with robotic automation, the MSD framework provides a 
scalable and effective solution for modern greenhouses, 
reducing reliance on manual labor, improving crop moni­
toring, and promoting sustainable farming practices.

Dataset
A quadrotor was utilized to capture crop data in video 
format during flying over the crop, and the detection 
model was later applied in offline mode to analyze the 
number of ripe strawberries. In this study, we utilized 
a dataset comprising 250 images from a CAD-based 
synthetic dataset and 800 images for real strawberries 
in greenhouse. The variation in the dataset enhance the 
model performance validity. So, total 1050 images were 
used in dataset. The dataset consisted of one class: ripe 
strawberries (0). The domain randomization technique 
is applied on the images by variations in brightness, dif­
ferent backgrounds, and shaded areas. Table 1 provides 
comprehensive details about the images contained within 
the dataset. To enhance training data diversity, various 
augmentation methods were implemented, including 
rotation, flipping, and blurring transformations. These 
techniques contribute to strengthening the model’s gen­
eralization capabilities across diverse operational condi­
tions. The dataset was divided using a standard 70:20:10 
split ratio, allocating 735 images for training, 210 images 
for validation, and 105 images for testing. This distribu­
tion ensures sufficient training data while maintaining 
adequate validation and test sets for reliable performance 
evaluation.

The research proposes a new architectural concept 
known as Generalized ELAN (GELAN), representing an 
advanced iteration of the original ELAN framework [25]. 
By integrating CSPNet and ELAN concepts, GELAN 
enables strategic gradient pathway management. The 
architecture’s development focuses on achieving mini­
mal computational overhead, accelerated inference per­
formance, and enhanced accuracy levels. The overall 
architecture of GELAN is depicted in Fig.  5. To assess 
GELAN’s effectiveness, we performed experiments using 
a mixed dataset containing both synthetic CAD-gen­

experiments took place in a controlled gazebo green­

that our proposed YOLOv9 model outperformed all 
other compared models across every evaluation metric

YOLOv9-GELAN architecture is structured into three 
primary components: the backbone, neck, and head. The 
model accepts input images of size 640 × 640 × 3, repre­
senting RGB color channels. Initially, two convolutional 
blocks with a kernel size of 3 and a stride of 2 are applied 
to reduce the spatial resolution. The number of channels 
in these convolution blocks varies based on the configu­
ration of the YOLOv9 architecture.
The output from the initial convolution layers is passed 
to the RepNCSPELAN block, which defines the number 
of replications and bottleneck blocks used. Addition­
ally, an ADown block is employed for downsampling, 
further reducing the feature map resolution. At the end 
of the backbone, the RepNCSPELAN block connects to 
the SPPELAN (Spatial Pyramid Pulling Efficient Layer 
Aggregation Networks) block. In the neck section, the 
SPPELAN output is upsampled to align with the fea­
ture map resolution of the RepNCSPELAN block using 
an Upsample block. A Concat block then merges the 
features from both the Upsample and RepNCSPELAN 
blocks by summing the channel values while preserving 
spatial dimensions. This alignment ensures that the fea­
ture resolutions from both the backbone and neck com­
ponents are consistent. Subsequently, the feature map is 
forwarded to the head of the architecture, which consists 
of three detect blocks.
Each block is designed to handle objects of different 
sizes: small, medium, and large. These blocks operate 
on feature maps with a resolution of 20 × 20 × 512. The 
training is done for 300 epochs with batch size 16 and 
image size 640.

PID controller Designing a PID controller for a 
quadrotor requires managing its motion across 
multiple axes, including roll, pitch, yaw, and altitude. 
A quadrotor has six degrees of freedom (DOF): 
three translational (x, y, z) and three rotational 
(roll, pitch, yaw). The PID controller plays a crucial 
role in stabilizing and regulating these movements. 
The PID controller is a classic model in control 
theory, designed to minimize errors by adjusting the 
proportional, integral, and derivative coefficients in 
the quadrotor control equation. Figure 12 depicts the 
closed loop PID controller for quadrotor system. The 
proportional term adjusts the error by multiplying it 
with a proportionality constant, either increasing or 
decreasing the response. The derivative term predicts 
the controller’s future behavior by analyzing the 
rate of change of error over time. The integral term 
accumulates all past error values, with the integral 
coefficient determining its influence to achieve the 
desired output in the shortest time. Therefore, tuning 
coefficients proportional, integral, and derivative 
is essential to reach the control system’s set-point 
effectively. Error (e) is defined as the difference 
between the reference input (R) value and the output 
of the system (Y) at any given moment. The output 
of the controller (U) is fed to the quadrotor system

LQR controller LQR (Linear Quadratic Regulator) 
is an optimal linear control algorithm designed to 
operate within a dynamic system by minimizing a 
predefined cost function. It is typically employed in 
scenarios where the system’s parameters are fully 
known, and external disturbances, such as wind, 
that could impact the system are negligible. In our 
research, the greenhouse environment parameters 
are well-defined, and there are no external 
disturbances, such as wind. Therefore, utilizing an 
LQR controller for quadrotor applications within 
the greenhouse environment is a suitable choice. 
Compared to a classical PID controller, the LQR 
controller is more computationally complex due 
to the need to calculate the full state feedback 
matrix K. This involves solving the Riccati equation, 
which requires more computational effort than the 
straightforward tuning of proportional, integral, 
and derivative gains in a PID controller. The LQR 
controller employs an optimal control algorithm 
designed to minimize a cost function derived 
from the system’s equations.This cost function 
incorporates both the state variables and the input 
parameters, weighted by the Q and R matrices, 
respectively, to achieve the desired balance between 
state regulation and control effort. The state vector x

vector u is u = (T1, T2, T3, T4)T. The two primary 
quantities to optimize in the quadrotor model are 
target reaching accuracy and response speed. To

The system performance of PID, LQR and PID + LQR 
(proposed) controllers are evaluated and compared using 
a step response analysis and mentioned in Fig. 16. Table 4 
shows the controller performance comparison. The PID 
controller exhibited the highest overshoot (30.77%) 
and the slowest rise time (0.79 s) and settling time (0.77 
s). Although it achieved a near-zero steady-state error 
(0.0001), its response was less stable with notable oscil­
lations. The LQR controller performed better with lower 
overshoot (23.44 %), faster rise time (0.53 s), and quicker 
settling time (0.51 s), achieving zero steady-state error. 
However, some oscillations were still present. The pro­
posed PID + LQR controller outperformed both, with 
the lowest overshoot (10.85%), good rise time (0.63 s), 
and stable settling time (0.61 s). It also maintained zero 
steady-state error, indicating a fast, smooth, and accu­
rate response. The proposed controller provides the most 
balanced and stable control performance, effectively 
reducing overshoot while maintaining fast response and 
accuracy—making it the most suitable choice for robust 
quadrotor control.
While the proposed hybrid PID-LQR controller dem­
onstrates superior trajectory tracking performance, sev­
eral limitations constrain its practical implementation. 
Dual-controller architecture increases computational 
complexity. The system exhibits significant sensitivity 
to model uncertainties, including center of gravity shifts 
from mounted equipment and motor parameter varia­
tions due to battery discharge or temperature changes. 
Additionally, in future the tuning of controllers against 
high-frequency disturbances common in greenhouse 
environments, such as sudden ventilation-induced air 
currents exceeding 2 m/s will be considered.

Simulation framework
The integration of quadrotor UAVs (Unmanned Aerial 
Vehicles) in greenhouse environments has revolutionized 
plant monitoring by providing autonomous, high-preci­
sion data collection with minimal human intervention. 
Traditional ground-based monitoring systems face chal­
lenges in maneuverability, scalability, and efficiency, 
making aerial solutions a compelling alternative. How­
ever, designing an effective control system for UAVs in 
a constrained greenhouse environment requires robust 
simulation frameworks to validate path planning, sensor 
integration, and plant inspection strategies before real-
world deployment.
A simulation framework serves as a cost-effective and 
risk-free platform to test and optimize UAV control algo­
rithms, enabling researchers to refine trajectory planning, 
obstacle avoidance, and sensor fusion techniques for real-
time plant health assessment. This study presents a com­
prehensive simulation framework for quadrotor control, 
incorporating realistic environmental modeling, physics-
based flight dynamics, and computer vision based plant 
monitoring techniques. By leveraging simulation, the 
proposed system facilitates precise way-point naviga­
tion, robust control, and imaging for crop analysis. The 
findings from this simulation framework will enhance 
the development of real-world quadrotor applications, 
contributing to precision agriculture and autonomous 
greenhouse management. In this research, the simulation 
environment is developed using ROS-I (Noetic) version 
on Ubuntu 20.04, alongside MATLAB 2024. The simula­
tion is done on Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-14900KF 3.20 GHz 
processor desktop computer.

trajectory tracking in Fig. 20a demonstrates that the con­

model response by stabilizing the quadrotor as it flies 
over the strawberry crop in the greenhouse environment.
The proposed deep learning based detection model 
and quadrotor control algorithm are validated in a 
Gazebo-based simulated greenhouse environment and 
actual greenhouse environment. The quadrotor navi­
gates through the strawberry greenhouse, following a 
predefined way-point trajectory to ensure accurate and 
controlled movement. Figures 21 and 22 shows the sim­

monitoring application.

Discussion
The achieved detection performance metrics of the inte­
grated MSD-quadrotor system demonstrate significant 
potential for transforming greenhouse agricultural oper­
ations, with the YOLOv9-GELAN model’s exceptional 
precision (1.00 at confidence 0.943) translating directly 
into labor reduction opportunities in commercial green­
house settings. Traditional manual strawberry monitor­
ing requires skilled workers to inspect approximately 
200-300 plants per hour, whereas the proposed system 
can systematically scan equivalent areas in 15-20 minutes 
with consistent accuracy, representing a 75-80% reduc­
tion in monitoring time and associated labor costs. The 
model’s ability to distinguish between ripe and unripe 
strawberries with 99.4% mAP@0.5 enables precise har­
vest timing decisions critical for maximizing fruit quality 
and market value.
The hybrid PID-LQR controller’s trajectory tracking 
accuracy (±5 cm positioning precision) enables seam­
less integration with existing greenhouse automation

While the system demonstrates excellent performance 
in controlled greenhouse environments, practical deploy­
ment considerations include the hybrid controller’s com­
plexity requiring specialized maintenance knowledge,

overhead limiting continuous monitoring capabilities,

The future research roadmap’s focus on IoT platform 
integration addresses critical needs for comprehensive 
farm management systems, where integration with exist­
ing greenhouse sensors will enable correlation analysis 
between environmental conditions and fruit develop­
ment patterns, supporting predictive modeling for yield 
forecasting and quality optimization. The planned model 
optimization through parameter reduction techniques 
will facilitate deployment on edge devices, reducing 
dependence on high-performance computing hardware 
and enabling cost-effective scaling for smaller agricul­
tural operations, positioning the system as a foundational 
component for next-generation precision agriculture 
implementations in controlled environment agriculture.
Conclusion
This research introduces an advanced deep learning 
framework for identifying ripe strawberries in greenhouse 
settings through quadrotor-based systems. The study 
implements a YOLOv9-GELAN algorithm on the aerial 
platform, demonstrating superior performance com­
pared to existing methodologies with achieved metrics of

at 23 fps. A comprehensive dataset incorporating both 
synthetic and real strawberry imagery was developed to 
train the deep learning architecture. Model validation 
was conducted across simulated and actual greenhouse 
environments to ensure robust performance. Addition­
ally, a hybrid trajectory control system combining PID
