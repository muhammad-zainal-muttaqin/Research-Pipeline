Evaluating Orchard Fruit Quality from Monocular
and Stereo RGB-D Imagery: A Comparative Study
Mirko Piani∗, Alessio Scalisi †, Gianmarco Bortolotti∗, Dario Mengoli‡ Luigi Manfrini∗,
{mirko.piani2, gianmarco.bortolotti, dario.mengoli2, luigi.manfrini}@unibo.it
{alessio.scalisi}@agriculture.vic.gov.au
∗DISTAL department - University of Bologna - Bologna, Italy
†Tatura SmartFarm - Agriculture Victoria - Tatura, Australia
‡DEI department G. Marconi - University of Bologna - Bologna, Italy
Abstract—Accurate fruit sizing and colour estimation are crit-
ical tasks for yield prediction, quality assessment, and precision
orchard management. This study evaluates and compares the
performance of a stereo camera system and a monocular camera
paired with monocular depth estimation (Depth Anything v2)
in capturing key fruit traits in an apple orchard. The stereo
camera was mounted on the casing of a static monocular system,
and both cameras were time-synchronised via a server–client
network using an external laptop and Network Time Protocol.
Each camera system was tested for crop load estimation, fruit
size distribution, and colour distribution at the experimental
plot level. Results showed that the stereo camera provided
more accurate estimations of fruit count (85% detection rate)
and size (mean bias error 0.59 mm), benefiting from enhanced
depth perception and a narrower Field of View. Conversely, the
monocular camera demonstrated strong potential for external
fruit quality monitoring (mean bias error 0.12) at high temporal
frequency throughout the growing season. This work supports the
integration of low-cost monocular systems for daily fruit moni-
toring, alongside stereo systems for detailed seasonal assessments.
Index Terms—computer vision, crop load, precision orchard
management, color development index
I. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM DEFINITION
Accurate and high-resolution assessments of fruit quality
parameters—such as size, colour, and surface uniformity—are
a fundamental requirement in modern orchard management
[1], [2]. These parameters not only influence market value
and harvest planning, but are also indicative of plant health,
phenological development, and stress conditions [3], [4].
Traditional methods of fruit quality evaluation often involve
manual sampling, which is labour-intensive, time-consuming,
and susceptible to inconsistency due to human error and
limited sampling spatial coverage.
Recent advances in agricultural robotics and computer vi-
sion have opened the possibility of automating fruit quality
monitoring by using image-based systems, often integrated
into mobile or fixed sensing platforms [5]–[8]. A central
aspect of such systems is the ability to acquire reliable depth
information, which is crucial for estimating fruit size in three
dimensions, separating overlapping objects, and assessing oc-
clusion conditions within the canopy.
Stereoscopic camera systems (also referred to as stereo
cameras), which derive depth maps through triangulation of
image pairs from two calibrated and synchronised lenses, have
shown promising results in accurately capturing the spatial
structure of orchard environments. When mounted on scouting
vehicles or robotic platforms, stereo systems are capable of
acquiring dense RGB-D (colour and depth) data across large
areas of the field, enabling high-fidelity 3D reconstruction
of fruit [9], [10]. This facilitates the measurement of fruit
volume, surface features, and relative positioning with respect
to surrounding foliage and trunk [10]. However, such systems
come with notable limitations: the hardware is more expensive,
installation and calibration are more complex, and the data
acquisition is often restricted by the path, schedule, speed of
the mobile platform, and memory requirements. Furthermore,
the frequency of data collection is constrained by vehicle
availability, which may not permit daily monitoring at high
spatial resolution.
On the other hand, monocular RGB cameras offer a cost-
effective and lightweight alternative that can be deployed at
fixed locations throughout the orchard, including on poles,
trellises, or weather stations. While monocular systems in-
herently lack true depth perception, recent progress in deep
learning–based monocular depth estimation, such as with the
Depth Anything V2 model [11], has enabled the approximation
of depth maps using only single RGB frames [12]. These
models can exploit prior knowledge from training data and
fixed installation geometry to infer relative distances with
increasing accuracy. The static nature of these installations
offers significant advantages in terms of operational simplicity,
energy autonomy (e.g., via solar panels), and high-frequency
data acquisition, including continuous or daily image capture
throughout the growing season. This temporal richness is
particularly valuable for detecting gradual changes in fruit
development and identifying spatial patterns of ripening or
stress over time.
Nonetheless, monocular depth estimation is inherently
less precise than stereo triangulation, particularly in scenes
with complex occlusions, low texture, or variable light-
ing—conditions frequently encountered in orchard environ-
ments.

II. MATERIALS AND METHODS
A. Experimental plot
The experiment was carried out in an E-W-oriented ’AN-
ABP 01’ apple orchard located at the Tatura SmartFarm,
Agriculture Victoria, Australia (36°26’23.3”S, 145°15’54.5”E,
113 a.s.l.). ’ANABP 01’ was developed in Australia and is
marketed as BravoTM within Australia and as SolunaTM in
overseas markets.
B. Sensor installation
A solar powered static computer vision node (Fig. 1 A-B)
was built specifically for the trial, in order to have a monocular
camera (Raspberry Pi camera module 3 wide, Raspberry Pi,
United Kingdom) installed in field. Above that camera, 0.15 m,
a stereo camera (Intel RealSense D435i, Intel, United States)
was installed (Fig. 1 A). The installation ensured both RGB
cameras having their centers aligned.
C. Network architecture
The sensing network was designed to connect a laptop (MSI
Katana GF66) both with the Intel RealSense D435i stereo
camera (1920 x 1080, 2 MP, HFOV: 69°, VFOV: 42°) and
the Raspberry Pi controlling the monocular camera module 3
wide (4608 x 2592, 12 MP, HFOV: 102°, VFOV: 67°) (Fig. 1
C). In order to have time synchronisation between the sensors,
a server-client (i.e., laptop-RPi) architecture was designed with
a Network Time Protocol over LAN cable. A Software was
written to take synchronised frames from both cameras and
save them locally.
D. Field setup
Four apple trees trained as spindle, with 4.5 × 2 m row
and tree spacing, were selected for the test. Considering the
difference in the Field-of-View of the cameras, a selective
manual thinning was done in order to remove fruit that were
non visible in both camera frames (Fig. 2). This was done to
ensure that the same range of fruit was visible for both camera
inferences. Image acquisition took place on 10 April 2025 at
12:00 a.m. The sensor network was positioned 2.5 m from
the canopies at a height of 1.2 m, and a total of 17 RGB-D
frames were captured with the stereo camera, along with 17
RGB frames with the monocular camera.
E. Fruit detection
To consider the impact of the performance of a detector on
both estimations of fruit count and size [13], six YOLO models
released by ultralytics (www.ultralytics.com) were selected for
their lightweight application (Table I). Then, for each camera
frame that was collected, apple detection was performed by
setting the confidence threshold at 0.1 and the intersection
over union threshold at 0.5.

H. Data analysis
The data analysis performed for each camera consisted in
determining the crop load to highlight the effect of the camera
Field-of-View. Then a comparison of fruit size distribution and
colour distribution was performed at plot level to highlight
the influence of depth inference on the sizing process, and
the effect of image resolution on the colour estimation. The
analysis was performed in Python 3.13.2, using the OpenCV
library for image manipulation, NumPy for numerical com-
puting, Matplotlib for plotting, and Pandas for data frame
analysis.

B. Fruit sizing performance
Monocular depth inference adversely affected the sizing
pipeline, resulting in a failure to size 64.1% of the detected
fruit in frames captured by the Raspberry Pi. In contrast, the
Intel RealSense D435i experienced this issue in only 9.2%
of detections. However, circle-fitting errors were observed
in the colour frames produced by the latter camera, leading
to an additional 21.2% of detected bounding boxes lacking
size information. A more in-depth analysis revealed that
approximately 50% of these unsized bounding boxes were,
in fact, false positives corresponding to leaves. Conversely,
the Raspberry Pi exhibited a much lower rate of circle-fitting
failure, affecting only 0.2% of the detections. This difference
may be attributed to the wider Field-Of-View of the Raspberry
Pi camera.

reference one (Table III) in the case of data produced from the
stereo camera, with the presence of outliers mainly due to false

IV. DISCUSSION
A. Crop load estimation
The crop load analysis highlighted significant differences in
spatial coverage between the stereo (ground sampling distance
– GSD: 0.74 mm · pixel−1) and monocular (GSD: 0.46
mm·pixel−1) systems. The stereo camera, typically mounted
on a scouting vehicle, provided a narrower and more stable
Field-of-View. In contrast, the monocular camera, due to its
broader Field-of-View, captured fewer fruit per frame, leading
to an underestimation of the actual crop load in some plots.
However, the higher temporal resolution afforded by static
monocular installations could partially compensate for this
limitation through more frequent observations.
B. Fruit sizing
The fruit size distribution analysis at plot level revealed
the critical impact of depth estimation accuracy. The stereo
camera, offering true depth measurements through disparity
calculations, achieved consistent and reliable sizing outputs
in range with the reference data. Conversely, the monocular
setup relied on inferred depth maps, which introduced scale
ambiguity, especially under challenging lighting or canopy
occlusion conditions. This often resulted in higher variance
in size estimations and a tendency to over-estimate fruit
dimensions depending on the installation geometry and the
performance of the depth inference model.
C. Fruit colour estimation
The CDI values estimated using the high-resolution monoc-
ular camera align well with the reference colour data, despite
the mean L∗and b∗values differing significantly from their
reference counterparts (Table V). This suggests a promising
potential for employing monocular cameras in monitoring
daily fruit colour characteristics and their evolution over time.
Referring to the Intel RealSense d435i camera, the CDI
values over-estimated the reference values because of the
estimated lower values of a∗and b∗. In fact, at a given value

of L∗, moving towards the origin of the CIELAB a ∗b∗
plane, causes the CDI to return bigger values, even though

However, the CDI index—originally developed for lab-
oratory trials where there is full control of the processed
fruit—has shown sensitivity to a singularity when colours
approach green hues, often resulting in CDI values close to
1.0, which may lead to misinterpretation of the data in case
of remote sensing applications.
Therefore, for computer vision applications in natural en-
vironments, a new colour development index should be de-
veloped to better capture fruit colour progression, including
both green and red hue variations in a continuous and robust
manner ( [16]).
V. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, the stereo camera proved to be superior for
reliable, depth-aware fruit sizing and spatial crop load assess-
ment. The monocular camera, while limited in depth accuracy
and field coverage, offered advantages in colour resolution
and deployment flexibility, making it a suitable candidate for
high-frequency monitoring in fixed locations. Future works
should focus on developing a computer vision compatible
color development index to track the evolution of apple skin
color over time.
