UAV-Based Optimization for Fruit Counting in Greenhouses
Jo˜ao V´ıtor R. Vasconcelos1, Thayron M. Hudson1, Andr´e G. dos Santos2,
Martin Saska3 and Alexandre S. Brand˜ao1,3
Abstract— This paper presents a solution for counting fruit
in agricultural greenhouses using Unmanned Aerial Vehicles
(UAVs). Initially, a heuristic based on Simulated Annealing
was used to optimize the UAV’s trajectory, ensuring efficient
coverage of the beds. Next, digital image processing (DIP)
techniques were implemented to count the fruit, including depth
segmentation, application of bounding boxes, color filtering,
and element counting. The DIP accuracy was evaluated in
multiple scenarios and the results indicate high reliability
in fruit counting, with the potential to optimize agricultural
operations and provide valuable information to producers.
Possible future improvements could include further refinements
in image processing to increase the accuracy of counting other
fruits. Ultimately, this work contributes to the advancement
of automation in agriculture by offering a viable and efficient
solution for counting fruit in greenhouses using UAVs.
I. INTRODUCTION
In recent decades, research involving the use of unmanned
aerial vehicles (UAVs) has attracted worldwide attention,
due to their vast applications in various sectors, from agri-
culture to public security. These remotely or autonomously
controlled aircraft offer a range of advantages over manned
aircraft, including greater safety, lower operating costs, and
greater flexibility [1]. In agriculture, UAVs are revolutioniz-
ing the way farmers manage their crops and monitor crop
growth [2], [3]. Equipped with high-resolution cameras and
sensors, these drones can fly over large tracts of land quickly
and accurately, collecting data on plant health, soil moisture,
nutrient distribution and even detecting pests and diseases
before they become visible to the naked eye [4].
When exploring the use of UAVs in agriculture, we
encounter optimization challenges where efficiency in data
collection becomes a priority. This scenario brings to mind
the well-known Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) [5].
Despite its simple premise, its resolution is complex, placing
this problem in the NP-hard class, meaning there is no
known algorithm to find an optimal solution in polynomial
time for all instances. In the search for solutions to TSP,
several algorithms and heuristic techniques have emerged to
find approximate results for the problem. These include the
nearest neighbor algorithm, the nearest insertion algorithm,

II. THE PROBLEM FORMULATION AND SOLUTION
In the broader context of UAV operation, ensuring an
accurate representation of the environment is paramount to
avoiding collisions. This is especially important in competi-
tive scenarios where any collision during mission execution
leads to the elimination of the team. So, the main details of
the environment include dimensions of 20x27x8 meters and
the layout of static obstacles, as shown in Figure 1. In turn,
Figure 2 shows the proposed grid for the UAV’s movement
in this environment.
To perform the fruit count, two readings of each bed are
required: one at the front and one at the back. Therefore, for
n number of beds listed for visitation, it will be necessary
to take 2n readings, in 2n different poses and up to 2n
different positions. To find the best route for the visits, this
traveling UAV problem is reduced to the Traveling Salesman
Problem (TSP). In this context, the positions to be visited are
considered to be cities, and the path taken by the UAV from
one position to another is comparable to the path from one
city to another in the TSP.
In the TSP problem, the usual cost measure used is the
Euclidean distance between cities, which is not suitable for
the present traveling UAV problem. Observing Figure 2, one
can see that when tracing a direct path between two points
in different corridors, the UAV will need to go around the
planting beds to avoid collisions, as it is forbidden by rule for
the UAV to pass directly over them, even if they are empty.
A. Maneuvering Around the Plant Beds
Before analyzing the navigation costs from one point to
another within the greenhouse, it is important to emphasize
the global origin of the orthogonal system O = (0, 0, 0),
as illustrated in Figure 2. Furthermore, all navigation tasks

restricted to the intervals: x ∈[0, 20], y ∈[0, 27], and z ∈
[0, 8].
Moreover, there are four vertical corridors visible from the
top view, designated as observation corridors, characterized
by abscissas x ∈{1, 7, 13, 19}, and ordinates constrained
within the range y ∈[1, 26]. To facilitate movement between
these observation corridors, two horizontal passages, termed
transition corridors, are present. These passages have fixed
ordinates at y ∈{1, 26} and their abscissas confined within
the interval x ∈[1, 19].
Given that each plant must be observed from both sides,
navigation through two observation corridors, with inter-
mediate transit through a transition corridor, is necessary.
Therefore, it is essential to devise a strategy for circumvent-
ing the plant beds to determine which transition corridor
will be used to optimize observation of the cultivation on
both sides. To do so, we check if the sum of the ordinates
of the visiting point exceeds the maximum ordinate value
(ymax = 27). If affirmative, it is more efficient to use
the upper transition corridor; otherwise, the lower one is
used. It is important to emphasize that the same approach
used for visiting points belonging to the same plant can be
applied to different viewpoints. This premise is utilized in
the optimization process.
Finally, it is worth noting that navigating around the
shelves is accomplished by reaching the reference points,
highlighted in Figure 2, where the black circles represent
points for stopping and observing the plants or for transi-
tioning between shelves.

Thus, the points to be visited for bed p are S(pf) and

from which a list is compiled, including the initial point P0.
Next, the cost of all possible combinations of pairs between
these points is calculated. The results of these costs are used
by the heuristic to determine the best path for the UAV to
traverse.
The heuristic employed to solve the Traveling Salesman
Problem (TSP) was Simulated Annealing due to its speed,
reliable approximation capabilities, and the availability of a
ready-to-use Python library6, additionally, the resolution time
for the TSP was factored into the simulation. Initially pro-
posed in [7]. Subsequently, this technique was successfully
applied to resolve the TSP [8]. Simulated Annealing operates
by probabilistically exploring the solution space, enabling the
algorithm to escape local minima. It begins with a randomly
generated initial solution and then makes small adjustments
to this solution, such as changing the order in which cities
are visited. If the new solution is better (i.e., it reduces
the total distance traveled in the case of the TSP), it is
accepted immediately. However, if the new solution is worse,
it may be accepted with a certain probability determined by
the current temperature of the system and the difference in
energy between the solutions [9]. The temperature decreases
along the algorithm, allowing it to explore regions of the
solution space that may contain better solutions, even if they
initially appear worse.

After finding a solution for the TSP, it is necessary to
build the route so that the UAV passes through all the points
in the order given. During the route assembly, we estab-
lished that the UAV will only perform rolling movements to
navigate the observation corridors. Pitching maneuvers will
be used to navigate the transition corridors. To minimize
the chances of colliding with shelves, yaw maneuvers will
only be executed when the UAV is hovering, after observing
a planting bed. It is also important to mention that, while
moving longitudinally or laterally, the UAV can make vertical
displacements to observe lower and higher beds. In the
simulation environment provided for the competition, there is
a route planner based on a sequence of points, called TOPP-
RA [10]. To enable a safe and smooth transition between
corridors, transition points are added to the sequence of
points, creating intermediate points at which the UAV does
not perform observation actions.
C. Image processing for fruit identification
To locate, identify and count fruit, the simulation platform
provides a front-facing RGB-D camera. Although the camera
captures frames during UAV navigation, in the proposed
strategy, images are captured and processed only at the
observation points.
To improve the fruit counting process, the proposed
strategy aims to remove artifacts that could interfere with
fruit segmentation and detection. For example, Figure 4(a)
depicts an image collected during navigation to count yellow
peppers. In this scenario, we can see that there are pep-
pers in both the foreground and background. To mitigate
counting errors and focus on foreground information, a
distance threshold is applied using depth information. This
segmentation ensures that only RGB information within 3
meters is analyzed. This process results in Figure 4(b).
Note that the distant information appears in black in the
new image, indicating that this part of the image should be
ignored during fruit identification and counting.
The second step involves identifying the white rectangles
where the plants are located. Then, after applying a color
filter, the results are boxes delimiting regions that may or
may not contain fruit. However, these regions have a much
smaller search area than the resolution of the original image.
In addition, the bounding box position information is used to
correlate the front and back images of the same plant. Figure
4(c) illustrates the result of segmentation by white color and
highlights only the resulting bounding box.
The third stage of processing involves identifying the fruit
based on their characteristic colors. To do this, based on the
values observed in the test images, thresholds were defined
in the HSV standards and then segmentation was applied
mainly based on hue. Once the segmented fruit had been
obtained, the regions with the highest concentration of pixels
were identified, thus removing the filtering noise. Based on
prior knowledge of the fruit’s dimensions in the image plane,
a maximum area limit was set for a fruit. Therefore, if
the radius corresponding to the pixels segmented as a fruit

Subsequently, after sending each segment of the route, the
code performs a series of checks. Firstly, it checks whether
the next point is the last one. If so, the code sends the
number of fruits counted to the /fruit count topic and ends
the simulation. If not, it waits for the UAV to reach the
next destination to capture the image. It then checks whether
this image is the second one on the fruit bed. If so, it
indicates that the front and rear photos of the bed have been
taken, allowing the image processing to count the fruit. After
processing, the code sends the route to the next visit point
and waits for the process to continue.
To evaluate the effectiveness of PDI in images, images
were captured of all the beds with peppers in ten different
environments, totaling 60 beds. The mosaic of these images
is shown in Figure 9(a). The PDI analyzed each pair of
images separately, and the results are illustrated in Figure
9(b), where green indicates a correct result and red indicates
an error in the pair. Based on these analyses, we can state
that PDI has an accuracy rate of over 86.67%, although
some errors can be attributed to the low precision with which
the UAV captured the images. To reduce this issue, several
captures are made of each visit point, and the most frequent
count by the PDI method is considered.
In conclusion, in order to evaluate the whole method,
25 scenarios were carried out for counting fruit, including

in counting all three fruits was excellent. Note that of the

IV. CONCLUDING REMARKS
This work presented a proposed solution for counting fruit
in greenhouses using UAVs. The solution was evaluated in
simulated scenarios. Initially, we employed a TSP Simu-
lated Annealing-based heuristic to optimize UAV trajectory,
ensuring comprehensive bed coverage with near-optimal
efficiency. Subsequently, Digital Image Processing (DIP)
techniques were deployed for fruit counting, encompassing
depth slicing, bounding box application, color segmentation,
and element enumeration. The precision of DIP was assessed
across diverse scenarios, showcasing remarkable accuracy in
quantifying peppers, eggplants, and tomatoes.
The proposed solution discussed here highlights oppor-
tunities to refine the routing algorithm and the simulation
process as a whole. Specifically, the analysis of path planning
using the Simulated Annealing algorithm reveals potential
areas for optimizing UAV trajectory planning. Identifying in-
efficiencies in certain routes calculated by the Simulated An-
nealing algorithm allows for adjustments aimed at increasing
overall efficiency and accuracy. In addition, the evaluation of
the image processing techniques employed for fruit counting
identifies areas where improvements can be implemented,
especially in terms of fruit identification based on color. After
all, discrepant fruit count values suggest possible challenges
in image capture and processing. In essence, these findings
contribute significantly to the continuous improvement and
optimization of UAV performance in fruit counting tasks in
agricultural environments.
