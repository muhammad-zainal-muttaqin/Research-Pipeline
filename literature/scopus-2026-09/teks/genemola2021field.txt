In-field apple size estimation using photogrammetry-derived 3D point 
clouds: Comparison of 4 different methods considering fruit occlusions

A B S T R A C T  
In-field fruit monitoring at different growth stages provides important information for farmers. Recent advances 
have focused on the detection and location of fruits, although the development of accurate fruit size estimation 
systems is still a challenge that requires further attention. This work proposes a novel methodology for automatic 
in-field apple size estimation which is based on four main steps: 1) fruit detection; 2) point cloud generation 
using structure-from-motion (SfM) and multi-view stereo (MVS); 3) fruit size estimation; and 4) fruit visibility 
estimation. Four techniques were evaluated in the fruit size estimation step. The first consisted of obtaining the 
fruit diameter by measuring the two most distant points of an apple detection (largest segment technique). The 
second and third techniques were based on fitting a sphere to apple points using least squares (LS) and 
M−estimator sample consensus (MSAC) algorithms, respectively. Finally, template matching (TM) was applied 
for fitting an apple 3D model to apple points. The best results were obtained with the LS, MSAC and TM tech­
niques, which showed mean absolute errors of 4.5 mm, 3.7 mm and 4.2 mm, and coefficients of determination 
(R2) of 0.88, 0.91 and 0.88, respectively. Besides fruit size, the proposed method also estimated the visibility 
percentage of apples detected. This step showed an R2 of 0.92 with respect to the ground truth visibility. This 
allowed automatic identification and discrimination of the measurements of highly occluded apples. The main 
disadvantage of the method is the high processing time required (in this work 2760 s for 3D modelling of 6 trees), 
which limits its direct application in large agricultural areas. The code and the dataset have been made publicly 
available and a 3D visualization of results is accessible at http://www.grap.udl.cat/en/publications/apple_size_ 
estimation_SfM.   
1. Introduction 
As low fruit and vegetable intake by people is related with a high risk 
of several diseases, their consumption should be increased to bring it 
closer to the recommended levels. In order to meet the increasing fruit 
needs, farmers are required to enhance the productivity and efficiency of 
fruit growing, potentially lowering fruit prices and boosting consump­
tion (Siegel et al., 2014). This can be accomplished through techno­
logical improvements in common fruit-growing agricultural tasks such 
as irrigation, fertilization, plant protection, pruning, thinning and har­
vesting, among others. To this end, in-field fruit monitoring provides 
useful information to optimize fruit-growing management. 
Recent technological and artificial intelligence advances have led to 
the development of fruit detection systems that are able to count fruits 
and map fruit production (Fu et al., 2020; Gongal et al., 2015; Koirala 
et al., 2019). In addition to their number and location, fruit size is also a 
relevant quality parameter and its monitoring is essential for agricul­
tural operations such as thinning or for future robotic harvesting. 
Currently, fruit size estimation is carried out on a regular basis by 
applying machine vision techniques within the framework of post­
harvest sorting and grading operations (Cubero et al., 2011; M´endez 
Perez et al., 2017; Moreda et al., 2009). However, in-field fruit size 
estimation is usually done manually, which is costly in terms of time and 
labour, as well as being prone to human errors. 
Automatic in-field size estimation has been the subject of various 
research works in recent years, the most numerous being those applied 
to apples, mangoes, oranges and grapes. Stajnko et al. (2004) used a 
thermal camera to estimate apple sizes, obtaining coefficients of

when comparing the fruit width and length estimations with manual 
measurements. Gongal et al. (2018) applied a time-of-flight (ToF)

resulting accuracy was lower than that obtained using pixel size (colour

from manual measurements. Tsoulias et al. (2020) also used a LiDAR

ating the trees. The lowest errors (9.5%) corresponded to measurements 
conducted in advanced growth stages. Kurtser et al. (2020) developed a 
method to estimate grape cluster size by means of an RGB-D camera, 
obtaining errors of 2.9 cm and 3.6 cm in length and width, respectively. 
Each of the above fruit-sizing methodologies presents its own advan­
tages and drawbacks and it is not possible to state that one of them is the 
most appropriate for all scenarios. Thermal cameras require a temper­
ature gradient between the fruits and the surrounding environment, 
which is influenced by the prevailing meteorological conditions. RGB-D 
cameras are inexpensive and simultaneously provide depth and colour 
information, although their performance decreases under medium and 
high lighting conditions (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020a). LiDAR systems have 
low sensitivity to light variations, but no colour data is available and 
their cost is higher. 
Due to their simplicity and low cost, RGB cameras have also been 
applied to fruit size estimation. In a recent work, D. Wang et al. (2020) 
used an RGB camera and calibration balls to monitor the apple size of 
fully visible fruits throughout the growth period. The mean average ab­
solute error in the horizontal diameter was only 0.90 mm. Apolo-Apolo 
et al. (2020) developed a method to detect oranges, estimate their size 
with the aid of a reference object and predict the yield using RGB images 
obtained from an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), applying deep learning 
techniques. However, occlusions are an important limitation when trying 
to size fruits in a 2D image, particularly in crops with high leaf density. 
One way to minimize the number of occlusions is to use multiple images 
(multi-view approach). Hemming et al. (2014) demonstrated that 
greenhouse sweet-pepper fruit detectability increased from 69% when 
considering a single camera position to 90% when combining five 
different views of the fruit in a greenhouse. 
The use of multiple images also allows high-resolution 3D point 
clouds to be generated by applying different photogrammetric tech­
niques, the most noteworthy of which is structure from motion (SfM). 
The SfM technique is based on identifying keypoints in different images 
(feature matching) and, from these, estimating the intrinsic and extrinsic 
parameters of the camera (Triggs et al., 2000; Ullman, 1979). Multi-view 
stereo (MVS) uses these parameters to generate a dense 3D point cloud 
from the original images. SfM and MVS have been applied to detect and 
locate fruits in crops such as apple (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020b; Rakun 
et al., 2011; Zine-El-Abidine et al., 2021), wine grape (Herrero-Huerta 
et al., 2015; Rose et al., 2016) and mango (Stein et al., 2016). 
Although the combination of SfM and MVS has shown high levels of 
performance in the generation of accurate 3D models, to the best of the 
authors’ knowledge these techniques have only been applied for 
measuring grape bunches at a single growth stage (Herrero-Huerta et al., 
2015; Rose et al., 2016). The shape and phenotype of grape bunches 
differ considerably from other fruit varieties such as apples, peaches or

The present work proposes a novel methodology for the automatic 
measurement of spherical fruits using SfM-MVS-based point clouds and 
considering different percentages of fruit occlusions. The main objective 
is to evaluate photogrammetry-derived point clouds and different shape- 
fitting techniques for fruit size estimation at different percentages of 
fruit visibilities. The most relevant contributions are: 1) An evaluation of 
the accuracy of SfM-MVS-based point clouds; 2) A comprehensive 
evaluation of different shape-fitting techniques for apple size estimation; 
3) A methodology to estimate the percentage of visibility of fruits; 4) The 
provision of code and datasets developed in this research. After this 
introductory section, the rest of the paper is structured as follows: Sec­
tion 2 describes the procedure followed for data acquisition and dataset 
generation and provides a description of shape-fitting techniques used 
for fruit size estimation; Section 3 evaluates the accuracy of 3D models 
generated using SfM and presents, discusses and compares the results 
obtained with different techniques, both in laboratory and in-field 
conditions; finally, the conclusions are presented in Section 4. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Experimental setup 
Tests were carried out in a commercial Fuji apple orchard (Malus 
domestica Borkh. cv. Fuji) located in the municipality of Agramunt, 
Catalonia, Spain (latitude: 41◦44′47.07′’N; longitude: 1◦01′52.23′’E; 
312 m a.s.l. ETRS89). Trees grown in the studied orchard were trained in 
a tall spindle system, with a planning pattern of 4 m × 0.9 m and a 
maximum canopy height and width of approximately 3.5 m and 1.5 m, 
respectively. 
Data was acquired at three different dates (Table 1). The first set of 
data (Orch2018 set) was acquired on October 3rd, 2018, when apples 
were at phenological growth stage BBCH 85 –Advanced ripening– 
(Meier, 2001). This set includes data from three consecutive trees con­
taining a total of 303 apples with a mean diameter of 77.4 mm. The east 
side of all trees was captured from 16:29 h to 16:34 h (UTC + 1) and the 
west side from 16:46 h to 17:01 h (UTC + 1). The second set of data 
(Orch2020 set) was acquired on July 16th, 2020, when apples were at 
phenological growth stage BBCH 77 –Fruits about 70% final size– 
(Meier, 2001). This set includes data from three consecutive trees con­
taining a total of 312 apples with a mean diameter of 54.1 mm. The east 
side of all trees was captured from 12:06 h to 12:29 h (UTC + 1) and the 
west side from 16:27 h to 16:53 h (UTC + 1), with similar illumination 
conditions in both faces. Finally, the third set of data (Lab2020 set) was 
acquired on October 2nd, 2020, when apples were at phenological 
growth stage BBCH 85 –Advanced ripening– (Meier, 2001). This set 
includes 25 randomly selected apples of diameters from 39 mm to 97 
mm that were hand-picked and scanned in laboratory conditions.

Images were acquired with an EOS 60D DSLR camera (Canon Inc.

Respective totals of 348 and 508 photographs were taken from 
different positions on both sides of the tree rows to scan the apple trees 
of the Orch2018 and Orch2020 sets. The higher number of photographs 
acquired in the Orch2020 set was due to the additional images required 
to capture the measurement tapes placed next to the scene and used to 
evaluate the point cloud accuracy (Fig. 1e). The photographs were taken 
freehand from approximately 2.5 m with respect to the base of the tree 
trunks. The image acquisition procedure was planned in accordance 
with the array shown in Fig. 2. The horizontal separation between 
consecutive images was approximately 20 cm. From each horizontal 
position, a 6-step ladder was used to take 12 photographs at different 
heights (2 photographs taken from each ladder step), imaging the trees 
from the lower part (soil-trunk) to the upper part. With this configura­
tion, the vertical and horizontal overlaps between neighbouring images 
were higher than 75% and 85%, respectively. This amount of overlap is 
considered to be adequate to facilitate camera alignment when applying 
SfM (Agisoft Metashape, 2019). 
The set of 25 apples measured in laboratory conditions (Lab2020) 
was scanned using the experimental layout shown in Fig. 3a. The camera 
used was the same as for orchard conditions, but in this case artificial 
lighting was added by means of four 60 W −800 lm incandescent lamps 
(Fig. 3a). Lamps were placed at a distance between 40 cm and 70 cm 
with respect to the apple surface and oriented at different angles to 
achieve a good distribution of light and reduce the effect of shadows. In 
some apple samples, this lighting design did not prevent the appearance 
of secular reflections (Fig. 3b), however this did not affect the generation 
of the 3D models when applying SfM-MVS. Apples were held in a vertical 
screw mounted on a turntable. The turntable was used to take 36 pic­
tures around the apple (approximately 10◦
of rotation between 
consecutive shots) from five different heights, resulting in a total of 180 
images per apple (Fig. 3d). The camera heights (Z axis in Fig. 3d) with 
respect to the apples centre were : −10 cm, 0 cm, 10 cm, 20 cm and 30 
cm. From each height, the camera was placed at a distance of approxi­
mately 45 cm with respect to the apple and oriented to the apple centre. 
The aim with this set of data was to have a set of apples scanned 360◦in 
optimal conditions. Thus, results obtained with this set of data (Section 
3.3.1) will not be influenced by the scanning conditions and will 
correspond to the maximum performance that can be achieved with the 
proposed fruit sizing methods.

First, the convolutional neural network Mask-RCNN (He et al., 2017) 
was trained with the Fuji-SfM dataset (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020c) and used 
to detect and segment apples (instance segmentation) in Orch2018 and 
Orch2020 images. Since lab images had a white background (Fig. 3b), 
this set was segmented applying a colour threshold (Fig. 3c) to the HSV 
(Hue, Saturation and Value) colour space: Background = (S < 0.4 & V >
0.55). These threshold values were selected after analysing the HSV 
histogram of a sample image. Then, the segmented images were used to 
generate the 3D model of all apples by means of SfM and MVS. Agisoft 
Professional Metashape software (v1.6.4, Agisoft LLC, St. Petersburg, 
Russia) was used to apply the SfM and MVS algorithms. The set of 
specific software configuration parameters is set out in detail in Ap­
pendix A. (Table A.1). With this configuration, point clouds from 
Orch2018 and Orch2020 sets had a point density between 0.5 
points⋅mm−2 and 2 points⋅mm−2, while point clouds from the Lab2020 
set had a point density between 55 points⋅mm−2 and 80 points⋅mm−2. In 
order to have similar point cloud resolutions, point clouds from Lab2020 
were subsampled to 2 points⋅mm−2. 
When applying SfM-MVS algorithms to entire images, a complete 
representation of the captured scene is generated (Fig. 4a). However, 
since the aim of the article is apple measurement, only segmented pixels 
(apple pixels) were used for the 3D reconstruction of apples (Fig. 4b). Note 
that, in Fig. 4b, background objects (leaves, branches, trunks and ground) 
are displayed in grey scale to facilitate the visualization, but these points 
were not generated when using SfM-MVS with segmented images. 
The last step of the Gen´e-Mola et al. (2020b) fruit detection meth­
odology involves the projection of image detections onto 3D point 
clouds. This step generates some apple multi-detections, the main source 
of error of the method (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020b). Since the present work 
aims to evaluate fruit size estimation techniques and not the fruit 
detection method, this step was manually carried out. To do so, the 3D 
point clouds generated with the Orch2018 and Orch2020 sets were 
cropped using CloudCompare software (Cloud Compare [GPL software] 
v2.9 Omnia), obtaining a unique point cloud for each measured apple. 
After the scanning and 3D modelling of the apples of all the datasets, 
their maximum horizontal diameter (diameter ground truth) was 
manually measured using a Vernier calliper (Fig. 4c2). This manual 
measure was estimated to have a mean absolute error of 0.3 mm. This 
error was estimated by measuring 20 selected apples twice. To facilitate 
the pair-wise correspondence between each apple measurement and the 
corresponding point cloud, all apples were labelled with a sticker con­
taining the apple ID number (Fig. 4c1). Then, the scene was captured for 
a second time, obtaining the same representation but now with the apple 
ID visible in both the images and the point clouds (Fig. 4c). The point 
clouds containing labelled apples were used to identify and annotate the 
3D point clouds of apples scanned before attaching the labels. 
Finally, the apple ground truth visibility was obtained by fitting a 
sphere with a diameter equal to the manual measure (ground truth 
sphere) and measuring the ratio of the sphere area that was visible in the 
apple point cloud. To do so, each apple 3D point was projected onto the 
ground truth sphere surface following the direction of an “imaginary” 
line that goes from the 3D apple point to the sphere centre. Then, the 
ground truth visibility was computed as the percentage of the ground 
truth sphere surface that intersected with point projections:

2.3. Methodology pipeline 
Fig. 5 illustrates the procedure followed to estimate fruit size. After 
detecting and generating the 3D model of apples (Fig. 5a and b), a sparse 
outlier removal algorithm (Rusu et al., 2008) was applied to remove 
noisy points usually located around apple boundaries (Fig. 5c). To this 
end, points which fell outside μ +α × σ were removed, where μ and σ are 
the mean and standard deviation of the k nearest neighbour distances 
and α is a parameter to tune the sensitivity of the algorithm to σ. In this 
work, k and α were set to 50 and 1, respectively. These values were 
selected empirically based on an analysis performed on a limited num­
ber of samples. Then, the remaining points were used to estimate the 
fruit size (Fig. 5d) using one of the following methods:  
• Largest segment (Lar Seg): The fruit diameter is assumed to be equal 
to the Euclidean distance between the two most distant points.

• M-estimator Sample Consensus (MSAC) (Torr and Zisserman, 2000): 
This is an iterative method based on the RANSAC algorithm (Fischler 
and Bolles, 1981). On each iteration, a total of 4 points were 
randomly selected and the sphere passing through these 4 points was 
calculated and considered a possible solution. Each possible solution 
was assessed by counting the number of points (inliers) that were 
closer to the possible solution than a distance δ (in this work δ =
0.003 m). This process was repeated I iterations (in this work I =
10000) or until the confidence percentage for finding the maximum 
number of inliers (∁) was reached (in this work ∁= 99.9). Finally, the 
solution that maximizes the likelihood -rather than the number of 
inliers used in RANSAC- was selected. All the algorithm parameters 
(δ, I, ∁) were selected empirically based on an analysis performed on 
a limited number of samples.  
• Template matching (TM): An apple 3D model generated with SfM in 
lab conditions was used as a point cloud template. This 3D model was 
scaled to obtain apple templates of different diameters from 20 mm 
to 96 mm. Each of these templates was matched with apple point 
clouds using the finite iterative closest point (ICP_finite) algorithm 
(Kroon, 2009). After applying the ICP, the apple size was determined 
by selecting the template size that presented the highest amount of 
point cloud inliers, with the inlier points being those that were closer 
than δ to the apple template (δ = 0.003 m). 
Since the level of fruit visibility is related with the accuracy of the 
estimated size (Section 3), an automatic visibility estimation is of in­
terest to determine whether an apple detection is a good candidate to 
estimate the diameter or not. To automatically estimate the fruit visi­
bility, apple 3D points were projected onto the shape fitted for fruit size 
estimation (Fig. 5e). Similarly to the procedure explained for the gen­
eration of ground truth visibility, each apple 3D point was projected 
onto the fitted shape following the direction of an “imaginary” line that 
goes from the 3D apple point to the centre of the fitted shape. Then, the 
visibility (Fig. 5f) was computed following Eq. (1), where SV and SO are 
the visible and occluded areas of the fitted shape (coloured in green and 
red in Fig. 5f, respectively).

2.4. Performance evaluation 
Since the point cloud quality can affect the performance of the fruit 
size estimation process, results provided in Section 3 evaluate both the 
point cloud accuracy and the fruit size estimation results. The point 
cloud accuracy was evaluated using the EPS foam spheres (Fig. 1d) and 
the two measuring tapes (Fig. 1e) introduced in Section 2.1. The seven 
foam spheres were manually clipped into seven individual clouds using 
CloudCompare software. The sphere maximum diameters in planes XZ 
and XY were measured in the cloud and compared to the actual sphere 
diameter manually measured with a Vernier calliper. Similarly, the two 
tapes were also measured in the cloud and compared to their actual 
length. For this analysis, the measurement error is defined as the signed 
difference between measured and actual values, while the relative error 
is the signed ratio between measurement error and actual values.

methods were evaluated at different visibility intervals of 10% size from

3. Results 
3.1. Point cloud accuracy 
The point cloud accuracy results evaluated using the EPS foam 
spheres are shown in Table 2. Absolute errors ranged from 1 mm to 2 
mm in the XZ-plane and increased to 2 mm to 4 mm in the XY-plane, 
with the conclusion that point clouds have a slightly better accuracy 
in the vertical than the horizontal plane. The measurement errors did 
not show a significant correlation with the sphere size. Similar results 
were obtained when measuring linear distances (Table 3). In this case, 
the point cloud measurements showed absolute errors ranging from 0.1 
mm to 2.2 mm and relative errors from 0.07% to 4.4%, which were 
larger in shorter measurements. The measurements of tapes oriented 
horizontally (along the Y-axis) also showed a higher accuracy than the 
measurements of tapes oriented vertically (along the Z-axis).

Fruit size estimation results obtained with the set of apples scanned in 
laboratory conditions are reported in Table 5. The worst performance

Fig. 10 illustrates the MAE results for all the tested methodologies at 
different levels of fruit visibility. The MAE of LS, MSAC and TM 
decreased with visibility until reaching a plateau at visibilities higher 
than 40%. In contrast, the Lar Seg error increased with visibility. At low 
visibility levels (V < 20%), the performance of the tested methods 
ranged from 12.5 mm to 26 mm, with TM the most accurate (Fig. 10a). 
At middle to high visibility levels (V > 20%), the most accurate method 
was MSAC, although LS and TM showed similar performances. Since the 
performance ofLS, MSAC and TM increased with visibility, a certain 
visibility threshold could be set as a confidence parameter, allowing 
only the measurement of sets of fruits that are good candidates for a 
good size estimation. As can be observed in Fig. 10b, an MAE > 7 mm 
was obtained when measuring all the detected apples (V > 0%). How­
ever, the performance improved by increasing the visibility threshold to 
V > 30%, with MAE < 5 mm being obtained in all three methods (LS, 
MSAC and TM). 
The correlation between automatic measurements and the diameters 
manually measured in the field is assessed in Table 10, Fig. 11 and 
Fig. 12. When measuring all the scanned apples (V > 0%), the strongest 
correlation was reported for the LS method (R2 = 0.68). However, for 
middle to high visibilities, the MSAC method showed the best coefficient 
of determination (R2 > 0.9) when measuring apples with visibility 
higher than 40% (Fig. 11). Similar results were obtained for LS and TM, 
with R2 > 0.88 at V > 40% (Table 10). The R2 increased with visibility in 
all the tested methods. In the case of Lar Seg, this contrasts with the 
results previously presented: while Lar Seg performance decreased with 
visibility in terms of MAE, MAPE and RMSE (Fig. 10, Table 6, Table 7, 
Table 9), the coefficient of determination increased (Fig. 11 and 
Table 10). This occurred because, although having higher errors in high 
visible apples due to a higher diameter overestimation, the measure­
ments were more closely correlated with the actual diameter. 
Fig. 12 plots the correlation obtained between the ground truth di­
ameters and the MSAC estimations. Similar plots were obtained for LS and 
TM but, for the sake of simplicity, only the MSAC results are shown. When 
assessing all the samples (V > 0%) (Fig. 12a), the number of apples that 
MSAC estimated as measuring>100 mm in diameter was much higher 
than at V > 20% (Fig. 12b) and V > 40% (Fig. 12c). From this, it is 
concluded that an important source of error occurred with highly occluded 
apples (V < 20%), leading to an important overestimation (>100 mm in 
diameter). When dealing with apples at V > 20%, the number of over­
estimations was significantly reduced, with the R2 increasing to 0.81 
(Fig. 12b), although some outliers were still observed. Finally, at V > 40% 
the number of outliers was reduced, obtaining an R2 of 0.91. 
The mean measured diameters of apples in the Orch2018 and 
Orch2020 sets are shown in Table 11. Once again, the worst estimation 
was reported with the Lar Seg method, which overestimated the mean 
diameter by between 17% and 28% depending on the visibility of the 
measured apples. Similar to the results reported in terms of MAE, MAPE 
and RMSE (Table 6, Table 7 and Table 9), the Lar Seg mean diameter 
error increased with visibility (Table 11). The best mean diameter es­
timations were reported with LS, MSAC and TM, obtaining mean 
diameter errors of between 0.5 mm and 5 mm in the Orch2020 set and 
between 0 mm and 2.3 mm in the Orch2018 set. Thus, higher errors 
were reported when measuring smaller apples. Besides the difference in 
mean diameter, the Orch2018 set differed from the Orch2020 set in that 
the lighting conditions of the east and west sides were different when 
acquiring data. In this study, this fact did not show an impact on the 
results.

of apples measured at different diameters) obtained at V > 0% 
(Fig. 13a), V > 20% (Fig. 13b) and V > 40% (Fig. 13c). The LS, MSAC

In terms of computational efficiency, the most efficient method was 
LS, which processed all the orchard dataset (615 apples) in 0.45 s 
(0.0007 s per apple). The second fastest method was MSAC, processing 
the 615 apples in 2.64 s, followed by the Lar Seg method which required 
75.66 s. The TM method was the most inefficient, requiring a total of 
1087.04 s (1.77 s per apple). Nevertheless, the most computational 
intensive operations of the methodology pipeline were found in the 
generation of the 3D model through SfM photogrammetry, which 
required 2760 s to generate all point clouds.

4. Discussion 
The accuracy of the 3D models obtained with SfM-MVS was evalu­
ated in Section 3.1, obtaining absolute errors between 0.1 mm and 4 
mm. These results demonstrate that SfM-MVS techniques can generate 
highly accurate point clouds compared to other 3D scanning systems. 
For instance, most of the RGB-D sensors reviewed in Fu et al. (2020) 
reported depth measurement errors of approximately 1% of the actual 
measured depth, which correspond to a 25 mm error when measuring at 
a distance of 2.5 m. In addition, RGB-D sensor performance decreases 
when operating under high lighting conditions (Gen´e-Mola et al., 
2020a). Similarly, LiDAR sensors reviewed in Horaud et al. (2016) re­
ported accuracy values of approximately 20 mm. The high accuracy of 
3D models generated using SfM-MVS makes this scanning technique 
interesting for fruit size applications. In contrast, the main drawback of 
this technique is the high computational time required to generate the 
3D models, which limits its use for real time applications. 
The proposed fruit size estimation methodologies were evaluated in 
Section 3.3. The worst results were obtained with the Lar Seg technique, 
while the other methods (LS, MSAC and TM) presented a similar per­
formance. When measuring all the scanned apples in orchard conditions, 
the best results were reported with the TM technique, which reported an 
MAE of 7.0 mm. However, when only measuring apples with higher 
visibility (V > 40%), the best results were obtained with the MSAC 
method, reporting an MAE of 3.7 mm. These results could be further 
improved by optimizing the scanning conditions to obtain a complete 
representation of the fruits (V = 100%). This was demonstrated with the 
measurement of 25 apples in laboratory conditions, which reported 
MAE values of 1.71 mm, 1.78 mm and 1.92 mm with the LS, MSAC and 
TM methods, respectively. 
In applications such as yield prediction, a good estimation of mean 
apples diameter is of interest to convert the fruit load prediction (fruits/ 
ha) to yield prediction (kg/ha). The mean diameter estimated with LS, 
MSAC and TM gave errors of between 0 mm and 5 mm (Table 11). These 
results are comparable with those reported for other methods in studies 
by Stajnko et al. (2004), Rose et al. (2016) and D. Wang et al. (2020), 
with mean diameter errors of < 6 mm, < 2 mm and < 2.8 mm, 
respectively. Having a strong correlation between estimated and actual 
apple size is also important for applications such as fruit growth moni­
toring. An R2 of 0.91 was obtained with the MSAC method applied to 
apples with a visibility higher than 40%. This result is comparable with 
those obtained with other methods in studies found in the bibliography 
which have reported R2 values of between 0.45 and 0.96 (Herrero- 
Huerta et al., 2015; Tsoulias et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2017). 
Since estimating the size of highly occluded apples is a challenging 
task that entails large error estimates, most of the works in the bibliog­
raphy limit fruit size estimation to visible fruits manually selected 
(Gongal et al., 2018; Herrero-Huerta et al., 2015; Stajnko et al., 2004; 
Wang et al., 2020, 2017). In order to automatically discriminate highly 
occluded apples, the present method not only estimates the fruit size, but 
also the fruit visibility. Results show a coefficient of determination of 
R2 = 0.92 between MSAC estimated visibility and ground truth visibility. 
In addition, an analysis of mean fruit size at different levels of visibility 
was performed, concluding that the mean apple diameter was almost 
constant at different visibility levels from V > 0% to V > 40%. These 
results are similar to those obtained by Wang et al. (2017), who 
demonstrated that fruits in the outer parts of the canopy (likely to be

5. Conclusions 
The proposed methodology, based on the use of SfM-MVS to 3D 
model apples previously detected in images, is an interesting and 
promising technique for fruit size estimation as the generated point 
cloud accuracy is an order of magnitude lower than those obtained when 
using other 3D sensors such as LiDAR or RGB-D cameras (between 0.1 
mm and 4 mm). Of the four different methods evaluated, the largest 
segment technique, based on measuring the two most distant points of 
an apple cloud (largest segment), was highly affected by outlier points, 
resulting in an MAE of 15 mm and an R2 = 0.64. The other three 
methods (Least Squares - LS, MSAC and Template Matching - TM) per­
formed better. For highly occluded apples, the TM method reported the 
best performance in terms of MAE (7mm). For middle to highly visible 
apples (V > 20%), MSAC was the most accurate method, reporting an 
MAE = 3.7mmand an R2 = 0.91 at V > 40%. Another output of the 
methodology is the estimation of the visibility percentage. As the error 
increases proportionally with occlusion, the methodology that has been 
designed allows highly occluded apples to be discarded, limiting the 
diameter estimation to the best candidate apples. In terms of computa­
tional complexity, the most efficient method was LS, which required an 
average of 0.0007 s to process a single apple. MSAC was also found to be 
a fast method, requiring 0.004 s per apple. Data acquisition (in this work 
carried out manually) and the high processing time required by SfM- 
MVS algorithms are the major bottlenecks of the method, preventing 
the monitoring of large agricultural areas. In order to transfer the 
technology to the agricultural community, future works should consider 
the automation of data-acquisition and pre-processing steps. This will 
require further studies to verify that automatically acquired images 
comply with the resolution and sharpness required by SfM-MVS algo­
rithms. Future works should also consider the optimization of SfM-MVS 
algorithms in order to reduce their computational complexity, although 
the expected evolution of hardware -with more powerful and faster 
computers- may also help to overcome this issue.
