Introduction
Pineapple (Ananas comosus) is a tropical fruit renowned for 
its distinct sweet flavor, vibrant yellow flesh, and its crown 
of spiky green leaves. Originally from South America, it 
is currently grown extensively in tropical and subtropical 
climates worldwide [38]. Pineapple production contributes 
to both domestic and international markets, making it an 
important fruit in global agriculture. Traditional farming 
practices involve manual inspection of pineapple for ripe­
ness which is labor-intensive, incurs high expenses, and is 
time-consuming [17]. According to the Food and Agricul­
ture Organization (FAO), post-harvest losses of fruits in 
tropical regions can reach up to 30%, with inaccurate ripe­

Abstract
The precise detection and classification of pineapples at various stages of maturity are crucial for early yield estimation in 
agriculture. This study presents a framework utilizing YOLOv8 for pineapple detection and ripeness classification through 
drone imagery. After detecting and classifying pineapples, we employ bounding box and binary mask fitting techniques, 
including Circle Fitting (CF), Ellipse Fitting (EF), Circle Enclosing (CE), and Rotated Rectangle (RR), to estimate pine­
apple size by converting pixel-based measurements to millimeters. Finally, the current research predicted the weight 
of the pineapples by employing linear and nonlinear models. This work used linear models such as Linear Regression, 
Polynomial Regression, and nonlinear-based machine learning models such as Support Vector Regressor, Decision Tree, 
and K-Nearest Neighbor, with pixel counts as the input parameter for predicting pineapple weight. The outcome showed 
that YOLOv8 outperforms other YOLO models such as YOLOv7, YOLOv11 and RT-DETR achieving 99.8% Precision, 
97.7% mean average precision, and 93.1% Recall for pineapple detection. For classification, this work has compared 
YOLOv8 with ML techniques, the result shows that YOLOv8 achieves better precision scores of 98% for unripe, 92% 
for semi-ripe, and 98% for ripe. For size estimation, this work has used BB and masking techniques, where the size error 
between measured and estimated (pixel to millimeter) pineapples were analyzed in terms of Root Mean Square Error, 
Mean Average Error, and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The MAPE value was below 6% for non-occluded 
pineapples when their size was estimated using EF and for occluded apples, it was 8% when the size was estimated using 
CE. The K-Nearest Neighbor model predicts pineapple weight with the highest accuracy, achieving an R² of 0.978 and 
RMSE of 4.12 g. In this work, we show that our integrated approach of YOLOv8-based detection and classification, binary 
mask-based size estimation, and machine learning-based weight prediction provides an effective framework for automated 
pineapple monitoring in precision agriculture.

In recent years, significant studies and applications of 
deep learning techniques have driven the wide adoption of 
computer vision in crop monitoring [33]. These techniques 
are used for the detection, classification, size, and weight 
estimation of agricultural products. Among the commonly 
used techniques, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) 
[10] serve as the base for many complex architectures due 
to their ability to automatically extract hierarchical features 
from images. Moreover, techniques like SSD (Single Shot 
MultiBox Detector) (Khoa et al.,[2023], YOLO (You Only 
Look Once) [19], and Faster R-CNN [30] are also used 
for detection. Faster R-CNN works well for high-accu­
racy applications since it combines CNNs and region pro­
posal networks for accurate localization and classification. 
YOLO, which is renowned for its real-time performance, 
offers a trade-off between computational speed and accu­
racy by simultaneously performing detection and classifica­
tion. By forecasting bounding boxes and class probabilities 
at various feature levels, SSD accomplishes multi-scale 
detection. As a result, these models are highly suitable for 
real-time monitoring of agricultural goals in challenging 
environments. In complex agricultural conditions, research­
ers have successfully enhanced these models to perform 
high-precision fruit detection and classification. Gu et al. 
[9] proposed an improved RT-DETR model for automated 
tomato detection. The paper also presents techniques for 
diameter estimate using edge detection and Hough trans­
form, color extraction using CIELAB space, and weight 
prediction using regression modeling. Wu et al. [36] pre­
sented a multispectral class-constraint attentional neural 
network that uses spectral channel information to improve 
class-specific attentions and capture conceptual links. It 
improves the features of foreground objects and reduces 
background noise in challenging scenarios by incorporating 
class-guided attention.

While deep learning methods have shown promising 
results in fruit detection, achieving high precision in real-
world field conditions remains challenging because of the 
following reason:
1)	 Technical challenges: These include occlusion (e.g., 
fruits hidden behind leaves or other fruits), illumination 
variability due to variation in natural lighting condi­
tions, and the difficulty of detecting small or partially 
visible fruits in cluttered scenes.
2)	 Data-related challenges: Variability in background con­
ditions, fruit size, and shape increases the complexity of 
model training and generalization.
3)	 Application-specific challenges: Integration into deci­
sion-making pipelines, such as harvest scheduling or 
yield estimation, requires highly reliable and consistent 
data.
Deep learning approaches have shown promising results in 
identifying and categorizing fruits such as apples, citrus, 
and mangoes, achieving high accuracy. However, these 
techniques often fall short when deployed in real-world 
scenarios due to challenges such as fruit occlusion, uneven 
illumination, and complicated backgrounds. Furthermore, 
pineapples have received comparatively less attention 
in this scenario. Multi-stage ripeness categorization and 
weight estimation, which are essential for harvest planning 
and market sorting, are rarely covered by existing studies. 
This paper aims to bridge this gap by concentrating on the 
size and weight estimation of pineapples, contributing to the 
advancement of precision agriculture for this fruit.

Data augmentation and image processing
In a field environment, various interference factors, such 
as lighting conditions and the inclusion of plant stems and 
leaves, can affect the data. Data augmentation techniques 
were employed to enrich and expand the dataset in order 
to reflect the real-world field condition, which improved 
the training model’s capacity for generalization. Rotating, 
flipping, modifying the image’s color and brightness, and 
adding noise are some of these augmentation techniques. 
LabelImg tool was used to annotate the images, and the 
annotations were saved in an XML format file. We used 
5-fold cross-validation to evaluate the effectiveness and 
generalization of our pineapple ripeness classification 
model. Using this procedure, five equal subsets (folds) were 
randomly selected from the full dataset. In each iteration, 
four folds were used for training and one was used as the 
validation set. To make sure that every subset was used as 
the validation set once, this procedure was carried out five 
times. To improve the evaluation’s reliability and reduce the 
bias that could result from a single train-test split, the final 
performance metrics were calculated by averaging the out­
comes from all five folds.
Pineapple detection using YOLOv8
The architecture consists of three main components: the 
backbone, neck, and head. The backbone is designed for fea­
ture extraction using the C2f (CSP Bottleneck with Fusion) 
model based on CSPNet (Cross-Stage Partial Networks), 
which improves gradient flow and reduces computational 
redundancy. It also incorporates focus layers to compress 
spatial dimensions early, enhancing efficiency. The neck of 
YOLOv8 uses a Path Aggregation Network (PANet) or its 
variant to combine high-level semantic features with low-
level detailed features, enabling precise object localization 
and classification. The head of YOLOv8 uses an anchor-
free design, simplifying training and inference by directly 
predicting object positions instead of relying on predefined 
anchor boxes. This results in faster processing and simpler 
adaptation to various datasets. The enhanced loss function 
used by YOLOv8 combines Objectness Loss to predict 
the likelihood of object presence, Focal Loss to address 
class imbalance, and Intersection over Union (IoU) Loss 
for bounding box regression. These enhancements make 
YOLOv8 highly effective for real-time detection making.

Materials and methods
Dataset collection
The dataset was collected using a drone (DJI Mini 4 Pro) 
from different pineapple farms in Molvum and Ruzaphema 
village, in the state of Nagaland, India. The locations of the 
farm are shown in Fig. 1(a) and 1(b). Both farms adopt a 
double-row planting system, where the distance between 
double rows is maintained at 0.6 m, the spacing between 
plants within a row is 0.5 m, and the gap between row-to-
row is 0.9 m. The videos were captured row-by-row from a 
height of 2 m above the ground, as shown in Fig. 1(c).
The dataset consists of 2,057 images of the Kew pine­
apple variety (Ananas comosus), with each image having a 
resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. In order to record the vari­
ous stages of pineapple ripening, video recordings were col­
lected from the farm on several days. The recordings were 
made in the early morning (07:00 to 09:00 IST) and late 
evening (15:00 to 17:00 IST) to guarantee constant light­
ing. The morning sessions offered the best lighting, ensur­
ing clear visibility of fruit characteristics, and the evening 
recordings were used to evaluate how backlighting (sunset) 
affected RGB-D readings. This made it possible to evaluate 
fruit ripening under a wider range of lighting conditions.

Ripeness assessment and classification
YOLOv8 can be effectively used for pineapple ripeness 
classification by leveraging its simplified classification 
architecture. This process begins by preparing a labeled 
dataset, where images are categorized into classes such as 
ripe, semi-ripe, and unripe, organized into separate folders 
for training and validation. YOLOv8’s classification model 
extracts feature from the detected images using a backbone 
based on CSPNet and its variants, processes these features 
through pooling layers to aggregate spatial information, and 
finally maps them to class probabilities via fully connected 
layers. The model minimizes the variance between the pre­
dicted and actual class probabilities during training by using 
cross-entropy loss, while augmentation methods like flip­
ping, cropping and color adjustment enhance the dataset’s 
robustness. Once trained, the YOLOv8 classification model 
can determine the ripeness of pineapples by predicting the 
class with the highest probability for a given image. The 
dataset for the ripeness classification task was divided using 
a 7:2:1 ratio for training, validation, and testing, respec­
tively, as shown in Table 1.
This architecture is well-suited for situations such as 
drone-based pineapple monitoring as it maintains a balance 
between accuracy and real-time performance. By combin­
ing the classification model with its object detection capa­
bilities, YOLOv8 can first detect pineapples using an RGB 
image and then classify their ripeness, providing an efficient 
solution for automated ripeness assessment.

Size estimation
Two approaches were employed to identify the region of 
interest (ROI): BB and binary mask. Both methods followed 
these steps: (1) size (length and width) estimation in pixels 
from the image, (2) depth estimation between the camera 
and the pineapple, and (3) pineapple size estimation in mm. 
A bounding box is a rectangular region drawn around the 
fruit, typically identified by object detection algorithms like 
YOLO. The width and height of the box are determined by 
its top-left and bottom-right coordinates. The size in pixels 
is then estimated as the product of the length and width. 
In contrast, a binary mask is generated through pixel-wise 
segmentation methods, which classify each pixel in the 
image as belonging to the fruit or the background. This cre­
ates a binary image where fruit pixels are marked as 1, and 
background pixels are marked as 0. The size in pixels is 
obtained by summing all the pixels marked as 1, providing 
a precise representation of the fruit’s actual shape. Masking 
techniques such as CF, EF, CE, and RR are used to define 
the ROI by fitting geometric shapes around the object for 
precise localization and analysis.

Depth estimation
Pineapples vary in size according to their depth (distance 
from the camera). Determining the distances (depth) from 
the camera to the pineapple is crucial to calculating the actual 
pineapple size (mm) from pixel measurement. To ensure 
that the pineapple’s measurements are precisely depicted 
in actual units (mm), depth estimation helps to mitigate the 
effects of perspective distortion. Without depth information, 
the object’s position in relation to the camera could distort 
the estimated size. The optimal shape that fits the binary 
mask for methods such as RR, EF, CE, and CF can vary 
based on the depth of the object. The distances had to be 
known to calculate the actual pineapple size from the mea­
surement in pixels. The RGB-D camera depth information 
is used to calculate each fruit’s estimated depth, expressed 
in mm. Three statistical measures, minimum (MIN), aver­
age (AVG), and modal (MOD) values, were associated with 
all methods to estimate the depth of each pineapple. For a 
given pineapple sample, Figs. 5 and 6 show the resulting 2D 
depth distributions, allowing a visual comparison between 
the non-occluded and occluded pineapple, respectively.

Weight prediction
Four main steps are usually involved in data preprocessing: 
feature extraction, data normalization, missing data analy­

new dataset was created for the estimation of weight in the 
laboratory, which includes the actual calculated fruit weight 
and size measurement for each pineapple. Imputation of 
missing data was not necessary because the measured data 
contained no missing values. The size of the training and 
testing partition may have an impact on how well machine 
learning models perform. During the model development 
process, 80% of the data was used for training while 20% 
was allocated for testing.

Discussion
This work focused on developing an integrated framework 
for pineapple detection, classification of ripeness level, size, 
and weight prediction. Non-uniform maturity of pineapple 
becomes a challenge for large-scale pineapple cultivation, 
making crop management and quality control more diffi­
cult, which requires more labor and is time-consuming. The 
results of this study showed the effectiveness of YOLOv8 
in addressing the challenges associated with detecting and 
classifying pineapples in large-scale agricultural fields. The 
precise identification of pineapple and classification of dif­
ferent maturity stages enhance post-harvest management, 
increase marketability, and optimize agricultural operations.
Additionally, issues including overlapping fruits, leaf 
occlusion, and different fruit sizes add complexity that 
might affect the accuracy of conventional monitoring sys­
tems. Handling variations in fruit position, overlapping, and 
variations in camera angles or distances are some of the main 
issues in estimating size and weight from camera. Address­
ing these challenges through calibration with real-world 
measurements and depth estimation ensures the reliability 
of our estimations. The correlation between size and weight 
is nonlinear, especially in fruits like pineapples, where fac­
tors such as irregular shapes and texture characteristics con­
tribute to weight variability. This work shows the potential 
of obtaining precise weight predictions by combining size 
estimations using BB and mask fitting approaches with ML 
models trained on actual weighted data. However, YOLOv8 
may struggle in certain edge cases, such as very low con­
trast images, heavy occlusion, and blurred or underexposed 
images, often resulting from motion or poor lighting con­
ditions during the image-capturing process. These factors 
may result in inaccurate classifications or missed detections, 
particularly in uncontrolled environments. Future research 
can explore advanced model fusion techniques to enhance 
detection and classification performance. Additionally, 
integrating YOLOv8 with CNN-based classifiers or using 
ensemble learning frameworks could leverage complemen­
tary strengths of different architectures.

In this study, YOLOv8 was used for both detection and 
classification of pineapple maturity stages, achieving higher 
detection accuracy than earlier YOLO versions and outper­
forming traditional machine learning classifiers in ripeness 
classification. The performance of the detection model was 
assessed using Precision, Recall, and mAP. Compared to 
YOLOv7, YOLOv11, and RT-DETR, YOLOv8 achieved
