A B S T R A C T
Shortage of labor in fruit crop production has become a significant challenge in recent years. Therefore,
mechanized and automated machines have emerged as promising alternatives to labor-intensive orchard
operations such as harvesting, pruning, and thinning. One of the key aspects of the automated machines in
accomplishing these tasks is their ability to identify tree canopy parts such as trunk and branches and estimate
their geometric and topological parameters such as branch diameter, branch length, branch angles, and
spacing between branches. By utilizing geometric parameters such as branch diameter, length, and orientation,
researchers then can develop automated pruning and thinning systems that make more effective decisions
to achieve optimal fruit yield and quality by accurately estimating the desired crop-load. In this study, we
propose a machine vision system for estimating one of the canopy parameters in apple orchards: branch
diameter. This parameter was used to estimate the optimal number of fruit that individual branches could bear
in a commercial orchard, which provides a basis for robotic pruning, flower thinning, and fruitlet thinning
so that desired fruit yield and quality could be achieved. Utilizing color and depth information collected
with an RGB-D sensor (Azure Kinect DK, Microsoft, Redmond, WA), a YOLOv8-based instance segmentation
technique was developed to identify trunks and branches of apple trees in the dormant season. We then applied
a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) technique to estimate branch orientation, which was subsequently
utilized to estimate branch diameter. The estimated branch diameter was used to calculate the Limb Cross
Sectional Area (LCSA), which was then used to estimate optimal crop-load, as a larger LCSA indicates a
higher potential fruit-bearing capacity of the branch. With this approach, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
for branch diameter estimation was calculated to be 2.06 mm (relative RMSE 10.82%) and the same for crop-
load estimation (Number of fruits per branch) to be 3.93 (relative RMSE 22.25%). Our study demonstrated
a promising workflow with a high level of performance in identifying and sizing branches of apple trees in
a dynamic orchard environment and integrating farm management practices into automated decision-making
for optimizing crop-load in apple orchards.
1. Introduction
Specialty crops account for around 30%–40% of the total value of
the United States (U.S.) crop production (Fuchs et al., 2021). In the
U.S., more than 200 thousand seasonal workers are invited each year
from other countries to work in the tree fruit orchards, one of the most
important specialty crop industries. These workers are employed in
performing field operations such as tree pruning, flower thinning, green
fruit thinning, and harvesting. However, in recent decades, growers
have faced challenges in finding enough farm labor to complete field
operations in various specialty crops (Bogue, 2020). Recently, the farm
labor crisis has become worse because of the COVID-19 pandemic
causing a reduction in agricultural labor, which has resulted in an

Huang et al., 2020; Kondo et al., 1996; Verbiest et al., 2021), auto-

Despite the tremendous research and development efforts in re-
cent years, there have been no reported automated machines com-
mercially adopted so far for crop-load management operations such
as selective tree pruning and fruit thinning in the outdoor orchard
environment. Automating crop-load management in a complex and
unstructured/uncontrolled environment with unpredictable variabil-
ity in lighting, landscape, and atmospheric conditions, is particularly
challenging. This complexity is further compounded by the temporal
and spatial limitations inherent to physical robots necessitating the
development of precise perception and manipulation systems (Bechar
and Vigneault, 2016). Crop-load management in U.S. orchards follows
a specific set of operations, such as tree pruning, flower thinning, and
fruitlet thinning (Zhang et al., 2019). Nonetheless, developing a robust
and reliable automated decision system for robots to effectively manage
crop-load using these operations remains a significant challenge for
researchers and engineers.
Fruit tree pruning involves selectively cutting certain branches of
a tree following specific guidelines or strategies (developed by re-
searchers and experienced growers) that allow the fruits to grow uni-
formly at desired locations. Additionally, tree pruning ensures proper
penetration of sunlight and air circulation into the canopies and reg-
ulates enough fruiting sites to achieve better yield and crop quality.
The thinning operation, on the other hand, involves the removal of
a portion of flowers and/or immature fruits (fruitlets) to ensure the
desired quality (including size) of the remaining fruit. These crop-load
management operations in tree fruit production are a balancing act
between maximizing yield (crop-load) while optimizing fruit quality
and ensuring adequate return bloom (Robinson et al., 2014). Thus,
to effectively perform most of these crop-load management operations
using a robotic system, it is critical to estimate the target crop-load
in each of these branches, which is determined based on tree traits
such as trunk size, branch diameter, branch length, and branch spac-
ing. The branches support the growth of leaves, fruits, flowers, and
buds, whose geometric properties can provide insights about normal
growth, and fruiting and flowering information because they are the
substantial indicator of crop growth and yield (Nyambati and Kioko,
2018). Therefore, automated estimation of geometric parameters/traits
(e.g., diameter and length) of fruit tree branches could provide crucial
information to further advance robotic systems for various crop-load
management operations.
Over the past decade, various 3D reconstruction and branch recog-
nition techniques have been proposed and tested in commercial or-
chards. Karkee et al. (2014) used a time-of-flight-based 3D camera for
the 3D reconstruction of apple trees and showed how such a geomet-
rical representation could be useful in developing pruning rules. Tabb
and Medeiros (2017) used a robot vision system RoTSE (Robotic System
for Tree Shape Estimation) to reconstruct apple trees in the dormant
season using a shape-from-silhouette method and used the structure to
measure geometric attributes of apple trees such as branch structure,
diameters, lengths, and branch angles. Likewise, Borrenpohl and Karkee
(2023), Tong et al. (2022) and Zhang et al. (2017, 2020) are some
recent studies in detecting branches in trees using deep learning tech-
niques such as Regions-Convolutional Neural Network (R-CNN), Faster
R-CNN, Mask R-CNN, and Cascade Mask R-CNN respectively. Song
et al. (2021) have recently developed a handheld device for mea-
suring the diameter at breast height (DBH) of a tree using a digital
camera, laser ranging, and image recognition. The handheld device
was designed to perform instant, automated, and non-contact DBH
measurements, which achieved an RMSE of 6.36 mm and measurement
BIAS of −1.78mm. Likewise, Yuan et al. (2021) developed an Intelligent
Electronic Device (IED) to measure DBH and tree height through high-
precision hall angle sensors and a dual in-line package (DIP) sensor.
This study reported an estimation bias of 1.6 mm and RMSE of 4.6 mm
compared with the caliper reading. Similarly, to measure tree DBH
remotely, Fan et al. (2018) used RGB-D images collected using a
mobile phone and simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) and
reported an RMSE of 12.6 mm and a measurement bias of 3.3 mm.
Likewise, McGlade et al. (2020) recently used an RGB-D sensor (Kinect
V2, Microsoft, Redmond, WA) to estimate tree stem DBH in an outdoor
environment. This study recorded 51 individual urban trees from one
viewing angle at a distance of 1 m to 5 m using various Field of View
(FOV) settings on the depth sensor. A circle-fitting approach was then
implemented on resultant point clouds to estimate the DBH. The study
reported an RMSE of 3.53 cm (relative RMSE of 0.11) and was not
applicable to those trees with non-circular and irregular stems.
Despite all these studies, extracting accurate 3D attributes of tree
structures in unstructured and dynamic orchard environments remains
challenging due to numerous obstacles such as occlusions, lighting vari-
ations, background noise, and inherent limitations of sensors (Akbar
et al., 2016). Additionally, the existing studies on tree traits identifi-
cation (e.g. branch and trunk detection), have primarily concentrated
on detecting larger trunks and extracting tree diameter at breast height
(DBH), which did not provide any insights on how to use that detected
branch/trunk information for an automated crop-load management in
commercial agriculture. Although studies were reported on estimating
existing crop-load in apple trees using machine vision-based apple de-
tection and counting in growing and harvesting seasons (Aggelopoulou
et al., 2011; Gongal et al., 2016; Ji et al., 2012; Linker et al., 2012),
previous studies had overlooked the utilization of branch size informa-
tion for crop-load management in commercial orchards. Our research,
therefore, focused on incorporating the diameter of branches to esti-
mate the crop-load capacity of individual branches, enabling a targeted
assessment of each branch’s ability to bear fruit.
This study aims at developing a robot vision system capable of
estimating the target crop-load for individual branches in a commercial
apple orchard during the dormant season. This estimation will not only
provide insights into the potential fruit-bearing capacity of individual
branches but also assist in making informed automated pruning deci-
sions, as farmers typically prune commercial trees during this period.
By bridging the gap between theoretical understanding and practical
application, this research aims to contribute to the development of
efficient and accurate crop-load management strategies, enabling im-
proved decision-making in commercial apple orchards. The specific
objectives of this study are:
• To train an instance segmentation model for accurately segment-
ing trunks and branches in apple orchards.
• To estimate branch size/diameter using the segmentation results
and RGB-D sensor data and, estimate the crop-load capacity of
individual branches based on their diameters.
2. Materials and methods
This study broadly included four steps (Fig. 1); i) Acquire image
data; ii) Detect and segment the branches and trunks of apple trees
using a deep learning approach; iii) Extract the 3D point clouds from
the segmented branch mask and estimate the limb cross-section area
(LCSA); iv) Estimate the desired crop-load (number of fruits) on a
particular branch section by integrating a commercial management
approach.

2.2. Segmentation of tree trunks and branches using YOLOv8 model
YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a widely used object detection
framework that is known for its high accuracy and performance
(Redmon et al., 2016). YOLO was first introduced by Redmon et al.
(2016) in the paper "You Only Look Once: Unified, Real-Time Object
Detection", which is called a single-stage detector because it performs
all the necessary steps for object detection in a single pass (Jiang
et al., 2022). YOLO works by dividing an image into a grid of smaller
regions and then predicting a bounding box and class probabilities
for each object that is present in each region. YOLOv8 is the latest
version of this framework created in January, 2023 by Ultralytics
(Ultralytics, Maryland, USA). It also provides a segmentation model
that uses the CSPDarknet53 feature extractor as a backbone, followed
by a C2f module instead of the traditional YOLO neck architecture.
C2f is a faster and lighter implementation of Cross Stage Partial (CSP)
bottleneck. CSP bottleneck works by splitting the feature map into two
parts, processing one part through a series of convolutional layers, and
then concatenating both parts. The C2f module is faster and lighter
because it uses two convolutional layers instead of three, optimizing
feature map processing for better performance.
Fig. 3 shows the YOLOv8 architecture implemented in this study
for the segmentation of branches and trunks in apple tree images. The
backbone consists of layers P1 to P5, where each layer progressively
extracts more complex features from the input image. U (Upsample)
blocks increase the feature map’s spatial resolution, helping detect
objects of various sizes. In the head, layers P3, P4, and P5 combine
features from different backbone stages. C (Concatenate) blocks merge
feature maps from different layers, while Con (Convolution) layers re-
fine these features. The final detection head, labeled as Head, processes
these features to output detected objects’ bounding boxes, class labels,
and confidence scores.
YOLOv8 also introduces an anchor-less approach, eliminating the
need for predefined anchor boxes and simplifying the detection
pipeline. YOLOv8 also provides five scaled versions, ranging from
lightweight models for low-resource devices to high-performance mod-
els optimized for speed and accuracy: YOLOv8n-seg (nano), YOLOv8s-
seg (small), YOLOv8m-seg (medium), YOLOv8l-seg (large), and
YOLOv8x-seg (extra large).
In this study, we utilized all five scaled versions of the YOLOv8-
based deep learning architecture for trunk and branch segmentation.
The selection of the YOLOv8 model was based on its reputation as
the new state-of-the-art model, known for its combination of low
inference time and high accuracies on the COCO dataset (Reis et al.,
2023). By employing these various scaled versions of the YOLOv8
model, we aimed to determine the optimal model that achieves a
favorable trade-off between inference speed and accuracy. Extensive
training and debugging trials were conducted to carefully determine
the optimal model parameters, including the learning rate and batch

2.4. Branch diameter estimation
The point cloud of each segmented branch was processed using PCA,
which helped identify the axis or direction along which the branch
extends. Using this information, the diameter of the corresponding
branch was estimated in 3D space. To minimize the variation, a com-
mon practice in commercial orchards is to measure the diameter at
a region approximately 3 cm away from the trunk–branch boundary.
This approach is based on the understanding that taking measurements
less than 3 cm away from the trunk boundary yields more accurate
results for the associated branch. Using this guideline, branch diam-
eters (ground truth data) were collected using a digital caliper, as
demonstrated in Fig. 5.
Using the 3D point clouds and the branch orientation information, a
cross-sectional plane (a plane perpendicular to branch orientation) was
established approximately 3 cm away from the trunk–branch junction.
Once the cross-sectional plane was established, points where the branch
intersected with the plane were identified, as shown in Fig. 6. These
intersection points exhibited circular arc shapes for cylindrical branch
structures and were used to extract geometric properties (like diameter
and cross-sectional area) of the branches. The diameter of this circular
arc represented the extent of points along the direction of the plane. By
analyzing this sliced geometry, an approximation of branch diameter
was obtained which was used to calculate the cross-sectional area of
the branches.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Trunk and branch segmentation
Fig. 7 presents representative examples of the tree trunk and branch
detection and segmentation results achieved using the YOLOv8-based
model. These results demonstrated the effectiveness of the model in
accurately identifying and delineating the tree structures within the
images acquired in complex orchard settings. Table 2 lists the k-
fold validation results average and standard deviation of the preci-
sion, recall, F1-score, AP0.5 (average precision at IoU threshold of
0.5), and AP0.5:0.95 (average precision at IoU threshold from 0.5 to
0.95) achieved by the model in segmenting tree trunks and branches
respectively.
As can be seen from Table 2, YOLOv8x-seg demonstrated the high-
est precision (92.7%), recall (95.5%), F1-score (94.1%) and average
precision (AP0.5 achieving 96.9% and AP0.5:0.95 achieving 65.7%) in
segmenting trunks. For branch segmentation, YOLOv8m-seg achieved
the highest recall (72.4%), F1-score (74.3%) and average precision
(AP0.5 achieving 76.2%), while YOLOv8x-seg obtained the highest
precision (77.1%) and average precision (AP0.5:0.95 achieving 30.5%).
It was noted that all models performed relatively worse for branch
segmentation, which was expected due to the challenging nature of this
task with thinner structures and complex environment in the image
space. The outcomes deemed as suboptimal encompassed instances
where a model either failed to detect branches or exhibited issues
of over-masking or under-masking the branch regions. Comparing the
overall performance of the models, YOLOv8x-seg showed the high-
est precision (84.9%) and average precision (AP0.5:0.95 achieving
48.7%), while YOLOv8m-seg achieved the highest recall (83.6%), F1-
score (84.1%), and AP0.5 (86.4%). By averaging the accuracy metrics
across all categories, YOLOv8m-seg scored the highest overall with a
score of 77.3. This averaging of accuracy metrics takes into account
precision, recall, F1-score, and average precision across both trunk
and branch segmentation tasks, providing a comprehensive measure
of the model’s performance. These findings suggest that YOLOv8m-seg
exhibits strong performance in both trunk and branch segmentation

3.2. Branch diameter estimation
The approach of applying PCA on 3D point clouds of the segmented
branch mask to find its orientation, filtering a cross-sectional plane, and
finding its length in the normal axis proved to be an effective method to
estimate branch diameter in a natural orchard environment. Accuracy
assessment with 43 sample branches showed an RMSE of 2.06 mm
and a relRMSE of 10.82%. Furthermore, a correlation coefficient of
0.83 was achieved between the estimated and actual branch diameter,
which indicated a strong positive linear relationship between them.
In comparison, our methodology for estimating diameter demonstrated
improved performance, achieving 97.67% accuracy within a 5 mm tol-
erance, as opposed to the 89.4% accuracy reported by Chattopadhyay
et al. (2016) for the same 5 mm tolerance (see Fig. 11).

3.3. Optimal crop-load estimation
The estimated limb diameter was used to calculate limb cross-
sectional area (LCSA) using Eq. (4), which was then used to estimate
optimal crop-load using Eq. (3). Fig. 13 shows the estimated vs actual
target crop-load in 43 branch samples used in this study. The proposed
method achieved an RMSE of 3.93 apples and a relRMSE of 22.25%.
The results suggest that the machine vision system-based approach
is applicable in estimating optimal/target crop-load for individual apple
tree branches. However, it is noted that the estimated target crop-load
should be used as a guide, and farmers should also utilize their expertise
and experience to make appropriate management decisions. For exam-
ple, slightly more than the suggested target crop-load could be left in
early crop management operations such as branch pruning to account
for potential under-estimation by the proposed algorithm as well as
for adjusting the impact of inefficient pollination and natural thinning.
Downstream operations such as flower and green fruit thinning could
take care of the impact of over-estimation of the target crop-load with
the proposed system. It is also important to note that the ‘‘6 apples per
cm limb cross-section area’’ guideline is based on the studies conducted
in specific regions and varieties as discussed in Section 2.5. Growers
may need to adjust the ‘‘number of apples per unit limb area’’ based on
their specific farming operations and practices including the variety of
apples, the age, and the health of the tree.

3.4. General discussion
Estimating optimal crop-load at the individual branch level is cru-
cial for helping orchard managers optimize various canopy and crop-
load management operations and achieve a high yield of quality fruit.
Manual estimation methods are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and
prone to errors. The proposed machine vision system offers an efficient
and accurate alternative, automating the process and providing timely
information for orchard management. The approach, when commer-
cially adopted, is expected to reduce labor costs, improve productivity,
and advance agricultural technology, enhancing the profitability and
sustainability of commercial orchards.
Additionally, the machine vision system has the potential to revo-
lutionize pruning and green fruit thinning practices. It estimates the
number of fruit in each branch based on the corresponding branch
cross-sectional area, enabling selective pruning and thinning. This opti-
mization technique has the potential to help improve fruit size, reduce
biennial bearing, and increase overall orchard productivity. By mini-
mizing resource usage, such as water, fertilizer, and labor, the system
promotes resource efficiency and environmental sustainability. The
combination of machine vision-based crop-load estimation and auto-
mated pruning has the potential to transform tree fruit production
systems.
4. Conclusion
The primary objective of this study was to develop a machine
vision system that could estimate the target crop-load for individual
branches in a commercial apple orchard during the dormant season.
To achieve this objective, an instance segmentation model was trained
using the YOLOv8 architecture for trunk and branch segmentation.
Our finding revealed that the YOLOv8 m model was overall the most
accurate in branch and trunk segmentation. Our machine vision sys-
tem evaluated branch diameter estimation accuracy against 43 branch
samples, yielding an average accuracy of 97.67% on the tolerance of
5 mm, demonstrating that the system was accurate in the estimation
of branches. We also found that the accuracy of our branch diameter
estimation was dependent on how accurate the depth profile was and
how correct the image masks were. For crop-load estimation, we did a
careful review of existing literature and proposed a farm management
approach of ‘‘6 fruits per cm square LCSA’’ for crop-load estimation.

Our system using this approach of ‘‘6 fruits per cm square LCSA’’
and estimated diameter, was able to estimate the desired number of
fruits per branch with a relative RMSE of 22.25%. Thus, the proposed
machine vision system provides a promising solution for efficient and
accurate crop-load estimation in commercial orchards.
The accuracy of branch diameter estimation relies on the quality
of the segmentation output and the performance of the depth sensor.
Therefore, it would be beneficial, in the future, to focus on developing
deep learning models with larger datasets and explore various image
processing techniques. Additionally, improving the robustness of the
depth-sensing tool would enhance the overall system performance. Fur-
thermore, future research could investigate the feasibility of integrating
this machine vision system with automated pruning and green fruit
thinning systems to assist in the automated decision-making process for
orchard operations. Ultimately, applying this machine vision technol-
ogy in orchard operations has the potential to lead to more efficient
and sustainable orchard management practices.
