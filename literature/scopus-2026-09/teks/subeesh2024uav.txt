A B S T R A C T  
Orchard yield estimation enables a farmer to make informed decisions. The limitations of visual inspection-based 
yield estimation approaches can be effectively addressed by the intervention of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) 
and advanced image processing using deep learning algorithms. This study proposes a methodology combining 
deep learning-driven UAV imagery and an in-house web-based application, “DeepYield”; to measure yield in a 
citrus fruit orchard. The state-of-the-art deep learning object detection models SSD, Faster RCNN, YOLOv4, 
YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 were evaluated for detecting “harvest-ready” and “unripe” citrus fruits from the tree 
images. Fruit size estimation was carried out using traditional as well as deep learning-based image segmentation 
models. YOLOv7 outperformed other models with a mAP, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score of 86.48, 88.54, 83.66 
and 86.03%, respectively. The developed solution was integrated into a web-based application as ‘DeepYield’ to 
enhance users’ convenience and equip them with an automated yield estimation solution.   
1. Introduction 
In the present times, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are one of the 
most popular low altitude remote sensing platforms, which are being 
preferred over satellite imagery for multiple reasons [1–3]. In the past, 
satellite images have been used for making agricultural production 
predictions, but given the limitation of satellite imaginary for capturing 
predominantly a two dimensional frame; the application has been 
limited to field crops area estimation in Vietnam [4], Myanmar [5], 
Japan [6] and Ukraine [7]. Suitability of UAVs for precision agriculture 
(PA) applications is bolstered by the inherent capability of this tech­
nology to capture real-time spectral and spatial field information at 
relatively low-costs [8,9]. The aforesaid can be evinced from several 
crop information applications of UAV imagery for crop monitoring with 
respect to spectral, structural, thermal and textural data [10–14]; a UAV 
with a multispectral camera on board can be helpful for major 
agricultural applications [15]. Advances in data processing software and 
their adaptability with contemporary sensors and subsequent ease in 
uploading the same to UAVs have attracted the attention of PA scientists 
and researchers globally [16,17]. 
Fruit yield estimation in orchards involves various methods, count­
ing and weighing being the most widely used [18]. Counting involves 
manually calculating the number of fruits in a sample and extrapolating 
that count to estimate the total yield. Weight estimation, on the other 
hand, entails weighing a sample of fruits and extrapolating that weight 
to predict the total yield. While weight estimation can be more accurate, 
however, it requires a representative sample of fruits and a precise 
weighing scale. These destructive techniques are error prone, time 
consuming, unreliable, biased, ineffective and laborious with high initial 
investment. The cost of yield calculation by these methods is more as 
compared to the benefit derived from yield estimation. Modelling of 
crop morphological, physiological, environmental and historical

Advent of digital imaging has propagated a method of yield esti­
mation that involves capturing the image of the plant/tree canopy 
manually while being perched on a raised platform or using some hand 
held device to mount the camera. The handheld RGB sensor, in partic­
ular, is commonly used for non-destructive yield estimation and enables 
differentiation between different types of fruits [23]. RGB-D sensors 
provide both colour and depth information, enabling the accurate esti­
mation of fruit yield in vineyards and apple trees, as well as a potent tool 
for crop size measurement [24]. Multispectral, hyperspectral and ther­
mal imaging sensors exhibited satisfactory results, but they are suscep­
tible to changes in humidity, temperature, and wind; hence they are 
rarely employed for fruit detection [25].Also these sensors are more 
expensive than digital RGB colour cameras. 
Prior to the prevalence of vision based machine learning advances, 
fruit detection involved capturing images of orchards and identifying 
significant fruit features such as size, shape, color, and texture [26]. 
Traditional image processing techniques include image thresholding 
operations for segmentation and filters for noise removal. The detection 
accuracy of model was found to be between 80 and 89 % [27]. A 
combine colour and Gabor features based technique for green citrus fruit 
detection and classification showed an accuracy of 75.3 % [28]. How­
ever, Hough circle and shape features based technique yielded an ac­
curacy of 80.4 % by removing the false positives [29]. The complexity of 
real-world field conditions can have a significant impact on the accuracy 
of colour, shape, and texture features used in fruit detection and clas­
sification, making this technique less reliable for practical use. 
Recent research has extensively utilized deep learning (DL) models 
for object identification and localization. DL-based computer vision 
approaches are promising as they attempt to mimic human vision sys­
tems and can accurately estimate yields in real-time [30,31]. DL based 
techniques were used in real-time yield mapping in mango [32], apple 
[33], citrus [34], pomegranate [35] and kiwi [36]. DL based fruit 
detection achieved high correlation coefficients (R2) in mango (0.98), 
pomegranate (0.92), tomato (0.96), apple (0.98), and citrus (0.95) even 
under varying imaging conditions [35]. A convolutional neural network 
model was used to detect mature and immature strawberries in green­
houses, resulting in a precision of 88.03 % [37]. Yield estimation from 
citrus orchards was performed using a combination of UAVs and DL, 
which involved deploying a Faster-Region based Convolutional Neural 
Network (RCNN) model. This method resulted in an error of 6.59 %, 
compared to traditional counting [34]. An android application for kiwi 
fruit yield estimation was developed that utilized a single-shot multibox 
detector (SSD) and MobileNetV2 and InceptionV3 models. The appli­
cation achieved a true detection rate of 90.8 % [36]. An integrated 
system for citrus fruits was proposed that included counting, size esti­
mation, and branch detection. The system employed the Mask RCNN as 
the object detection model for recognizing citrus fruits with an average 
accuracy of 88.15 % [38]. The Mask RCNN, a CNN-based object detec­
tion algorithm, was employed to recognize tomatoes in greenhouse, 
which identified the objects in the images as well as the corresponding 
pixels [39]. Faster RCNN was employed to identify green citrus in or­
chard, and achieved an object detection accuracy of 85.49 % on the test 
data set [40]. An object detection accuracy of 95.5 % was achieved by 
combining color and thermal image information of green citrus fruits 
using the Faster RCNN algorithm [25]. 
The aforementioned studies demonstrated that due to the powerful 
feature extraction capability of deep convolution neural network 
(DCNN), fruit recognition studies have gradually shifted their focus to 
recognition methods based on deep learning [41]. Compared to mature 
fruits with color that contrast with the background of the fruit, green 
fruits are more difficult to identify in natural settings. The natural 
environment includes complex lighting, with varying light intensities 
and uneven illumination at various times of the day, which would make 
it difficult to acquire high-quality images. Citrus fruits are spread in a 
random manner on the tree and frequently overlap one another; as a 
result, they are susceptible to being obscured by background elements 
such as leaves and branches. These issues are motivation for the present 
study, which vouches to address the complex yield estimation problem 
and brings forth a novel systematic approach to tackle the cumbersome 
process of human-vision based yield estimation and attempts to over­
come the limitations of phenotyping based yield estimation techniques. 
The primary objective of the present study was to develop and evaluate 
state-of-the-art deep learning based object detection models on UAV 
captured citrus tree images for fruit detection. Following that, the fruit 
size calculation was carried out using traditional image processing and 
deep learning approach. Additionally, a novel intelligent-web based 
application was developed with best performing DL model to estimate 
yield of citrus fruit in a farmer’s orchard field.

2.2. Data pre-processing 
Some of the images of the citrus trees were removed, which were 
visually less appealing and were deemed unsuitable for further analysis. 
Deep learning models require a huge amount of data to learn the un­
derlying pattern in the images and make generalized decisions. Data 
augmentation is a strategy that significantly increases the diversity of 
the data available to train complex deep learning models without col­
lecting additional datasets [42]. Data augmentation techniques such as 
cropping, rotation and flipping were applied to diversify the dataset and 
capture data invariances (Fig. 2). Collected images were divided into 
training, validation and testing dataset in the ratio of 70:15:15. The 
images were resized to fit the model architecture. Image sizes for Faster 
RCNN, SSD, YOLOv4, YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 were 600 × 600, 512 × 512, 
416 × 416, 640 × 640 and 640 × 640 pixels, respectively. In the data 
annotation phase, each fruit was annotated into “harvest” and “unripe” 
classes, using the open source tool “LabelImg”. The bounding boxes 
were manually drawn around the objects indicating the location of the 
fruit. Further, data is exported for the training process. 
2.3. Deep learning models for object detection 
2.3.1. You only look once (YOLO) 
Over the past few decades, deep learning approaches to computer 
vision have achieved impressive success. YOLO model endeavors to 
detect and classify objects using a single neural network and displays 
annotated bounding boxes with confidence scores and probabilities of a 
class simultaneously [43]. The operating speed of YOLO is higher than 
other DL based object detection models like SSD, R-CNN. YOLO based 
models have an efficient frame rate and can detect objects in a single 
stage, all this with limited computational resources that are compatible 
with onboard real-time object detection systems. YOLO estimates 
several bounding boxes along with the probability of classes in each grid 
rectangle. With time YOLO has matured from through several versions, 
presently in vogue is YOLOv7 [44,45]. This upgraded version of YOLO 
could overcome the shortcomings of the previous versions of YOLO in 
terms of greater effectiveness and higher detecting accuracy [46]. 
The YOLO models YOLOv4, YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 models were 
evaluated on the citrus orchard dataset to compare the performances. 
Each annotation for the YOLO model was saved in YOLO format con­
sisting text file with the object class and the coordinates of the bounding 
box, and later exported to the training process. 
2.3.2. Single shot detector (SSD) 
The SSD is one of the simplest algorithms which can be directly used 
to detect objects. This method relies on a feed-forward convolutional 
network to create a fixed-size bounding box and provides scores for the 
occurrence of object class instances within these boxes. A non-maximum 
suppression step is then used to create the final detections [47]. SSD 
offers benefits over YOLO for it estimates bounding boxes from multiple 
layers and it is computationally more efficient than RCNN and is better 
suited for real-time applications [48]. The narrow layers of SSD do not 
make it incapable to extract sufficient information from small objects. 
SSD use six feature maps with dedicated shallow network for small 
objects and deeper networks for larger objects [49]. In SSD model, the 
Pascal VOC format is used for annotating the objects and each image is 
stored as an XML containing the bounding box coordinates. 
2.3.3. Faster RCNN 
The Faster RCNN algorithm is an improved version of RCNN and Fast 
RCNN. This algorithm employed two similar softmax classifiers to pro­
duce the bounding boxes and class scores after receiving an image and 
the associated region proposals as inputs [50]. Faster RCNN is a two 
stage method, in first stage, it uses Regional Proposal Network (RPN) to 
create bounding boxes, regions of interest (ROIs) and estimate object­
ness scores. While in second stage, algorithm refines location and per­
forms regional categorization on the basis of generated ROI. Faster 
RCNN produces a new network by merging Fast RCNN approach with 
RPN, thus having an improved end-to-end learning accuracy [51]. Faster 
RCNN comprises convolution layer, RPN, ROI pooling layer, classifica­
tion layer and regression layer. The convolution layer is a first layer 
which extracts features from input image, then RPN generates bounding 
boxes and ROI pooling layer mapping all proposals estimated from RPN 
into single feature. At last, classification and regression layer categorize 
the objects into different classes and regression layer adjusts the location 
of ROI to finally detect the objects [52]. In the present study, Faster 
RCNN was implemented using transfer learning approach and the 
annotation is performed in PASCAL-VOC format and bounding box co­
ordinates are saved as XML files. The pre-trained model Inception- 
Resnet-V2 as backbone CNN model with tensorflow object detection 
API was used for the implementation of Faster RCNN. The schematic of 
Faster RCNN, YOLO and SSD is shown in Fig. 3.

2.5. Fruit size estimation using image processing 
Estimation of fruit size was an essential requirement for successful 
yield prediction. Algorithm for the same was developed in Python 
environment using Open CV and associated libraries. Traditional image 
processing aspects were carried out with colour models which are less 
sensitive to varying illumination, i.e. HSV, YCrCb and CIELab [53]. 
Perusal of the grayscale images (Fig. 5) of citrus plant with HSV, YCrCb 
and CIELab reveal that Cr channel, carrying the yellow colour infor­
mation, could represent the best contrast between the foreground (citrus 
fruit) and background (leaves and branches). Further image processing 
was carried out with Cr for background removal, while Otsu method was 
used for automatic thresholding [54] followed by the noise removing 
morphological operations like, −
erosion and dilation. Intervention of 
Otsu method was required as it was observed that Cr channel was sen­
sitive to variations in illumination which was an obvious situation in 
orchards. The image segmentation performance of colour YCrCb model 
with Otsu threshold technique was expressed in terms of precision, recall 
and F1-score. Watershed algorithm was also employed in present study 
to overcome problem of overlapping fruits. The number of fruits present 
in the image was compared with the number of fruits predicted by the 
watershed algorithm (Fig. 6). However, this approach was not well 
suited for real-time application. 
A state of the art deep learning model YOLOv7 for semantic image 
segmentation was also employed in the present study to improve the 
accuracy of segmentation. The DL based image segmentation model 
predicts object labels, bounding box, and the classification of each pixel 
belonging to the object. The YOLOv7 for image segmentation was an­
notated and trained with pytorch framework. The pre-trained weight of 
coco dataset was used as initial weights for model training. 
To map the pixels to major axis length and to further relate it to the 
weight, images of seventy-five citrus fruit of different shape, size and 
colour were captured in the laboratory. During image capture, the fruit 
and camera were kept 2.5 m apart so that the pixel density of laboratory 
images remains comparable with images captured in the orchard by the 
UAV. The weight and major axis diameter of fruits were measured with 
help of digital weighing scale (Valma, India) and digital vernier caliper 
(Orion, India), respectively (Fig. 7). A linear relationship was found to 
exist between major diameter and weight of fruit in order to map the 
weight accurately from pixel values. Relationship between pixels and 
diameter was established by counting the total number of pixels on the 
major axis and mapping it against the actual major axis length of the 
citrus fruit. The same experiment was repeated in orchard conditions for 
mapping and calculation the fruit size.

accessibility of potential users for an enhanced convenience for esti­
mating fruit yield in a citrus orchard. The application is platform- 
independent, capable of running across various devices, and has the 
potential to provide service to users from any place which has internet 
connectivity. DeepYield boasts a centralized architecture, inbuilt secu­
rity and integration of deep learning solutions that ensure a high level of 
flexibility and seamless user experience. The optimal model derived 
from the experiments was used as the central pillar for decision-making

The user interface in the web application comprises visual and

HTML, CSS, Javascript, JQuery and Bootstrap framework. The entire 
application is built on the Django framework using Python. Django of­
fers features for handling powerful authentication, and URL routing in 
building scalable applications. The pages developed using Bootstrap’s 
grid system provided responsive layout for the application. All the pages 
were developed incorporating HTML components such as forms, but­
tons, paragraphs, headers and media elements, providing users with 
clean navigation and organized content presentation. Complemented by 
a well-crafted CSS file, the layout is made aesthetically pleasing with 
careful selection of fonts, colors and styling. The grid system utilizes CSS 
media queries for automatic adjustments of the layout with respect to 
the viewport width, allowing the contents to reflow and adapt accord­
ingly. This ensures the optimal readability and usability on devices of all 
sizes. The landing page of the application serves as the entry point for 
the users to understand the application’s features and capabilities. The 
main page of the application provides general information that gives 
instructions for users, upload functionality and help options. In the 
upload functionality, user is given the option to input the tree images. 
The upload form includes an input field of type ‘file’ for selecting the file 
to upload. When the user clicks the upload button, the files selected by 
the user are sent to the server as a POST request. The URL patterns are 
configured in Django’s urls.py file to map the incoming request to the 
corresponding view function. Further, the data are processed on the 
server side and Django view function is created to handle the incoming 
requests.

cludes the model architecture, parameters and associated metadata. In 
the server side, this file containing the learned parameters of the model 
was used for inference. Upon integrating it into the views.py file in the 
server code, the model could perform accurate detection of fruits from 
the uploaded images. The weight files of the trained and evaluated

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Performance of the models in fruit detection 
The key objective of identifying the best model for fruit detection 
was to enhance the overall performance of the web-based application for 
yield estimation. The state-of-the-art object detection models SSD,

The sole reason behind reduction in overall model performance was 
the challenge involved in accurately detecting the unripe fruits. Accu­
rate detection of harvest-ready fruits is relatively easy and less compli­
cated. Unripe fruits often exhibit same visual characteristics as that of 
the surrounding foliage and possess similar colour, texture or size as the 
leaves, often seamlessly blending with the background. YOLOv7 model 
was able to detect them fairly accurately as compared to the faster RCNN 
and SSD models. The model was able to detect the fruits from UAV 
captured images with an average response time of 15 ms when deployed 
in the server and integrated into the web application (Fig. 12). This 
feature of YOLOv7 can be attributed to the single-stage detection algo­
rithm which results in faster processing and improved results. Compared 
to other models, the key factors contributing to the improved perfor­
mance of YOLOv7 are the advancement and optimization in network 
architecture and training approach, leading to the right balance between 
accuracy and speed. The several modifications to the YOLOv7 network 
architecture includes the expanded efficient layer aggregation, model 
scaling techniques, and parameterization re-planning. The E-ELAN 
(Extended Efficient Layer Aggregation Network) module incorporated in 
YOLOv7 is based on the inspiration from previous sequences of studies 
on network efficiency. It facilitates more efficient learning compared to 
the other YOLO predecessors, all while preserving the original gradient 
route. YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 address few major limitations of their 
predecessor models but still face problems with accuracy, especially in 
scenarios with small fruits and densely clustered fruits. Faster R-CNN, 
despite its decent accuracy, has relatively slower performance as a result 
of its two-stage architecture consisting region proposal generation and 
fruit detection. The inclusion of region proposal is a significant bottle­
necks of the algorithm that reduces its performance. SSD, on the other 
hand compromises accuracy in favor of speed and may encounter dif­
ficulties in detecting small fruits and objects of varying scales. The ar­
chitecture heavily relies on the generation of bounding boxes and the 
extraction of feature maps. The supreme predictive power and faster 
processing of YOLOv7 have been the key criteria for integrating it into 
the backend of the web-based application developed in the present 
study. The results underscores the correctness of the approach employed 
in accurately locating individual fruits in a citrus tree from aerial images 
captured by a UAV for precise fruit counting and yield estimation 
thereof.

3.2. Fruit size estimation and yield calculation 
Along with the fruit count, the fruit size estimation can give the 
realistic estimate of the actual yield. Initially, the actual weight and 
diameter of the fruits were measured and recorded (Fig. 13). Linear 
regression fitted to the data for mapping the major axis length against 
the actual weight of the fruit exhibited a good correlation (R2 = 0.92). 
An efficient segmentation approach is the key for deriving the major axis 
length and thus, to obtain the weight of fruit close to the actual. Two 
different approaches viz., traditional image segmentation approach and 
deep learning based image segmentation, were evaluated in the present 
study. In the traditional image processing, the fruit segmentation was 
performed by integrating the YCrCb color model, Otsu based thresh­
olding and watershed algorithm (Fig. 14). It was observed that the 
desired accuracy was obtained only when the image possesses high 
contrast among harvest ready fruits, leaves and tree branches. In such 
high contrast cases, YrCrCb-Otsu-watershed approach delivered a good 
performance with an average precision, recall and F1-score of 88.21, 
86.75 and 87.47 %, respectively. However, in real-orchard conditions, it 
was evident that the conditions will not be conducive for this approach 
to give record worthy results. Such conditions would make segmentation 
complicated and the calculation of major axis tough, while watershed 
segmentation would be irrelevant as delineation of fruit boundary would 
be difficult. This would always lead to a misreporting of the number of 
fruits. Thus, the inherent limitations of the algorithm in terms hyper 
parameter tuning brings to the fore the limitations of traditional image 
processing approach renders it disqualified for integration into the web 
based yield estimation solution. 
YOLOv7 model was trained for 100 epochs with an image resolution 
640 × 640 with outstanding output segmentation. YOLOv7 was able to 
accurately segment both unripe and harvest ready fruits, which was 
otherwise not possible with traditional image processing approaches. It 
also demonstrated good performance under noisy backgrounds and 
complex environments. The model has shown excellent F1-confidence of 
85 % for both unripe and harvest-ready classes of fruits (Fig. 15). Thus 
the YOLOv7 based segmentation model was chosen for the fruit size

3.3. Validating object detection results in actual orchard conditions 
Considering the data driven yield estimation approach, it is well and 
true that even though the algorithm can detect most of the fruits present 
in the tree, it still has limitations in detecting the fruits occluded by 
foliage, leaves, or other fruits. For this reason, 25 randomly selected 
trees from the orchards were counted manually in two phases and the 
results were compared with the YOLOv7 based detection (Fig. 16a). 
In the first phase, only the harvest ready fruits were counted from the 
trees and compared with the detection results for harvest ready fruits by 
YOLOv7 (Table 2). The model had shown excellent detection capability 
for harvest ready fruits when compared with the manual counting. The 
model exhibited an error of approximately 6 ± 3 fruits. This inspection 
helped in navigating the scenarios of occlusion, complex overlapping 
fruits, etc. and provided an accurate counts or corrections that the model 
has failed to accommodate. For this reason, a simple linear model was 
fitted to map the actual harvest ready fruit count to the harvest ready

following equation:

3.4. DeepYield web based application 
The developed web based application seamlessly integrates the ob­
ject detection model, image segmentation approach and regression 
analysis into a single window solution where the user can interact 
directly. DeepYield has been developed in bootstrap grid design; it was 
fully responsive and provided a good user experience (Fig. 18). The 
application consists of two different user profiles, one for “admin” user 
and for “common” users. The admin user has been given the privilege of 
managing the users and the overall functionalities of the application. 
DeepYield was tested by uploading 2 to 20 tree images captured by a 
UAV for estimating yield of an orchard with 43 trees. The application 
exhibited higher error rate (>20 %) for lower number of image uploads 
and the error rate was found to be lower when more images were 
uploaded into the system (Table 4). While uploading more than 10 tree 
images, the yield estimation results were found to be promising. After 
certain stage, even after increasing the tree images, there were only 
minor improvements observed. This was due to the challenges involved 
in the fruit weight calculation using image based approach. Overall, it 
was observed that DeepYield performed best when the input data 
comprised UAV images of 20 % fruit bearing trees present in an orchard.

4. Conclusion 
Estimation of yield prior to harvest is of vital importance for the 
farmers, it helps in making assertive decisions for achieving impactful 
economic goals. This study was aimed at developing a web-based 
application for orchard yield estimation integrating UAV imagery and 
deep learning-based computer vision models. Key attainment achieved 
by this investigation can be enumerated as (i) Evaluation of state-of-the- 
art object detection models for fruit detection, (ii) Comparison of 
traditional image segmentation and deep learning-based image seg­
mentation for fruit size estimation using images captured by an UAV, 
and (iii) Integrating the different components to help farmers and other 
stakeholders with a web-based application for estimation of yield in 
citrus orchards. In a citrus orchard tree dataset, the YOLOv7 model 
could detect the harvest-ready and unripe fruits with an overall mAP of 
86.48 %. The size estimation of citrus fruits performed using deep 
learning based image segmentation model was far superior as compared 
to the traditional image processing approach carried out using Otsu and 
watershed algorithm. Further, the calculated size was mapped to the 
fruit weights for yield calculation. The overall yield estimates by the 
aforesaid approach demonstrated linearity (R2 = 0.98) with actual yield 
from the orchards. This web-based approach, “DeepYield” has the po­
tential to revolutionize orchard management activities leading to 
improved agricultural productivity and contributing to sustainable food 
production. In future work can be taken up to increase accuracy of 
DeepYield by working with optimizing the path of UAV in the orchard 
and exploring the possibilities of mounting image capturing device of 
varying field of vision.
