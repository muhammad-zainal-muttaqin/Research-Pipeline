A B S T R A C T
Automated vision-based detection and counting are critical for accurate tomato yield estimation, which 
contribute to precise yield management strategies and an efficient food supply chains. Special conditions, 
including background clutter, occlusion, and varying sunlight, affect the accuracy of crop detection and 
counting. To determine the most suitable algorithms for this yield estimation context, we herein establish a 
public multi-object tracking (MOT) dataset for tomato cluster counts, while evaluating and comparing state-of- 
the-art target detection and MOT-based algorithms. The evaluated detectors consist of YOLOv8 and RT-DETR, 
which represent algorithms that achieve a balance between accuracy and speed. The tracking algorithms 
included state-of-the-art methodologies such as SORT, DeepSort, ByteTrack, and BotSort. Initially, the perfor­
mance of the detectors was rigorously evaluated, followed by a comprehensive assessment of the four tracking 
algorithms within a multi-target tracking database tailored for this research and structured in the MOT context. 
The findings reveal that YOLOv8 and RT-DETR achieve 93.6% and 94.9% results at mAP@75, respectively, with 
RT-DETR exhibiting fewer false detections. When combined with the RT-DETR detector, the ByteTrack-based 
algorithm registers the highest counting accuracy at 95.5%, whereas BotSort achieves the highest MOTA score 
with 84.6%. Notably, the trackers without the ReID module (i.e., SORT and ByteTrack) demonstrate greater 
adaptability to frame rate variations in the test videos. At a 30-fps frame rate, the incorporation of ReID modules 
in DeepSort and BotSort algorithms significantly enhances the MOTA metric. Looking ahead, we plan to leverage 
these algorithms into an autonomous inspection platform that aims to estimate crop yield in real-time.
1. Introduction
With the development of modern facility agriculture, the greenhouse 
fruit and vegetable planting pattern has attracted much attention due to 
its stable and reliable yield and quality [20,21,24]. This planting pattern 
allows for close collaboration with the food supply chain, that is, 
tailoring the yield according to market demand. Significantly, accurate 
yield estimation constitutes a pivotal linkage in building an efficacious 
food supply chain. Tomatoes, a ubiquitously cultivated fruit and vege­
table, have gradually increased the level of greenhouse cultivation and 
area in recent years [26]. The manual counting of tomato quantities is 
laborious and time-consuming. In contrast, the use of computer vision 
technology to estimate tomato yield as an automated and efficient 
approach to reduce labor costs is currently the predominant methodol­
ogy for determining tomato population statistics [1,23].
Object detection technology is one of the most widely used vision 
technologies [16,34,37]. Research in this field has evolved from manual 
feature design to the use of deep learning-based enhancements that 
show superior performance. This approach is capable of precisely 
separating elements such as fruits and crops from the background and 
accomplishing the localization of each object, providing a reasonable 
basis for counting fruits [2,6]. Constantly evolving detectors have hel­
ped in the accurate detection of fruits and crops in unstructured agri­
cultural environments. Deep learning-based detectors have evolved 
from a two-stage process to a single-stage one. Single-stage detectors can 
be distinguished by the underlying deep learning architecture, such as 
those constructed on the basis of convolutional neural networks (CNN) 
and transformer architectures. it is noteworthy that single-stage de­
tectors based on the CNN architecture, as demonstrated by the YOLO 
series, have achieved trade-off between accuracy and efficiency

[8,9,22,29]. In contrast, detectors utilizing transformer architecture 
offer superior accuracy; however, their application in real-time detec­

studies have overcome the performance shortcomings of transformer 
architecture [15,18]. It is also of importance mentioning that due to the 
diversity of the growing environment and agronomic conditions of 
crops, object detection methods often should to be combined with other 
techniques. For instance, Zhang, et al.[36] employed the SIFT image-

outlines. Xiong et al. [32] adopted drone technology in conjunction with 
object detection algorithms to achieve accurate detection of the surface 
of young mangoes in tree canopies. The primary objective of current 
study is to conduct a thorough assessment of counting and estimation of 
tomato yield under ridge planting conditions. This is effectively per­
formed by acquiring a sequence of images (or videos) from each planting 
row, which presents complex technical challenges in cross-frame object 
detection and identification.
Several studies have applied video tracking methodologies to esti­
mate crop yield in agricultural scenarios and have achieved good results 
[2,7,19,25,31,33]. In the field of video tracking technology, multi-object 
tracking (MOT) is capable effectively dealing with the identification of 
targets that appear multiple times in video frames [11,17]. Among them, 
the tracking-by-detection strategy is an effective approach for MOT. The 
main part is to first locate the tomato clusters in the video frames 
through a target detector, and then use a tracker to assign the same 
identification number to the same targets in different frames. These 
identification numbers can be effectively utilized as the basis for fruit 
cluster counting. In such a framework, both the object detector and the 
tracker are key factors affecting the counting accuracy (X. [13,27]. The 
accuracy of the detector directly affects the performance of the tracker, 
which focuses on the data association of the target objects in the frames. 
The corresponding trackers based on the Kalman filter algorithm and the 
Hungarian matching algorithm are a class of promising algorithms 
suitable for crops and fruit counting. Some studies have compared the 
potential applications of these algorithms with other algorithms and 
highlighted their significant advantages [1,28,31,33]. However, some 
former investigations have mainly focused on improving the detector 
output results (P. [12,23,31]. The existing literature lacks an analysis of 
the challenges and limitations of applying various tracking algorithms in 
agricultural scenarios, and there is also a lack of a systematic evaluation 
of the performance and robustness of these algorithms. By this view, 
study also aims to compare the performance discrepancies of several 
advanced trackers using two representative state-of-the-art target de­
tectors for counting and estimating tomato yield. Simultaneously, we 
focus on the challenges fronting video-based tomato cluster counting, 
which two major ones of them are as: 
• Interference from the background row of tomato clusters. Detectors are 
able to detect as many fruit clusters as possible in the image but this 
approach does not achieve the goal of accurate counting. In esti­
mating yield performance, it is necessary to accurately count the 
number of fruit clusters in the current row without considering those 
in the background rows. Therefore, we only mark and train the fruit 
clusters located in the current row in the image, and this presents a 
significant challenge for the detector.
• Occlusion, appearance similarity, and sunlight interference. Some 
trackers measure whether the detected fruit clusters in the current 
frame and the previous frames are the same tomato by comparing 
their appearance. However, occlusion, similar appearance, and 
lighting discrepancies under various angles could cause certain in­
terferences in comparing of the appearance of tomatoes.
In addition to the above two main issues, there is also no public 
database to analyze the impact of advanced detectors on trackers and 
compare the performance of MOT-based algorithms in fruit bunch 
counting. On these bases, we originally contribute to the following 
issues: 
• Creating a public dataset for MOT of tomato clusters.1
• Evaluating the performance discrepancies of advanced detectors and 
trackers in agricultural environments.
• Examining the prospects of various MOT algorithms in the applica­
tion of tomato cluster counting.
The organization of this research work is mainly as follows: Section 2
introduces the utilized databases, advanced detectors, and tracking al­
gorithms; Section 3 presents the results and discussion obtained from the 
sensitivity analysis and application tasks; and finally, Section 4 presents 
the main results obtained from this investigation.

This study develops and compares the performance of advanced 
object detectors and multi-target trackers in a tomato cluster yield 
estimation system and presents its overall structure as illustrated in 
Fig. 1. The data were captured via a camera fixed on mobile platforms or 
unmanned vehicles. Subsequently, the data were divided into two main 
categories: images and videos, which were utilized to evaluate the 
performance of detectors and trackers, respectively. The process pre­
sented in the dashed black box has effectively evaluated the perfor­

detectors can be summarized in three main steps: feature extraction,

principle of the trackers is illustrated in Fig. 4, and a detailed description

2.1. Data preparation
2.1.1. Data acquisition
The experimental datasets (i.e., original images and original videos) 
were collected in Hongfu Agricultural Tomato Production Park, Daxing 
District, Beijing (39.603538◦N 116.283601◦E). The greenhouse adopts a 
high-yield, and high-input Dutch greenhouse planting pattern. The 
agronomic conditions are row-row planting with a spacing of 1600 mm 
between every two rows and approximately 10–12 kernels per tomato 
bunch. To maintain a fixed growth height of the tomato plants, the vines 
are artificially dropped at regular intervals. This pattern allows the to­
mato clusters in the color-turning and mature stages to be distributed on 
the main stems with a vertical height range of 1400 mm (see Fig. 2a,b). 
Some researchers have utilized Realsense or Azure Kinect cameras to 
collect data; however, the relatively small field of view of these cameras 
makes it difficult to fully cover the tomatoes at the color-turning and 
mature stages in narrow crop rows. Therefore, we employed the OAK-D- 
Pro-W camera [14] with a wide field of view (FOV) as a data collection 
device. The camera’s horizontal and vertical FOV of the camera are 95◦
and 70◦, respectively. After testing, the images obtained by the camera 
could completely cover the tomatoes in at the coloring and ripening 
stages in this scene, as shown in Fig. 2c. The camera was mounted on a 
platform vehicle moving along the track. The platform vehicle moves 
along the track at a speed of about 0.3 m/s. We collected 15 raw videos 
from 2023 to 2024 to test the performance of the tracker. For the de­
tector, we captured several sequential videos and extracted them at one 
every 15 frames to form a dataset for target detection. In this way, 1700

2.2. Cluster detection
After constructing the dataset used for object detection, we briefly 
describe the detector architectures used in this work for tomato bunch 
detection. The performance of deep learning-based approaches on 
detection tasks outperformed classical computer vision algorithms. 
Fig. 3 demonstrates a schematic of the main architectures of a CNN- 
based and transformer-based single-stage target detectors. In current 
study, we aim to compare the performance discrepancies between the 
two types of detectors used for tomato detection and their impact on the 
MOT-based algorithm for future practical applications.
2.2.1. YOLOv8 based on the CNN architecture
The YOLO algorithm family of algorithms is a representative single- 
stage object detection algorithm based on the CNN architecture [22]. 
Due to the excellent trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, it has 
been widely used in the industry since its introduction. After many it­
erations by the community, it is now broadly employed in various 
agricultural scenarios [12,27,36]. In the present work, YOLOv8 is uti­
lized as a representative of a single-stage detector to compare its per­
formance with other detection algorithms [10]. In the YOLOv8 
architecture, the features are first extracted by the backbone structured 
as a CNN model, then combined by the neck layer for different scales, 
and finally the results are output by the detection classification detector 
and regression detector headers, and then processed by the NMS to 
obtain the final results. Additionally, YOLOv8 further optimizes the loss 
function, enabling the model to converge quickly and achieve better 
performance metrics.
2.2.2. RT-DETR based on the transformer architecture
In recent years, the accuracy of the transformer architecture de­
tectors has exceeded that of CNN architecture; nevertheless, their 
memory consumption and slow operation remain a substantial draw­
back. Therefore, many efforts have been devoted to optimizing the 
transformer architecture, with RT-DETR [15] being a reprehensive 
example. RT-DETR first attempts to analyze various factors affecting the 
model’s inference speed, such as post-processing NMS modules. It ach­
ieves efficient inference by mixing CNN and transformer architectures 
for processing. Additionally, the post-processing of the NMS is elimi­
nated to achieve end-to-end detection results, which further reduces 
time loss. Its backbone also adopts the CNN model and employs an 
efficient hybrid coding approach to encode features from different 
layers. The decoding prediction head utilizes an IoU-aware query se­
lection technique, which can directly output results without the need for 
NMS, simply by filtering through a specific threshold.

2.3.1. Tracking via SORT
Simple Online Real-time Tracking (SORT) represents a real-time 
MOT algorithm [3], which is mainly constructed based on the algo­
rithm uses Kalman filtering to predict the state of the detected bounding 
boxes. The state prediction results are intersected with the newly 
detected results by intersection-over-union (IoU) prediction and the 
similarity calculation and the data association problem is resolved via 
the Hungarian algorithm. Additionally, if the IoU between the predicted 
box and the newly detected bounding boxes is too small, the minimum 
IoU is applied to reject the allocation. The SORT algorithm only takes 
into account the movement of the bounding boxes without dealing with 
the ID switch due to the change in appearance. The state prediction for 
each bounding can be expressed as follows: 
xt = [ut, vt, st, rt, Vut, Vvt, Vst]
(1) 
where utandvtrepresentthe coordinates of the center point of the bound­
ing box at moment t; st and rt in order denote the area and the aspect 
ratio, Vut and Vvt represent the velocities of the center point, and Vst 
signifies the surface velocity.
2.3.2. Tracking via DeepSort
DeepSort [30] emerged to address issues pertinent to occlusion that 
could not be considered by the SORT algorithm chiefly achieves tra­
jectory assignment only through IoU matching. In the face of occlusion, 
this could lead to frequent ID switches. Therefore, the DeepSort algo­
rithm performs deep association metrics on objects by incorporating an 
appearance feature extraction module on the basis of CNN networks. 
Additionally, it optimizes the matching strategy by considering both the 
motion information and the appearance features. This DeepSort strategy 
could deeply influence subsequent trackers. In this algorithm, the pre­
dicted motion states are optimized: 
xt =

ut, vt, γt, ht, Vut, Vvt, Vγt, Vht

where utandvt represent the coordinates the center of the bounding box 
at time t; γt is the aspect ratio; ht denotes the height; and Vut, Vvt, Vγt,
andVht signify the velocities of the remaining four states.
2.3.3. Tracking via ByteTrack
ByteTrack, introduced by Zhang, Sun, et al. [35], presents a distinct 
yet efficacious approach to data association, discriminating that from 
conventional methods like DeepSort. The tracker initiates the associa­
tion process by correlating high-confidence detection boxes with exist­
ing tracks. However, some tracks often fail to find matches, 
predominantly due to occlusions, motion blur, or significant size vari­
ations. To address these issues, the methodology subsequently associates 
lower-confidence detections with these unpaired tracks, thereby facili­
tating object retrieval in frames with lower scores. The underlying logic 
assumes that low-scoring bounding boxes frequently encapsulate in­
stances marred by substantial occlusions or motion blur, rendering 
appearance features unreliable. As a result, the algorithm adopts a dual- 
stage matching strategy, leveraging high-confidence detections for 
identification while avoiding reliance on potentially misleading low- 
confidence features. This selective approach not only enhances the 
robustness of target identification but also contributes to a reduction in 
computational overhead, thereby optimizing efficiency.
2.3.4. Tracking via BotSort
BotSort [38] is also a SORT-like multi-object-tracking algorithms, 
with key innovations that include the integration of the state-of-the-art 
FastID as the appearance feature extractor. Additionally, it features a 
camera motion compensation module to enhance tracking accuracy. 
This module reduces the Kalman filter state vector deviations arising 
from positional changes caused by the camera jitter. Similar to Byte­
Track, it uses a two-step matching process for both high and low con­
fidence detections. However, during the initial matching step, it 
integrates both IoU and Re-Identification (ReID) metrics to enhance data 
association robustness. Furthermore, in the BotSort state estimation 
matrix, the aspect ratio adjustment is reformulated to width. The spe­
cific formula of this algorithm reads: 
xt = [ut, vt, wt, ht, Vut, Vvt, Vwt, Vht]
(3) 
where: the width is wt. The velocities of the width is Vwt.
2.4. Counting method
Despite the continuous improvements in the performance of trackers, 
they still fail to meet the requirements of accurate tomato cluster 
counting. The main challenge lies in the frequent identity switches, 
leading to discontinuous tracked identifications of bunches. Utilizing the 
maximum ID value as the count would remarkably overestimates the 
actual number of bunches. As a result, we have adopted a region-based 
counting approach [23] to gauge the number of tomato clusters. This 
technique involves delineating a consistent region on each image frame. 
Each video frame displayed in Fig. 8 demonstrates a fixed mask region, 
which is designated as the fixed area. As the frames change, tomato 
bunches continuously enter this designated area. The coordinates of the 
upper left corner of the fruit cluster bounding boxes also enter this area 
accordingly. We determine whether each fruit cluster tracked in the 
current frame should be counted by checking whether the upper left 
coordinates of the fruit cluster are in the fixed area. Real-time counting 
is achieved by counting the number of tomato bunches IDs that have 
entered this fixed area. If a new cluster of tomato ID enters the area, one 
cluster should be added to the total number. In this way, the number of 
tomato cluster IDs that enter the region can be considered as the esti­
mated number of fruit clusters. This method is capable of effectively 
enhancing the counting accuracy.

3. Result and discussion
This section first presents a comparison of the performance of the 
two object detectors. Then, the performance of the trackers is compared 
on fifteen test videos. Finally, a discussion is given on the application of 
the tracking counting strategy in tomato counting.
3.1. Tomato detection results
We utilized the metrics as defined in Eq. (6) to compare the perfor­
mance difference between YOLOv8 and RT-DETR. With an IoU 
threshold of 0.5, both models achieve comparable performance see 
Table 2). However, RT-DETR outperforms YOLOv8 under more rigorous 
conditions. Fig. 5 illustrates the detection outcomes of each model when 
processing two frames from a test video. Specifically, Fig. 5a,b demon­

MaxID and estimated counts are observed for DeepSort and BotSort, 
highlighting the increased sensitivity of CNN-based appearance feature 
extraction to changes in frame rate. The visualization in Fig. 8 reinforces 
these findings, sorting frames by equivalent frame rate horizontally and 
tracker types vertically. By plotting the centers of tracked objects in 
multiple frames in a single snapshot, the continuity and trajectory 
consistency of the bounding box paths are visually assessed. At 30 fps, all 
trackers track every target well with coherent trajectories. When the 
frame rate is reduced to 15 fps, DeepSort misses a target, consistent with 
previous observations. At 10 fps, DeepSort’s performance worsens and 
BotSort also loses a target. The SORT-based algorithm continues to track 
all targets but suffers significantly from persistence, whereas ByteTrack 
outperforms with its consistent tracking capability. These visual ana­
lyses, taken together, confirm the quantitative assessments from Fig. 7, 
and highlight the diverse resilience of trackers to frame rate changes.
3.3. Counting results
The performance of the counting system was mainly affected by both 
the detector and tracker components. Compared to YOLOv8, RT-DETR 
exhibited fewer false detections, which was consistent with a negative 
impact on the tracking efficacy (see Fig. 5). Despite this, several 
appropriate experiments were conducted via YOLOv8 and RT-DETR 
models at a frame rate of 30 fps as detectors, each paired with four 
distinct trackers. The MOTA and Prel metrics for these pairings are pro­
vided in Table 3. The superior MOTA and Prel were consistently achieved 
based on the RT-DETR model, except for DeepSort, which demonstrated 
an increasing trend for Prel, although most likely due to an increased 
frequency of false detections by YOLOv8. This led to the production of 
more IDs and, consequently, a smaller final counting discrepancy when 
compared to DeepSort using RT-DETR. The trackers that have a ReID 
module, such as DeepSort and BotSort, ensure a higher MOTA score, 
indicating the potential of the ReID module to reduce ID switches. 
However, batches with analogous postures and ripening stages may 
confuse the appearance-based identification module and render it inef­
fective in certain scenarios. Conversely, trackers that rely solely on 
bounding box detection and motion prediction, which include SORT and 
ByteTrack (see Table A.1), can effectively distinguish bunches of various 
fruits regardless of appearance similarities due to the inability to overlap 
the locations of similar-looking tomato bunches in a single frame. 
Although BotSort, has a ReID module like DeepSort, the addition of a 
motion compensation feature in BotSort contributes to its superior 
identification accuracy and yields better counting results. The compar­
ative bar charts of the counting results based on various detector-tracker 
combinations are plotted in Fig. 9, which demonstrate that each pair 
with RT-DETR outperforms those utilizing YOLOv8. Notably, ByteTrack 
and BotSort demonstrated lower error rates. Given the previous sensi­
tivity analyses, we can rationally conclude that the combined utilization 
of RT-DETR and ByteTrack leads to the most accurate and robust 
counting algorithm.

3.4. Discussion
Unlike traditional agricultural settings, modern greenhouse farming 
standardizes various agronomic elements, such as uniform growing 
platforms and fixed vehicular pathways (see Fig. 2b). This reduces the 
complexity of deploying agricultural robots in the diverse and often less 
structured agricultural fields. For the specific task of counting tomatoes 
in the growing area, it is essential that the device utilized could capture a 
complete tomato object. Hence, an OAK camera with an extended FOV 
was implemented to collect visual data. The fixed position of the camera 
helps in the detector task to enhance accuracy in counting.
During the tomato detection process, bunch detection commonly 
occurs in cases of leaf culling, where complete occlusions are rare and 
most instances involve partial ones. The advanced detector is highly 
skilled at identifying these clusters. The main factors that interfere with 
the detector include variations in lighting and the presence of tomatoes 
in the background ridge. These situations could lead to false detection by 
the detector and have an adverse impact on the tracker. Compared with

YOLOv8, RT-DETR possesses the advantage of reducing false detection. 
Although RT-DETR-based model has made significant optimization be­
tween speed and accuracy compared with the original DETR, it still 
exhibits a disadvantage in speed when compared with YOLOv8; how­
ever it is sufficient for real-time fruit cluster detection.
When tracking tomato clusters, different trackers exhibit dissimilar 
performances. In addition to the detector, the tracker also possesses 
three key components (feature extraction, similarity calculation, and 
data association). In general, more complex trackers, apart from the 
above key components, may also add other useful modules for target 
tracking, such as a camera motion compensation module, which may 
achieve better performance. When tracking general targets, factors such 
as occlusion, appearance similarity, and camera movement should be 
considered. In the scenario involved in the current investigation, a fixed 
camera could reduce the misinterpretation by the detector and basically 
keep the camera stationary, except for jolts when the camera moving. 
The standardized track design could enable the platform vehicle to 
achieve more stable driving speeds. These factors help to improve the 
tracking effects. However, the movement speed of the platform carrying 
the camera remains a certain influencing factor that cannot be ignored. 
In practical applications, the platform may experience deceleration due 
to different situations, which could lead to inconsistent image quality. 
These conditions not only affect the performance of the detector but also 
the performance of the tracking algorithms. For detectors, differences in 
image quality directly lead to unstable detection results, such as false 
positives and false negatives. Object detectors could mitigate the nega­
tive effects of speed variations to some extent through various data 
augmentation techniques. As for trackers, the algorithms we compared 
mainly rely on the Kalman filtering to predict the position of the 
bounding boxes. Therefore, excessively fast or slow speeds will cause 
fluctuations in the prediction of bounding box positions, which, in turn, 
affects the data association phase. The results listed in Table A.1 reflect 
the tracking results in the presence of various frame rates and indirectly 
exhibit the impact of speed variations on the tracking performance, with 
noticeable discrepancies in robustness observed among various trackers. 
Overly complex component designs, although increasing the value of the 
MOTA metric, have less impact on the counting results. The frame rate 
fluctuations leading to worse tracking outcomes are similar to the ob­
servations of Villacr´es et al. [28]. However, contrary to their opinion, 
the decline is more pronounced in ReID-based trackers. The benefits of 
the ReID and camera movement compensation modules are limited. 
Moreover, in the case of frame rate fluctuations, the ReID module 
actually incorporates an increase of more ID conversions, which is not 
beneficial for effective deployment in real-world scenarios. This may be 
due to the platform moving along a straight path, where overly complex 
gain modules cannot adapt to simple linear movements, and overly 
similar appearances of the fruit bunches might disturb data association 
through the ReID module, resulting in more ID switches. Although 
BotSort achieved the best MOTA metric, its robustness is weaker than 
SORT and ByteTrack. Considering the speed of the algorithm, SORT and 
ByteTrack also better meet the needs of real-time performance 
requirements.
As a result, the ByteTrack tracker, integrated with the RT-DETR 
detector and without the ReID module, achieves the best counting ef­
fect and the most robust tracking results. In future research, we plan to 
further integrate the previously maturity estimation methods for fruit 
clusters into the counting algorithm. Our goal is to deploy the algorithms 
on a standalone mobile inspection platform and transmit the data to the 
control center via the network, realizing real-time counting and matu­
rity estimation, which will help to quantitative produce of tomatoes.
4. Conclusions
To conduct a comprehensive assessment of the performance and 
application prospects of based on the tracking by detection approaches 
applied to the yield estimation for greenhouse tomato clusters, this study 
evaluated a various of methods based on video data. To evaluate the 
performance, we constructed a public large-scale MOT dataset on to­
mato bunches. To this end, we evaluated several metrics for both de­
tectors 
and 
trackers, 
specifically 
comparing 
two 
real-time 
detectors—YOLOv8 and RT-DETR—and four trackers—SORT, Deep­
Sort, ByteTrack, and BotSort. Under various video frame sampling fre­
quencies, SORT and ByteTrack demonstrate better robustness. While 
ReID-based trackers such as DeepSort and BotSort exhibit superior 
performance under constant sampling. Using RT-DETR as the detector, 
BotSort produces the highest MOTA of 84.6 %, and ByteTrack ensures 
the highest counting accuracy at 95.5 %. In addition, sensibility analysis 
to frame rate fluctuations reveals that based on non-ReID trackers (SORT 
and ByteTrack) outperform their counterparts in tracking stability. In 
near future, the proposed algorithms have strong potentials to be 
deployed for in autonomous inspection platforms to facilitate real-time 
counting and aid in managerial decisions for tomato production.
