1  Introduction
Machine vision is widely used to support precision crop production, offering automated 
solutions for tasks traditionally performed manually. In the context of tomato cultiva­
tion, machine vision provides opportunities to automate processes, delivering accurate 
and efficient solutions for agricultural activities. While adopting such technologies is still 
in its early stages, machine vision combined with machine learning algorithms allows for 
the rapid and accurate analysis of large volumes of data, even big data. This enables the 
monitoring of vast areas and the implementation of precision interventions.

Abstract
In recent years, machine vision, deep learning, and artificial intelligence have 
garnered significant research interest in precision agriculture. This article aims 
to provide a comprehensive review of the latest advancements in machine 
vision application in tomato cultivation. This study explores integrating cognitive 
technologies in agriculture, particularly in tomato production. The review covers 
various studies on tomatoes and machine vision that support tomato harvesting, 
such as classification, fruit counting, and yield estimation. It addresses plant health 
monitoring approaches, including detecting weeds, pests, leaf diseases, and fruit 
disorders. The paper also examines the latest research efforts in vehicle navigation 
systems and tomato-harvesting robots. The primary objective of this article was to 
present a thorough analysis of the image processing algorithms utilized in research 
over the past two years, along with their outcomes.

Nowadays, there are three main approaches to CV in agriculture, each working differ­
ently and evolving over time. (Outside of agriculture, other approaches like Generative 
Adversarial Networks (GAN), Graph Neural Networks (GNN), and Auto encoders are 
part of the CV ecosystems but these are not yet relevant to agriculture.)
The evolution of CV techniques can be categorized into three main approaches: tra­
ditional computer vision, Convolution Neural Networks (CNN)-based methods, and 
transformer-based methods, often complemented by hybrid approaches. Below, these 
approaches are compared in the context of their application to tomato cultivation.
1.1  Traditional CV-based methods
Traditional CV techniques rely on handcrafted features and rule-based methods to pro­
cess images. These methods often involve:
 	•
Image-to-image methods: Techniques such as edge detection, color thresholding, 
and morphological operations are used to identify tomato fruits, leaves, or diseases. 
For example, detecting red regions in Red Green Blue (RGB) images can help locate 
ripe tomatoes. While computationally inexpensive, these methods lack robustness 
under varying lighting or background conditions.
 	•
Image-to-vector methods: Feature extraction techniques like Histogram of Oriented 
Gradients (HOG) or Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) convert image 
data into feature vectors. These vectors are then classified using machine learning 
algorithms, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), to differentiate healthy leaves 
from diseased ones. However, these methods require extensive feature engineering 
and often struggle with complex patterns.

 	•
Machine learning classification: Combining extracted features with traditional 
classifiers like k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), Random Forest (RF), or SVM enables 
basic classification tasks. Although useful for simple problems, these models are 
limited by their reliance on predefined features and lack the adaptability of deep 
learning.
1.2  CNN based methods
CNNs have revolutionized CVs by automatically learning features from raw image data, 
making them highly effective for complex tasks in tomato cultivation. CNN is the first 
true artificial intelligence method in the field of CV. Some subcategories include:
 	•
Two-Stage Detectors: Methods like Faster Region-Based CNN (Faster R-CNN) 
propose Regions of Interest (ROI) and then classify them. These detectors are 
accurate but computationally intensive, making them suitable for offline tasks like 
disease diagnosis from high-resolution images.
 	•
One-Stage Detectors: Models such as Single Shot Detector (SSD) or YOLO detect 
and classify objects in a single step, offering faster performance. These methods are 
widely used for real-time applications like identifying ripe tomatoes during robotic 
harvesting.
 	•
YOLO Family: The YOLO series (e.g., YOLOv5, YOLOv8) is particularly popular in 
tomato research due to its balance of speed and accuracy. YOLO models can detect 
multiple objects simultaneously, such as fruits and pests, making them ideal for tasks 
requiring localization and classification.
1.3  Transformer-based CV methods
Initially designed for natural language processing, transformer architectures are increas­
ingly applied to CV tasks as methods of artificial intelligence. They offer advantages in 
modeling long-range dependencies and handling multimodal data.
 	•
Vision Transformers (ViT): ViTs divide images into patches and process them using 
self-attention mechanisms. They are effective for classification and segmentation 
tasks, such as distinguishing between healthy and diseased tomato leaves. ViTs 
require large datasets and are computationally demanding, but they outperform 
CNNs in scenarios with high data variability.
 	•
Multimodal transformers: These models enhance performance by integrating 
multiple data sources, such as RGB and depth images. Combining visual and spectral 
data can improve the accuracy of fruit maturity estimation or disease detection in 
tomato plants. In rare cases, they also combine image and text descriptions.
1.4  Hybrid approaches
Hybrid methods combine traditional CVs, CNNs, and transformers to leverage their 
respective strengths. For instance, a pipeline might use traditional image processing 
for initial segmentation, a CNN for object detection, and a transformer for contextual 
understanding. Such approaches are particularly effective for complex tasks like 3D 
modeling of tomato plants, where data from multiple modalities and viewpoints must be 
integrated.

Each CV approach offers unique advantages and limitations in tomato cultivation. 
Traditional methods provide simplicity and speed but lack adaptability. CNNs deliver 
high accuracy and versatility, while transformers excel in handling complex and multi­
modal data. Hybrid approaches offer a promising direction, combining the strengths of 
multiple techniques to address the diverse challenges of modern tomato farming.
2  Methods
A comprehensive review of the latest research related to CV application in tomato culti­
vation was performed to write this study.
2.1  Criteria of search and paper selection
The data collection process involved accessing the Elsevier and Springer databases and 
filtering the search results based on the following criteria:
 	•
Date range: Publications were restricted to 2023 and 2024 to ensure the inclusion 
of the most recent advancements in the field. This research was initiated in January 
2025. Given the rapid evolution of CV and machine learning, selecting only the latest 
studies allows for focusing on the most up-to-date methodologies, technologies, and 
findings. Moreover, recent research reflects emerging challenges, novel solutions, 
and cutting-edge innovations, making it more relevant to contemporary scientific 
discourse. Older methods may be outdated or less effective in light of recent 
progress, further justifying the emphasis on the most current literature.
 	•
Keywords: The search utilized the keywords"Tomato"and"Computer Vision"to target 
studies specifically focused on tomato-related applications within the field of CV.
 	•
Publisher selection: The review focused exclusively on publications from Elsevier 
and Springer, two of the most prestigious academic publishers known for their high-
impact journals. Their publications ensure high-quality, credible, and scientifically 
sound research, making them reliable sources for this study. Furthermore, both 
publishers have a strong presence in computer vision, artificial intelligence, and 
agricultural technology, hosting leading journals that frequently feature cutting-edge 
research in these domains.
This search yielded 555 publications. These studies were analyzed to identify key trends, 
methodologies, and advancements in applying CV techniques to tomato cultivation, 
covering areas such as yield estimation, disease detection, quality grading, and robotic 
harvesting.
The selected articles represent various approaches, including traditional image pro­
cessing techniques, Deep Learning (DL) models like CNNs, and newer transformer-
based methods like ViTs. Additional emphasis was placed on hybrid approaches and 
their integration into practical agricultural applications. While other potentially valu­
able computer vision methods may exist, those that have not been applied specifically 
to tomato-related studies were not included in our analysis. Some of these methods are 
briefly referenced in the future directions section. This systematic review aims to provide 
a clear understanding of the current state-of-the-art in tomato-focused CV research.

2.2  Article processing and review methodology
A custom auxiliary application was developed to manage and analyze the collected lit­
erature efficiently. This tool allowed us to systematically process all 555 selected articles 
by loading key metadata and access links into the articles. Each article was individually 
opened and reviewed, during which potential analytical categories were identified, and 
relevant tags were assigned based on the content. If an article discussed specific topics, 
methodologies, or algorithms, these were marked accordingly on its corresponding data 
sheet.
By the end of the review process, these tags enabled efficient filtering and categori­
zation of the articles. As part of the review, key abbreviations, terminology, algorithm 
names, notable approaches, and innovative ideas were documented directly on each arti­
cle's datasheet. This structured approach facilitated a more comprehensive and system­
atic analysis of the literature. A user interface of the application is illustrated in Fig. 1.
The review process followed a structured methodology. All articles published in 2023 
and 2024 containing the keywords ‘computer vision’ and ‘tomato’ were collected from 
Elsevier and Springer databases. The literature collection was conducted in January 
2025. Each retrieved article was recorded in an Excel sheet, including the title and DOI. 
This dataset was then imported into a custom-developed article management system, 
which assigned a unique internal ID to each entry.
The user interface displays the list of articles on the left panel. Upon selecting an 
article, only the basic metadata—title, DOI, and ID—are initially shown. The user then 
opens the full text in a browser and begins annotating it within the system.
Articles are categorized using a hierarchical tagging framework. The right panel dis­
plays predefined main categories such as Approach, Backbone, and Dataset. Each main 
category contains specific tags (e.g., under Approach: Traditional, CNN-based, ViT-
based, etc.). When reading a new article, the system displays previously used categories 
and tags, allowing users to annotate the new entry by simply clicking on relevant tags. If 
a new concept is encountered, a corresponding tag can be added, incrementally expand­
ing the classification system.
Articles can later be filtered by tags—either to show those that include a specific tag 
or those that do not—enabling query and comparison capabilities across the dataset.

2.3  Framework for comparative analysis
After filtering out articles that were not directly relevant, an in-depth analysis of the 
assigned tags and their corresponding categories was conducted. Several categories were 
either non-relevant or lacked meaningful tags for further examination. However, five key 
categories, or analytical dimensions, were identified that provided a valuable framework 
for evaluating the methodologies in the selected articles. These dimensions were the 
foundation for structuring the study, ensuring a systematic and focused comparison of 
different approaches. The following section provides a brief introduction to each of these 
dimensions.
The 163 studies were examined across 5 dimensions:
 	•
Plant Parts: This dimension explores which part of the tomato plant was examined in 
each study and the reasons behind focusing on that specific part. Commonly studied 
parts include leaves (often for disease detection), fruits (for counting or ripeness 
analysis), and flowers. Understanding this focus helps identify gaps and areas of 
emphasis in tomato research.
 	•
Type of Dataset: Image processing relies heavily on datasets, which are collections of 
images used as input. Researchers either create their own datasets—a highly valuable 
but resource-intensive process—or use pre-existing datasets such as Plant Village or 
images collected from the Internet. This review analyzes what datasets were used, 
whether good tomato-specific datasets are available online, and the challenges of 
dataset quality and accessibility.
 	•
Image Type: The image data used in studies varies. While most research utilizes 
standard RGB images, others employ specialized types such as depth, stereo, infrared 
(IR), near-infrared (NIR), or hyperspectral images. This review explores the use of 
these image types and their impact on research outcomes.
 	•
CV Problem Type: Studies were categorized based on the type of CV problem 
addressed. Some focused on classification tasks, others on detection problems, and 
some required instance or semantic segmentation. Each problem type presents 
unique challenges and opportunities, particularly in agricultural applications.
 	•
CV Approach Type: From a technical perspective, this review evaluated the 
approaches used in the studies. These include traditional image processing 
techniques, CNNs, ViTs, or hybrid models combining multiple approaches.

3.1  Comparison according to plant parts direction
One of the trends of interest was identifying which part or parts of the tomato plant 
were the focus of these studies. Most studies concentrated on a single part of the plant, 
such as flowers, seeds, leaf diseases, tomato counting, or fruit ripeness. However, some 
studies had a multi-target approach, simultaneously examining multiple parts of the 
tomato plant.
As expected, many studies focused on leaf diseases and tomato fruits. Surprisingly, 
only 2 studies attempted to analyze diseases affecting the tomato fruit. Additionally, 
only 2 of the studies addressed capturing insects that could cause significant damage in 
a tomato field, which was unexpected given their potential impact on crop health and 
yield.
One of the insect-focused studies [166] focused on developing a robust automated sys­
tem for identifying plant pests. The research utilized 334,000 images covering 78 com­
binations of four plant parts (leaf front, leaf back, fruit, and flower) from cucumbers, 
tomatoes, strawberries, and eggplants. The system successfully identified 20 pest spe­
cies across 27 farms, achieving an average accuracy of 91.0% and a macro F1 score of 
88.5%. The findings underscore the significance of using diverse test data, pre-extracting 
ROIs to enhance accuracy, and including closely related species to improve pest identi­
fication. The study assessed the model's performance under varying conditions, reveal­
ing key challenges related to farm-to-farm variability. In the same-farm scenario, the 
model achieved a mean accuracy of 95.2% and a macro F1 score of 90.4%. However, 
performance dropped sharply in the different-farm scenario, with accuracy and macro 
F1 scores falling to 36.5% and 34.1%, respectively. Notably, F1 scores for all 13 classes 
declined significantly in the different-farm scenario, highlighting the model's struggle to 
generalize across farms. These findings emphasize the need for more robust solutions to 
address farm variability.
The second insect-focused article (L. [142, 143]) developed a novel CNN, Semantic 
Segmentation Network (SSNet), which combined SqueezeNet and Squeeze-and-Exci­
tation Network (SENet) to diagnose tomato pests and diseases effectively. The model 
achieves impressive accuracies of 98.80% for pest detection and 98.39% for disease 
diagnosis with just 0.398 million parameters. The study highlights the influence of data­
set balance, data volume, and hyperparameters on performance through comparative 
experiments. SSNet offers a lightweight yet highly accurate insect detection and plant 
health monitoring solution.

3.2  Comparison according to dataset direction
In computer vision, the choice of dataset plays a crucial role in determining a system's 
overall performance and reliability. In the study conducted by Mashamba et al. [95], the 
researchers utilized the Plant Village dataset, selecting a subset of images for model vali­
dation. The model achieved a validation accuracy of 95.08%. To further assess its robust­
ness, additional testing was conducted using field-acquired data, where the performance 
declined significantly, yielding testing accuracies ranging from 64.34% to 69.61%. This 
discrepancy highlighted a key limitation, as the model exhibited reduced effectiveness 
when applied to real-world field conditions compared to online dataset images.

The most interesting and valuable studies utilized datasets that were either self-collected

The dataset includes images of individual labeled leaves against simple backgrounds, 
making it ideal for algorithm development. The primary goal of these studies was to

4.1  Comparison according to image sensor and camera viewpoint
4.1.1  Sensor type
It is also important to consider the types of images or cameras researchers use in their 
studies. There are several options available, each suited to specific applications:
 	•
RGB Images: The most commonly used type. RGB images utilize the Red, Green, 
and Blue spectra. Most standard cameras operate this way, making RGB the default 
choice for many studies.
 	•
NIR Images: These images are particularly useful for detecting plant health and stress 
levels, as NIR light penetrates plant tissues and provides invisible insights in the RGB 
spectrum.
 	•
Hyperspectral Images: Captures using specialized cameras; hyperspectral images 
measure data across multiple narrow spectral bands. This allows researchers to assess 
plant physiology and nutrition status and even detect subtle changes in vegetation.
 	•
RGB-D (Depth) Images: These include an additional depth channel, enabling 
researchers to create 3D reconstructions of the scene. Depth data can also enhance 
applications like fruit size estimation and object segmentation.
 	•
Binocular Stereo Images: These images, captured from two slightly different angles, 
are also used for 3D reconstruction. They help estimate distances and volumes, 
making them valuable for applications like robotic harvesting.
 	•
Ultraviolet (UV) Images: Using the ultraviolet spectrum, these images are ideal 
for detecting specific properties such as surface texture changes or early disease 
symptoms that may not be visible in other spectra.

reflect real-world agricultural environments, where leaves appear in complex, cluttered

Various task types exist in the field of CV, including classification, object detection, seg­

each task type requires a unique form of data annotation, specialized loss function, and 
distinct evaluation metrics. Consequently, these tasks are treated separately to ensure 
methodological clarity and rigor.
Collectively, these CV tasks facilitate the development of advanced tools and systems

in the visual data to differentiate between various diseases. Classification is essential for

4.2.2  Object detection (classification + localization)
Object detection extends classification by identifying what objects are present and 
where they are located within an image, typically through bounding boxes. In tomato 
research, object detection can be applied to identify and locate pests, diseases, or fruits 
on a plant. For instance, a model could detect tomato fruits and determine their matu­
rity levels based on color or detect pest species and identify their location for targeted 
pest management. Combining classification and localization is crucial for precision agri­
culture tasks that require spatial awareness.
A study by [161–163] utilized an enhanced YOLOv8s network, called NVW-YOLOv8s, 
for the simultaneous detection of tomato fruits. The N comes from the Normalization-
based Attention Module (NAM), the V comes from Variable Focal Loss (VFL), and the 
W comes from Wise-IoU (WIoU). Tests conducted on an expanded and balanced data­
set showed that the proposed NVW-YOLOv8s network achieved a detection mAP_0.5 
of 91.4% and an F1 score of 85.4%. The simultaneous detection and segmentation pro­
cess also operated at a speed of 60.2 Frames per Second (FPS). The proposed method 
successfully met both accuracy and real-time performance requirements.
This study [175, 179] addressed multiple tasks, including detecting occluded and non-
occluded tomatoes and classifying tomato ripeness. A pose classification algorithm 
based on an enhanced YOLOv5 was applied after detection. The primary goal was to uti­
lize this information for a robotic arm. Experimental results showed that the detection 
accuracy for non-occluded tomatoes reached 82.4%, with a recall of 90.9%. The average 
accuracy for ripeness classification was 96.9%, the average accuracy for 3D pose classifi­
cation was 89.1%, and the image processing speed reached 20 FPS.
4.2.3  Semantic segmentation
Semantic segmentation assigns a class label to each pixel in an image, resulting in a pixel-
wise classification map. In tomato-related applications, this can be used to segment and 
differentiate between various plant parts (e.g., leaves, stems, fruits) or to identify dis­
eased versus healthy areas on a leaf. This method provides detailed spatial information, 
allowing researchers and farmers to understand the extent and distribution of diseases 
or to measure canopy cover, which is essential for growth monitoring.
However, Lin et al. (2023) [85] built on advancements in soft robotics and proposed a 
soft gripper capable of simultaneously classifying fruit firmness through flexible defor­
mation and tactile sensing. Using tomatoes and nectarines as test subjects, the U-Net 
semantic segmentation model was applied with image masking to extract the contours 

Page 25 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
of the soft gripper’s fingers from the images. The robot’s fingers and the tomato plant 
required precise semantic segmentation in this approach.
Kaur et al. [67] focused on the semantic segmentation of tomato leaves with multiple 
diseases. Researchers proposed a Hybrid-DSCNN model to segment diseased regions on 
tomato plants. The Hybrid-DSCNN combined U-Net and Segmentation Network (Seg-
Net) pre-trained models, incorporating segmentation for improved object recognition. 
The proposed model achieved an accuracy of 98.24%, outperforming other modified 
segmentation models.
4.2.4  Instance segmentation
Instance segmentation takes semantic segmentation a step further by identifying indi­
vidual instances of objects within an image. For example, it can distinguish between 
multiple tomato fruits in a single image and assign each fruit a unique label. This tech­
nique is valuable for applications like fruit counting, yield estimation, or assessing the 
quality of individual tomatoes. It is especially useful when overlapping objects must be 
separated for accurate analysis.
Qi et al. [116] proposed a specialized method for measuring tomato stem diameter. 
The approach involved instance segmenting the main stem and utilizing depth images in 
the segmented area to calculate the diameter with high accuracy. YOLOv8 for Segmen­
tation (YOLOv8-seg) and RGB-D data were employed for this task. The results demon­
strated a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 1.5 mm and a Mean Absolute Percentage 
Error (MAPE) of 12.37% compared to manual measurements, showcasing the method's 
precision.
On the other hand, [77, 78, 82] proposed a Multitask-Aware Network YOLACT 
(MTA-YOLACT) capable of simultaneously detecting and instance-segmenting regions 
of tomato clusters, stems, and main stems. The model demonstrated remarkable perfor­
mance, achieving an F1 score of 95.4% for tomato cluster detection and mAP50 of 80.2% 
for stem and main stem segmentation. Additionally, it grouped fruit clusters, stems, and 
main stems originating from the same target cluster. This method achieved real-time 
detection and instance segmentation at a speed of 13.3 FPS.
4.2.5  Tracking
Tracking involves following objects across a sequence of frames in a video. In tomato-
related research, tracking can be applied to monitor the movement of pests across a plant 
or to observe the growth of individual tomatoes over time. By analyzing the motion and 
changes of objects, tracking enables dynamic monitoring and provides temporal insights 
that static images cannot offer. This is particularly useful in robotic harvesting systems, 
where precise tracking of fruits is required for efficient picking.
A study by [122, 123] presents an improved tomato cluster counting method that 
combines object detection, multi-object tracking, and counting within specific track­
ing regions. The YOLOv5-4D model, which uses both RGB and depth images as inputs, 
was introduced to reduce background tomato misidentifications. ByteTrack method was 
applied for tracking tomato clusters across frames and a specialized counting method 
was developed to address ID-shifting issues in tracked clusters. In testing, the enhanced 
YOLOv5-4D achieved an impressive detection accuracy of 97.9%.

4.2.6  3D vision
3D vision involves extracting three-dimensional information from images or videos, 
often using stereo cameras, depth sensors, or photogrammetry. In tomato research, 
3D vision can be used to model the plant's structure, measure fruit size and shape, or 
assess canopy volume. For example, 3D models of tomato plants can aid in identifying 
areas with uneven growth or optimize robotic systems for tasks like pruning or harvest­
ing. This technology is instrumental in understanding spatial relationships and creating 
more accurate models for agricultural robotics.
The study by Roshan et al. [124] presented Structure from Motion (SfM), a widely used

4.2.7  Pose estimation
Pose estimation involves determining objects'position and orientation or key points in 
an image or 3D space. In tomato-related research, pose estimation can be applied to 
identify the spatial arrangement of tomato fruits, branches, or leaves. For example, it 
can help assess the angle and position of tomatoes on a vine, which is critical for robotic 
arms designed for harvesting or pruning tasks. Pose estimation can also aid in evaluat­
ing the structural alignment of tomato plants, which is valuable for optimizing trellising 
systems or studying plant morphology. This task is vital in automation, as precise pose 
information enables machines to interact effectively with plants in complex agricultural 
environments.
Kim et al. [72] proposed a 2D pose estimation method for multi-stem tomatoes. First, 
all components related to the tomato pose were detected in an image and then the pose 
of each object was estimated. The model was trained on four key points defined in this 
study: tomato center, calyx, abscission zone, and branch point. The results show that the 
Percentage of Detected Keypoints for (PDK_0.3) was 0.85, and 0.80 for the key points 
and their connection points. The distal stem, which is an important harvesting cutting 
point, showed a performance of over 0.94.

Traditional feature extraction and mathematical methods: This is the oldest

 	•
CNNs: The second approach began revolutionizing the field in 2012 with the success 
of DL and AlexNet. However, its mathematical foundation dates back to the 1970s.

4.3.3  Transformer-based approaches
Originally developed for NLP tasks, Transformers have made significant inroads into 
CV with the advent of ViTs. ViTs offer an alternative approach to CNNs for image-based 
tasks by leveraging self-attention mechanisms and learning long-range dependencies. 
This section explores the development, innovations, and challenges of ViTs, highlighting 
their potential to redefine computer vision.
Transformers were first introduced in the paper “Attention Is All You Need” [159]. The 
original paper was submitted in 2017 but had several revisions with version number 7 as 
the latest version. While transformers revolutionized NLP with models like Bidirectional 
Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and Generative Pre-Trained Trans­
formers (GPT), their application to vision tasks initially faced hurdles due to differences 
in input structure. ViTs [38] successfully adapted transformers for image data. Key con­
tributions of ViTs include:
 	•
Patch-based representation:
o	 Unlike CNNs, which process images as a whole, ViTs divide an image into fixed-
size patches (e.g., 16 × 16 pixels).
o	 Each patch is treated as a"token,"similar to NLP words, and flattened into a 1D 
vector.
 	•
Learnable positional encoding:
o	 Since transformers lack an inherent sense of spatial structure, positional encodings 
are added to patch embedding to provide spatial information.
 	•
End-to-end training:
o	 ViTs learn image representations directly through attention mechanisms without 
relying on convolutions, offering flexibility and generality.
ViTs demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on classification tasks, particularly with 
large-scale datasets like ImageNet. Maybe the need for large labeled datasets is the rea­
son why only a few studies exist in tomato-related research. ViT's main elements can be 
seen in a study by Pacal et al. [109].

Page 31 of 56

Below are some of the key Transformer-based technologies utilized in agricultural 
computer vision:
 	•
DETR (DEtection TRansformer): DETR approaches object detection as a set 
prediction task. It employs a Transformer encoder-decoder architecture to directly 
predict object-bounding boxes and their associated class labels.
 	•
ViT: ViT applies the Transformer framework to image patches, enabling it to model 
global dependencies and enhance the accuracy of object detection tasks.
 	•
Shifted Window (Swin) Transformer: The Swin Transformer introduces a 
hierarchical architecture that integrates the benefits of CNNs and Transformers. This 
design ensures efficient processing of high-resolution images and enables precise 
object detection.
By harnessing the capabilities of self-attention mechanisms, transformer-based models 
hold immense potential for advancing state-of-the-art techniques in object detection 
and instance segmentation. This is particularly advantageous in scenarios characterized 
by complex backgrounds or occlusions, such as those commonly encountered in agricul­
tural environments.

4.5  Real-time DETR (RT-DETR)
DETR introduces a transformer-based approach to object detection, framing it as a 
direct set prediction problem [23]. By eliminating the need for hand-crafted components 
like anchor generation and non-maximum suppression, DETR simplifies the detection 
pipeline and achieves competitive performance. RT-DETR focuses on real-time object 
detection by introducing an adaptive clustering mechanism within the transformer 
architecture. This method balances accuracy and speed, making it suitable for rapid pro­
cessing applications.
Huang et al. [54] trained multiple versions of the YOLO model for tomato detection.

object detection tasks, particularly in scenarios requiring high-speed and accurate per­

Gu et al. [49] introduced a novel method for detecting tomato fruits and calculating

model detects the tomatoes, after which traditional CV techniques are employed to 
extract the phenotypic feature vectors from the highlighted tomato images. These fea­

4.14  Meshed-memory transformer (M2-transformer)
The M2 Transformer [33] is an advanced, fully attentive architecture specifically 
designed for image captioning. It consists of two main components. A memory-aug­
mented encoder makes component analyses across different regions of an image and 
learns the relationships between them. Leveraging a memory mechanism can incorpo­

mesh decoder uses the information processed by the encoder to generate words and sen­
tences that describe the image. It works across multiple levels, ensuring that all image 
details are considered during caption generation. This dual structure enables the M2 
Transformer to excel at capturing the intricate relationships between image regions and 
generating precise and contextually rich descriptions, thus improving the accuracy and 
efficiency of image captioning.
An article by [144, 145] discussed the DFYOLOv5m-M2 Transformer model designed 
to recognize vegetable diseases and interpret the results. The model consists of two 
main stages. The first stage is a Disease Detector (DFYOLOv5m), which is a CNN-based 
phase that extracts disease features and identifies the affected areas in the image. The 
second stage is a M2-Transformer, a captioning-focused transformer phase that gen­
erates descriptive textual explanations based on the extracted features, explaining the 
detected diseases in the image. This hybrid approach combines the strengths of CNNs 
for feature extraction with transformers for textual interpretation, offering a comprehen­
sive solution for both detection and explanation.
5  BERT
BERT is a transformer-based model developed by Google [37] that revolutionized NLP 
tasks. Unlike traditional models that process text sequentially (left-to-right or right-to-
left), BERT processes text bi-directionally, allowing it to understand the full context of 
a word by looking at both its left and right surroundings simultaneously. BERT is not a 
vision transformer model but a very successful model for language processing.
In Ref. [175, 179] presented a name recognition model designed for detecting agricul­
tural diseases by injecting visual prompts. The aim of this research was to enhance the 
extraction of information related to agricultural diseases, specifically by addressing the 
lack of semantic features in textual contexts. The model utilizes a dataset of 7000 image-
text pairs, where the ResNet network serves as the primary component for detecting 
leaf diseases and extracting visual features. These features and textual annotations are 
injected into a transformer-based architecture, employing BERT and its variations 
to integrate and interpret the multimodal data. In addition, the study evaluates multi­
ple YOLO series models, identifying YOLOv5 as the most effective for disease detec­
tion. Combining ResNet's visual feature extraction capabilities with the transformer’s

6  Discussion
The findings of this review highlight the rapid evolution of artificial intelligence and 
machine vision technologies in tomato cultivation. The integration of traditional image 
processing methods, CNN-based deep learning models, and transformer-based archi­
tectures has significantly improved the accuracy and efficiency of various agricultural 
applications. However, despite these advancements, several challenges remain that 
impact the broader adoption of these technologies in real-world agricultural settings.
One of the primary observations from this review is the diversity of approaches 
applied to tomato-related CV problems. Traditional CV methods, while computation­
ally inexpensive and interpretable, struggle with variations in environmental conditions, 
such as changes in lighting and background complexity. These limitations have led to the 
widespread adoption of CNN-based methods, which can automatically extract relevant 
features and improve detection accuracy. The YOLO family of object detectors, in par­
ticular, has demonstrated strong performance in tasks such as tomato fruit detection, 
ripeness assessment, and pest identification, due to its real-time capabilities and balance 
of speed and accuracy.
Despite the effectiveness of CNNs, they are not without drawbacks. Many CNN mod­
els require large annotated datasets for training, which can be challenging to obtain in 
agricultural settings. Furthermore, while CNNs excel at local feature extraction, they 
struggle with capturing long-range dependencies and contextual relationships within 
images. Transformer-based methods, such as ViT, address this limitation by leveraging 
self-attention mechanisms to process entire images holistically. ViTs have demonstrated 
promising results in disease detection and classification tasks, but their high computa­
tional requirements make them less practical for real-time applications on resource-con­
strained devices commonly used in agriculture.
Hybrid approaches that combine traditional image processing, CNNs, and transform­
ers offer a promising direction for overcoming the limitations of individual methods. For 
example, initial image segmentation using classical techniques can enhance CNN-based 
detection, while transformers can further refine object classification. Such multi-stage 
pipelines are particularly beneficial for complex tasks like 3D modeling of tomato plants, 
where integrating multiple viewpoints and data modalities is crucial.
Another key aspect of this review is categorizing studies based on five dimensions: 
plant parts analyzed, dataset types, image types, CV problem types, and CV approaches 
used. The analysis reveals that most research focuses on leaves and fruits, reflecting their 
importance in disease diagnosis, yield estimation, and harvesting. However, there is rel­
atively limited research on other plant components, such as stems and flowers, which 
may offer additional insights into plant health and productivity.
The availability and quality of datasets remain a significant challenge in agricultural 
machine vision. While some publicly available datasets exist, such as Plant Village, many 
researchers rely on custom datasets collected under specific conditions. The lack of 
standardized, high-quality datasets for tomato cultivation limits the reproducibility and 
generalizability of research findings. Moreover, studies using hyperspectral and near-
infrared (NIR) imaging techniques suggest that multimodal image data can improve 

Page 38 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
accuracy, but these specialized imaging systems are often costly and require additional 
expertise for data interpretation. Hence, we only found a limited number of studies of 
this kind.
Regarding CV problem types, classification tasks remain the most studied, followed 
by object detection and segmentation. While classification provides valuable insights 
into disease identification and fruit ripeness, detection and segmentation approaches 
offer more detailed spatial information, essential for applications like robotic harvesting 
and precision spraying. Future research should focus on advancing instance segmenta­
tion and semantic segmentation methods to enhance object differentiation and improve 
decision-making in automated agricultural systems.
The comparison of different CV approaches underscores the trade-offs between tra­
ditional, CNN-based, and transformer-based methods. Traditional methods offer sim­
plicity and efficiency but lack adaptability to diverse conditions. CNNs provide high 
accuracy but require extensive labeled datasets and computational resources. Trans­
formers present a more holistic image analysis framework but are computationally 
demanding. Hybrid approaches emerge as a potential solution, leveraging the strengths 
of each technique to address different aspects of CV tasks in tomato cultivation.
Overall, this review underscores the transformative impact of machine vision in 
tomato cultivation while also identifying key challenges that need to be addressed. These 
limitations, ranging from dataset and annotation issues to environmental and resource 
constraints, present significant barriers to the full realization of the potential of com­
puter vision in agriculture. In the following section, we explore these challenges in more 
detail and highlight key areas that require further development to enhance the effective­
ness and applicability of these technologies in real-world farming scenarios.
6.1  Limitations
6.1.1  Dataset limitations
One of the major challenges in applying computer vision techniques to tomato culti­
vation is the limited availability of high-quality datasets, particularly those containing 
RGB images captured in real-world agricultural conditions. While some publicly avail­
able datasets exist, many of them are relatively small, lack diversity, or contain controlled 
laboratory images that do not accurately represent field conditions. This limitation hin­
ders the generalizability of machine learning models, as they often struggle to perform 
effectively in varying lighting conditions, plant growth stages, or complex backgrounds 
commonly found in agricultural environments.
Furthermore, there is a notable absence of specialized image datasets that incorporate 
additional spectral information beyond standard RGB. Multispectral and hyperspectral 
imaging, which can capture details beyond human vision, are rarely available in open-
access datasets. Such data could be highly beneficial for tasks like disease detection, 
stress assessment, and fruit ripeness evaluation. Similarly, depth images, 3D point cloud 
data, and NIR images are largely missing from publicly accessible datasets, despite their 
potential for improving plant segmentation, canopy structure analysis, and biomass esti­
mation. The lack of these specialized datasets significantly limits the development and 
application of advanced computer vision models in precision agriculture, particularly 
those relying on multimodal or sensor-fusion approaches.

Page 39 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
Without diverse and extensive datasets, the progress of computer vision in tomato-
related agricultural research remains constrained. Future efforts should focus on col­
lecting and sharing larger, more diverse datasets, including multimodal imaging data, to 
enhance model robustness and enable more sophisticated analysis techniques.
The structure and hierarchy of a dataset are also important factors in computer vision 
applications. The study by Sheikh et al. [136] provides a good example of a well-orga­
nized, multidimensional image dataset. It includes original images stored separately 
from preprocessed, CV-ready resized versions. The dataset is divided into three sub­
directories—flowers, leaves, and fruits. The'leaves'category is further subdivided into 
healthy and diseased classes, reflecting a meaningful hierarchical structure.
6.1.2  Annotation limitations
A critical challenge in computer vision applications for tomato cultivation is the lack 
of annotated datasets, which significantly restricts the development and evaluation of 
machine learning models. While some datasets exist for classification tasks, they are 
typically limited to leaf disease identification and often lack the diversity needed for 
robust model performance. These datasets usually consist of isolated leaves on uniform 
backgrounds, making them less suitable for real-world applications where environmen­
tal factors introduce additional complexity.
For object detection, the situation is even more constrained. There are few or no well-
annotated datasets available for tasks such as detecting tomatoes at different growth 
stages, identifying flowers, or distinguishing between healthy and diseased plants in a 
natural setting. This limitation makes it difficult to train and benchmark deep learning 
models effectively, as detection algorithms require a substantial amount of labeled data 
to generalize well across various field conditions.
The most significant gap is in annotated datasets for segmentation. High-quality 
instance or semantic segmentation datasets for tomato plants, fruits, and their surround­
ing environment are practically non-existent. Creating such datasets is particularly chal­
lenging due to the labor-intensive process of drawing precise boundaries around each 
object in a dense and variable agricultural scene. Unlike standard object detection, which 
only requires bounding boxes, segmentation demands pixel-level annotations, which are 
time-consuming and require domain expertise. As a result, segmentation-based applica­
tions, such as automated harvesting, disease spread analysis, and plant growth monitor­
ing, are severely hindered by the lack of labeled data.
Addressing these annotation limitations is essential for advancing computer vision 
applications in tomato cultivation. Future research should focus on collaborative data­
set creation efforts, crowdsourced annotation initiatives, and the development of semi-
supervised or synthetic annotation techniques to overcome these constraints.
6.1.3  Environmental challenges or limitations
The successful application of computer vision in tomato cultivation faces significant 
environmental challenges that impact the accuracy and reliability of image-based mod­
els. Unlike controlled laboratory conditions, real-world agricultural environments intro­
duce various sources of variability, making image processing more complex. To achieve 
strong results, as demonstrated by [58, 59], a well-curated dataset is essential. While 
some studies included a wide variety of object types, they were often limited by small 

Page 40 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
dataset sizes. Conversely, other studies employed larger datasets but lacked sufficient 
varietal diversity. Most works struggled to find a balance between dataset volume, vari­
etal representation, and the color-textural variability introduced by different weather 
conditions and plant types. These factors are crucial for developing robust and general­
izable computer vision models.
One of the primary challenges is lighting variation. Natural light conditions fluctuate 
throughout the day and across seasons, affecting image quality and model performance. 
Shadows, overexposed areas, and changing sun angles create inconsistencies that make 
it difficult for models trained on ideal datasets to generalize to real-world scenarios. 
While artificial lighting can help in controlled environments like greenhouses, it is not a 
feasible solution for large-scale field applications.
Weather conditions further complicate the situation. Factors such as rain, fog, dust, 
and wind can obscure features in images, reducing the effectiveness of object detection 
and segmentation algorithms. Moisture on camera lenses, dust accumulation, or plant 
movement due to wind can introduce noise that makes it harder for models to distin­
guish objects correctly.
Another limitation is plant variability. Tomato plants exhibit significant differences 
in growth patterns, leaf structures, fruit sizes, and colors depending on the variety, cul­
tivation method, and environmental conditions. Traditional computer vision models 
struggle to adapt to this natural diversity, leading to decreased accuracy when applied 
to different regions or farming techniques. Additionally, plant occlusion—where leaves, 
stems, or other objects partially cover the target areas—makes tasks like fruit detection 
and disease identification more difficult.
Furthermore, background complexity in open-field cultivation presents a challenge. 
Unlike indoor settings where backgrounds can be controlled, outdoor environments 
include soil, other crops, weeds, and farm equipment, which can confuse computer 
vision models. The ability to distinguish tomatoes from similar-colored objects or 
cluttered backgrounds remains an issue, especially when using simple RGB-based 
approaches.
Addressing these environmental challenges requires a combination of robust data aug­
mentation techniques, advanced domain adaptation strategies, and multispectral imag­
ing to enhance model resilience. Additionally, sensor fusion, combining RGB data with 
depth, thermal, or hyperspectral information, could help mitigate some of these limita­
tions and improve recognition performance in dynamic field conditions.
6.1.4  Resource limitations for real-time application
Implementing computer vision for real-time applications in tomato cultivation faces sig­
nificant resource constraints, particularly when deployed on small agricultural robots 
and low-power embedded systems. Unlike high-performance computing environments, 
these platforms must operate efficiently with limited processing power, memory, and 
energy availability.
One of the primary challenges is the computational demand of modern deep learn­
ing models. But also the traditional feature extractor, machine learning methods can 
work with a big amount high dimensionality vectors. In these cases a dimensional reduc­
tion method like diffusion maps can help [161–163]. CNN-based architectures, such as 
YOLO and Faster R-CNN, require significant processing power, while ViTs demand even 

Page 41 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
more memory and computational resources. Running these models in real time on small 
robots with embedded processors often results in slow inference times, reducing their 
practicality for fast decision-making in the field.
Another limitation is energy consumption. Small robots operating in agricultural envi­
ronments often rely on battery power, which must be conserved for navigation, actua­
tion, and other essential functions. Running complex vision models on power-hungry 
GPUs or TPUs drains batteries quickly, limiting the robot’s operational time.
Furthermore, latency issues affect real-time performance. Tasks like object detec­
tion, fruit counting, and disease identification need to be performed on the fly to enable 
autonomous decision-making. However, due to hardware limitations, many vision-based 
models contain delays, making it difficult for robots to react in real time. In such cases, 
lightweight architectures like MobileNet or YOLO-nano may be preferred over heavier 
models despite potential trade-offs in accuracy.
To address these resource constraints, future research should focus on developing 
lightweight, energy-efficient models, leveraging edge artificial intelligence techniques, 
and improving hardware acceleration for deep learning on low-power agricultural 
robots.
6.1.5  Communication or data connection limitation
Deploying computer vision in agricultural fields is significantly hindered by limited com­
munication infrastructure and data transfer constraints. Unlike urban areas with stable 
high-speed networks, remote agricultural environments often lack reliable internet 
connectivity, making it difficult to send large amounts of image data to powerful cloud-
based servers for processing.
One major challenge is bandwidth limitations. High-resolution images and video 
streams required for tasks like disease detection, fruit counting, and weed identification 
generate large volumes of data. Transmitting these datasets over weak or intermittent 
network connections—such as cellular networks (4G, 5G) or satellite links—is impracti­
cal due to slow upload speeds and potential data loss. This limitation prevents real-time 
remote processing and decision-making.
Additionally, the latency associated with cloud-based processing further reduces feasi­
bility for real-time applications. Even if data transmission were possible, sending images 
to a remote server, processing them, and receiving a response causes delays, which are 
unacceptable for autonomous robots or precision spraying systems that require immedi­
ate action.
As a result, on-device processing becomes essential. Instead of relying on external 
servers, agricultural robots and UAVs must perform edge computing, where models are 
optimized to run directly on embedded processors. Techniques such as model compres­
sion, quantization, and hardware acceleration can help overcome computational con­
straints while minimizing dependency on unreliable network connections.
Future advancements should focus on developing highly efficient on-device models, 
improving local processing capabilities, and exploring hybrid approaches where only 
critical data is transmitted while the majority of processing occurs on the edge.

6.1.6  Multimodal limitations
Multimodal learning, which integrates data from multiple sources such as RGB images, 
depth information, multispectral data, and textual descriptions, holds great promise for 
agricultural applications. However, its adoption in tomato-related research and preci­
sion agriculture faces several challenges.
One primary limitation is the lack of multimodal datasets. Most existing agricul­
tural datasets contain only RGB images, with little to no accompanying data from other 
modalities such as NIR, thermal, hyperspectral, LiDAR, or textual annotations. The 
absence of such diverse datasets makes it difficult to train and evaluate multimodal mod­
els effectively. Additionally, collecting multimodal agricultural data is complex, requiring 
specialized sensors, synchronized data capture, and precise calibration to ensure align­
ment between different modalities.
Another significant challenge is the computational cost of multimodal models. Com­
bining multiple data sources increases the complexity of machine learning architectures, 
requiring more memory, processing power, and storage. For real-time applications in 
agriculture, such as autonomous robots and UAV-based monitoring, this added compu­
tational burden is a major limitation. Embedded hardware used in these systems often 
lacks the power to handle deep multimodal architectures efficiently.
Furthermore, data fusion techniques for multimodal learning in agriculture are still 
underdeveloped. Aligning and integrating different data types (e.g., merging 2D images 
with 3D point clouds or linking textual disease descriptions with spectral signatures) 
remains a challenging research problem. Many multimodal approaches rely on hand­
crafted fusion strategies, which may not generalize well to diverse field conditions.
To overcome these limitations, future research should focus on creating high-quality 
multimodal agricultural datasets, optimizing lightweight fusion strategies, and develop­
ing efficient multimodal models that can operate on low-power agricultural hardware.
6.1.7  Model and algorithm limitations
In the application of computer vision to agriculture, particularly in the context of 
tomato-related research, the limitations of models and algorithms present significant 
challenges. Despite the advances made in deep learning and other machine learning 
techniques, these models still face several critical issues that hinder their broader imple­
mentation in real-world agricultural settings.
One major limitation is the generalizability of models. Many algorithms, particularly 
those based on CNNs and transformers, are often trained on highly curated datasets 
that may not reflect the variability and complexity of real-world agricultural environ­
ments. In tomato farming, for instance, factors such as changing lighting conditions, 
varying plant health, and environmental influences can lead to poor model performance 
when models are applied outside their training domain. The lack of diverse, real-world 
data for training leads to models that struggle to adapt to new conditions, resulting in 
suboptimal performance during actual deployment.
Additionally, many models suffer from a lack of interpretability. In agriculture, it is 
crucial to understand why a model makes specific predictions, especially when it comes 
to decisions related to crop management and disease detection. The black-box nature 
of deep learning models often means that farmers and agricultural experts cannot eas­
ily verify or trust the model's predictions without external validation. This limitation is 

Page 43 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
particularly concerning when errors in model predictions can lead to incorrect treat­
ment applications, potentially harming crops or wasting resources.
Finally, algorithmic limitations are also evident in tasks such as detection and segmen­
tation. For instance, while CNNs are effective at classifying and detecting objects, they 
often struggle with precise boundary delineation in complex agricultural scenes. The dif­
ficulty in separating plants from background clutter, distinguishing overlapping leaves, 
or detecting small-scale diseases in dense foliage can lead to low accuracy in real-world 
applications.
6.1.8  Limitations of this study
During our literature review, we found that Elsevier and Springer had published a com­
parable and substantial number of articles relevant to our research topic. Although we 
also reviewed journals from other publishers, the number of related publications was 
significantly lower. Based on the titles and scopes of these articles, we did not identify 
any journals offering distinct or complementary perspectives on tomato-related com­
puter vision research. For this reason, we limited our study to publications from these 
two major publishers.
It is important to acknowledge that the field of computer vision encompasses a wide 
range of algorithms, methods, and neural network architectures. Our primary objective 
was to identify which of these approaches had been applied to tomato-focused research 
within the past two years. Consequently, many otherwise valuable and well-established 
techniques were excluded from this review. A few particularly novel and promising algo­
rithms are briefly mentioned in the discussion section, as we believe they may soon play 
a significant role in tomato-related applications.
We also examined the specific aspects of the tomato plant targeted in the reviewed 
studies. One striking observation was the overwhelming focus on leaf disease detec­
tion, while no studies were found addressing the detection of diseases in tomato fruits. 
Despite the clear importance of fruit health, there appears to be a lack of research in this 
area. This gap may be due to the absence of publicly available datasets on tomato fruit 
diseases, in contrast to well-established datasets for leaf diseases, such as PlantVillage 
and PlantDoc. As a result, current research directions may be driven more by data avail­
ability than by real-world needs. This represents a limitation not only of our review but 
potentially of the field as a whole, as the existing literature may not fully reflect the prac­
tical challenges and priorities of tomato production.
6.2  Future directions
Having discussed the various limitations in the current state of agricultural computer 
vision, we have highlighted several key areas in need of development. Moving forward, 
there are several promising directions for future research and innovation that could help 
overcome these challenges and advance the field. Below, we outline some of the main 
development directions for the coming years.
6.2.1  Integration of multimodal data
The future of agricultural computer vision lies in the integration of multimodal data 
sources such as RGB, multispectral, thermal, and 3D point cloud data. By combining 
data from multiple sensors, models can achieve more robust and accurate results. For 

Page 44 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
instance, multispectral and NIR imaging can offer insights into plant health that are 
invisible to traditional RGB cameras, helping to detect diseases, nutrient deficiencies, or 
water stress early. Developing models that can process and integrate these diverse data 
types will significantly enhance crop management capabilities.
6.2.2  Integration of broader multimodal data
To further enhance the precision of agricultural systems, there will be an increased focus 
on the integration of broader multimodal data. This includes not only visual data from 
cameras and sensors but also historical weather data, soil data, and pest management 
records. By integrating weather history (e.g., temperature, precipitation, humidity), soil 
properties (e.g., pH, moisture levels, nutrient content), and real-time soil data, artifi­
cial intelligence systems can gain a more comprehensive understanding of the farming 
environment. Furthermore, integrating historical pest data (e.g., past pest outbreaks, 
spraying records) and nearby area information on pest and disease occurrences can help 
predict future risks and optimize management strategies. This holistic approach allows 
for a more precise and context-aware farming system, helping farmers make better deci­
sions about irrigation, fertilization, pest control, and overall crop management.
Multimodal data integration—such as the combination of visual, environmental, and 
sensor data—can significantly enhance tomato cultivation by improving the accuracy of 
disease detection, yield prediction, and growth monitoring. For example, in addition to 
RGB images, the availability of environmental parameters such as soil moisture, ambi­
ent temperature, and short-term weather forecasts (e.g., 1–2 weeks ahead) enables more 
precise yield estimation. These data sources can also support the early identification of 
potential disease or pest outbreaks, allowing for timely interventions.
For stakeholders, such integrated systems facilitate better decision-making, reduce 
labor dependency, and enable real-time responses to field conditions. This helps address 
key challenges in adoption, including cost-efficiency, scalability, and reliability across 
diverse agricultural environments. Furthermore, an AI-driven expert system can be 
designed to monitor market conditions and price trends, synchronize yield prediction 
with ripening progress, and support logistics planning. This just-in-time coordination 
can optimize workforce allocation and supply chain readiness, ultimately contributing to 
more sustainable and economically viable production.
6.2.3  Development of small, efficient, real-time models for edge computing
As agricultural robots and drones become more prevalent, there will be an increased 
demand for small, efficient models that can run in real-time on edge devices. Future 
research should focus on optimizing deep learning models to be lightweight and com­
putationally efficient, allowing them to run on the limited hardware of small robots or 
autonomous drones without sacrificing performance. This includes model pruning, 
quantization, and the use of efficient neural network architectures like MobileNet or 
YOLO nano.
6.2.4  Synthetic data generation and augmentation
To address the scarcity of annotated datasets in agriculture, especially for specialized 
tasks like segmentation and detection, the development of synthetic datasets could play 
a major role. Advances in generative models like GANs could enable the creation of 

Page 45 of 56
Moldvai and Nyéki Discover Applied Sciences           (2025) 7:975 
realistic synthetic images that simulate various growing conditions, crop types, and envi­
ronmental changes. These datasets can help to supplement real-world data and improve 
the generalizability of models, especially when real-world data is sparse or difficult to 
obtain.
6.2.5  Improved model interpretability and explainability
As the adoption of artificial intelligence systems in agriculture increases, there will be 
a growing need for models that are interpretable and explainable. This is particularly 
important in sensitive applications like pest and disease detection, where incorrect deci­
sions can lead to significant crop loss or the misuse of pesticides. Future research should 
focus on developing Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methods that provide clear 
reasoning behind the predictions made by models, making them more trustworthy for 
farmers and agricultural professionals.
6.2.6  Autonomous and collaborative agricultural systems
The future of agriculture will likely see the development of fully autonomous farming 
systems that can handle tasks like crop monitoring, pest control, and harvesting. These 
systems will rely heavily on computer vision and artificial intelligence to make decisions 
in real-time, operating with minimal human intervention. Additionally, there is potential 
for collaborative systems where multiple robots, drones, and machines work together, 
exchanging data and tasks in a coordinated manner. Research into multi-agent systems 
and robot-to-robot communication will be essential for realizing these collaborative 
approaches.
In recent years, the adoption of drones equipped with monitoring cameras has become 
increasingly prevalent in agricultural fields, indicating rapid growth in this sector. The 
book by [31] provides a comprehensive overview of the various aspects of computer 
vision and drone technology as applied in agriculture.
6.2.7  Adaptability to dynamic agricultural environments
Agriculture is highly dynamic, with conditions changing daily due to weather, pests, dis­
eases, and crop growth. Future artificial intelligent systems should be adaptive to these 
changes. This involves developing models that can continuously learn from new data, 
even in real-time. Few-shot learning and transfer learning techniques will play a criti­
cal role here, enabling models to quickly adapt to new environments or unknown crop 
diseases with minimal labeled data. To ensure adaptability to dynamic agricultural envi­
ronments, an AI-based smart monitoring system is essential. This book [134] presents 
such systems, including examples of generative models like GANs, which have promis­
ing applications in agriculture.

6.4  Conclusions
Based on the analysis of 163 studies, several interesting trends emerge.
Currently, the majority of image-processing approaches rely on CNN-based technol­
ogies. However, traditional feature-based methods are still widely used and appear to 
remain effective for certain tasks. This is likely due to their lack of dependence on large 
amounts of training data. Similarly, transformer-based technologies, the newest innova­
tion in the field, are also being applied at a comparable scale. These technologies have the 
potential to outperform CNN-based approaches in certain cases, but the need for large 
training datasets and high computational demands significantly limits their adoption.

As the new technology appears like SSM based models like the Mamba family that can 
work fast, even on high resolution images, than it can move the CV from computer to 
robots and that can make a big change in agriculture field.
From a dataset perspective, a substantial number of studies utilize proprietary or part­
ner data. These studies are closest to real-world conditions and, therefore, represent 
the most valuable contributions. However, the majority of studies rely on publicly avail­
able datasets from the internet. A common characteristic of such studies is their lack of 
direct relevance to tomato cultivation. In most cases, these studies focus on advancing 
or developing new image processing methods, with the image data serving only as nec­
essary input.
Many researchers rely on the Plant Village dataset, which consists of simple images 
of leaves against clean backgrounds. Unfortunately, algorithms trained on such datasets 
are unlikely to perform well in real-world environments. A frequent issue is that these 
images are single-shot captures, leading to a lack of diversity in size, variety, and lighting 
conditions. Consequently, such algorithms often fail to perform adequately in practical 
applications.
Creating an agricultural image database is not easy. While capturing tens of thousands 
of images may be feasible, these images need to be taken across multiple periods, loca­
tions, crop varieties, and times of day, which complicates the process. Even more chal­
lenging is the precise annotation of these images. Drawing accurate boundaries around 
every object in the images requires significant time, precision, and expertise. As a result, 
high-quality, diverse, and accurately annotated large-scale agricultural datasets are cur­
rently unavailable.
Synthetic database generation could provide a solution to the issue of dataset scarcity. 
However, this approach is still in its experimental phase. Nevertheless, the few studies 
that have explored this avenue report promising results. Synthetic datasets may generate 
sufficient data and facilitate the broader adoption of transformer-based image-process­
ing technologies.
Recently, several self-supervised learning (SSL) models have emerged, and this tech­
nology has the potential to be a game-changer in agricultural applications. One of its 
key advantages is that it eliminates the need for manual image annotation. When images 
are collected directly from the field and used to train an SSL-based model, the results 
can be highly promising. However, a major challenge lies in generalization. Models 
trained on data from one field often show a sharp drop in performance when applied to 
another, due to environmental differences that significantly affect the visual character­
istics of images. In traditional supervised learning approaches, the need for annotated 
datasets can delay model development by several weeks—during which time plants 
and environmental conditions may change, reducing the relevance of the data. In con­
trast, SSL-based approaches enable immediate use of raw, unlabeled images for training, 
allowing models to be prepared within hours rather than weeks. If these methods prove 
successful, they could also reduce or eliminate the need for synthetic datasets, thereby 
streamlining the model development process and accelerating real-time applications in 
the field.
One particularly experimental branch of transformer technology, multimodal trans­
formers, holds significant promise. These models can associate textual descriptions with 
images and even provide textual explanations for their classifications. This opens up new

possibilities, including the potential for few-shot or zero-shot models. This capability is 
especially important in agriculture, where rapidly changing climates can lead to the sud­
den emergence of unknown diseases or the unique characteristics of new hybrid variet­
ies. Such technologies may offer a way for models to adapt quickly to these changes.
ViTs represent a significant shift in computer vision, challenging CNNs'dominance 
and opening new possibilities for understanding visual data. While they come with 
challenges, such as high computational demands and data requirements, ongoing inno­
vations in architecture, training techniques, and multimodal integration are rapidly 
addressing these issues. As ViTs evolve, they promise to unlock new frontiers in com­
puter vision, shaping the future of intelligent agriculture systems and robotics.
It appears that for simple object detection, CNN-based models, particularly YOLO, 
are generally faster and more accurate than ViTs. This advantage is especially crucial for 
low-power, hardware-constrained robotic applications, where efficiency and real-time 
performance are key factors. As a result, CNN-based systems are likely to remain domi­
nant in this domain for some time. However, in scenarios requiring additional contextual 
information, text association with images, or zero-shot and few-shot detection capabili­
ties, ViT models offer significant advantages and appear to be the more suitable choice.
Overall, the field of tomato-related computer vision research is advancing rapidly, with 
CNNs remaining the backbone of real-time applications, while transformers introduce 
new possibilities for more complex and adaptive image analysis. Moreover the SSL based 
SSM can fasten the whole process. The continued evolution of datasets, synthetic data 
generation, and multimodal learning approaches will be crucial in shaping the next gen­
eration of intelligent agricultural technologies.
While not currently the most pressing challenge, the absence of autonomous monitor­
ing robots capable of operating in the field for extended periods without human inter­
vention remains a significant gap. Such robots are essential for continuously collecting 
visual and environmental data, which can then be used to train and update AI and deep 
learning systems.
Another critical limitation is the lack of centralized platforms for agricultural data 
sharing. At present, there are no widely adopted, usable repositories for large-scale agri­
cultural datasets. This prevents researchers and farmers from accessing valuable data 
generated by others. Most farmers, if they collect data at all, only use it locally and pri­
vately. Overcoming this barrier requires not only technological advancements in data 
standardization and integration but also legal and regulatory frameworks that support 
secure and ethical data sharing across stakeholders.
