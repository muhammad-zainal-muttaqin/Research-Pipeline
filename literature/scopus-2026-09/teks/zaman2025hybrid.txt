Abstract—This paper presents a method that integrates Loss 
Rank Mining (LRM) into the YOLOv11 object detection 
framework to enhance training efficiency by focusing on harder 
examples. A key issue in detecting Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFB) in 
UAV imagery is occlusion, caused by overlapping fronds, 
clustered bunches, and inconsistent viewing angles, which often 
leads to undetected or misclassified objects. By prioritizing 
instances with elevated loss values, the model is guided to learn 
from complex and informative cases, particularly those related 
to partial occlusions a prevalent challenge in oil palm 
plantations. We evaluated the proposed YOLOv11-LRM model 
against other YOLOv11 variants using standard object 
detection metrics, specifically focusing on mean Average 
Precision (mAP). Experimental results show that YOLOv11-
LRM outperformed the other models, achieving an mAP50 of 
99.2% and mAP50-95 of 85.6%. These findings demonstrate 
that LRM is effective in improving object detection under 
challenging agricultural conditions, making it especially 
suitable for UAV-based FFB monitoring systems.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Recently, artificial intelligence methodologies based on 
computer vision have experienced increased use in 
agriculture, especially in the field of precision agriculture. A 
wide range of research has concentrated on applications 
including fruit ripeness detection, fruit size estimation, and 
plant disease diagnosis.Numerous research studies have 
investigated the identification of Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFB) 
in the context of oil palm agriculture for diverse practical uses. 
A significant area of research centers on the automated 
identification and measurement of FFB through image-based 
techniques[1][2][3][4]. 
Despite 
these 
investigations 
demonstrating reasonable Even though these studies showed 
good accuracy, the methods still struggled with blocked views 
and often required a lot of processing power. 
A separate study compared different object detection 
models, such as MobileNetV2 SSD, EfficientDet, and 
YOLOv5, to see how well they classify the ripeness of FFB 
[5]. Of these models, YOLOv5 exhibited the most rapid 
detection performance while maintaining a high degree of 
accuracy. We have employed the EfficientNet model to assess 
FFB ripeness using color composition, achieving an accuracy 
rate of up to 99.49%. [6]. Detection of Fresh Fruit Bunches 
(FFB) has also been performed using the Visual Attention 
method [7]. Nonetheless, image based static methodologies 
exhibit constraints in addressing the diversity of plantation 
environmental conditions, especially when images are 
acquired using unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV). Many more 
experiments have been done, including making labeled 
datasets to improve how well deep learning models can tell 
the ripeness of oil palm fruit [8]. This study demonstrates that 
the YOLOv4 model effectively identifies inferior palm fruit 
bunches by analyzing color and size attributes via an Android 
smartphone. The model attains a mean Average Precision 
(mAP) of 97.64% for single-class identification with 
YOLOv4-CSPDarknet53. The maximum achieved mAP for 
multi-class detection in a single video frame is 70.21% 
utilizing YOLOv4-Tiny. 
A smartphone based system using YOLOv4 has been 
developed to recognize and classify Fresh Fruit Bunches 
(FFB) in real-world situations [9][10]. This system functions 
in real-time; nevertheless, its detection accuracy diminishes 
in inadequate lighting and has difficulties with inter object 
occlusion. Recent studies have commenced utilizing 
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to enhance monitoring 
coverage and detection efficiency for large-scale FFB 
detection [11]. The research adeptly addressed issues of 
diverse

identifying objects partially hidden by vegetation or tree 
branches. Prior research has emphasized that the precise 
identification of obstructed Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFB), 
particularly with UAV-based imaging systems, continues to 
pose a significant problem. This study introduces a new 
system that merges YOLOv11 with Loss Rank Mining 
(LRM) to improve detection accuracy in the busy and 
complicated conditions found in plantations. 
YOLOv11 represents the next progression in the YOLO 
architecture, engineered to enhance feature extraction and 
object identification efficacy. The model attains enhanced 
detection accuracy, especially in recognizing obstructed 
objects, by the use of C3k2 blocks, SPPF (Spatial Pyramid 
Pooling - Fast), and C2PSA (Convolutional Block with 
Parallel Spatial Attention) modules, in contrast to its 
predecessor [12][13]. On the other hand, Loss Rank Mining 
(LRM) is a method that focuses on difficult-to-classify 
samples by giving more importance to prediction mistakes 
during training [14][15]. This study aims to develop a more

II. MATERIALS AND METHODS 
This section outlines the complete process for developing 
an AI-driven computer vision system to detect occluded FFB  
in UAV imagery, covering everything from data preparation 
and model development to performance evaluation. 
A. Hardware and Software Equipment 
In this study, hardware and software support the entire 
process of this research, from data collection to training and 
model evaluation. In terms of hardware, the ASUS TUF 
Gaming A15 laptop has an AMD Ryzen 7 4800 H processor, 
16 GB of RAM, an NVIDIA GTX 1650 Ti GPU, and 1 TB 
SATA SSD storage, which runs on top of Windows 11 Home. 
For the acquisition of image data from the field, a DJI Mavic 
Air drone was used. 
 On the software side, model training is carried out using 
Google Colaboratory, with annotation and augmentation 
support through Roboflow. Programming is done using 
Python 3.10 in the PyCharm environment, as well as libraries 
such as OpenCV, YOLOv11 and Scikit-Learn. For GPU 
optimization, NVIDIA CUDA Toolkit v12.3 is used, while 
documentation and presentation of research results are 
compiled with Microsoft Office 365. 
B. Dataset Preparation 
The dataset for this study was primarily sourced by the 
research team from small oil palm plantations owned by 
smallholders in Polewali Mandar Regency, West Sulawesi 
Province. During the data collection phase, the team worked 
closely with a skilled palm fruit harvester to identify mature 
fruit bunches. Data acquisition was carried out using a DJI 
Mavic Air drone equipped with distance sensors, which 
captured video footage of FFB still attached to the trees. All 
videos were recorded during clear, sunny weather between 
09:00 and 13:00 local time to ensure optimal visibility and 
consistent lighting. These uniform conditions may influence 
model generalizability across varying weather or illumination 
scenarios, which we acknowledge as a limitation and suggest 
for future exploration. The configuration of the drone's 
distance sensors is shown in Fig. 1. 
 
Fig. 1. Distance sensor on DJI mavic air drone 
Fig. 1. demonstrates that the estimated distance between 
the drone and the oil palm FFB during data collection was 
roughly 2 meters. On days with sunshine, the data was 
gathered between 10 am and 2 pm.  Using a resolution of 
1920 x 1080 pixels and 30 frames per second (FPS), each 
image was captured in a 5 to 8 second timeframe and stored 
in.MP4 format.  We used image extraction to break down the 
video recordings of the oil palm FFB into individual frames 
after we obtained the recordings.  About twenty-five images, 
taken at eight-frame intervals from the FFB movie, make up 
each oil palm FFB sample.  In all, 2,643 images were 
produced by this process.   
Seventy percent of the dataset was reserved for training 
purposes, while fifteen percent was used for validation and 
fifteen percent for testing..This division comprises 2313 
photos for training, 165 for validation, and 165 for testing. 
This distribution guarantees that the model is trained on a 
varied dataset and assessed using novel samples to test its 
capacity to generalize to new inputs.Each image was 
subsequently annotated manually to indicate the regions 
corresponding to the FFB. This study made labeling easier by 
using just one class label, "Oil Palm Fresh Fruit Bunch," 
because the main goal of the segmentation model is to 
accurately find and highlight the FFB areas in each image.  
To optimize model training and diminish computational 
complexity, all annotated images were downsized from their 
original resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels to a reduced 
resolution of 640 × 360 pixels. This scaling accelerates the 
training process and guarantees consistency in input 
dimensions. 
The resizing procedure employed the "fill-within" 
method, wherein each image was scaled to conform to the 
target dimensions while preserving its original aspect ratio. 
This method guarantees the preservation of the visual 
proportions of items in the image throughout preprocessing. 
This approach was executed to diminish computational 
complexity, expedite the model training procedure, and 
enhance storage efficiency. Furthermore, maintaining aspect 
ratio during resizing is particularly important in object 
detection tasks, as distortion may lead to misalignment 
between annotated bounding boxes and actual object shapes, 
ultimately degrading model performance. An illustration of 
the image scaling procedure is provided in Fig. 2.

C. Model Development 
The YOLOv11 model was selected as the base 
architecture in this study due to its strong performance in real-
time object detection tasks [12], particularly in scenarios with 
dense object distributions and partial occlusion. Compared to

other architectures such as SSD or EfficientDet [5], 
YOLOv11 offers a better balance between detection accuracy 
and inference speed, which is crucial for UAV-based 
agricultural applications.  
This paper presents a new version of the YOLOv11 model 
that uses the LRM technique to detect oil palm FFB in 
plantations where they may be hidden by fronds, leaves, or 
other bunches. This optimization aims to enhance the model's 
resilience in recognizing objects, even when they are partially 
concealed. This study incorporated LRM into the training 
process of the YOLOv11 model by altering its original loss 
function. 
This 
modification 
enables 
the 
model 
to 
autonomously recognize and prioritize challenging samples 
from the training dataset during the training phase. 
Challenging examples are data points that produce the 
greatest loss values. We chose only the top 35% of the most 
challenging samples, characterized by the highest loss values, 
for the parameter updating process. This technique 
guarantees that the model prioritizes learning from the most 
significant detection failures, particularly when the oil palm 
FFB is obscured by foliage, tree branches, or other bunches. 
By changing the learning weights based on how hard it is to 
detect in each training round, the model becomes better at 
handling different situations where things are blocked from 
view. Fig. 3. illustrates the training process of the YOLOv11 
model, which integrates the LRM technique. 
 
Fig. 3. LRM process in YOLOv11 
Fig. 3. demonstrates that the integration phase of the LRM 
technique in YOLOv11 training commences with the input of 
a data batch including images from the training dataset into 
the model. Each image features diverse object situations, such 
as items obscured by greenery, photos with fluctuating 
illumination, or intricate backdrops. The model subsequently 
executes a forward pass to produce preliminary predictions. 
At this juncture, YOLOv11 derives features from the input 
image utilizing the backbone network and produces bounding 
boxes and segmentation masks for identified items.  
Upon generating predictions, the loss value for each 
sample in the batch is computed based on the discrepancy 
between the forecasts and the ground truth. The primary loss 
components 
assessed 
in 
this 
procedure 
encompass 
classification loss to quantify classification mistakes, 
bounding box loss to appraise the precision of bounding box 
predictions, and objectness loss, which ascertains the model’s 
confidence in identifying an object within a specified region. 
After calculating all loss values, we implement the LRM 
process by ranking all samples in the batch according to their 
respective loss. Samples with the highest losses are classified 
as hard examples. From this ranking, only the top 35% are 
selected for focused learning. The 35% threshold was 
empirically chosen to strike a balance between prioritizing 
challenging examples and maintaining sufficient diversity 
within training batches. Preliminary experiments showed that 
lower thresholds led to underfitting, while higher thresholds 
diminished the effectiveness of hard example mining. 
Samples with lower loss values are not discarded. Instead, 
they are assigned reduced weights during backpropagation. 
This ensures that they still contribute marginally to learning, 
while allowing the model to focus adjustments on the more 
difficult examples.  
Right now, the loss gradient is calculated only for the 
tough cases, allowing the model to make bigger changes to 
the weights for the parameters related to these hard examples. 
This methodology facilitates the model's rapid adaptation to 
obstacles encountered in object detection, especially with 
obscured objects or those exhibiting diverse appearances. 
After backpropagation, an optimizer revises the model 
parameters to adjust the weights based on the most recent 
learning outcomes. The model subsequently evaluates 
whether to proceed with or terminate the training. Should the 
convergence conditions remain unfulfilled, the training will 
recommence with a new dataset. If the model attains the 
requisite performance level, the training process concludes, 
and the modified model weights are preserved for subsequent 
inference.

demand. Although the LRM technique introduces a selective 
mechanism in loss calculation during training, our 
experiments demonstrate that the training time of the 
YOLOv11-LRM model does not significantly increase 
compared to the baseline YOLOv11s-Seg model.  
In fact, YOLOv11-LRM tends to converge faster because 
it utilizes pre-trained weights from the baseline model, 
allowing training to start from an advanced stage and achieve 
stability more quickly. Furthermore, since LRM is applied 
only during the training phase without modifying the 
inference pipeline, inference time remains unaffected. Thus, 
LRM provides significant improvements in detection 
accuracy without sacrificing training or inference time 
efficiency.

IV. CONCLUSION 
This study demonstrates the successful adaptation of the 
LRM technique to the state-of-the-art YOLOv11 detector to 
address the practical challenge of occluded oil palm FFB 
detection in UAV imagery, while also highlighting its 
contribution to the advancement of precision agriculture. 
Compared to other YOLOv11 variants, the YOLOv11-LRM 
model, enhanced with the LRM technique, achieved the best 
performance. Evaluation results showed a highest precision 
of 99.6%, recall of 98.5%, and mAP50 of 99.2%, making it 
the top-performing model among those tested. The increase 
in mAP50-95 to 85.6% indicates the model’s improved 
capability to detect objects across a wider range of scales 
more effectively. Confusion matrix evaluation revealed a 
very low error rate, with only four false negatives and no false 
positives. An F1 score of 99.05% confirms an optimal 
balance between precision and recall. Based on these 
findings, it can be concluded that YOLOv11-LRM 
significantly enhances the accuracy of FFB detection 
compared to other YOLOv11 models and shows great 
potential for application in automation systems within the 
palm oil industry. Nevertheless, the dataset used still reflects 
a realistic scenario of occluded FFB detection in West 
Sulawesi plantations. Therefore, broader generalization 
requires further validation under diverse weather conditions 
and geographic locations. Future research is recommended to 
expand the dataset with more varied environmental 
conditions and to test the model’s robustness across different 
regions.
