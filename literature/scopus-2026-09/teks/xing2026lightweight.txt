A B S T R A C T
Crop yield prediction is one of the key measures for dynamically adjusting harvest schedules and minimizing 
production losses. To address the production requirements for multi-object real-time detection and accurate 
counting of tomatoes in complex greenhouse environments, this study proposes a lightweight “detection–­
tracking–counting–compensation” cascaded network framework, optimized for deployment on inspection robots. 
First, a sparse convolution module (G-FConv) and a lightweight multi-scale feature aggregation module (L-MFA) 
were designed, and a lightweight feature extraction network was constructed to reduce computational load and 
complexity. Second, a lightweight asymmetric decoupled head incorporating the SimAM attention mechanism 
was developed to enhance critical feature extraction while further reducing parameter count. Third, a multi-view 
cooperative detection and compensation method was proposed to optimize multi-object tracking performance in 
dense and occluded scenes. Finally, TensorRT-based inference acceleration was applied to improve the robot’s 
real-time processing capability. The proposed model achieves high detection (90.8%) and counting (95.2%) 
accuracy with a compact size of 3.98 MB and 5.3 GFLOPs, while the proposed MVC method reduces counting 
errors by 95.6%. This study provides an effective technical solution for precise tomato yield prediction in 
greenhouses, offering significant practical value for optimizing harvest schedules and reducing production losses.
1. Introduction
Tomato is a globally significant economic crop, with an annual 
production exceeding 180 million tons cultivated over more than 5 
million hectares. Its strategic importance and substantial economic 
value are well established. The expanding global market, projected to 
reach US$261.2 billion by 2030 [1], reflects strong and growing con­
sumer demand. However, current tomato yields frequently fail to align 
with actual market requirements, resulting in regional oversupply. This 
imbalance disrupts supply chain stability and undermines brand credi­
bility, leading to considerable economic losses. Accurate yield estima­
tion is therefore critical for maintaining efficient supply chain 
operations and promoting sustainable agricultural practices. Object 
detection and tracking are critical techniques to enhance precision 
farming and pave the way for robotization in the agricultural sector, as 
they provide accurate results and insights for crop management while 
optimizing resource allocation [2].
Traditional object detection methods primarily rely on hand-crafted 
features and classical image processing. These approaches can be cate­
gorized into three types: Color-space-based segmentation (e.g., HSV or 
Lab thresholding) extracts target regions but is highly sensitive to 
lighting conditions and color variations [3]. Edge detection and 
morphological operations (e.g., Canny and Sobel filters combined with 
erosion/dilation) facilitate contour extraction but are prone to noise and 
perform poorly with occluded or clustered objects [4]. Feature 
descriptor-based classifiers (e.g., HOG + SVM) use Histograms of Ori­
ented Gradients (HOG) for texture representation and classification [5]; 
however, these methods suffer from limited feature expressiveness, poor

parameter tuning. While traditional methods offer advantages in sim­
ple scenarios—such as lower computational costs and reduced 
latency—they face critical limitations in complex, unstructured agri­
cultural settings. These challenges include extensive reliance on 
expert-driven parameter adjustments, difficulties in segmenting densely 
occluded or overlapping targets, and limited adaptability to fluctuating 
lighting conditions. Collectively, these constraints significantly hinder

Traditional counting methods predominantly rely on hand-crafted 
features, such as Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) and color 
histograms for pedestrian tracking [6]. However, these methods exhibit 
limited generalization in dynamic environments and are highly sus­
ceptible to noise interference during data association. Given the 
inherent limitations of hand-crafted features, Multi-Object Tracking 
(MOT) methods have become prevalent, following a "detection-feature 
extraction-data association" framework. The performance of such 
methods is heavily dependent on the accuracy of object detection, the 
discriminative power of extracted features, and the robustness of the 
association strategy. In complex scenes, challenges such as object oc­
clusion, deformation, appearance variation, similar-object interference 
leading to identity switches (ID switches), and real-time processing re­
quirements significantly exacerbate tracking difficulties, posing serious 
challenges for traditional counting methods [7]. The OC-SORT algo­
rithm proposed by Cao et al. [8] introduces a stable tracking framework 
by integrating Kalman filtering, the Hungarian algorithm, and multiple 
association recovery strategies. Building on this, Deep OC-SORT [9] 
incorporates stability-enhancing mechanisms to improve adaptability in 
complex scenarios. Nevertheless, substantial challenges remain in 
handling occlusion, object deformation, appearance changes, and 
interference from similar targets, which continue to limit the perfor­
mance gains of current approaches and present bottlenecks to further 
advancement.
The rapid progression of deep learning technologies is driving a 
transformative shift towards automation and intelligence in tomato 
cultivation [10], with researchers increasingly integrating these meth­
odologies for crop yield estimation. For instance, while Fast R-CNN [11] 
offers high detection accuracy, its substantial computational resource 
demands and deployment complexity present practical challenges. 
Similarly, the SSD model [12], reduces the computational load, but its 
intricate parameter tuning and sensitivity to small objects limit its effi­
cacy for yield estimation, making it more suitable for small-target ap­
plications like pest and disease monitoring [13]. Tenorio and Caarls [14] 
developed an automated tomato detection model capable of classifying 
fruit maturity levels. Xu et al. [15] proposed an edge-friendly framework 
that enhances inference efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Sapkota 
et al. [16] developed an instance segmentation algorithm for robotic 
tasks. Gao et al. [17] introduced an unstructured detection framework 
that enables seamless integration into harvesting robots. Paul et al. [18] 
presented a complete solution for agricultural robots that integrates 
detection, localization, tracking, and application, thereby advancing the 
development of automated capsicum harvesting technology. These ap­
proaches provide feasible solutions for tomato detection, counting, and 
algorithm deployment.
Tomato detection and counting remain challenging tasks, especially 
in complex greenhouse environments. Specifically, truss tomatoes in 
greenhouse settings often exhibit severe fruit overlap and mutual oc­
clusion, leading to blurred object boundaries and difficulty in dis­
tinguishing individual fruits. Meanwhile, the image acquisition process 
introduces significant motion blur due to robot movement or fruit 
cluster swaying, and the pose variations of tomatoes further increase 
detection difficulty. These factors collectively make it difficult for 
traditional detection and tracking methods to meet practical re­
quirements in terms of accuracy and robustness. Therefore, there is an 
urgent need for a lightweight detection framework that balances 
accuracy, speed, and computational efficiency, especially one that can 
effectively address overlapping occlusion, motion blur, and pose varia­
tion. However, the large parameter size and high computational de­
mands of deep learning models hinder their deployment on resource- 
constrained edge devices, and existing edge intelligence techniques 
still face challenges in interpretability, security, and energy efficiency 
[19]. At present, counting algorithms specifically designed for such 
highly challenging scenarios are still lacking.
To address the above challenges, this study proposes a lightweight 
cascaded neural network for tomato detection and counting. By opti­
mizing the network architecture, integrating multi-object real-time 
tracking algorithms, and developing a multi-view compensation 
method, the proposed framework achieves real-time, efficient, and ac­
curate detection and counting of tomatoes in complex greenhouse en­
vironments. This offers a promising solution to the labor shortages and 
inaccurate yield estimation currently faced by the tomato cultivation 
industry.
2. Data collection and processing
From November 2024 to March 2025, image acquisition of cherry 
tomatoes was conducted at the Kaisheng Haofeng (Laixi) Smart Agri­
culture Industrial Park in Qingdao, Shandong Province, China. The to­
mato cultivation base adopts a modern planting model with 
standardized plant layouts. Each row consists of two lines of tomato 
plants growing in opposite directions, and the growth conditions are 
largely consistent. However, occlusion and overlap among fruits are 
common. The image acquisition device used was the Intel RealSense 
Depth Camera D455, which was mounted on a mobile inspection robot. 
The distance between the camera and the cherry tomato plants ranged 
from 400 mm to 600 mm. Videos were recorded at a resolution of 1280 
× 720 with a frame rate of 30 FPS. The collected videos encompass 
cherry tomatoes captured across various planting zones during both 
morning and afternoon time periods, viewing angles, and environmental 
conditions, with all weather conditions being sunny. In the sample 
collection scenarios, normal lighting accounts for approximately 65%, 
low lighting for about 25%, and strong lighting for about 10%. The 
sample proportions across different time periods are generally balanced. 
An overview of the typical greenhouse cultivation scene is shown in 
Fig. 1.
In this study, the collected video data were divided and processed in 
two ways. A portion of the data was directly used as the validation set for 
evaluating the model’s detection performance. The remaining video 
data were processed by the keyframe extraction at 60 FPS intervals. A 
total of 1792 images were extracted and annotated using the Makesense 
AI platform [20]. Based on the color and size characteristics of cherry 
tomatoes, the detected targets are divided into two categories: ripe to­
matoes and unripe tomatoes, marked as "rt" and "ut" respectively. The 
dataset comprises 17,940 “ut” instances and 33,433 “rt” instances. For 
occluded fruits, annotators inferred the complete boundary based on 
visible contours and contextual information, drawing the bounding box 
within the occluded boundary to ensure object completeness under oc­
clusion. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the full contours of occluded tomatoes 
were annotated to improve the accuracy of object representation. All 
annotation results were standardized and saved in .txt format, and the 
dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing subsets in a 
7:2:1 ratio.

To meet the real-time and efficient detection requirements of 
greenhouse inspection robots, this study proposes a lightweight multi- 
task cascaded neural network capable of performing real-time multi- 
object detection and multi-region tracking-based counting. The overall

In operation, the network first integrates a lightweight detection

3.2. Lightweight multi-object real-time detection network
3.2.1. Lightweight feature extraction backbone network
The input image first passes through a GhostConv module for light­
weight convolution, generating redundant feature maps via cheap linear 
transformations to reduce computational cost. It then enters L‑MFA, to 
extract and fuse multi‑scale contextual information. Next, the G‑FConv 
module further compresses parameters and computational cost using 
sparse convolution and linear expansion. This G‑FConv is stacked twice 
to enhance feature abstraction. Finally, the Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast 
module, denoted as SPPF, performs multi‑scale pooling on the feature 
maps, aggregating information from different receptive fields and 
outputting multi‑level feature maps such as P3, P4 and P5 for subse­
quent detection tasks. Among these components, the G‑FConv module is 
specifically designed to reduce computational overhead and memory 
access, and its architecture is detailed as follows. G-FConv is a sparse 
convolution module derived from the lightweight GhostNet [21], 
designed to reduce computational overhead and memory access. The 
architecture of the proposed module is illustrated in Fig. 4. The core idea 
is to generate redundant features through linear transformations, while 
integrating PConv [22] to perform convolution on a subset of the input

To further reduce model computational complexity and enhance 
real-time processing performance, this study proposes a lightweight 
multi-scale feature aggregation module (L-MFA). As illustrated in Fig. 5, 
the module is primarily composed of Conv and CG Blocks. By concate­
nating the outputs of different CG Blocks with the original feature maps, 
the module achieves more effective multi-scale information aggrega­
tion. In addition, the convolutional operations within the module effi­
ciently compress the feature maps, substantially reducing computational 
cost. The L-MFA module adopts the classical Cross-Stage Partial (CSP) 
structure characterized by a “split–process–integrate” design, ensuring 
efficient gradient flow, while incorporating the context-guided (CG) 
Block to further strengthen feature fusion. The L-MFA module uses a 
Split operation to directly pass a portion of the features across layers, 
while the other portion is processed through multiple CG block for deep 
feature extraction. Finally, the multi‑level information is fused via 
concatenation and a 1 × 1 convolution. Given an input feature map of 
size h × w × cin, it first passes through a 1 × 1 convolution (stride 1, 
padding 0) that compresses the number of channels to cout, resulting in 
an output size of h × w × cout. Then a Split operation is performed, 
which evenly divides the feature map into two branches along the 
channel dimension, each having 0.5 × cout channels, i.e., h × w ×
0.5cout. The left branch remains unchanged and is directly kept for later 
concatenation. The right branch goes into a sub-network consisting of n 
CG bBock modules stacked in series. Each CG Block has an optional 
shortcut connection and maintains the same spatial size and channel 
count, thus its output is still h × w × 0.5cout. Finally, a Concat operation 
concatenates the original left branch feature map and the n outputs from 
the right branch along the channel dimension. This design not only in­
herits the advantages of the CSP architecture but also leverages the su­
perior capability of CGNet [23] in extracting contextual information. To 
enhance multi-scale contextual information without significantly 
increasing computation, we introduce the CG Block, which captures 
local, surrounding, and global features across multiple receptive fields. 
The CG Block, a key component of the CGNet model, employs a 
four-dimensional context fusion mechanism to capture and integrate 
local features, surrounding context, and global context. In doing so, it

3.3. Multi-view compensation tracking and counting network
In complex scenarios, real-time multi-object tracking and counting 
often encounter multiple interferences such as occlusion, motion dy­
namics, and viewpoint variations. To overcome the limitations of 
traditional approaches, this study introduces the Deep OC-SORT algo­
rithm and proposes a multi-view compensation (MVC) method. By 
integrating multiple collaborative modules, the robustness and accuracy 
of both tracking and counting are significantly enhanced.
3.3.1. Deep OC-SORT algorithm
Deep OC-SORT is a multi-object tracking algorithm that integrates 
deep learning with Kalman filtering. The algorithm comprises three key 
modules: Camera Motion Compensation (CMC), Dynamic Appearance 
(DA), and Adaptive Weighting (AW). These modules are cascaded to 
enable real-time tracking in dynamic scenes. The algorithm utilizes a 
pre-trained convolutional neural network to extract target appearance 
descriptors and fuses appearance information with motion cues through 
weighted similarity measures. During data association, the Hungarian 
algorithm is employed to solve the optimal assignment problem, while 
Kalman filtering enhances robustness in tracking fast-moving objects.
After the object detector produces a set of bounding boxes from a 
single frame, the DA module refines the detection results by extracting 
highly discriminative Re-Identification (ReID) features using a deep 
neural network, and constructing target appearance representation 
vectors. Existing trajectories are predicted using Kalman filtering, after 
which the CMC module applies an affine transformation matrix to 
geometrically correct the predicted states, thereby mitigating state drift 
caused by camera motion and enabling more accurate motion estima­
tion. Finally, the AW module dynamically adjusts the fusion weights 
between motion information (Mahalanobis distance) and appearance 
information (cosine distance) based on the uncertainty represented by 
the covariance matrix output from the Kalman filter. This results in an 
optimized association cost matrix, thereby improving data association 
accuracy in complex scenarios. The overall pipeline is illustrated in 
Fig. 7.
3.3.2. Multi-view compensation method
To address the issues of false detections and missed detections during 
inspection robot operations, this study proposes an algorithmic frame­
work based on multi-view compensation (MVC). By performing real- 
time multi-view cooperative detection and tracking on the video 
streams captured by the robot, the method significantly improves 
counting accuracy within continuous spatial regions. As illustrated in 
Fig. 8, the morphological features of the same target in two-dimensional 
images vary markedly due to differences in spatial position. Therefore, 
the detection area in this study is divided into three subregions located 
at different positions. The MVC method partitions each frame into 
distinct regions and performs multi-view cooperative detection and 
compensation, thereby decomposing the complex global multi-object 
tracking task into a set of parallel local subtasks.
The implementation of the MVC method proceeds as follows: first, a 
spatial partitioning module generates K adjacent subregions; second, 
each frame is processed by the cascaded network to produce unified 
outputs for ID assignment; finally, in the cross-view compensation 
module, contextual information across subregions is utilized to refine 
counting decisions. The method is built upon two key principles: (1) 
Local Appearance Consistency - by exploiting the fact that target

whether an ID changes is determined by a comprehensive cost matrix. 
This matrix fuses motion information (Mahalanobis distance) and 
appearance feature similarity (cosine distance), and the optimal 
assignment is solved using the Hungarian algorithm. To ensure tracking 
quality, a detection confidence threshold is set to 0.5: only detections 
with confidence higher than 0.5 are fed into the tracker; low‑confidence 
detections are directly filtered out, reducing false alarms. Additionally, 
an IoU matching threshold of 0.3 is defined: when the Intersection over 
Union (IoU) of two bounding boxes exceeds 0.3, the algorithm considers 
them as potentially the same object and allows subsequent identity 
matching. If the IoU is equal to or below 0.3, they are treated as different 
targets and no association is performed. Under short-distance tracking 
conditions, where the effects of occlusion and illumination variation are 
relatively small, the target ID remains stable without switching, thereby 
enabling stable tracking over short ranges.
The Deep OC-SORT algorithm exhibits clear limitations when 
handling multi-target scenarios with complex backgrounds and highly 
variable target postures. The integration of the MVC method effectively 
addresses these deficiencies by employing a multi-region parallel 
detection and counting strategy. This not only mitigates false and missed 
detections caused by local occlusions or restricted viewpoints but also 
improves adaptability and accuracy in dense fruit distributions and 
complex greenhouse scenes with severe background interference, 
enabling a more comprehensive and precise assessment of tomato pro­
duction conditions in greenhouses.
3.4. Edge device deployment
The greenhouse inspection robot deployed is equipped with the 
Jetson Orin Nano as its main control unit—an edge computing device 
developed by NVIDIA that combines low power consumption with high- 
performance AI inference and computational capability. To ensure 
efficient operation under resource-constrained hardware conditions, 
this study applies lightweight architecture optimization and parameter 
tuning to both the backbone and detection head of the model, thereby 
achieving high detection performance while maintaining computational 
efficiency. Furthermore, TensorRT is employed to accelerate model 
inference. Techniques such as weight and activation precision calibra­
tion, layer and tensor fusion, and multi-stream execution are utilized to 
significantly enhance inference performance without compromising 
accuracy. The model is first converted from ONNX format to a TensorRT 
engine using the trtexec command-line tool provided by TensorRT, and 
then deployed for inference using TensorRT Runtimes. The deployment 
workflow of the developed multi-task cascaded neural network on the 
edge device is illustrated in Fig. 9.

4.2. Result analysis of model training
During robotic inspection, performance is often influenced by factors 
such as occlusion, illumination variability, and other environmental 
disturbances. The mean Average Precision (mAP) serves as a compre­
hensive metric to evaluate the model’s effectiveness under such complex 
conditions. In real-world operation scenarios, challenges such as back­
ground clutter and dense fruit overlap impose a dual requirement on the 
detection model: accurately identifying all fruits ("no misses") while 
avoiding false identification of non-fruit objects ("no false positives"). A 
low Recall would lead to underestimation of crop yield, whereas a low 
Precision could result in yield overestimation. The F1 Score, as a har­
monic mean of Precision and Recall, provides an effective measure of 
how well the model balances these two objectives. Consequently, this 
study places particular emphasis on analyzing both mAP and F1 Score to 
comprehensively evaluate model performance.

occurs because a higher frame rate reduces inter‑frame displacement of

can be attributed to the reduced inter-frame motion variation at higher 
frame rates, which enables the model to obtain more stable positional 
information, thereby effectively reducing false negatives (FN) and 
enhancing counting robustness. Furthermore, MVC consistently out­
performs SVC across all frame rate settings. In complex scenarios under 
low frame rate conditions (10 FPS), MVC achieves a 15.3% reduction in

attains its best performance (MAE = 38.33; RMSE = 38.41). Relative to 
SVC, MAE and RMSE are reduced by 35.8% and 39.5%, respectively, 
further widening the performance gap. These results confirm that the 
MVC method effectively compensates for missed detections in single- 
view settings through multi-view feature fusion, demonstrating strong 
robustness and environmental adaptability.

significant computational overhead.
For tracking and counting tasks, the study addresses the ID switch 
issue inherent in Deep OC-SORT by introducing the MVC method. 
Through a region-based divide-and-conquer strategy, this approach 
enables multi-angle feature complementation, substantially reducing 
counting errors in complex greenhouse scenes. Nevertheless, challenges 
such as ID switches caused by occlusion and overlapping, as well as 
positional state shifts of tomatoes due to camera motion across consec­
utive frames, still require further refinement and optimization. Addi­
tionally, the current multi-view compensation (MVC) strategy relies on 
fixed regional partitions, which might lack flexibility when the inspec­
tion robot undergoes non-uniform motion or drastic vibrations. The 
cumulative error in counting over extremely long planting rows also 
remains a technical bottleneck. Integrating inertial measurement units 
(IMU) data with visual odometry could potentially mitigate these 
motion-induced tracking failures in future iterations.
6. Conclusion
In response to the application requirements for multi-object real- 
time detection and accurate counting of tomatoes in complex green­
house environments, this study proposes a lightweight cascaded 
framework integrating detection, tracking, counting, and compensation. 
Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed network achieves 
real-time processing performance of 31.3 FPS on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin 
Nano edge device, while maintaining high detection accuracy (90.8%) 
and counting precision (95.2%). In terms of model optimization, 
architectural refinements and parameter compression reduced compu­
tational cost to 5.3 GFLOPs (a 40.4% decrease) and shrank the model 
size to 3.98 MB, significantly enhancing the feasibility of edge deploy­
ment. The proposed MVC method utilizes a multi-view redundant 
complementarity mechanism to significantly suppress miscounting 
caused by identity switching. This approach effectively reduces the 
counting error in dense and occluded scenarios by 95.6%. Validation in 
real-world production settings confirmed a yield prediction accuracy of 
93%, providing robust technical support for dynamic harvest planning 
and supply chain loss reduction. The proposed method offers an inno­
vative solution for tomato yield assessment in complex greenhouse en­
vironments and can be extended to the detection of other crop 
production scenarios, holding substantial theoretical significance and 
practical application potential.
