segments under uncontrolled conditions

Abstract
Precision agriculture covers a wide range of information and communications technologies aimed at supporting current
challenges in crop estimation, productivity increase, and food security. In particular, crop yield estimation can provide very
valuable information on crop distribution, with the aim of optimising logistics and harvest timing. This paper focuses on
deep learning-based regression solutions for estimating the number of visible oranges on trees, from real-world crop row
videos captured by a camera placed on a farm vehicle. Count predictions based on individual frames were compared with
those based on variable size sequences of frames centred on each tree (video segments). The performance of three deep
neural networks designed for regression was evaluated in terms of the regression error and the uncertainty of the estimates,
and differences were analysed using nonparametric hypothesis testing. Experiments were conducted on a new dataset
composed of annotated video segments of orange tree rows acquired under uncontrolled conditions, which has been made
publicly available. Results statistically prove the value of considering multiple frames and the feasibility of yield esti-
mation by regression in the wild. These ﬁndings are expected to contribute to optimising decision-making in crop resource
management. Unlike most efforts so far, which involve counting fruits by detection in tree images usually captured
manually, this work explores counting fruits by regression on trees from real-world video data.

1 Introduction
Precision agriculture (PA) [1] is an emerging applied
research ﬁeld that aims to optimise farm practices, through
the adoption of sensor and smart technologies like Internet
of Things (IoT) [2] and artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) [3–5].

Over the entire crop life cycle, production estimation

The unprecedented progress of the machine learning and
computer vision methods in recent years has allowed the
automation of very effective counting solutions [8]. Peri-

provide rich information on the crop evolution as well as
on the efﬁcacy of agricultural practices such as irrigation,
fertilisation, etc. Besides, knowing the yield spatial distri-
bution can promote more rational resource assignments.
Counting objects in images is a very popular task in the
ﬁeld of computer vision [9]. Most solution methods can be
classiﬁed into two dominant paradigms: counting by
detection [4, 10] and counting by regression [11]. The ﬁrst
one entails a ﬁrst step in which object locations are elicited,
and a second step in which detected objects are counted.
The success of this process strongly depends on the
detection accuracy, which is usually very sensitive to
object density, object occlusions, and changing lighting
conditions [12, 13]. Another drawback of counting by
detection is the considerable effort required to annotate
images by marking objects of interest. However, when
counting is the ultimate goal and object locations are not
required, counting by regression has proved to be steadily
more accurate than counting by detection when density of
agricultural objects increases [8]. Regression can poten-
tially deal with more general scenarios, where part of the
production might remain fully hidden by branches, leaves,
or other product units. In addition, regression would lead to
a direct estimation of the total amount of product biomass
(e.g. the total weight in kg), assuming that visible pro-
duction evidence is correlated with the total amount. A
weakness of counting by regression, however, could be a
greater difﬁculty in explaining its predictions.
In the case of fruit load estimation, two main scenarios
can be stressed: counting the number of visible fruits in a
single view of a tree [8, 14] and counting the total number
of fruits on a tree [15–18]. Assuming that visible fruits on a
tree canopy image are a representative sample of the entire
fruit population, the former can be sufﬁcient to provide
reliable fruit distribution statistics, what can support the
comparison of production between different crop regions
(spatial distribution), different seasons (temporal distribu-
tion), and the optimisation of resource assignment in farm
activities. The second scenario usually involves images
from multiple (two or four) tree views (sides), from which
the total load is estimated. This approach seems appropri-
ate for orchards where separation between trees admits a
systematic

According to [12], two-side imaging methods usually suf-
fer from fruit load underestimation, while four-side imag-
ing, from fruit load overestimation.
Unlike previous efforts focused on a single image per
side, this work addresses the estimation of the number of
visible oranges on a tree side, by combining counts
obtained by direct regression over multiple crop row video
frames. Using multiple frames per tree is expected to
provide robustness to inaccuracies in tree detection and to
scene complexities such as fruit overlapping, foliage den-
sity, illumination conditions, and sensor-to-fruit angle and
distance. To our knowledge, no previous work has dealt
with fruit count estimation based on multiple video frames.
Experiments are based on a new database composed of
four real videos captured by a camera installed in a farm
vehicle that moved along crop rows. From each video,
frame sequences centred on each tree were extracted. Each
tree-centred sequence was annotated with the average of
the corresponding series of frame-based manually anno-
tated counts.
The installation of video cameras and edge computing
hardware on farm vehicles, instead of, for example, on
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV or drones), is driven by
two fundamental advantages: high frequency of use and
low cost. Over the course of a year, farmers use their farm
vehicles many times to perform different agricultural tasks,
including planting, fertilising, spraying, and harvesting.
Recording videos of side views of trees each time would
entail negligible costs. In addition, handling the equipment
involved would not require speciﬁc training or regulation.
Therefore, our solution can be considered simple, cost-ef-
fective, scalable, and easily integrable.

2 Related work
For simplicity, this analysis is limited to yield estimation
from images using deep neural networks (DNN), with a
focus on counting solutions. Unlike conventional image
processing based on hand-crafted features, DNN are able to
discover complex features on their own and to use them to
feed an optimal or near-optimal decision rule usually
inferred within a single learning process. More extensive
reviews on smart agriculture based on artiﬁcial intelligence
can be found in [21–23]. Compared to other surveys on
precision agriculture, [21, 23] proposed taxonomies driven
by machine learning techniques rather than by smart
farming applications. They put special emphasis on
advanced ensemble approaches in the context of crop
recommendation systems [22], while exploring opportuni-
ties to deploy them through interactive Streamlit-powered
web applications. A comprehensive review on generative
AI in crop recommendation was conducted in [23], mainly
including solutions based on variational autoencoders
(VAE) and generative adversarial networks (GAN). These
techniques are capable of generating synthetic samples that
closely mimics real-world data, to promote better gener-
alisation of DNN models in contexts of real data scarcity.
The exploration of related works was divided into two
categories considering the nature of the images: aerial
acquired by UAV or satellites and ground images acquired
by mobile devices or farm vehicles. In the former,
depending on the pixel resolution that could be tens of
metres per pixel in satellite images, yield is usually esti-
mated by regression in amounts of product biomass since
product units are difﬁcult to detect. In the second category,
both paradigms, detection and regression, are suitable.
The use of remote sensing images to forecast crop yield
is an emerging research area [24]. DNN models learn how
to relate spectral and spatial data from hyperspectral ima-
ges or from vegetation indexes, such as NDVI (normalised
difference vegetation index), to ground-truth yield data. A
wheat yield prediction model was introduced in [25],
where yield estimates in kg/ha based on the leaf area index
(LAI) and leaf nitrogen content (LNC) retrieved from UAV
hyperspectral imagery achieved a mean absolute percent-
age error (MAPE) value of 9.36%. A Bayesian neural
network model was trained for corn yield prediction from
heterogeneous data sources such as time series satellite
products, sequential climate observations, soil property
maps, and historical corn yield records, leading to corn
yield estimates with a MAPE value of 9.56% [26].
Examples of paddy rice mapping can be found in [27, 28].
Despite the signiﬁcant breakthrough achieved by satel-
lites and drones in PA, the use of detection algorithms such
as YOLO [29] on ground images is still the dominant trend
probably due to its lower costs, regulations, and need for
training. A version of YOLOv4 [30] to count olive fruit ﬂy
is proposed in [31]. In [12], YOLOv2, v3, and v4 models
were assessed through transfer learning in detecting and
counting ripe oranges. YOLOv4 precision and recall in
orange detection over test images were 91.23%, 92.8%,
respectively. The impact of lighting conditions on detec-
tion effectiveness was investigated, as well as the use of
two-side and four-side manually acquired imaging in total
load estimation. In [32], an AI-based drone system was
introduced to detect and count tomato ﬂowers and fruits
through YOLOv5 [33]. Class accuracies were 85%, 99%,
and 50% for red tomato, green tomato, and ﬂowers,
respectively.

More recent counting solutions can be found in [34–36].
A Mask R-CNN model [40] integrating Swin Trans-
former [41] as the backbone was introduced in [34] to
segment (detect) fruits on a peach orchard. Experiments
were performed on a new database with 125 images of four
trees and nine bunches acquired at different distances. The
average number of peaches per image was 12 and 2.7 in the
images focused on trees and bunches, respectively. The
detection performance was measured in terms of the mean
average precision (mAP), reaching a value of 0.60. Another
solution for peach identiﬁcation and counting was proposed
in [35]. A lightweight YOLOv8 model using the Mobile-
NetV3 [42] as its backbone achieved precision and recall
values of 0.979 and a mAP of 0.99 on fruit-focused images
acquired by a smartphone. YOLOv8s was also the base
model in a rice panicle counting study from drone remote
sensing images [36]. Using the panicle count along with
the thousand-grain weight, number of grains per panicle,
and actual yield data, a number of machine learning models
were trained to ultimately perform rice yield estimation.
Results showed mAP, precision, and recall values of 0.98,
0.98, and 0.968, respectively, for the counting solution, and
a R2 value of 0.84 for the ﬁnal yield estimation.
In an effort to deploy a real-world solution, a mobile
application to assist farmers estimate apple tree yield is
presented in [37]. It includes a linear segmentation model
with empirically determined coefﬁcients, which classiﬁes
pixels based on their values in different colour bands. The
performance of counting apples from segmented images
was a MAPE value of 8.52%. Experiments relied on two-
side images of 40 trees from a single row of a Pink Lady
apple tree orchard. The Pearson correlation coefﬁcient
between counted apples and the actual number of apples on
trees was calculated (0.6), showing the opportunities for
estimating the actual yield from the observed yield. This
proposal does not involve any machine learning process.
When object locations are not needed, counting by
regression can provide more efﬁcient load estimates [8].
Counting by regression learns how to map image features
into a ﬁnal count estimate or into a density map. This
paradigm is particular suitable in complex scenes, where
high densities of overlapping objects could make detection
of every unit (instance) of interest virtually impossible.
In [8], two methods based on regression and one based on
detection were compared on 12 agricultural datasets for
counting fruit and grains. Results proved that counting by
regression clearly outperforms counting by detection in
images with large numbers of objects and high levels of
occlusion, while performs comparably at lower densities.
In [38], a regression model based on Inception-ResNet was
trained to directly estimate the number of visible fruits on a
tomato plant. Training was carried out on synthetic images,
while test involves only real images. The counting accu-
racy on test images, based on the relative error of the
regression results, was 91.03%.
In [39], two strategies of counting plant leaves were
proposed: counting by direct regression and counting by
detection/density estimation. The ﬁrst one involves a fea-
ture pyramid network [19] designed to estimate leaf counts
from multiple image scales and to combine these estimates
into an extra DNN output. The second strategy was based
on fusing a density map with estimates by direct regression
through a network driven by two losses: detection based
and regression based. Both methods showed similar per-
formance.

Count efforts reported so far are based on a single image
per tree view and have been mostly focused on controlled
scenarios. Of these, a majority has adopted counting by
detection over counting by regression. This paper explores
a different use case: to count by regression visible oranges
on trees from video data acquired by a farm vehicle in the
wild. Each tree’s load estimate involves a sequence of
frames (roughly) centred on that tree, from which a cor-

obtained. The set mean is proposed as the tree load esti-

addition, multiple measurements admit the calculation of
uncertainty measures.
3 Materials and methods
A real use case is recreated: (1) a camera on a tractor
records videos of rows of orange trees; (2) trees are
detected and represented by tree-centric frame sequences,
and (3) each tree’s visible fruit load is estimated by com-
bining count estimates by frames. Figure 1 depicts a
workﬂow overview.
Since this work focuses on multi-frame load estimation,
it is assumed that there exists a method, outside the scope
of this work, capable of extracting frame sequences ideally
centred on target trees. For the purposes of this work, frame
sequences centred on each tree were extracted manually.
We hypothesise that it would be enough for a sequence to
contain relevant frames not necessarily centred on the
target tree. This loose detection method is expected to be
tolerant to inaccuracies in tree location.

3.2 Data annotation
Data annotation consists in assigning each sample a piece
of information (e.g. a label or a scalar) that represents what
needs to be learned and, eventually, predicted. In the
counting task, the annotation is the number of visible
objects in a scene. In our use case, it is the number of
visible oranges on a tree image.
The annotation process is intended to determine a
sequence of frames centred on each tree (loose detection),
and to annotate both individual frames and the sequence
with the number of (clearly) visible oranges. Due to the
effort involved in manual labelling, just eleven frames per
tree were considered. Sequences were semi-automatically
extracted. First, for each tree, a key frame was manually
identiﬁed; then, segments of 2n þ 1 frames centred on each
key frame, with n 2 N (n ¼ 5 in this study), were auto-
matically extracted to build the tree-centric sequences (tree
representation). Eleven frames were considered sufﬁcient

3.2.1 Annotation by detection: limitations
Automatic annotation of frames with pretrained detection
methods was considered, in particular, with the YOLOv5
and YOLOv7 models. They have both been trained to
detect oranges from a signiﬁcant number of diverse sam-
ples, so their output can be post-processed to count the
number of objects detected. However, exploratory tests
showed a number of systematic detection defects; thus, it
was decided not to use them. Figure 5 shows examples of
detection outputs. First, YOLOv5 tends to overestimate the
number of oranges, as it counts some oranges repeatedly
and detects oranges in dark regions (with lots of shadows)
unsystematically. Second, YOLOv7 hardly detects oranges
in heavily shaded regions. Both methods are also affected
by the presence of orange clusters (instances are not
detected separately) and by oranges on the ground. In the
latter case, the system could learn (from the context) to
differentiate oranges on a tree (should be counted) from
those on the ground (should not be counted).
Despite these limitations, the noisy output of YOLOv5
and YOLOv7 can be considered coarse (and useful)
approximations of the target count. Thus, in order to vali-
date manual annotations, correlations between the outputs
of YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 (reference values) and manual-
driven annotations for each sequence length (see Eq. 3) are
calculated as part of the experiments.

3.5 Regression models
Two DNN models were adapted for regression: Efﬁ-
cientNetB0 [20] and feature pyramid network [19] (FPN)
using the former model as the backbone to make results
comparable. EfﬁcientNetB0 is a convolutional neural net-
work resulting from a scaling method that optimises depth,
width, and resolution jointly until reaching a light and
balanced architecture. FPN combines low-resolution maps
with strong semantic information, with high-resolution
maps with weak semantic information, on the so-called
top-down pathway. It was originally designed for multi-
scale object detection, although it is reshaped here to cope
with regression.
Table 3 summarises the EfﬁcientNetB0 model and
identiﬁes the lateral connections used by FPN to inject
spatial details into multi-scale semantic maps. When used
alone, the convolutional base of EfﬁcientNetB0 is con-
nected to a small fully connected network (FCN) as
described at the bottom of the table.
It is expected that combining FPN multi-scale maps will
result in better opportunities to represent oranges of dif-
ferent sizes. Two types of feature combination schemes are
proposed. The ﬁrst one combines maps after linearising
them. The second one combines maps after upscaling them
to a common resolution; thus, it considers the spatial dis-
tribution of concepts. They will be denoted as FPN-linear
and FPN-map, respectively. Figures 6 and 7 show both
schemes.

5 Discussion
The problem of counting by regression the number of
visible oranges in trees from crop row videos has been
studied. This is a real-world complex scenario affected by a
number of conditions that make data annotation, model
learning, and model evaluation challenging. As has been
proved in previous studies [48], under heterogeneous
conditions in terms of fruit density, fruit colour, fruit size,
and light exposure, performances of machine learning
solutions drop signiﬁcantly.
In this study, acquisition conditions of each crop row
video were unique, since they were captured at different
times and at various locations in the ﬁeld. A second factor
was the wide range of orange tones observed, going from
very soft and bright colours close to white, in the presence
of high solar radiation, to very dark colours in regions with
a lot of shadows. Third, overlaps and shadows produced by
orange clusters or by branches and leaves were also very
frequent. In addition, data partitions respected the natural
distribution of trees in rows, so different acquisition con-
ditions were not shared between training and testing pha-
ses. This reinforced realistic conditions and allowed
unbiased measurements of model generalisation. This data
use pattern is recommended for future research. All of the
above contributed to creating an inherently complex
regression task.
Data annotation (assigning each frame the number of
visible oranges) became a ﬁrst challenge. How much visual
evidence should be considered to count an orange? How
much human experience is involved in identifying an
orange? How much visual evidence is necessary for the
regression model to learn an effective mapping? After
evaluating several alternatives, a conservative manual
labelling strategy was chosen aimed at minimising sub-
jective decisions, although perhaps to the detriment of the
number of false negatives. The result was successfully
validated by calculating correlations with the outputs of
pretrained detection methods (YOLOv5 and YOLOv7),
which were considered rough (noisy) approximations of the

discussed above, the number of visible fruits on a tree is
not the ultimate goal, but only a means (a sample of the
unknown fruit load) to achieve an end (estimation of actual

Regression solutions for fruit counting in more con-
trolled and simpler tasks have shown error rates around
9% [38]. Therefore, the MedAPE of 13:7% shown by FPN-
map for 7-frame sequences can be considered a promising
rate in a complex scenario.
Results were obtained from a speciﬁc acquisition con-
ﬁguration, including the frame rate (30 FPS), the FOV
(about 2 ms wide by 2.5 ms high), and the farm vehicle
speed (3-5 km/h). At this speed, the row segment occupied
by each tree would have been described by at least 30
frames. Considering that tree-centric 7-frame sequences led
to the best results, the proposed solution should be robust to
increases in vehicle speed and decreases in frame rate, and
even to different degrees of video subsampling. Regarding
the FOV, considering that the estimated number of oranges
is expected to be used as a sample of the unknown popu-
lation of oranges in the tree, a signiﬁcant reduction in the
FOV should not have a signiﬁcant impact on the ability of
the estimates to characterise the relative yield distribution
across the crop ﬁeld, as long as the observable area in the
image includes a signiﬁcant portion of the tree canopy. The
relationships between acquisition parameters and the per-
formance of machine vision-based methods offer future
research opportunities.
To date, estimating production in orange orchards still
relies mainly on human observers by simple inspection of
the visible fruits. They empirically relate observed distri-
butions of visible fruits with physical magnitudes of pro-
duct biomass (e.g. kg.) based on their experience over
many years. Given the manual and subjective nature of this
practice, it is considered inaccurate, time-consuming,
tedious, non-scalable, and observer dependent. The ulti-
mate goal of this research is to create an automatic and
systematic observer which, based on objective measure-
ments of the distribution of visible oranges along the crop
rows, makes a robust and accurate estimate of the pro-
duction of an orange orchard. This learning objective
requires yield annotations from past harvests not yet
available.
Meanwhile, the robust estimation of the number of
visible fruits per tree could have a straightforward appli-
cation. Assuming that the number of visible fruits on a tree
is positively and signiﬁcantly correlated with the total,
unknown fruit load for most trees, it is already possible to
estimate the relative frequency distribution of fruit load
across the orchard. This information can support the
comparison of production between different crop areas
(spatial distribution) or different seasons (temporal distri-
bution), as well as the optimisation of resource assignment
in farm activities.
6 Conclusions
This work explores the opportunities of counting the
number of visible oranges on trees by direct regression,
from crop row videos captured in real scenarios. To the
best of our knowledge, no previous work has dealt with a
similar use case.
Three
regression
models
were
compared
(Efﬁ-
cientNetB0 and two others based on Feature Pyramid
Networks) when computing count estimates from individ-
ual frames and from tree-centred sequences of frames. The
feasibility of counting by regression the number of oranges
from tree images in the wild was demonstrated. In partic-
ular, the beneﬁt of frame sequences in calculating more
accurate estimates was statistically established, as well as
the advantages of combining FPN multi-scale maps. The
lowest regression errors were obtained from sequences of 7
and 11 frames, achieving reductions of 25% of the error
calculated from individual frames.
A new annotated database was also created. It is com-
posed of crop row videos together with annotations
(number of visible oranges) of a set of relevant tree-related
frames. It is now available to the scientiﬁc community.
Future extensions of these ideas could consider new
annotation strategies, including more practical (although
difﬁcult) ways of measuring production such as the total
weight in kg of fruits on the tree. Solutions that process
entire video sequences, rather than combining frame-level
decisions, could also be evaluated.
