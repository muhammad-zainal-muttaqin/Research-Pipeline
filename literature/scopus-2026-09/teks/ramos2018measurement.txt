A B S T R A C T
In this article, a method for determination of the ripening rate of coﬀee branches is presented. This is achieved
through analysis of 3D information obtained with a monocular camera in outdoor environments and under
uncontrolled lighting, contrast, and occlusion conditions. This study was performed on 30 coﬀee branches,
monitored throughout the entire harvest season, at each of nine collection times, directly in the countryside. On
each branch checked, the fruit was counted in three maturation stages: unripe, semi-ripe and ripe. Subsequently,
the real ripeness percentage was computed for each stage of development. Images were acquired from the same
manually checked branches, and the developed algorithm was run. This algorithm performs a reconstruction
producing a point cloud, employing Structure from Motion (SfM). In all, 17 features were selected to describe the
three maturation stages, leaves, and stem. These features were calculated in a region proportional to the size of
the point cloud. The 3D structures of the coﬀee fruits on the branch were obtained and classiﬁed, with between
42% and 92% of correct classiﬁcation percentage for the unripe, semi-ripe, and ripe developmental stages, and
the best classiﬁcation model was selected, with a neighborhood that was 6.5% of the total size of the point cloud.
A statistical estimation model was constructed for each stage of development, and it was found that the model
overestimates the percentage of ripe fruit, underestimates that of semi-ripe fruit, and the unripe fruit estimate is
one to one. The errors in estimation were between 3.7% and 8.7%. Correlation was between 0.64 and 0.98.
Finally, temporary monitoring was performed for the ripening of coﬀee branches during the harvest season, with
nine collection times. A maturation index was determined, with which the state of a branch as ready or not for
harvest was correctly determined with 83% eﬃciency.
1. Introduction
Coﬀee is the most highly commercialized product in the world,
second only to petroleum. Its distinguishing characteristic is high
quality, generated by the high altitude at which it is cultivated, and
selective harvesting exclusively of ripe fruit. Year after year, coﬀee
producers incur labor costs, which are dependent on the crop's per-
formance level, and which generally corresponds to 71% of total pro-
duction costs [15]. Additionally, as coﬀee fruits do not all ripen at the
same speed, they are harvested a number of times in the same area,
during the harvest season. This generates expenses which approach
40% of the total cost of production [11]. Various options to reduce the
cost of harvest are to: (i) mechanize harvest, which is diﬃcult in
mountainous terrain, and in areas of high crop density; (ii) wait more
time between two collection times; and/or (iii) harvest ripe, semi-ripe
and green fruit all at the same time. Any of the options described would
directly aﬀect product quality, and thus, the market. However, it is
possible to improve the provision of labor, through use of appropriate
task scheduling on the plantation. In order to do this, one must have
knowledge of the ripeness percentage on coﬀee branches, trees, and
parcels, as well as the quantity of coﬀee to be collected.
This study aims to determine the ripeness index on coﬀee branches
in a non-destructive way, in order to contribute to the development of a
solution which helps to appropriately schedule harvest, based on the
availability of ripe coﬀee fruit present on the branches of the crop. This
index determines a global ripeness factor for the branch, using the
percentages of ripe, unripe, and semi-ripe fruit to assign greater sam-
pling weight to ripe fruit than unripe fruit, and thereby evaluate whe-
ther or not the branch is ready for harvest.
A method is presented to determine the ripeness index of coﬀee
branches, by way of techniques applied to 3D images acquired using a
monocular camera from a mobile device, in uncontrolled lighting,
contrast, and occlusion conditions. The proposed system is able to ob-
tain reconstructions and classiﬁcations from point clouds, tolerating

1.1. Related work
Currently, there are diﬀerent ways to obtain information regarding
agricultural crop production: (i) agroclimatic simulation models; (ii)
plantation phytosanitary status surveys, completed by the farmer; and
(iii) direct measurements taken of the crop. Particularly with coﬀee,
one ﬁnds direct measurements in the ﬁeld, reporting estimations with
an error of between 6% and 40% [29,26] and destructive methods
[35,7], which require picking fruit oﬀof the trees in order to determine
their number and weight. In this way, a production projection is per-
formed, but the ripeness percentage of a parcel is not determined.
Obtaining production information is useful for agricultural labor
scheduling. However, with a crop like coﬀee, mentioning only its pro-
ductive potential is insuﬃcient, as one must know the concentration
and distribution of the fruit on the tree branches, and whether they are
distributed throughout the tree at each collection time. This informa-
tion must be obtained in order to determine not only the number of
workers necessary, but also the harvest strategy for each lot.
Some studies have proposed methods for manual measurement of
the fruit ripeness index [22,1]. Others have developed systems based on
color and machine vision, in order to detect and classify each ripeness
stage based on color, shape, and surface features [5,8,21,17]. However,
all of these studies are based on ripeness detection in post-harvest
processes. They are able to correctly classify fruit 82% of the time, and
operate under controlled light and contrast conditions.
Studies reported by [16,4,14] show systems for fruit maturation
measurement in the ﬁeld, using 2D processing algorithms for oranges,
apples, olives, and bell peppers. In the majority of cases, they require
external illumination systems [4], controlled backgrounds, and con-
trolled distances between the camera and object. In the case of coﬀee,
there is a report of a ﬁeld fruit counting system that uses images ac-
quired in uncontrolled conditions. However, the system, developed by
[26], shows that excessive radiation and self-shading reduce detection
algorithm performance by 50%.
Other systems based on 3D information have been developed for
ﬁeld applications in various crops. For example, with grapes [9], cor-
rect classiﬁcations were obtained 97% of the time, for diﬀerent stages
of fruit ripeness. Other applications measure plant growth parameters;
for example, the height of the plant and foliage area is measured. With
cotton, this is carried out with less than 4.5% error [18]. Others detect
large fruits, and are able to make production estimates, as is the case
with pineapple [23]. In general, 3D vision systems require stereo
cameras, controlled lighting, and control of characteristics such as
distance between the camera and object, and of camera movement
[13,24].
As shown in these related studies, generally, the ripeness index is
measured for each individual fruit [14,34]. However, in investigations
performed by [31], a maturation index related directly to the propor-
tion of available fruit on branches that is ready for harvest, is shown
and expressed as the degree of ripeness (MD) (Eq. (1)). In this expres-
sion, the authors consider four levels of fruit ripeness (dry, overripe,
ripe, and green), and each state is considered with a weight factor
which inﬂuences the MD index, considering lower weighting values for
unripe fruit, and higher weighting values for dry and ripe fruit. Thus,
the index is greater when the branch has mature fruit, or fruit ready for
harvesting.

In this study, the intent is to obtain a branch's fruit ripeness
percentage for each stage of ripeness: unripe, semi-ripe, and ripe.
Additionally, the hope is to determine a ripeness index for the total
ripeness of the coﬀee branch fruits, through use of individual detection

1.2. Research problem and contributions
This work proposes a statistical ripeness index of fruits on a coﬀee
branch, and determines if the fruit on branch is or is not harvestable.
This development contributes with the generation of a tool for coﬀee
farmers to use, in order to determine with direct measurement, and
without destructive samples, whether coﬀee fruits are ready for harvest.
Currently, they do not have an automatic method for this type of direct
measurement in the ﬁeld.
The contributions are the following: (i) volumetric reconstruction of
a coﬀee branch using 3D processing techniques, employing a mobile
device's monocular camera, which tolerates uncontrolled outdoor
conditions; (ii) an estimation model for the ripeness index of fruit on
coﬀee branches with 8% error; and (iii) analysis of coﬀee branch ri-
peness index for the harvest season.
A system for obtention of 3D information from coﬀee branches in
ﬁeld conditions, as shown in Fig. 1, is proposed. The branches marked
in the ﬁeld are manually checked, counting the number of fruits ac-
cording to their ripeness stage, and they are simultaneously checked
with the mobile device's camera. The algorithm developed identiﬁes
vegetative structures present on the branches, and generates point
clouds, which correspond to the coﬀee fruit in diﬀerent stages of ri-
peness, using various classiﬁcation models. Thereafter, models for
branch ripeness percentage estimation are constructed using informa-
tion acquired by images which are processed automatically and ac-
quired manually. Lastly, the statistical models are validated in the
production monitoring of one harvest season with nine collection times.
This paper adheres to the following structure: Section 2, in which
the process for image acquisition, camera calibration, 3D + 2D tech-
niques used for classiﬁed point cloud obtention, and experiments car-
ried out, are shown. The construction and evaluation of statistical
models for ripeness percentage estimation and ripeness index compu-
tation are shown in Section 3. Finally, conclusions, impacts, and po-
tential future studies are mentioned in Section 4.
2. Materials and methods
Coﬀee branches are of plagiotropic nature, which means that they
grow horizontally. Coﬀee production is found in the middle third of the
tree, which makes it impossible to take suitable pictures of the entire
branch, and which instead requires multiple pictures to be taken of the
branch, in order to obtain information about the entire branch.
Additionally, coﬀee fruit develops 360° around the branch.
By way of a 3D analysis of each coﬀee branch checked in ﬁeld
conditions, it was possible to obtain information regarding the ripeness
percentage in each stage of development for fruit present on the
branches. This section shows how the 3D image algorithm was per-
formed to obtain information on vegetative structures.
Structure from Motion (SfM) is a computational technique, to gen-
erate 3D structures (point clouds), from corresponding points in mul-
tiple views of an object in space. In order to obtain a 3D model of the
branches, high-resolution videos were acquired with a mobile phone
device, along the branches, as shown in Fig. 2. The best quality frames
were selected, as were certain frames in which the fruit was not blurry,
owing to camera movement or fuzziness. For this, a sharpness index
was calculated, just as in the case of [28]. This sharpness index was
based on an edge quality analysis, using classic ﬁlters, such as Sobel,
Laplaciano, and Canny. If the index was inferior to a value determined
in [28], the image was discarded, and another frame from the video was
selected. Then, the Bundle Structure from Motion (Bundle SfM)

2.1. Image acquisition
Two image obtention techniques were experimented, in ﬁeld con-
ditions, on the coﬀee branches: (i) zig-zag movement and (ii) linear
movement. Both movements began at the outermost part of the bran-
ches and proceeded toward the center of the plant until encountering
the tree trunk. Video information was obtained with the main camera
on a Samsung Galaxy S5 mobile device, without using ﬂash, and setting
exposure and white balance to automatic settings. Additionally, video
acquisition was conﬁgured at 30 fps in HD. The videos acquired had an
average duration of 15 s, and an image was extracted from every ﬁve
video frames, for an average of 90 images per branch for videos ac-
quired linearly, and 180 images per branch for videos acquired with
zig-zag movement over the branch. The number of images depends
directly on acquisition velocity and branch length. Due to lighting
conditions and monocular camera acquisition, it was necessary to take
videos at an approximate velocity of 5 cm s−1, in order to obtain clear
images of the coﬀee branches.
Thirty coﬀee branches on a 3-year-old plantation were marked at
the beginning of the harvest season in the second semester of 2015.
Before each collection time, for each branch, the number of ripe, semi-
ripe, and unripe fruits were manually counted. Additionally, solar ra-
diation information was collected for each branch checked, as well as
the type of light on the branch (direct or diﬀuse), time, the lux on each
branch, and climatic conditions at the time of acquisition.
Image acquisition occurred between 7:30 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. With
the mobile device's luxometer, the luxes for each of the checked bran-
ches were measured. There were two cases: (i) direct solar light on the
checked branch and (ii) indirect or diﬀuse solar light on the checked
branch. In both cases, no parasol or umbrella was used to avoid glare.
When it rained, light information was also acquired for branches. The
algorithm developed works adequately with branch illumination ranges
between 300 and 165,000 lux, as minimum and maximum values,
measured experimentally. Table 1 shows that the light variation was
wide, on average, both for direct and indirect light, throughout the

2.3. 3D reconstruction
3D reconstruction implies aspects such as: (i) key point detection
and matching; (ii) fundamental matrix calculation; (iii) recovering
projection matrices; (iv) sparse point cloud generation using the Bundle
adjustment; and (v) dense point cloud generation.
2.3.1. Detection and matching of key points
To ﬁnd key points in each image, an algorithm that detects scale
invariant feature transform (SIFT) [19] key points is used, due to its
tolerance for transformations present in the images. The SIFT algorithm
ﬁnds features invariant to the image's scale and rotation. Additionally,
it is resilient in the face of small distortions, changes in 3D points of
view, the addition of noise, and light changes.
Fig. 4 shows the key points found by SIFT in two images of coﬀee
branches, acquired in the ﬁeld. For each key point, a descriptor is
created.
The key points given by SIFT are paired using the Approximate
Nearest Neighbor (ANN) approach, developed by [2]. In order to pair
key points, in two images, I1 and I2, a kd-tree is created, with the de-
scriptors from each point in image I2. Later, for each key point in I1, the
nearest neighbor is found in I2, using the search priority algorithm for
the nearest neighbor. Additionally, false matches are classiﬁed using
the ratio test described by [19], where, for a descriptor from image I1,
the distances of two nearest neighbors is found in I2, d1 and d2. If

2.3.5. Dense point clouds
The focus of the Bundle SfM is the obtention of 3D structures using
multiple views of an object from diﬀerent viewpoints. However, as seen
previously, only key points, which match between various images are
projected. This generates disperse 3D structures. In order to obtain
dense 3D structures, the Patch Multi-View Stereo (PMVS) [12] algorithm
is used. This algorithm reconstructs rigid structures, and takes a group
of images and extrinsic parameters from the camera, in order to re-
construct a 3D structure.
PMVS generates a group of oriented points, where 3D coordinates
and the surface normal are calculated for each oriented point. The

2.4. Suitability of 3D structures
3D reconstruction resulting from the use of SfM and PMVS presents
atypical points (outliers) in 3D structures. These outliers are errors in
the triangulation process. These unwanted data are eliminated using a
statistical analysis in the neighborhood of each point. For each q point
in the 3D structure, there is a neighborhood with N neighbors.
Following, the Euclidian distance (D(q, pi)) is calculated for each pi
point in the neighborhood. Points whose Euclidian distance is greater
than the standard deviation (σm) of neighborhood distances are re-
moved. The largest number of atypical values is present in the back-
ground of the scene. Each dense point cloud has on average 300,000
points. The neighborhood number with which each point was processed
(to determine whether it corresponded to an outlier or not) corresponds
to 0.1% of the total shape of the point cloud.
One of the principal advantages of working with 3D information in
outdoor environments is the possibility of being able to remove the
background from scenes easily. For that, it is possible to use a range
ﬁlter, where points which are outside of a speciﬁc range, in the desired
dimension (direction), are removed. The range ﬁlter operates on the
speciﬁed axis, whether that be x, y or z. In order to remove point p, one
must evaluate whether p is within the previously deﬁned axismin and
axismax range. That is if axismin < q < axismax. In the case that the
point is within the established range, it is kept within the 3D structure;
if not, it is eliminated.
The objective of this work is to determine the ripeness process for

The study referenced in [26], segments and classiﬁes fruits, in-
dividually, on coﬀee branches, using a group of 3–6 images per branch.
Estimation and count models are created, and in one of its applications,
the mature fruit percentage is estimated. That technique is based on
color and form features. However, it presents disadvantages with
overexposed and underexposed photos, and on some occasions, the
presence of the background generates false positives in fruit detection.
This increases fruit detection error, as well as mature fruit percentage
computation error. The present work in this article seeks to overcome
problems generated by outdoor environment characteristics, as with
acquired images, from coﬀee branches. In this case, an average of 90
images per branch were used. Individual detection of each fruit was not
the goal. Rather, the goal was to ﬁnd the composition of a branch in
three states of development, so as to calculate the maturation index,
and determine whether or not the branch was harvestable. The com-
position of the maturation states for each branch were analyzed using
the number of points from the reconstructed, segmented, and classiﬁed
point clouds. Using techniques mentioned in the document, the back-
ground was removed, the fruit, branches, and leaves were segmented,
and the fruit was classiﬁed into three states of maturation: unripe, semi-
ripe, and ripe.
Fig. 10 illustrates a comparison of the results obtained in [26] and in
this study. In the ﬁgure, some of the source images used in the 3D
branch construction and its respective classiﬁcation, using [26], can be

process, as well as the point cloud classiﬁcation, using the method
proposed here, are shown. As demonstrated in the source images, there
are overexposures which directly aﬀect the classiﬁcation result using
[26]. In consequence, individual fruit detection error may reach 40%,
and the mature fruit percentage error is in excess of 12%. On the other
hand, in the 3D reconstruction process, over and under exposures, as
well as occlusions, are mitigated, as the projection of a point uses color
information averaged from various images.

3. Results
In this article, an estimation of branch ripeness level is performed,
using the number of points encountered and classiﬁed in each re-
constructed point cloud. Owing to the image acquisition system, it is
not possible to guarantee the same distance between the branch and
camera. For this reason, point clouds are not scalable by themselves to
real size in metric units. In this results section, the relationship of size
with the number of points, and the strategy used for determination of
subregion size, so as to calculate branch color and curvature features
and classify coﬀee fruits, independent of the acquisition conditions, is
shown. At the same time, a statistical model is given, which is used to
estimate the percentage of each state of ripeness on the branch, without
using fruit counts, as in studies reported by [26], but instead using a
classiﬁed point count within each point cloud, which represents each
branch. With the information obtained from classiﬁed point clouds, a
global ripeness index was found for each branch. This involves the
percentage of fruit in each state of development, and a sampling weight
which indicates whether the branch is or is not ready for harvest.
3.1. Coﬀee fruit classiﬁcation models
From diﬀerent sizes used for the curvature calculation (mentioned
in Section 2.7), eight total classiﬁcation models were trained, with
diﬀerent curvature subregion sizes. Results were compared to those
obtained by [3] at a ﬁxed 2.5 cm size.
The accuracy of fruit classiﬁcation was calculated based on ground
truth information, shown in Table 7. In order to train SVM, ground
truth point clouds were generated using the developments by [27,3].
Fig. 13 shows an example of generated ground truth point clouds. Each
point in the point cloud is annotated with a speciﬁc color. Ripe fruits
are identiﬁed with red, stems with brown, leaves with blueish green,
unripe fruits with light green, and semi-ripe fruits with yellow.
Accuracy oscillated between 42% and 92%, with an average spe-
ciﬁcity of 86%, considering the stem and leaf classes. One can observe
that the best classiﬁcation model is found in a region size measuring
6.5% of total point cloud size, unlike the classiﬁcation with a ﬁxed
2.5 cm size. For this reason, the 6.5% classiﬁcation model was im-
plemented in the classiﬁcation of the 90 branches used in the experi-
ment.

In regards to the ripeness index, a single factor was calculated,
which was able to relate to the unripe, semi-ripe, and ripe fruits, con-
sidering a factor pondered for each stage of development, as with
computations made with point cloud information and information ac-
quired directly from coﬀee branches via fruit count.
The factor considered was determined experimentally, assigning a
speciﬁc weight to each stage of development, depending directly on the
feasibility of fruit harvesting at each stage. A 0.2 factor was assigned to
unripe fruit. This was the lowest factor, as these fruits should not be
harvested. A 0.3 factor was assigned to semi-ripe fruits, as on some
occasions, these fruits had to be removed. A 0.5 factor was assigned to
ripe fruits. This is the highest factor, since ripe fruits are those that must
be harvested. Additionally, the ripeness index was considered, in the
computation, to be a denominator which divides the principal expres-
sion. This denominator corresponds to the summation of the percen-
tages of each stage of development, obtaining a factor of one in all
cases.

4. Conclusions
The results presented show that there is a way to determine the
ripeness percentage of a coﬀee branch, with direct measurements and
non-destructive methods, using 3D images processing algorithms.
The use of 3D information allowed for the mitigation of over and
under exposure problems present when working in outdoor environ-
ments, given that, in the 3D reconstruction process, the point projection
uses averaged color information from various images. Additionally,
structural occlusion problems are mitigated, because when using var-
ious images from the length of the branch, a structure may be occluded
in one image, but may not be in others. Through use of 3D information,
it is possible to remove the background, which improves classiﬁcation
of structures of interest on the branch, as compared to techniques in
which 2D approaches are used. By the same reason the system can
process images with diﬀerent ambient light and shadows.
The main diﬃculties with 3D image processing, in the conditions of
this study, may be in the computational cost, and scaling problems for
information on a distance level. For the latter, a system which calcu-
lates curvature and color features, based on a percentage of the size of
the point cloud, was implemented. This is a signiﬁcant advantage, as
independent of the acquisition conditions or that which they may infer
in the reconstruction, the computation of features is proportional to
each particular point cloud.
The ripeness percentage per branch becomes a tool for computation
of the ripeness index, with which it is possible to determine whether a

There was an estimation error of between 3.7% and 8.7% for de-

Additionally, the algorithm developed, allows for the cleaning of
background elements which correspond to other coﬀee branches pre-
sent on the same or neighboring trees. It also tolerates lighting pro-
blems generated by direct solar radiation, self-shading problems on the
branches, and occlusion between coﬀee fruits on the same branch.
In a future study, a system for volume measurement may be de-
veloped, with which it would be possible to segment fruits individually,
simultaneously calculate their volume, and infer the mass produced by
each, independent of its stage of development.
