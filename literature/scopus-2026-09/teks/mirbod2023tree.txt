Apple (Malus domestica) fruit size plays an integral role in orchard management decision-
making, particularly during chemical thinning, fruit quality assessment, and yield pre-
diction. A machine vision system was developed using stereo cameras synchronised to a
custom-built LED strobe to perform on-tree sizing of fruit in images with high measure-
ment accuracy. Two deep neural network models (Faster ReCNN and Mask ReCNN) were
trained to detect fruit candidates for sizing followed by extrapolation of occluded fruit
regions to improve size estimation. The segmented fruit shapes were converted to metric
surface areas and diameters using spatial resolutions and depth information from the
stereo cameras. Monthly ﬁeld trials from June to October using the camera system were
conducted, measuring fruit diameters ranging from 22 to 82 mm, and compared against
ground truth diameters. Diameter estimates had a mean absolute error ranging from 1.1 to
4.2 mm for the ﬁve-month trial period, an average error of 4.8% compared to ground truth
diameter measurements. Standard deviation errors ranged from 0.7 to 1.9 mm. Using
neural network models for intelligent sampling of fruit in images followed by extrapolation
of missing regions can be an alternative method of handling fruit occlusion in agricultural
imaging and improving sizing accuracy.

1.
Introduction
Fruit size estimation in apple orchards is important for man-
aging early season crop load and harvest operations. During
the early stage of fruit development, growers need size mea-
surements to determine the efﬁcacy of chemical thinning
applications. An accurate mid-season estimate of fruit count
and fruit size can allow growers to better predict yield and
fruit size distribution, which are important metrics to help
determine the amount of labour, numbers of fruit bins, and
storage space that will be needed at harvest.
For fruit size estimation to be useful for growers, the ac-
curacy needs to be within millimetres to assist in manage-
ment decision-making. One example is in precision crop load
management, a strategy that involves deﬁning the optimum
number of fruits per tree, then applying sequential chemical
thinning sprays) to obtain a crop load near the target fruit
number (Robinson et al., 2013). The efﬁcacy of thinners is
strongly inﬂuenced by fruit size at the time of application. In
one study, it was shown that ‘Delicious’ fruitlets were most
sensitive to Carbaryl and Naphthalene Acetic Acid (NAA)
when fruitlet diameter averaged 8e14 mm and ethephon was
most effective on fruitlets that were 17e31 mm (Marini, 1996,
2003). To determine the need for repeat applications, growers
measure individual fruitlets every two or three days for up to
two weeks (Greene et al., 2013). Therefore, precision crop load
management requires accurate estimates of fruit count per
tree and fruit diameter with millimetre resolution as di-
ameters can increase by 1 mm per day during the fruitlet
stage.
Fruit size also has important relationships to yield and
packout rates. A principal factor in determining yield for apple
trees is fruit count, as was indicated by Elfving and Schechter
(1993) in a nine-year study which they observed a positive
linear relationship between fruit count and yield. Fruit size
however tends to decrease linearly with increasing fruit count
(Forshey & Elfving, 1977). It can take about 3000 apples of grade
size 150 (67 mm diameter) to ﬁll a 20-bushel apple bin whereas
it takes 2000 apples of grade size 100 (79 mm diameter) to ﬁll
the same bin (Omafra, 2021). This difference in apple count
and fruit handling can become more signiﬁcant in larger or-
chards affecting the amount of labour, time, and materials
needed to pick and store fruit. Therefore, knowledge on fruit
size distribution and fruit count can help growers better
forecast incoming proﬁts and assist with budget planning.
Currently, on-tree fruit sizing for apples is measured
manually using callipers, sizing rings, or band-loop callipers.
The band or ring is wrapped around the largest circumference
of the fruit with the centre being the axis going through the
calyx and stem. This circumference is then converted to
diameter. These methods depend on human judgment for
sample selection, are labour-intensive, and prone to errors. In
addition, apple fruit diameter alone cannot accurately predict
fruit weight without taking into consideration the fruit shape
(Marini et al., 2019). This follows a similar observation made
by Forshey (1971) from a six-year study on ‘McIntosh’ apples
in which the author showed the rate of enlargement was more
uniform when measured in volume versus diameter. Also,
fruit shape of the same cultivar can differ (more round or

Machine vision systems can have beneﬁts regarding esti-
mating fruit’s 2D and 3D shape. Especially, with the recent
advancement in machine learning algorithms, the machine
vision system can utilize neural network models such as Mask
ReCNN (He et al., 2017), to incorporate fruit shape in its sizing
estimate. Butters et al. (2019) used semantic segmentation of
apples to obtain fruit shape and then performed ellipse ﬁtting
to estimate fruit diameter. With the emergence of fruit har-
vesting robots, segmenting fruit shape can also be helpful for
end-effector grasping. Kang and Chen (2020) developed
DasNet-v2, a neural network model for segmenting apple
fruits and branches to help harvesting robots grasp fruit of
different sizes and effectively navigate the end-effector
through densely arranged branches.

night-time imaging with artiﬁcial lighting (Fu et al., 2015;

A common method for size estimation using images is
utilising the number of pixels in fruit surface or diameter to be
converted back to a metric coordinate system. Roy and Isler
(2016) took a random sample of apples in their image data-
set, found their metric to pixels conversion factor, and con-
verted the remaining apple diameters to the metric coordinate
system. However, they reported the mean error in size esti-
mation using their monocular camera to be 90 mm with the
standard deviation of 290 mm compared to their stereo vision
measurements. Another method to make the conversion is by
knowing the metric depth from the camera system to the
fruit. This can be achieved by stereo triangulation or by having
a secondary depth sensor such as LiDAR or Time-of-Flight
(ToF) camera to ﬁnd the distance to the target object. Peng
et al. (2016) used a Red-Green-Blue (RGB) camera to detect
and size apples in pixel coordinates and fused LiDAR infor-
mation on the detected apples to convert their pixel diameters
to metric coordinates. The system was mounted on a ground
vehicle traveling at 8 km h1. Wang et al. (2017) conducted a
comparison study using stereo camera, RGB camera with
LiDAR, and a Microsoft Kinect Red-Green-Blue-Depth (RGB-D)
sensor. The images were acquired at night-time to avoid
interference from the sun and only fully visible fruit were used
for measuring dimensions. Their systems were mounted on a
vehicle traveling at 5 km h1. The RGB-D sensor resulted in the
best accuracy with root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 4.9 mm
and 4.3 mm for mango fruit length and width, respectively.
The current published studies indicate a diameter accuracy
of near 5 mm is attainable for on-tree imaging of fruit trees
(Roy & Isler, 2016; Underwood et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017),
with most studies conducted using night-time imaging with
artiﬁcial lighting. Travel velocity for collecting data have
ranged from walking speed for handheld devices to 8 km h1
for vehicle mounted systems. One difﬁculty in obtaining
higher accuracy has been image resolution, as many RGB-D
cameras do not provide high-resolution depth images. Using
higher resolution cameras in a stereo pair setup can be one
approach for increasing sizing accuracy (Mirbod et al., 2016;
Qadri, 2021). Better handling of fruit occlusion can also help to
improve accuracy by increasing the number of visible fruits to
be measured and preventing partially occluded fruit from
being sized.
The contribution from this paper focuses on using neural
networks for addressing the occlusion problem in apple or-
chard imaging. This study also discusses the rationale behind
choosing certain machine vision system components to target
a desired fruit size accuracy in an orchard, which has not been
well addressed in previous studies. Whilst it can be more
economical and practical to choose off-the-shelf, readily

different stages of maturity in the ﬁeld on a moving platform.
Speciﬁc objectives were to:
1. Estimate the amount of error caused by sizing apples in
images using 2D fruit shape versus 1D fruit diameter in
a controlled experiment for determining the effective-
ness of 2D sizing,
2. Design an agricultural imaging system using a high-
resolution stereo camera pair and active illumination
source that is resistant to daytime lighting variability,
3. Train deep neural network models to disqualify heavily
occluded fruit from ﬁeld images, improve sizing on
moderately occluded fruit by extrapolating occluded
regions, and use computer vision algorithms to esti-
mate apple diameter from 2D fruit shape.
Primary design criteria of the system in objective 2 were (1)
providing apple sizing precision of 1 mm without using night-
time imaging or enclosure, (2) capturing tall spindle trees (up
to 4.3 m height) using 3D vision, and (3) reducing inﬂuences
from motion blur and lighting condition variability in images.
In addition, the system would need to overcome challenges of
imaging in an agricultural ﬁeld environment such as fruit
occlusion from leaves, branches, and other fruits.
In section 2.1, an indoor controlled experiment is con-
ducted to determine the effect of fruit shape on sizing accu-
racy. Section 2.2 explains how speciﬁc hardware components
of the machine vision system were chosen to obtain a desired
accuracy in ﬁeld conditions. Section 2.3 describes the data
collection procedure in ﬁeld trials using the machine vision
system and section 2.4 outlines the software pipeline used to
size apples including a method for handling fruit occlusion.
Section 3 shows the results from both the indoor and ﬁeld
experiments and discusses potential reasons for the differ-
ence in sizing accuracy between the two. In section 4, sug-
gestions for further improving sizing accuracy using the
machine vision system are given.

2.1.
Impact of fruit shape in size estimation in a
controlled environment experiment
Forshey and Dominick (1965) demonstrated that different
apple varieties could have the same diameter because the
shape of the fruit is not taken into account (Fig. 1A). An oblate
and conic shaped apple might therefore have the same
diameter, as measured by a calliper, but their 2D surface areas
would be different (Fig. 1B). A controlled laboratory experi-
ment was conducted to determine the impact of fruit shape in
size estimation and the best possible accuracy of size esti-
mation using images. Fruit shape, in this experiment, was

A custom-made active light source was used to improve image
quality for outdoor ﬁeld conditions. The ﬂash system was
based on studies by Mirbod et al. (2021) and Silwal et al. (2021).

The system consisted of a set of 16 chip-on-board (COB) LEDs
(model XLamp CMA3090, Cree Inc., Durham, NC, USA) that
were overcurrent driven for short durations, creating a
powerful strobe effect. Each LED was rated for 173 Watts (W)
and 12,372 luminous ﬂux. The power was increased to 1.6 kW
for short bursts on each LED, creating a total output power of
25.6 kW for a single ﬂash. The complete camera setup can be
seen in Fig. 6. The three main components were: (1) the image
acquisition hardware which included the camera system and
LED panel, (2) the ﬂash circuit and power delivery system, and
(3) the control system for image acquisition and strobe
synchronisation.
The control system was responsible for capturing images
and synchronizing the cameras with the ﬂash system. It
included a mini-desktop computer (model GB-BXi5-5200,
GIGA-BYTE TECHNOLOGY CO., New Taipei City 231, Taiwan)
and an Arduino MEGA 2560 (Arduino LLC, Somerville, MA,
USA). The computer used Ubuntu Linux version 16.04 with the
Robot Operating System (ROS) (Quigley et al., 2009) version
Kinetic Kame (ROS, 2016) installed. The Arduino synchronized
the camera shutter to stay open for the LED strobe duration.
The ﬂash circuit was powered using a 12 V 18 Ah sealed lead-
acid battery and the rest of the system using a separate bat-
tery source.
Artiﬁcial lighting during image capture helps in two ways:
(1) lessening the effect of sunlight and drop shadows, which
create more uniform brightness and coloured images, and (2)
allowing the shortening of shutter duration on cameras,
which helps to reduce the effect of motion blur in images from
movement of the camera system in the ﬁeld. As the camera
system moved through the rows, the images were susceptible
to vertical motion blur due to ground vibrations; however,
given the short shutter duration of the cameras, no motion
blur effects were observed in images during any data collec-
tion trials for the entire summer. This was important since a
single-pixel error due to motion blur could have resulted in
1 mm difference in diameter estimation. Figure 7 shows
samples of the ﬁeld imaging data taken on August 13, 2020 at
9:00 AM, 1:00 PM, and 9:00 PM. During the daytime, the ﬂash
system reduced the undesirable effects of the sunlight such as
shadow drops and background scene visibility.
In addition, the accuracy of diameter estimation can be
affected by point of size estimation (pixel location in an image)
due to characteristics of depth measurement using stereo
vision. The distance to an object determines the metric size of
each pixel on that object. Once distance to the fruit is known,
the pixel to metric conversion is most accurate on the plane
facing the camera at that distance. Since the apple fruit sur-
face is curved, the diameter estimations would be different
between the depth measurement at the surface of the fruit
and that at the centre of the fruit. When fruit is small this
difference is negligible, but for larger fruit it can become more
signiﬁcant. Figure 8 illustrates how the diameter calculation
changes when fruit is directly facing the camera. In this sce-
nario, the depth measurement to the centre of the fruit (d þ a)
would give a more accurate diameter estimation than depth
measured to the fruit surface (d). To consider this factor, the
possible maximum errors at different locations in images
were calculated and analysed in section 3.2.
More details on the ﬂash and camera system, including
speciﬁc components used, power consumption, and perfor-
mance of the system undergoing motion blur from different
velocities in the ﬁeld are discussed in Mirbod et al. (2021). The
strobing mechanism was chosen over constant lighting to in-
crease the power and light output from each LED. Overcurrent
driving of LEDs can damage the components due to the heat;
however, for short durations and proper cooling no damage
was observed. In a laboratory environment, the LED strobing
mechanism was tested for 5 h, continuously capturing images
at 5 Hz with no noticeable degradation on the images. An in-
depth analysis on the maximum frame rate and heat which
the LEDs can withstand has not yet been investigated.

2.3.
Apple sizing algorithm pipeline for on-tree size
estimation
Figure 9 shows an overview of how the apple sizing method
was executed. Once a stereo image pair was captured, a CNN
model detected apples in one of the images. To ﬁnd the 3D
coordinates, the detected apples were matched in the second
image. Another CNN model would then extract all the pixels
for each detected fruit. Once the unit metric area of one of the
pixels is known using depth information, the summation of all
the pixels gave the surface area for one fruit.
The Faster ReCNN model was used for apple detection and
Mask ReCNN was used for fruit segmentation. Ideally, the full
resolution image (2160  4096) would be fed into the Mask
ReCNN model for apple detection and segmentation, but this
would have required more GPU memory than what was used
in this study. Splitting the image into multiple patches and
then feeding them to Mask ReCNN directly was also an op-
tion; however, this risked the image being split on an apple

resulting in partial regions of a fruit being segmented and
sized, and thus lowering the overall mean apple diameter
estimation for a tree. It was not ideal to feed a down-sampled
image into Mask ReCNN then up-sampling the image again,
since an up-sampled segmented fruit would have likely
resulted in a different pixel count of the fruit compared to the
original image. Therefore, for each month’s dataset, all fruits
were ﬁrst detected using Faster ReCNN by scaling the full
resolution image in half and then splitting the image once. For
each detected fruit, an image patch containing the apple was
then extracted from the original image and fed into the Mask
ReCNN model for segmentation. The image patch size was
128  128 pixels for June and July and 256  256 pixels in
August, September, and October as the fruit increased in size
throughout the season. Average fruit diameter for June and
July were 24 mm and 51 mm, respectively, and average fruit
diameter for August, September, and October were 63 mm,
73 mm, and 75 mm, respectively.
The TensorFlow 2 Object Detection API (Huang, Rathod,
Birodkar, et al., 2017) was used for training and deploying the
fruit detection models. The Faster ReCNN and Mask ReCNN
models were provided through the TensorFlow 2 Detection
Model Zoo (Huang, Rathod, Sun, et al., 2017) with speciﬁc
model names “Faster ReCNN Inception ResNet V2 1024  1024”
and “Mask ReCNN Inception ResNet V2 1024  1024”. All CNN
model training and inference on the apple datasets were done
on a computer with an Intel i9 X-Series processor (Intel, Santa
Clara, CA, USA) and an Nvidia Tesla K80 graphics card (NVIDIA,
Santa Clara, CA, USA). The computer used Ubuntu Linux
version 18.04 as the operating system with TensorFlow version
2.2 and Python version 3.8. Polygonal annotations for image
labelling was done using the Labelme tool (Wada, 2016) and
bounding box annotations using the LabelImg tool (Tzutalin,
2015). To train the Faster ReCNN model, an average of 40 im-
ages (1024  1024 pixels) of apple trees (450 labelled apples)
were used from each month’s data collection trial. The
network was trained to distinguish between apples that were
visible and heavily occluded. For each month’s data collection
trial, an average of 100e250 apples were used to train the Mask
ReCNN model for fruit segmentation. Mask ReCNN images
used for training, validation, and testing of segmented apples
were from the same dataset as the Faster ReCNN model. More
details on each month’s image dataset used for training the
neural network models is shown in Table 1.
To overcome some of the fruit occlusion in images, the
neural networks were trained to extrapolate fruit boundaries,
therefore the visible class consisted of fruit that were fully
visible or had some occlusion. The threshold used to consider
fruit as visible enough for sizing was based on whether a
human could accurately extrapolate the fruit boundary even if
there was some overlap by leaves, branches, or other fruits.
Figure 10A shows a sample of apples that were considered
good candidates for sizing. Figure 10B shows apples that were
trained to be detected but classiﬁed as bad candidates for
sizing due to severe occlusion. Table 2 shows the instances of
visible and highly occluded apples identiﬁed for each dataset.
Identifying good candidate apples on a tree was important
because sizing heavily occluded fruit would have lowered the
overall average fruit diameter calculation for that tree. This
error can become more signiﬁcant as sample size of detected
fruit for a tree decreases.
The models were evaluated by comparing the average fruit
size measurements obtained by the machine vision system
for each tree with the ground truth measurements collected
on image acquisition dates during the growing season and at
harvest (October 8, 2020). Linear regression analysis, using the
R2 statistical measure, was used to compare the model’s fruit
diameter output with that of calliper-measured diameter and
fruit weight. The mean absolute error (MAE) and standard

2.4.
In-ﬁeld machine vision setup and data acquisition
Field experiments were conducted at the Russell E. Larson
Agricultural Research Center at Rock Springs (Pennsylvania
Furnace, PA). A summary of ﬁeld experiment trial dates and
types of data collected is shown in Table 3.
Once a month, during the 2020 season from June until
harvest in October, images were collected from one row (Row
1) of ‘Golden Delicious’ cultivar. This row contained 50 trees of
both the tall spindle and bi-axis training systems. Images
were continuously captured at 3 frames s1 whilst the system
moved down an orchard row. Apple size measurements for
each tree using the vision system were done on images with
the tree trunk near the centre of image. The boundaries of the
trees were also manually labelled so that the same fruit would
not be associated with multiple trees. Of the 50 trees, 20 were
kept separate to build the training and validation image
datasets. The apple sizing algorithm pipeline was run on the
remaining 30 trees, or 60 images (one image from each side of
the tree). Ground truth data on fruit diameters were collected
for the 30 trees at each trial. To get an accurate ground truth
representation of average fruit diameter per tree, at least one-
third of the fruit on each tree were measured using the band-
loop calliper. At harvest, fruit weight and fruit diameter were
also recorded for those 30 trees. Additionally, at harvest time,
imaging and ground truth data were obtained for another row
(Row 2), which was adjacent to Row 1. In Row 2, ground truth
diameter measurements were recorded for 45 trees and
average fruit weight for 38 trees.

There were several possible sources of error for diameter
estimation in the ﬁeld trials. First, an imprecise calibration of
the camera system which led to image distortion from the
camera lens. Distortion can affect diameter estimation espe-
cially with apples located at very bottom or top portion of the
image. This distortion was less inﬂuential near the centre of
the image and most imaged apples in the ﬁeld were not
located at the image extremities. For the laboratory experi-
ment, fruits were always placed at the centre of the image
therefore the error was not present. A second source of error,
primarily for months of June and July, may have been that
ground truth data of the top portion of the trees was not
collected while the camera system sized fruit from all loca-
tions. From August and onward, a ladder was used to sample
fruit from all locations of the trees. Also, in the month of June,
less fruit overall were visible and classes visible and severely
occluded were more difﬁcult to distinguish during data
labelling, therefore more severely occluded fruit were being
sized. A third source of error, which may have largely
contributed to the discrepancy between the July and August
results, was that tree canopy vegetation were at their fullest in
July and were pruned before the August imaging, allowing
more fruit to become visible (Fig. 15).

4.
Conclusions
This study proposed, developed, and tested an effective ma-
chine vision system to carry out on-tree sizing of apples in an
orchard. The system was designed to be used regardless of
illumination conditions in the ﬁeld. Controlled experiments
showed that surface area measurements using the camera
system were a stronger predictor of weight compared to
calliper-measured diameters, with R2 values of 0.98 and 0.93
respectively. A total of 30 trees from one row in an apple or-
chard were imaged at ﬁve different periods of the growing
season, with an additional 45 trees at harvest from an adja-
cent row to validate system performance. Apples were
detected using the Faster ReCNN model, and then Mask
ReCNN was trained to segment the surface area of each fruit.
The neural network models handled fruit occlusion in images
by extrapolating the missing regions. Stereo vision was used
to convert surface area pixels to metric coordinates and
compared to ground truth apple diameters. Field imaging
analysis showed the system capable of ﬁnding the diameter of
apples from 25 mm at the fruitlet stage, to mature stage in the
range of 70e80 mm. The system was able to estimate di-
ameters at different stages of fruit development with mean
and standard deviation errors as low as 1.08 and 0.73 mm,
respectively.
Future work to improve on these results can include
generating a denser 3D point cloud to obtain a contour rep-
resentation of the fruit. Using a sequence of images for each
tree at different viewpoints will also increase the sample size
of visible fruit. Higher resolution lenses which can better
resolve individual pixels will help to improve fruit boundary
deﬁnition. Accurate camera calibration in combination with
lower lens distortion should help to remove inaccuracies in
diameter estimation for fruits located closer to the edges of an
image.
The results from this study showed a promising method to
give growers the ability to perform on-tree sizing of their crop
that is quick and can scale efﬁciently. Whilst the data collec-
tion speed of this platform was limited to 2 km h1, a previous
study on the imaging system showed that the effect of higher
ground vehicle velocity (~7 km h1) impacted the diameter
accuracy by less than 2 mm. This system can therefore be
useful for future chemical thinning applications since it can
monitor small changes in fruit size following sprays. It can
also help growers with better approximations on harvest
materials and labour requirements by quickly providing an
estimate of the distribution of fruit size for their crop.
