Optimization of the EfficientNet-Swin Transformer Hybrid Model with Dynamic 
Attention and Multi-task Learning for Accurate Palm Fruit Classification

1Faculty of Engineering and Computer Science, Department of Information Systems,

Abstract: Manual grading of the maturity of oil palm fruits is subjective and unproductive in an industrial context. 
This paper presents a hybrid EfficientNet-Swin Transformer model with Dynamic Attention and Multi-Task Learning 
(MTL) for the effective classification of Fresh Fruit Bunches (FFB) maturity. The dataset employed in that was of 
12.550 RGB images distributed within five classes: Defect, Overripe, Ripe, Underripe, and Unripe. The model 
integrates local feature extraction of EfficientNet-B3, global dependency modeling of Swin Transformer, and dynamic 
attention for adaptive feature refinement. Using 5-fold cross-validation during training, the model achieves an overall 
accuracy of 97.16% (±0.47%), precision of 97.17%, recall of 97.16%, F1-score of 97.16%, and mean AUC of 99.79%, 
higher than the baseline EfficientNet-B3 (95.21%). Defect detection-based multi-task learning scored an IoU of 0.7305 
and a Dice coefficient of 0.7342, respectively. The model demonstrates good robustness against the variations in 
lighting conditions and visual diversity, thus proving to be an effective approach for online grading of palm oil fruits 
at plantations and factories. 
Keywords: EfficientNet, Swin transformer, Dynamic attention, Multi-task learning, Palm fruit classification. 
 
 
1. Introduction 
As the world’s largest producer of palm oil, 
Indonesia plays a major role in the global supply 
chain. Riau Province is always a region that owns the 
largest area of oil palm plantations in Indonesia, from 
2.86 to 3.49 million ha or 19-21% of the Indonesian 
total plantation [1]. 
In the global palm oil industry, the quality of fresh 
fruit bunches (FFB) is important throughout the 
process. The ripeness of the fruit affects how much 
oil can be collected and the quality of that oil. It also 
changes how easy it is to refine the oil. However, 
current methods to check if fruit is ripe rely on people 
making judgments, which can be subjective, 
inconsistent, and time-consuming [2].  
To address these challenges, AI-driven non-
invasive or minimally invasive methods (including 
spectral 
sensing, 
hyperspectral 
imaging, 
and 
computer vision, among others) have become 
transformative solutions. These methodologies have 
been backed by machine learning and deep learning, 
which can learn visual patterns such as color, texture, 
and chromatic features of FFB to classify the ripeness 
more objectively and reproducibly. Smartphone-
based technologies extend these capabilities by 
providing low-cost, user-friendly, real-time field 
solutions [3].  
Despite these improvements, even though there 
can be milestones with CNN and multispectral-based 
methods, major challenges remain and hinder their 
application in the real world. These challenges can be 
classified as environmental robustness (lighting 
condition changes, shadows, occlusions from 
leaves/other fruits, or perspective distortion) since the 
model performance often fails under these covering 
conditions [4].

divided into the following categories: Single-Task

CNNs and Transformers It is recognized that the 
hybrid architecture of CNNs and transformers is 
superior to the single models in terms of embedding

solution to the limitations of previous methods, 
utilizing effective dynamic attention mechanisms and

cultivation practices, and environments is also scarce.

The expected determinative criteria to separate

This study holds both theoretical and practical

2. Literature review 
The classification of the oil palm fruit ripeness 
has been widely studied by using several network 
architectures, 
including 
convolutional 
neural 
networks (CNNs) and transformer-based models, and 
by implementing transfer learning and multitask 
learning methods that enhance the accuracy and 
robustness of the system. 
Limitations of conventional techniques manual 
grading remains subjective and labor-intensive, 
producing inconsistent labels and operational 
bottlenecks in large estates  [17]. Classical computer-
vision pipelines-color thresholding (HSV/YCbCr), 
handcrafted texture descriptors (LBP/GLCM), and 
shallow classifiers (k-NN/SVM) - depend on careful, 
per-site calibration and are brittle under illumination 
changes, specular highlights, mud/dust artifacts, 
occlusions, and complex backgrounds; prior surveys 
have 
repeatedly 
noted 
limited 
cross-site 
generalization in plantation settings [18]. Although 
computationally light, these methods struggle 
precisely where real deployments demand stability: 
variable lighting, camera heterogeneity, and cultivar 
diversity  [7].  
For comparison, the work by [5] proposed a 
successful transfer learning method by modifying the 
existing implementations of EfficientNet. Their 
research applies the EffNetB0-LR and EffNetB4-LR 
models to the FFB for defect detection, using 
EfficientNet-B0, which has computational efficiency, 
and EfficientNet-B4, which allows for deeper 
features, combined with an LR classifier. These 
architectures present a state-of-the-art practice 
relying on pure CNNs and are well-suited for 
resource-limited computing environments. However, 
as CNN-based models, their long-range context 
modelling and global dependencies between fruit 
bunches are inherently less powerful. Our work 
intentionally pursues counters to these theoretical 
restrictions, for which we not only transfer learn a 
state-of-the-art CNN (EfficientNet-B3), but also 
include global context information modelling with 
Swin Transformer and Dynamic Attention techniques 
in our pipeline by considering feature fusion based on 
it adaptively. 
Positioning 
relative 
to 
modern 
deep 
models.CNN-only classifiers deliver high accuracy 
yet primarily capture local texture/color cues and can 
under-represent long-range dependencies across 
fruitlets; transfer learning helps but can be sensitive 
to domain shift. EfficientDet-like detector backbones 
are latency-efficient but detection-focused, which can 
miss pixel-level maturity signals in densely packed 
bunches, with reported accuracy plateaus under field 
noise  [19]. Transformer-only models (ViT/Swin) 
provide powerful global modeling but are more data-
hungry and training-sensitive, especially in limited-
labeled agricultural imagery. Our approach is 
positioned 
to 
bridge 
these 
gaps: 
a 
hybrid 
EfficientNet–Swin model with dynamic attention and 
multitask learning leverages EfficientNet’s compute-
efficient 
low-level 
stability 
while 
injecting 
hierarchical self-attention for multiscale context; 
multitask heads are chosen to encode physically 
meaningful ripeness correlates and are loss-weighted 
to avoid negative transfer. This combination targets 
field 
robustness 
and 
real-time 
feasibility, 
differentiating our method from conventional 
pipelines, CNN-only classifiers, detector-oriented 
designs, and transformer-only stacks. 
Manual determination of ripeness in oil palm fruit 
has been a relatively subjective and laborious activity 
until now, leading to high interest in developing 
machine vision systems that offer automated and 
objective methods based on deep learning [20]. Other 
approaches tried are well-established methods of 
machine vision and machine learning (its main 
advantage being speed, as it always works the same). 
Nonetheless, 
Convolutional 
Neural 
Networks 
(CNNs) are currently the state of the art and the 
preferred method for this purpose [21]. Models like 
ResNet-50, known for its 97% accuracy in 
classification tasks, demonstrate their effectiveness 
[22]. The field has been further advanced through 
transfer learning, where applying pre-trained weights 
to related datasets consistently boosts performance 
[23]. 
More recent models that have been adopted are 
architecturally complex in order to optimize 
operational efficiency. The EfficientDet-Lite2 model, 
for example, makes a trade-off that is more 
appropriate to the relatively lower throughput of that 
network type, leading to about 84% accuracy. It 
consists of an efficient feature extraction backbone, 
EfficientNet, and a novel erasing mechanism to 
suppress background features [24]. 
A paradigm change is being witnessed with the 
advent of Vision Transformers (ViT) and hierarchical 
versions such as Swin Transformer. The results 
suggest that transformers have more capacity to

3.2 Dataset 
For this study, we examined a set of fresh palm 
oil fruit bunches (FFB), as outlined in Table 1, which 
had been subdivided into five maturity classes: 
overripe, ripe, underripe, unripe, and defective. For 
this study, oil palm fruit classification was performed 
into five classes based on maturity grade and 
morphological characteristics. Overripe fruit has an 
increased FFA transmission factor and is over-
darkened, turning dark to red, purple, or a dark color, 
with the surface color lost. Ripe fruit has a greater 
size than unripe fruit, and the surface color is dark red, 
with a high transmission coefficient and many seeds 
compared to overripe fruit. Underripe fruit contains 
at least ten empty fruit sockets and has higher 
concentrations of flavonols and anthocyanins 
compared to ripe and overripe fruit. In contrast, 
unripe fruit has low output due to being dry 
(pistillate) and is characterized by dark purple or 
black skin with no empty sockets. Worst of all, 
defective fruit is classified as dry and shriveled, 
rotten and moldy, or bruised. This indicates that 
unripe oil palm fruit exhibits abnormal conditions 
and signs of dryness. 
According to Table 2, a comparison of 
classification and segmentation datasets, we use two 
independent but correlated datasets for classification 
and segmentation/defect detection tasks, which are 
the palm oil fruits. The classification dataset includes 
12,550 images covering the classes of Defect, 
Overripe, Ripe, Underripe, and Unripe, split into a 
training set (80.4%, 10.085 images), a validation set 
(13.0%: 1.630 images), and a test set (6.6%: 835 
images). On the other hand, the dataset with respect 
to segmentation/defect contains 12.450 images 
designed for partitioning into 80.3% training data 
(9.986 images), 12.9% validation data (1.608 images), 
and 6.7% test data (856 images), which only focuses 
on four types of fruit defects: crack, rotten, abnormal 
color, and damage. A major contrast between these 
two datasets occurs in the annotation process: the 
segmentation/defect dataset is all labeled and 
elaborately masked for different defect types, while 
for the classification dataset, only the class label, but 
no segmentation mask-producing step, is needed 
since it only needs to classify fruit ripeness level as a 
whole.

The segmentation mask generation process was 
performed by computer vision image processing 
techniques, 
namely 
edge 
detection, 
color 
segmentation, and intensity analysis. Masks are 
generated for crack defects with masks created by 
Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization 
(CLAHE) preprocessing (contrast optimization, 
window size 32) and Canny edge detection (σ=30-
100), following morphological filtering using 
elongated kernels (1×15, 15×1) to identify the crack 
pattern of at least length>20 pixels. Rotten and 
abnormal color defects are detected via HSV color 
space transformation, respectively, by multi-range 
thresholding and morphological closing (5×5 kernel) 
for region consistency, while damage defects adopt 
adaptive thresholding for dark areas and edge 
detection applying Gaussian smoothing (σ=5). All 
found masks are further processed by a set of post-
processing steps: the application of the polygon 
approximation (vectorization) approach using the 
Douglas-Peucker algorithm with an ε value equal to 
1% of the perimeter and zero-base contour filtering 
according to minimal area as follows: Crack ≥50px², 
Rotten ≥100px², Abnormal Color ≥150px², and 
Damage ≥80px². This annotation step makes it 
possible for each image out of the 12,450 in the 
encoding/defect dataset to be correctly labeled and 
masked based on the types of defect found by first 
localizing the fruit ROI (Region of Interest) using 
HSV-based 
segmentation, 
followed 
by 
morphological operation, before proceeding with the 
defect annotation process.

experiment 
is 
the 
categorical 
cross-entropy. 
Callbacks have been implemented as early stopping 
with patience such that training can be halted if 
validation 
loss 
is 
not 
improving, 
and 
ReduceLROnPlateau, which reduces the learning rate 
adaptively when a plateau is noted to tune 
convergence.

3.4 Hybrid efficientNet-swin transformer 
The 
Integrated 
Hybrid 
EfficientNet-Swin 
Transformer is designed to leverage the benefits of 
both vision transformer models and convolutional 
neural networks for end-to-end feature learning in 
palm oil fruit image analysis. 
By combining the Swin Transformers' capability 
of long-range dependency modelling and global 
context with the EfficientNet-B3’s capability of 
detailed local texture feature extraction, including 
color, edge, and texture pattern, this hybrid model is 
the perfect way to tackle both local and global 
features.

3.7 Multi-task learning with task-specific gates 
It 
conducts 
joint 
classification 
and 
segmentation/detection by learning in a common 
framework. The Shared Encoder utilizes a large 
number of generic features, at no effective cost to the 
parameters. This allows for few-shot training on 
semantics while benefiting multiple tasks at once, all 
thanks to a transferable representation that stabilizes 
cross-task 
learning. 
A 
task-specific 
gating 
mechanism 
dynamically 
redistributes 
channel

suppresses negative interference, as well as promotes 
channel adaptation. The main task is aimed at dealing 
with the ripeness classification and aligns features to 
class ripeness probabilities such that accuracy is 
maximized for shared representations. The defect 
segmentation/detection task in the auxiliary task is 
detecting and locating defect pixels/regions as an 
additional learning signal to boost the relationship of 
shared features without compromising classification 
accuracy. 
3.8 Evaluation & analysis 
Joint evaluation scores the model on both tasks 
simultaneously. The overall performance dynamics 
are measured by accuracy and loss, and the 
Classification Reports (Precision, Recall, F1-score) 
display the strengths, weaknesses, and biases specific 
to each class in the classification process. ROC 
curves 
and 
AUC 
measure 
classification 
discriminability independent of thresholds, while 
confidence distribution and calibration analysis 
ensure reliable probability scores. Performance 
evaluation in segmentation/detection problems is 
done by means of IoU (Intersection over Union) and 
the Dice coefficient, which measure the pixel-wise 
accuracy in the defect area localization, as well as the 
spatial overlap between predicted and ground-truth 
masks, guaranteeing a robust presence of defect 
detection, in addition to the classification accuracy. 
4. Result 
Five maturity stages of fruit were classified: 
Defect, Overripe, Ripe, Underripe, and Unripe. The 
input images were resized to 224×224 pixels as 
required by most pretrained models. The k value of 5 
was applied to perform a 5-fold cross-validation to 
expectantly balance the robustness and overfold bias. 
The model was trained for 50 epochs with a 
minibatch size of 16, using a learning rate of the 
optimizer of 1×10⁻⁴ and L2 regularization (weight 
decay 1×10⁻⁴) to avoid overfitting. The fully 
connected layers were regularized with dropout 
(p=0.5). The architecture utilized an 8-head multi-
head self-attention mechanism with a Swin 
Transformer backbone and the 7×7 window 
partitioning for hierarchical feature encoding. All 
experiments with different architectures were 
performed using the same random seed (seed=42) for 
reproducibility. 
An adaptive logic is used in the proposed defect 
segmentation model with respect to the defective 
pixels ratio: low (10%). Variegation and probability 
parameters are adaptively tuned. We fine-tune the 
prediction mask with morphological operations (1×1 
kernel erosion/dilation) to maintain an optimal trade-
off between true and false positives. The iterative 
refinement algorithm maximizes the Dice Coefficient 
at each level to minimize oversegmentation.

The EfficientNet+Swin Transformer+Dynamic 
Attention model achieves promising and consistent 
classification results in terms of cross-validation. The 
average test accuracy of the model is 97.16% (± 
0.34%), which shows good prediction in the test set. 
The harmonic mean of precision and recall (F1-score), 
which is 97.47% (± 0.45%) for the prediction model, 
indicates that not only does the model successfully 
identify positive classes, but also it labels most 
instances in the actual positive class, hence limiting 
both types of false predictions, i.e., FPs and FNs. An 
F1 score of ≈ 97.16% (±0.34%) is indicative of the 
optimal trade-off between precision and recall; this 
scenario represents a critical (real-world) aspect. The 
low standard deviation (<0.5%) for all evaluation 
criteria implies the good stability and generalization 
ability of our model, which demonstrates that this 
hybrid architecture is insensitive to variations in data 
and can deliver consistently trustworthy predictions. 
By integrating local feature extraction of EfficientNet, 
capturing 
global 
dependencies 
of 
the 
Swin 
Transformer, and the dynamic attention mechanism, 
our 
proposed 
L-RDAS 
enhanced 
model’s 
representation capacity in handling more complex 
classification tasks. 
This hybrid design integrates two networks, 
EfficientNet-B3 and Swin Transformer, as an 
ensemble 
network 
for 
multi-class 
image 
classification under a multi-task learning scheme. We 
use EfficientNet-B3 as a base convolutional feature 
extractor to produce initial feature maps and then 
process them by two Swin Transformer blocks (W-
MSA and SW-MSA) for global context capturing via 
a window-based self-attention mechanism. The 
generated feature maps are further improved by 
CBAM dynamic attention, where channel-wise 
attention and spatial-wise attention structures are 
performed in a nested manner to enhance the most 
information-rich features. The classification head 
uses pooling and feature transformation to compute 
class predictions, and the loss function combines the 
main classification loss with the object detection loss 
(IoU + DICE) as an auxiliary task.

Algorithm 1: EffNetB3+SwinT+ DA (Multi Head) 
and Segementation/Detection 
Input: Image batch X ∈ R^{B×224×224×3} 
1. EfficientNet-B3 Branch (CNN Feature 
Extraction):

     backbone ← 
EfficientNetB3(weights='imagenet', 
include_top=False) 
  b. Extract feature maps: 
     F_cnn ← backbone(X)  R^{B×7×7×1536} 
2. Swin Transformer Branch (Global Context): 
  a. Swin Block 1 (W-MSA): 
     x ← LayerNorm(F_cnn),  x ← Reshape(x, [B, 
H×W, C])   
     x_attn ← MultiHeadAttention(x, x, 
num_heads=8) 
     x ← Reshape(x_attn, [B, H, W, C]) + F_cnn   
     x_norm ← LayerNorm(x), x_mlp ← 
Dense(6144, activation='gelu')(x_norm) 
     x_mlp ← Dropout(0.1)(x_mlp), x_mlp ← 
Dense(1536)(x_mlp) 
     F_swin1 ← x_mlp + x  
  b. Swin Block 2 (SW-MSA): 
     x ← LayerNorm(F_swin1), x ← Reshape(x, [B, 
H×W, C]) 
     x_attn ← MultiHeadAttention(x, x, 
num_heads=8), x ← Reshape(x_attn, [B, H, W, 
C]) + F_swin1  
     x_norm ← LayerNorm(x), x_mlp ← 
Dense(6144, activation='gelu')(x_norm) 
     x_mlp ← Dropout(0.1)(x_mlp), x_mlp ← 
Dense(1536)(x_mlp) 
     F_swin2 ← x_mlp + x   
Branch (Feature Refinement): 
  a. Channel Attention (CBAM): 
     squeeze ← GlobalAvgPool(F_swin2)   
     excite ← Dense(96, activation='relu')(squeeze)   
     excite ← Dense(1536, 
activation='sigmoid')(excite) 
     excite ← Reshape(excite, [B, 1, 1, 1536]) 
     F_channel ← F_swin2 ⊙ excite

     avg_pool ← ReduceMean(F_channel, axis=-1, 
keepdims=True)   
     max_pool ← ReduceMax(F_channel, axis=-1, 
keepdims=True)    
     concat ← Concatenate([avg_pool, max_pool], 
axis=-1)   
     spatial_attn ← Conv2D(1, kernel=7×7, 
activation='sigmoid')(concat) 
     F_attention ← F_channel ⊙ spatial_attn   
3. Classification Head: 
  a. Global Average Pooling: 
     F_global ← GlobalAvgPool2D(F_attention)   
  b. Dense layers: 
     x ← Dropout(0.3)(F_global), x ← Dense(512, 
activation='relu')(x) 
     x ← BatchNormalization()(x), x 
←Dropout(0.3)(x) 
  c. Output layer: 
     y_pred ← Dense(num_classes=5, 
activation='softmax')(x)   
4. Multi-Task Loss: 
  a. Classification loss (primary): 
     L_cls ← 
CategoricalCrossentropy(y_pred,y_true) 
  b. Detection loss (auxiliary, if applicable): 
     L_det ← IoU_Loss(mask_pred, mask_true) + 
DICE_Loss(mask_pred, mask_true) 
  c. Total loss: 
     L_total ← L_cls + λ × L_det  
Output:  
  - Class predictions: y_pred ∈ R^{B×5} 
  - Total loss: L_total

  Initialize: models ← [], results ← [], groups ← 
extract_groups(D_train)

StratifiedGroupKFold.split(D_train, y_train, 
groups, k=fold) 
        X_train, y_train ← D_train[train_idx], X_val, 
y_val ← D_train[val_idx]

     IoU ← |mask_true ∩ mask_pred| / |mask_true ∪

increase in robustness against field changes.

5. Conclusion 
In this paper, we introduce a novel hybrid 
Efficientnet Swin Tansformer (EST) with dynamic 
attention and multi-task learning for accurate 
classification of the ripeness of oil palm fruits. 
Experimental results demonstrate that our model 
consistently outperforms various advanced methods 
under a large range of viewing conditions and 
classification 
settings, 
delivering 
an 
average 
performance of 97.16% (±0.47%) in terms of 
validation accuracy as opposed to the baseline 
performance value obtained from EfficientNet-B3, 
which equaled 95.21%. In addition, the precision 
(97.17%), recall (97.16%), and F1-score (97.16%) 
results also support that this hybrid model can 
outperform the state of the art in the detection of the 
ripeness stage for palm oil fruits with a low 
classification error rate even further. 
An ablation study indicated that the adaptive 
application of dynamic attention (DA) with a multi-
head mechanism on both spatial and channel levels 
can substantially enhance semantic representation 
and feature refinement so that the model can achieve 
better fruit ripeness detection performance. Another 
alternative (Defect): The model performed with a 
mean AUC of 99.79%, which indicates an excellent 
discrimination ability in all ripeness classes (Defect, 
Overripe, Ripe, Underripe, and Unripe). The 
combination of two learning streams (i.e., a global 
dependency modeling stream, Swin Transformer, and 
a local feature-extraction branch, EfficientNet-B3) 
was found to enhance the model's generalization 
ability across different field conditions, such as light 
changes, occlusions, and background complexity. 
Using 
multi-task 
learning 
with 
maturity 
classification as the primary task and defect 
detection/segmentation as an auxiliary task, robust 
and effective feature learning can be achieved. The 
model obtained an IoU of 0.7305 and a Dice 
coefficient of 0.7342 on the defect segmentation task, 
which is a 53% improvement in terms of IoU and 
38% for Dice over the initial baseline. The stability 
and generalization ability of the model on data 
variations are shown by the longstanding consistency 
across 5-fold cross-validation (std < 0.5%). 
However, such a hybrid model cannot escape 
from the computational time efficiency issue (275-
287 seconds per epoch in the first 50, and then 
stabilizing at 82-90 seconds per epoch). In the future 
we will work on making the model efficient for real-
time deployment, which could be achieved via 
lightweight transformer models or knowledge 
distillation, and more efficient attention mechanisms 
such as sparse attention. Additionally, a broader 
range of tests, including multispectral data and the 
creation of a continuous learning-based detection 
system, will help improve the adaptability of our 
model for dynamic plantation settings. Screens in the 
field Test robustness by testing with compression 
artifacts, frame drops, and extreme lighting changes 
to determine how well (if at all) the model will 
respond under other "field" conditions.
