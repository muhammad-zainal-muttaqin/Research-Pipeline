The advanced electronic technology makes the mobile phone suitable for citrus yield
estimation with image processing techniques. This article proposes a new method to es-
timate the yield of citrus two weeks ahead of the harvesting season for an individual tree
via image processing using an Android mobile phone (AMP). The procedure is essentially
fruit-counting algorithm software developed in Java that uses AMP touch panel monitor to
estimate citrus yield. The target photos were taken in natural conditions by the AMP. The
image processing was used to choose colour, segment image, generate binarisation image,
remove noise, and estimate fruit number. A modiﬁed 8-connectedness chain code to es-
timate the number of clustered citrus was introduced. The performance of the fruit-
counting algorithm software was validated with 40 samples of citrus trees and the
method achieved good precision and recognition ratio of 90%. The results indicated that
the approach could be utilised to estimate the fruit yield of an individual tree which is
valuable information to forecast yields, plan harvest schedules and generate prescription
maps for site-speciﬁc management practices on an individual tree basis within the grove.
Aided by the proposed approach, one can estimate the citrus yield without expensive
equipment.

1.
Introduction
Production forecasting is an important factor in precision
management. It can provide effective guidance for farmers on
product price and give a foundation for managing market
variables.
Yield estimation of individual citrus trees would provide
valuable information for generating prescription maps for
site- and tree-speciﬁc application of precision management
(Annamalai & Lee, 2003). During the past couple of decades,
there had been several studies on citrus fruit yield estimation.
Miller and Whitney (1999) evaluated different fruit-weighing
options as alternatives to fruit volume measurement in tubs.
Whitney, Miller, Wheaton, Salyani, & Schueller (1999) sug-
gested that spatially variable yield could be correlated to tree
canopy size determine from aerial photography. Noguchi,
Sakai, Asada, Garciano, and Sasao (2003) and Sakai, Noguchi,
and Asada (2003) investigated the alternate bearing dy-
namics in citrus trees by collecting fruit yield data of 97 indi-
vidual trees over a period of 7 consecutive years. Schumann,
Hostler, Miller, and Zaman (2004) developed and evaluated
a
sensor-based
automated
yield
monitoring
system
for
manually harvested citrus. Furthermore, Schumann and
Zaman (2005) developed and evaluated a Windows-based
software application for an ultrasonic tree size measure-

Asada, and Akira (2006) conducted a preliminary step for

However, these methods require expensive and cumber-

It was felt a simple, inexpensive and convenient approach was
quite important for citrus yield estimation. This study reports
our attempts to predict the citrus yield of an individual tree
two weeks ahead of the harvesting season using an Android
mobile phone (AMP) with image processing. The citrus yield
estimation method used only the popular AMP and did not
require any additional hardware.
2.
Materials
The image processing hardware was a smart mobile phone,
type I9300, obtained from Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd. (Korea).
This module incorporates a Samsung Exynos4412 1.4 GHz high
speed 4-core CPU. It also incorporates on-board volatile and
non-volatile memories and the Android 4.0 operating system.
Other components such as an 800-megapixel video camera
associate with LED ﬂashlight, GPRS module, Universal Serial
Bus interfaces, and 12.2 cm touch panel monitor are included. It
supports photographs up to a maximum 3264  2448 pixels to
be taken. These items make this ideal for fast prototyping,
evaluation, and development of software for applications.
The fruit-counting algorithm (FCA) software for image
capture and image processing was developed in Java, an
object-oriented programming language (Sun Microsystems),
and compiled using i.e. Eclipse (Eclipse Foundation, Version
eclipse-pulsar-helios-SR1-win32).
Android
Development
Tools (ADT) is a plug-in for the Eclipse IDE that is designed to
give a powerful, integrated environment to build Android
applications.
To test and verify the AMP-based FCA, 40 Gannan citrus
trees (growing in Ganzhou, Jiangxi, China) were selected for
the experiment. In situ images were obtained using the camera
of the AMP under natural daylight conditions in the two weeks
ahead of the harvesting season.
3.
System development
3.1.
Image collection
The AMP was placed at random angles relative to the ground
with the intention of covering the whole tree canopy. Fig. 1
shows the steps of the fruit yield estimation for a typical cit-
rus tree. The steps were to identify fruits from an image and
improve precision in counting the number of fruits.
3.2.
Image segmentation
Segmentation is a very commonly used term in machine vision
and refers to several kinds of image classiﬁcation techniques
(Pla, Juste, & Ferri, 1993; Slaughter and Harrell, 1989). For
example, segmentation has been used in the extraction of
contours and regions of an image (Falah, Bolon, & Cocquerez,
1994) and boundary detection (Ercal, Moganti, Stoecker, &
Moss, 1993). Features provide data reduction while preserving
the information required for segmentation. Once independent
objects of interest have been clearly segmented from an image,
the next step in the image analysis process is to measure the
individual features of each object. The simplest way to
segment an image is by a grey-level threshold or global
threshold (Annamalai, Lee, & Burks, 2004). Unfortunately, the
fruit portion, leaf portion, and background are not easily
differentiated using this method because the grey-level histo-
gram or colour histograms of these features are not unimodal.
Taking the characteristics of the AMP as well as citrus
colour into account, in this work, the operator selected the
colour of citrus from the image using the touch pen by clicking
the touch screen and then drawing a line on an individual
citrus. It must be noted that the line should not go outside the
selected object. After the line is conﬁrmed, the FCA will auto-
matically perform a 3 by 3 median ﬁlter to reduce noise caused
by sunlight scatter, and then search the R (red), G (green), and B
(blue) value of the sampled pixels for statistical analysis. The
citrus colour is primarily determined by the score of R and B,
where G was a constraint value. It was found that the citrus
could be separated from the background according to the in-
equalities 1 and 2. If the value of N is modiﬁed, a different
segmented image could be obtained. The FCA then calculated
the pixels of the individual citrus (PsIC) for the selected single
fruit and estimated the number of clustered fruits.

3.3.
Image binarisation
Once the CPU had determined that the pixel satisﬁes the in-
equalities (1) and (2), the value of the pixel was set as 255
(black); otherwise, its value took 0 (white). After image
reconstruction, binarisation was carried out in the colour
plane containing a clear distinction between the fruit and
background, resulting in black pixels representing the fruit
and white pixels for all other classes. Fig. 1b shows a
segmented and binary image.

3.4.
Noise reduction
The binarisation images contained noise mainly because of
the overlap of the leaves, tree branches, and sunlight (Fig. 1b).
It was necessary to ﬁlter out or otherwise reduce this noise. In
general, by applying a threshold of 30 pixels (based on an area
of the extracted features), noise from smaller regions could be
removed from the images. However, such a method also could
remove those fruits that were only a quarter of a single citrus
or less. The reason was that not all the fruits growing in nat-
ural conditions are visible in the same plane. To overcome this
problem, regions with less than 50 pixels were computed.
Subsequently, the neighbourhood distances were obtained. If
the distance was smaller than half of the diameter of the re-
gion, it was considered as noise and one of the regions was
removed from the binarisation image. There existed cavities
and holes in some target area, caused by sunlight or shelter
from the leaves or tree branches. These areas should be ﬁlled
up because they could inﬂuence further image processing,
and this can be done using ‘seeds ﬁll up’ algorithm (Heckbert,
1990; Shao and Zhou, 1995) which is a computer graphics to ﬁll
the graphics algorithms. Fig. 1c shows the image after noise
removal and area ﬁlling.
3.5.
Citrus quantity counting
The images with noise removed could subsequently be
employed to count the number of fruits using the FCA. How-
ever, the fruits are randomly distributed about the tree in
natural conditions, and are often clustered in an image. In the
binarisation image, they can be grouped into the same region,
and if the clustered fruits are counted as a single fruit, it would
lead to an unacceptable error in the estimated number of
fruits. The proposed approach computed the number of the
single citrus ﬁrst.
3.5.1.
Single citrus quantity estimation of the image
The regions where the pixels were smaller than the PsIC were
regarded as an individual fruits. These regions were counted
and would be removed from the image in order to avoid pro-
ducing additional errors by further image processing. The
image then contained only clustered fruits after image
reconstruction (Fig. 1d).

image

occupied by clustered fruits were relatively large, when
compared with other single fruits. The size of a fruit in the

was chosen during the process of colour selection. However, if
this size was directly used to estimate the number of clustered
citrus, the risk of misjudgement was still high because fruits

It was found that the pixel gradients along the surface of
the citrus abide by the elliptic. These pixels gradients could be
utilised, instead of using only the pixel values, emphasising
the shape of the object rather than the texture. Therefore, to
decrease the risk of misjudgement and increase the operation
speed of the algorithm, the proposed approach introduced a
modiﬁed 8-connectedness chain code (M8CCC) combined
with area estimation to compute the number of clustered
fruits.
3.5.2.1. Modiﬁed
8-connectedness
chain
code.
The
ﬁrst
approach for representing digital curves using chain code was
introduced by Freeman (Freeman, 1961; Gonzales and Woods,
2002). The direction of each segmented region is encoded using
a numbering scheme. A chain code is generated by following
the outline of an object and assigning a direction associated
with each sequential pair of pixels. A reasonable modiﬁcation
for 8-directional chain code is introduced as following.
 The region of interest is searched for a starting point and
transition point, i.e. the starting point is a peak ( y axis
minimum value) of the region boundary and the transition
point is a bottom ( y axis maximum value) of the region
boundary. The starting point and the transition point are
encoded as “S” and “9” respectively.
 The algorithm searches for the next neighbour pixel from
the starting point (only border pixels) according to the
connectedness deﬁnition used. Except for the transition
point, i.e. “9”, these pixels which change the orientation of
boundary, such as downward turn to upward or leftward
turn to rightward, are encoded “8”. If the code of the next
neighbour pixel is repeated then only one code is recorded.
Contour tracing ends when the algorithm reaches its start-
ing point.
 Finally, the result is a list of M8CCC showing the direction
taken in moving from each boundary pixel to the next, as
shown in Fig. 2. The number “8” of the M8CCC is counted
and the number of clustered fruits can be calculated ac-
cording to the equation (3).

3.5.2.2. Clustered citrus counting. For those regions where the
pixels were greater than 4/3 of the PsIC, an erosion and open
operation process was used to smoothen the edge of the re-
gions (Davies, 2005; Duda, Hart, & Stork, 2001), and the M8CCC
was used to distinguish the number of fruits (Fig. 1e). The
M8CCC could achieve the result similar to that obtained using
the LaplacianeGaussian segmentation algorithm method
while reduced a large number of data analysis process of the
software.
After processing through the above-mentioned steps, the
software counts the number of citrus (Fig. 1f). The estimation
result is displayed on the screen and stored in a database.

4.1.
Performance of the image segmentation algorithm
The N of in equation (1) was a global segmentation threshold.
Two of the forty images were used here to demonstrate the
performance of the segmentation algorithm at three thresh-
olds, i.e. 90, 110, and the algorithm automatically adjusted
(Fig. 4). The marked areas “1” to “7” of Fig. 4 point out some of
the major differences as the achieved images. Areas “1” to “4”

4.4.
Image processing time
Processing time is a major concern in a real-time machine
vision application. It was well known that the handheld de-
vice is quite slow to process data, when compared with a
computer. Thus, the algorithm should simplify the process as
far as possible to save CPU time, which might cause a slow
display or the device to crash.
When compared with the computer, the CPU and memory
limitation of the handheld device make the process of image
processing more complex. Owing to the limitation of the basic
frequency of the hardware, 1.4 GHz in this study, during the
experiment, the time for estimating one image of a tree was
about 3 s. Fortunately, the basic frequency of the embedded
handheld device is being improved, which will further
improve the image processing speed.
In general, the results showed that the individual citrus
tree yield several weeks ahead of harvesting could be esti-
mated in real time.
5.
Conclusions
A methodology using AMP for estimating the yield of citrus
two weeks ahead of harvesting was introduced. The auto-
matically adjusted threshold method for image segmentation
suggested that it was able to obtain sufﬁcient statistical
accuracy for citrus yield estimation. According to the char-
acteristics of the outline of the spherical fruit, a modiﬁed
8-connectedness chain code algorithm was used to determine
the number of fruits when they are clustered in the image. The
test result showed that the modiﬁed 8-connectedness chain
code algorithm contributed a satisfactory performance in
clustered fruit regions when the cluster contained less than
ﬁve fruit. Using the methodology for a single tree, a satisfac-
tory average estimation ratio (90%) between the actual and
predicted fruit yield was achieved. Thus, this study presents a
new method based on portable device for future citrus yield
prediction. Yield estimates of individual citrus trees obtained
by the developed methodology would provide valuable infor-
mation for planning harvest schedules and generating pre-
scription maps for site- and tree-speciﬁc application of
precision
management.
This
method
could
perhaps
be
applied for yield estimation of other crops whose fruit colour
shows obvious differences from their leaves.
