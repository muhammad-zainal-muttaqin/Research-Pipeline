Abstract
Grape caliber distributions are critical for packing, grading, yield estimation, and post-
harvest logistics. However, estimating reliable caliber histograms from single images
remains challenging due to occlusion and dense bunch structure. This work presents
a two-stage monocular pipeline that integrates instance segmentation, geometry-aware
representations, residual quantity correction, and structured histogram prediction. In the
first stage, a YOLO-based model detects grape instances and a calibration object, enabling
the construction of geometry-aware auxiliary channels and a segmentation-derived count-
ing prior. In the second stage, these representations are used to estimate total grape count
and caliber distributions. Results show that RGBDT consistently outperforms RGB, indicat-
ing that geometry-aware cues improve both histogram fidelity and counting accuracy. The
framework achieves stable performance under realistic conditions while maintaining low
runtime, supporting practical deployment in agricultural environments.
Keywords:
grape caliber estimation; grape caliber histogram prediction; instance
segmentation; monocular vision; distance transform; residual count estimation; lightweight
deep learning; ONNX deployment
1. Introduction
1.1. Problem Context and Motivation
Grape caliber, defined as the diameter of individual grape berries, plays a critical role
in viticulture and post-harvest operations, as berry-size distributions directly influence
packing, grading, yield estimation, and logistics planning [1]. However, in real-world envi-
ronments, grape bunches are captured under variable illumination, cluttered backgrounds,
viewpoint changes, and self-occlusion, which significantly complicates accurate estimation
from a single image. Consequently, relying solely on scalar descriptors such as mean
berry size is insufficient for many agricultural applications. Instead, distribution-level
representations, particularly caliber histograms, provide richer information by captur-
ing variability, skewness, and concentration across discrete size classes [2]. Therefore,
this study focuses on predicting the complete caliber distribution, reflecting a deliberate
effort to align computational modeling with practical decision-making requirements in
viticultural workflows.

1.2. Limitations of Existing Approaches
Existing vision-based solutions for grape measurement typically rely on controlled
acquisition setups, depth sensors, stereo rigs, or multi-view systems, which can deliver
accurate metric estimations [3]. However, these approaches introduce significant practical
constraints, including increased hardware cost, complex calibration procedures, synchro-
nization requirements, and reduced portability. As a result, their applicability in real-
world agricultural environments remains limited [4]. In contrast, field conditions demand
solutions that are robust to variability, simple to deploy, and computationally efficient.
The reliance on specialized acquisition systems restricts scalability and hinders widespread
adoption, motivating the exploration of lightweight, monocular alternatives better aligned
with operational constraints in viticulture.
1.3. Challenges of Monocular Estimation
Monocular RGB imagery provides a practical and scalable alternative for grape analy-
sis; however, it introduces significant challenges related to scene complexity and limited
visual information [5]. In particular, dense bunch structures produce frequent self-occlusion,
which hinders reliable counting and diameter estimation. Moreover, a single viewpoint
offers only partial evidence of the true caliber distribution, while neighboring berries in
clustered regions often appear visually ambiguous. Thus, these factors complicate the
inference of both total grape count and the full histogram of calibers from RGB appearance
alone. Therefore, addressing these limitations requires approaches that can compensate for
missing information and ambiguity inherent in monocular observations [6].
1.4. Opportunity: Learning-Based Monocular Pipeline
Recent advances in lightweight instance segmentation and deployable deep learning
models offer new opportunities to address monocular grape analysis more effectively [7].
Reliable detection of grape instances and a calibration object can support segmentation
outputs to serve not only for localization but also as structured intermediate representations.
Consequently, these representations may encode spatial relationships and approximate
geometric cues that are otherwise implicit in RGB data [8]. Therefore, a two-stage design
is motivated, where segmentation-derived features provide geometry-aware inputs and
an initial count prior, which are subsequently refined by dedicated models for total-count
correction and histogram estimation. This approach reflects a deliberate effort to decouple
perception from structured prediction while maintaining efficiency and deployability.
1.5. Proposed Approach
This work proposes a two-stage monocular pipeline for grape caliber analysis from
single images, combining instance segmentation with structured prediction to address
limitations of RGB-only inference [5]. In the first stage, a YOLO-based model detects
grape instances and a calibration object, enabling the construction of geometry-informed
feature channels via distance transforms and a segmentation-derived counting prior [9].
Subsequently, these representations are processed by a residual quantity model and a
histogram model to estimate total count and normalized caliber distribution, which are
combined to reconstruct the final histogram [10]. Furthermore, RGB and RGBDT modalities
are compared across multiple histogram-modeling families to assess representational and
parametric effects. Importantly, the two-stage design reflects a deliberate effort to decouple
geometric reasoning from distribution inference, improving robustness, interpretability,
and alignment with practical workflows requiring full distribution estimation.

1.6. Hypotheses
This study investigates whether monocular grape caliber estimation can be improved
by incorporating geometry-aware representations derived from segmentation outputs
rather than relying solely on RGB appearance. In particular, the proposed framework
evaluates the role of structured intermediate representations and modeling choices in
enhancing both distributional and counting accuracy.
Hypothesis 1. Augmenting RGB input with segmentation-derived geometric cues, specifically
grape and ping-pong distance-transform channels, improves both caliber histogram prediction and
total-count estimation compared to RGB-only representations.
Hypothesis 2. Leveraging a segmentation-derived counting prior with residual correction, com-
bined with flexible histogram formulations such as direct-bin prediction, provides more accurate
and robust estimation of grape count and caliber distributions than direct regression or strongly
constrained parametric models.
For completeness, the corresponding null hypotheses are defined as follows: (i) the
inclusion of geometry-aware channels does not produce measurable differences in his-
togram or count estimation compared to RGB-only input, (ii) residual correction does not
improve total-count estimation relative to direct prediction approaches, and (iii) different
histogram-modeling strategies do not produce statistically distinguishable performance
under the evaluated protocol.
1.7. Research Questions
The proposed framework introduces a geometry-informed, two-stage framework for
monocular grape caliber estimation, combining segmentation-derived representations with
structured prediction models. To systematically evaluate its effectiveness and practical rele-
vance, the study is guided by a set of research questions that address performance, represen-
tation design, and modeling strategies. In particular, the following questions aim to assess
the capability of the pipeline under realistic conditions, the contribution of geometry inputs
and residual learning, and the suitability of different histogram-modeling approaches for
capturing complex caliber distributions while maintaining deployment feasibility.
RQ1: To what extent can a two-stage monocular pipeline estimate grape caliber
histograms and total grape count from single images under realistic acquisition conditions?
RQ2: Does the incorporation of segmentation-derived geometric representations,
together with residual correction of a counting prior, improve performance compared to
RGB-only input and direct estimation approaches?
RQ3: Which histogram-modeling strategy provides the best trade-off between flexibility,
distributional fidelity, count consistency, and deployment efficiency in practical settings?

1.9. Contributions
The contributions of this work are fourfold. First, a two-stage monocular pipeline
is proposed, integrating instance segmentation, geometry-informed feature construction,
residual quantity estimation, and histogram prediction for grape caliber analysis. In this
context, “caliber” denotes berry diameter expressed in millimeters and used to define size

2. Related Work
This section reviews prior work related to vision-based fruit sizing, instance segmen-
tation for agricultural measurement, reference-based scale recovery, and structured output
prediction. The discussion highlights the limitations of existing approaches under real-
world conditions and motivates the need for geometry-aware, monocular pipelines capable
of reliable distribution-level inference and efficient deployment.
2.1. Vision-Based Fruit and Grape Sizing
Classical approaches to fruit sizing rely on geometric measurements derived from
controlled RGB imaging, often assuming uniform backgrounds, fixed camera distances,
and minimal occlusion. More recent methods incorporate depth sensors or stereo vision
to recover metric scale and improve diameter estimation accuracy [12]. Learning-based
approaches using convolutional neural networks have further improved detection and
segmentation performance; however, many still depend on structured acquisition setups or
multiple viewpoints [13]. In the context of grape analysis, dense clustering and frequent
occlusions pose additional challenges, making it difficult to generalize these methods to
field conditions where lighting, viewpoint, and scene complexity vary significantly.

2.2. Instance Segmentation for Agricultural Measurement
Instance segmentation has emerged as a powerful tool for agricultural measure-
ment tasks, enabling precise localization and delineation of individual objects such as
fruits, leaves, and clusters. Architectures such as Mask R-CNN and YOLO-based seg-
mentation models have been successfully applied to crop monitoring, yield estimation,
and phenotyping [14]. These models provide pixel-level masks that can be used to derive
geometric features, including size and shape descriptors. However, segmentation accuracy
may degrade under occlusion, overlapping instances, and complex backgrounds, which
are common in grape bunches [15]. Moreover, while segmentation facilitates object de-
tection, it does not directly solve the problem of inferring complete distributions from
partial observations.
2.3. Physical References for Metric Scale Recovery
Recovering real-world scale from monocular images is a long-standing challenge in
computer vision. One practical solution involves the use of physical reference objects with
known dimensions, such as calibration markers or standardized objects placed within the
scene [16]. These references allow the estimation of scale factors and geometric transfor-
mations that map image measurements to real-world units. In agricultural applications,
reference-based methods have been used for fruit sizing and plant measurement under
field conditions [17]. Nevertheless, their effectiveness depends on consistent visibility, accu-
rate detection, and proper geometric modeling, and they often do not address distortions
caused by perspective or non-planar arrangements.
2.4. Histogram and Structured Output Prediction
Predicting structured outputs such as histograms has gained relevance in tasks where
aggregate properties are more informative than individual predictions [18]. In particular,
methods based on direct bin regression, parametric distributions, and constraint-aware
learning have demonstrated effectiveness in controlled environments. However, under par-
tial observability, including occlusion and incomplete views, models must infer missing
information while preserving global consistency [19]. Consequently, techniques such as
distribution matching and auxiliary supervision have been explored, yet their application
to dense agricultural scenes remains limited due to variability and scarce annotations.
Despite these advances, existing approaches either emphasize geometric estimation or de-
tection and counting, rarely addressing full histogram prediction under realistic monocular
conditions [20]. Therefore, this gap motivates the development of a modular, geometry-
aware pipeline that bridges perception and distributional inference.
2.5. Limitations for Edge Deployment
Despite significant advances in vision-based measurement and deep learning, many
existing solutions are not optimized for edge deployment. High computational require-
ments, large model sizes, and reliance on specialized hardware limit their applicability in
real-time and resource-constrained environments [21]. Additionally, robustness to envi-
ronmental variability, including lighting changes and occlusions, is often insufficient for
field deployment. Lightweight architectures and model compression techniques, such as
quantization and pruning, have been proposed to address these issues [22], yet achieving
a balance between accuracy, efficiency, and scalability remains an open challenge. These
limitations motivate the development of approaches specifically designed for low-power,
real-time agricultural applications.

2.6. Research Gap and Contribution
Despite prior advances, a fundamental gap remains at the intersection of monocular
perception, structured distribution prediction, and deployable system design. Specifically,
existing approaches often address geometric estimation, detection and counting, or ef-
ficiency in isolation, while the joint problem of inferring complete caliber distributions
under realistic conditions remains insufficiently explored [23]. To address this, a modular
two-stage framework is introduced, integrating instance segmentation, geometry-aware
representation learning, and structured histogram prediction. Rather than focusing on
object-level outputs, the approach leverages segmentation-derived representations as inter-
mediate variables to support distribution-level inference [24]. This enables estimation of
full-caliber histograms while jointly modeling count scale and distribution shape through
residual correction. Furthermore, systematic evaluation across input modalities and model-
ing strategies, combined with deployment-oriented validation, positions the contribution
as a coherent and practical framework for monocular grape caliber analysis.
3. Materials and Methods
This section presents a framework for grape caliber histogram prediction and total-
count estimation from monocular imagery. Specifically, the approach is organized into
four components: the system pipeline for model construction, the inference pipeline defin-
ing operational behavior, the modeling formulation, and the design rationale guiding archi-
tectural choices. In addition, the dataset consists of monocular RGB images acquired under
realistic vineyard conditions, capturing variability in illumination, viewpoint, and occlu-
sion. Each sample includes a grape bunch and a ping-pong reference object for calibration.
The dataset covers multiple grape varieties and colors, with several hundred annotated
images and thousands of grape instances, exhibiting class imbalance toward individual
grapes. All samples were manually annotated using an instance segmentation protocol,
enabling both geometry-aware representation learning and structured histogram prediction
under practical conditions.

The system pipeline describes the process used to construct, train, and deploy the
models involved in the proposed framework. This includes dataset annotation, segmenta-
tion model training, generation of intermediate representations, and subsequent training of
prediction models. The overall system pipeline, including data preparation, model training,

3.2. Inference Pipeline
The inference pipeline defines how the proposed method operates on a single input
image at test time. The overall process is illustrated in Figure 2.
Figure 2. Overview of the proposed monocular pipeline for grape caliber histogram prediction.
A single RGB image is processed through three sequential stages: (1) perception, where prepro-
cessing, instance segmentation, and postprocessing extract grape instances and a reference object;
(2) geometric encoding, where segmentation outputs are transformed into geometry-aware repre-
sentations, including RGB and distance-transform channels; and (3) prediction, where a histogram
model and a residual quantity module jointly estimate the caliber distribution and total grape count.
The design emphasizes a single-input, feature-transformation pipeline suitable for robust estimation
under partial visibility and real-world conditions.
Given a single image of a grape bunch, the objective is to estimate both the total number
of berries and the full-caliber histogram over the range from 7 to 32 mm. First, a segmentation
model detects grape instances and the ping-pong calibration object. Second, the segmentation
output is converted into geometry-aware auxiliary maps, specifically a grape-instance distance
transform and a ping-pong distance transform. Third, these representations are used as input
to two prediction branches operating in parallel: a histogram prediction model that estimates
the normalized caliber distribution, and a residual quantity model that refines the total count
by correcting the segmentation-derived prior. The final predicted count per caliber bin is
obtained by combining both outputs.
3.3. Modeling Formulation
From a modeling perspective, the proposed method can be interpreted as a structured
decomposition of the inverse problem associated with monocular perception. The first stage
approximates a latent geometric representation of the scene through instance segmentation
and distance-transform encoding, effectively lifting the input from raw RGB appearance to
a spatially structured domain. The second stage operates on this representation to infer
global properties, namely the caliber distribution and total count, which are not directly
observable from the input.
This formulation enables the use of intermediate supervision and reduces the com-
plexity of the mapping between input and output spaces. By operating on structured
representations rather than raw images alone, the model benefits from improved stability

and enhanced interpretability, particularly in scenarios with occlusion and dense object ar-
rangements. The comparative evaluation focuses on variations of the proposed framework
under controlled conditions. While the design enables consistent comparison across input
modalities and histogram-modeling strategies, it does not include a direct baseline perform-
ing count estimation solely from raw image features. Therefore, the reported comparisons
should be interpreted within the scope of the defined experimental framework.
3.4. Design Rationale
The adoption of a two-stage formulation is motivated by the inherent difficulty
of jointly learning geometric structure and distributional outputs from raw image data.
In dense and occluded scenes, such as grape bunches, end-to-end approaches must im-
plicitly learn both instance separation and global aggregation within a single mapping,
increasing model complexity and sensitivity to noise.
By decoupling perception from structured prediction, the proposed design allows
segmentation to specialize in spatial delineation while the prediction stage focuses on
distribution-level inference. This separation improves learning efficiency, facilitates error
analysis, and enables modular experimentation. Furthermore, it supports practical deploy-
ment by allowing lightweight segmentation and prediction models to be independently
optimized and exported for edge inference.

3.5.2. Instance Segmentation Model and Training
Instance segmentation was performed using a YOLO-based segmentation model
operating at 512 × 512 input resolution. The model predicts masks for grape instances,
bunch regions, and the ping-pong reference. These masks constitute the first stage of the
proposed pipeline and provide the geometric information required by the downstream
histogram and quantity models.
The segmentation model was trained on the curated multi-class instance dataset
described above. During optimization, training progress was monitored through the
evolution of the segmentation losses and the corresponding box- and mask-based metrics
across epochs. After training, the segmentation model was exported for deployment,
preserving the mask-level predictions required by the complete inference pipeline.
3.5.3. Segmentation-Derived Inputs and Base Counting Prior
Before training the histogram and quantity models, each image is first processed by
the segmentation model. The predicted masks for grape_green, grape_red, and grape_black
are used to build the grape distance-transform channel, whereas the predicted pingpong
mask is used to generate the calibration-object distance-transform channel. In parallel,
the number of detected grape instances is computed and stored as the segmentation-derived
base count, denoted by seg_count_base.
To avoid recomputing segmentation at every training epoch, the segmentation outputs
and derived representations are cached in .npz format. Each cache stores the resized RGB
image, the grape distance transform, the ping-pong distance transform, and metadata such
as the number of grape detections, the number of ping-pong detections, the original image
size, and letterbox information.
3.6. Representation and Dataset Construction
3.6.1. Histogram Dataset and Ground-Truth Targets
The histogram dataset is organized as a structured table in which each row corresponds
to a single-image capture containing one bunch and one ping-pong reference. The schema
stores image identifiers; grape attributes such as variety, color, form, and size; total grape
count; minimum and maximum caliber; and per-bin caliber counts. For the histogram
prediction experiments, adjacent half-millimeter caliber annotations are aggregated into
integer-millimeter bins, producing the discrete target histogram used for supervision.
In parallel, the histogram-derived mean caliber is used as an additional supervisory signal
for the histogram models, while the total grape count is used to supervise the residual
quantity model. A representative example of the dataset, including the image capture and
associated annotation context, is shown in Figure 4.
It is important to note that, although the dataset captures variability in grape variety,
color, and bunch structure, it may still reflect biases associated with specific acquisition
conditions and sampling strategies. In particular, the distribution of calibers and total
counts is not uniform, with a concentration of samples in intermediate ranges, as observed
in the exploratory analysis. This imbalance may influence model training by favoring more
frequently observed configurations. Therefore, the reported results should be interpreted
within the context of the dataset characteristics, and future work should consider broader
data collection to improve generalization across diverse vineyard conditions.

3.7. Stage 2: Histogram and Total-Count Prediction
The input representations defined above serve as the foundation for the second stage
of the pipeline, where the objective shifts from geometric encoding to structured prediction.
In particular, the RGB and RGBDT modalities provide complementary information that is
exploited by the downstream models to infer both the global grape count and the caliber
distribution. The following section describes how these representations are processed
through a shared backbone and task-specific prediction modules. To ensure a consistent
comparison across input modalities and prediction tasks, all models share a common
feature extraction stage. This design isolates the effect of the input representation while
maintaining a unified visual encoding, allowing differences in performance to be attributed
to representation and modeling choices rather than architectural variability.
3.7.1. Shared Visual Backbone Architecture
All model families use the same visual backbone architecture, implemented through
an ImageVarietyBackbone. The image branch is based on a pretrained MobileNetV3-Small.

The histogram models are trained using a composite objective combining L1 loss

ducibility, a fixed experimental protocol was adopted, including consistent data partition-
ing, identical backbone architectures, and controlled hyperparameters. The use of cached
segmentation outputs ensures identical input representations across models, enabling fair
comparison of modeling strategies. Furthermore, preliminary repeated runs indicated
consistent trends across random initializations, suggesting stability of the reported results.
While full statistical characterization was not included, these observations support the
reliability of performance differences under the defined protocol.
The deployment experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an
NVIDIA GPU using ONNX Runtime for inference. The exported models were evaluated
without post-training optimizations, such as quantization or pruning, to preserve numerical
fidelity with respect to the original PyTorch (version 2.1.0) implementation. The reported
runtime reflects end-to-end inference, including preprocessing, segmentation, geometric
encoding, and prediction stages. From a practical perspective, the compact model size and
low latency suggest that the pipeline can operate in resource-constrained environments,
such as edge or mobile platforms, provided that hardware acceleration is available. How-
ever, deployment remains subject to constraints related to memory, energy consumption,
and robustness under variable acquisition conditions.
3.8.2. Evaluation Metrics
Histogram prediction quality was measured using histogram probability MAE,
Wasserstein-1 distance, histogram count-space MAE, and mean caliber MAE derived
from the histogram.
Peak localization was measured using exact peak accuracy,
peak ±1 accuracy, peak ±2 accuracy, and peak MAE in bins. Counting performance was
evaluated through total-count MAE, total-count RMSE, residual MAE, and residual RMSE.
3.9. Ethics and Reproducibility
The proposed methodology was developed using image data collected under stan-
dard agricultural conditions, without involving human subjects or sensitive information,
and therefore no ethical approval was required. The dataset consists exclusively of grape
bunch images and associated annotations, without privacy concerns. To ensure repro-
ducibility, all experiments followed a controlled protocol, including fixed data partitions,
identical backbone architectures, and standardized training hyperparameters. Cached seg-
mentation outputs and derived representations were reused across experiments, ensuring
consistent inputs and enabling fair comparison across model variants. Furthermore, the
implementation, including training scripts, configurations, and evaluation procedures,
will be publicly released in a GitHub repository (https://github.com/ScaleAI-cl/paper_
grapes_histogram_prediction, accessed on 1 June 2026) upon acceptance. This will facilitate
independent verification and extension of the proposed framework. Additional documenta-
tion of experimental parameters and pretrained models will further support reproducibility
and transparency.

consistently better performance under the evaluated protocol and improved stability in

4.2.4. Effect of Histogram Parameterization
The comparison between histogram-modeling families showed that highly constrained
output parameterizations were not optimal in this dataset. The unimodal and bimodal

hybrid variants ranked immediately below the best direct-bin model, suggesting that shape-

baseline model performing count estimation solely from image features was not included,
and therefore conclusions should be interpreted within the scope of the proposed frame-

5. Discussion
The results support four main conclusions. First, the instance segmentation stage is
sufficiently reliable to support the downstream pipeline, both as a source of geometry-aware
inputs and as the origin of the segmentation-derived counting prior. Second, geometry-
aware channels derived from segmentation consistently improve histogram prediction and
total-count estimation. Third, flexible histogram parameterizations outperform strongly
constrained ones in this dataset. Fourth, residual count correction is an effective strategy
for exploiting segmentation outputs without forcing the model to reconstruct cardinality
from scratch.

5.1. Segmentation as an Enabling Stage
The proposed framework depends on segmentation not only for object localization
but also for the construction of the grape and ping-pong distance-transform channels and
for the computation of the base counting prior. In this context, the segmentation results
are important because they validate the feasibility of the two-stage design. The high mask-
level performance, especially on the pingpong class and grape subclasses, indicates that
the downstream models receive sufficiently reliable geometric cues to support histogram
and count prediction. At the same time, the segmentation results also clarify the main
sources of downstream error. The confusion patterns suggest that the most relevant
segmentation failures are missed grapes and background fragments confused as grapes [25].
These failure modes are not isolated: they can propagate to the RGBDT channels and to
seg_count_base, thereby affecting both the histogram branch and the residual quantity
model. Thus, segmentation should be interpreted as an enabling stage whose quality
directly conditions the performance ceiling of the full pipeline.
5.2. Influence of Geometry-Aware Input Channels
The most consistent trend across the experiments was the superiority of RGBDT over
RGB. All top four-ranked histogram models used RGBDT inputs, and each histogram
family improved when the distance-transform channels were included. This indicates that
the geometric structure encoded by the grape and ping-pong distance transforms provides
information that is not recoverable from RGB appearance alone.
A likely explanation is that these channels make local object extent, separation between
touching berries, and calibration-reference structure more explicit. This is particularly
useful in dense bunches, where appearance alone may be insufficient to distinguish adjacent
berries or to preserve shape-related cues relevant for caliber prediction. Importantly, these
gains were obtained without introducing large downstream models, since the additional
channels are lightweight once segmentation has been computed.
5.3. Why Direct-Bin Prediction Performed Best
A second important finding is that the best overall model was rgbdt_direct_bins, rather
than a unimodal or bimodal alternative. This suggests that the empirical caliber histograms
in the dataset are not adequately described by overly restrictive shape assumptions. Real
bunches may exhibit asymmetric, broad, skewed, or mildly multimodal distributions that
benefit from direct prediction of bin probabilities.
The competitive performance of the CDF and CDF-direct hybrid variants further
supports this interpretation. Some amount of output structuring appears helpful, especially
because caliber bins are ordered, but excessive parametric restriction reduces fidelity. In this
sense, the direct-bin RGBDT model offers the best compromise between flexibility and
stability, preserving the ability to represent irregular histogram shapes while still learning
from geometry-aware input cues.
5.4. Value of Residual Count Learning
The residual quantity model also proved to be a strong design choice. Rather than
replacing segmentation-based counting, the model uses the number of detected grapes as a
prior and learns to correct its systematic bias. This is advantageous because segmentation
already captures a large amount of counting information but tends to undercount when
berries are occluded, merged, truncated, or fragmented.
By learning only the missing correction, the quantity branch solves a simpler prob-
lem and reaches a lower total-count error than a direct cardinality estimator would likely
achieve under the same conditions. This strategy also integrates naturally with the his-
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16126225

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 6225
21 of 25
togram branch, since the final count-per-bin prediction depends directly on a reliable
estimate of total bunch cardinality. This formulation aligns with established residual-
learning approaches in regression and counting tasks, where coarse predictions are refined
through learned corrections to improve robustness and stability under noisy or incomplete
observations [26].
5.5. Insights from Qualitative Examples
The qualitative examples shown in Figure 8 complement the aggregate metrics by
illustrating model behavior across varying difficulty levels. In the best cases, the model
captures dominant caliber modes, preserves distribution spread, and closely matches total
count, indicating that reliable segmentation-derived inputs support accurate reconstruction
of both shape and scale. In intermediate cases, the global trend is maintained, although mass
is redistributed across neighboring bins, suggesting that residual errors arise from local
ambiguity rather than misinterpretation. From an application perspective, such deviations
are less critical than large count errors or distribution shifts. In contrast, failure cases
exhibit peak displacement, over-smoothing, and count misestimation. In some instances,
qualitative inspection suggests mismatches between visible structure and annotations,
likely reflecting labeling challenges under occlusion rather than systematic errors. Overall,
performance degrades under occlusion, high density, and segmentation uncertainty.
5.6. Analysis of Hypotheses and Research Questions
The experimental results provide consistent evidence to analyze the proposed hypothe-
ses and research questions from both performance and modeling perspectives. With respect
to RQ1, the two-stage monocular pipeline enables stable estimation of total grape count
and caliber histograms under realistic conditions, suggesting that separating perception
and inference helps address partial observability. Regarding RQ2, the comparison between
RGB and RGBDT indicates that geometry-aware channels improve both histogram fidelity
and count estimation, likely by encoding spatial cues not captured by RGB alone. For RQ3,
residual correction enhances robustness by compensating systematic biases in segmentation-
derived priors. From a modeling perspective, flexible approaches better capture irregular
distributions. Furthermore, residual learning aligns with established correction-based
frameworks in regression and counting tasks [27] that refine coarse predictions to improve
stability under uncertainty [28]. Overall, the results highlight a robust yet constrained
framework, with remaining challenges linked to occlusion and segmentation uncertainty.
5.7. Practical Implications for Deployment
From a deployment perspective, the reported runtime results are encouraging.
The learned histogram and quantity models are lightweight, compact, and faithfully ex-
portable to ONNX. The dominant computational cost lies in the segmentation stage, not
in the downstream predictors. Therefore, future optimization efforts for real-time or em-
bedded deployment should focus primarily on accelerating the segmentation network
or reducing the cost of channel construction. This is a practically favorable outcome.
Since the histogram and quantity models contribute only a small fraction of the total run-
time, the method remains deployable even when multiple histogram parameterizations
are explored during development. The compact ONNX footprint also supports use in
phenotyping and quality-control settings where lightweight inference is desirable [29].
From an application perspective, the proposed framework can be integrated into existing
viticultural workflows through mobile or edge-based acquisition systems. For instance,
the lightweight ONNX models and low runtime suggest feasibility for deployment on
handheld devices or field robots, enabling near real-time estimation during harvesting
or inspection [30]. Such integration would require minimal user interaction, relying on

single-image capture and automated inference, thereby aligning with practical constraints
in agricultural environments.
5.8. Limitations
Despite favorable results, several limitations remain. First, the effectiveness of the
residual count prior depends on segmentation quality, where occlusion, mask fragmen-
tation, or domain shift may degrade both auxiliary representations and base counts.
Second, histogram targets rely on annotation quality, and limited label noise may in-
fluence training and evaluation. Third, the evaluation is conducted on a single dataset
under bounded conditions and therefore does not explicitly assess cross-domain general-
ization across vineyards, seasons, or acquisition setups. Furthermore, although RGBDT
consistently outperforms RGB, its benefits depend on the robustness of the segmentation
stage, and errors may propagate through geometric encoding. From a methodological
perspective, the reuse of a shared quantity model ensures that histogram comparisons
isolate parameterization effects while maintaining consistent count estimation. Overall,
these findings highlight the need for improved robustness, interpretability, and cross-
domain validation in future work.
5.9. Future Work
Future work should explore several directions. First, improving segmentation robust-
ness may enhance both total-count estimation and histogram accuracy. Second, uncertainty-
aware histogram prediction could provide confidence estimates in ambiguous or low-
support regions. Third, multi-view fusion may improve robustness by mitigating self-
occlusion and partial visibility in dense bunches. Furthermore, evaluating the framework
across diverse acquisition conditions and domains is necessary to better characterize gen-
eralization. Overall, the results indicate that combining segmentation-derived geometric
cues, residual quantity correction, and flexible histogram modeling provides an effective
and deployable strategy for grape caliber analysis. From a practical perspective, future
work will also explore quantization and hardware-specific optimizations to further reduce
latency and energy consumption for embedded deployment.
6. Conclusions
This study introduced a two-stage monocular pipeline for grape caliber analysis from
single images. The proposed approach combines instance segmentation, segmentation-
derived geometric channels, residual quantity correction, and histogram prediction within
a lightweight and deployable framework.
The results show that the segmentation stage provides a sufficiently reliable basis for
downstream prediction, both for geometry-aware feature construction and for the initial-
ization of the counting prior. They also show that RGBDT inputs consistently improve
performance over RGB alone and that the best-performing model is rgbdt_direct_bins,
indicating that flexible direct-bin histogram prediction is better suited to the observed grape
caliber distributions than more constrained unimodal or bimodal formulations. In addition,
residual learning over a segmentation-derived count prior proved effective for total-count
estimation, improving the final reconstruction of count-level histograms. The effectiveness
of residual correction is demonstrated relative to a segmentation-derived counting prior;
however, a direct comparison against a baseline model performing count regression solely
from image features was not included in the current study. Therefore, the reported improve-
ments should be interpreted as evidence of consistency within the proposed framework
rather than as a definitive comparison against all possible counting strategies.

From a deployment perspective, the exported ONNX models preserved numeri-
cal fidelity, and the complete pipeline achieved lightweight runtime, with segmentation
remaining the dominant computational cost. Although the method still depends on seg-
mentation quality and some difficult samples may include substantial prediction errors
or possible annotation discrepancies, the overall results support the proposed design
as a practical strategy for monocular grape caliber estimation. Some discrepancies ob-
served in qualitative examples may reflect inherent challenges in defining precise ground-
truth distributions under dense and partially occluded conditions, rather than systematic
annotation errors.
Future work should address segmentation robustness, uncertainty-aware prediction,
multi-view extensions, and broader cross-domain evaluation. Importantly, these directions
are presented as prospective developments rather than conclusions directly supported
by the current evidence, reflecting a deliberate effort to guide further investigation. Be-
yond this scope, the proposed framework illustrates a broader paradigm for structured
prediction from monocular imagery, combining perception, geometry-aware representa-
tions, and distribution-level inference within a lightweight architecture. This design enables
both local object understanding and global aggregate estimation under partial observability.
Furthermore, the integration of efficient perception models with structured prediction
highlights a scalable approach for deployable agricultural systems, where robustness, com-
putational efficiency, and adaptability are essential. Overall, this perspective suggests a
transferable design principle for similar tasks involving dense object arrangements and
constrained operational environments.
