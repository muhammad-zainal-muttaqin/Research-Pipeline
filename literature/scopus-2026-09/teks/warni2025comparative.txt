Abstract—Automated detection of loose fruits has become a 
critical factor in minimizing harvest losses in oil palm 
plantations. This study aims to evaluate and compare the 
performance of four generations of YOLO models (YOLOv5, 
YOLOv8, YOLOv9, and YOLOv11) for detecting loose fruits 
palm oil, focusing primarily on accuracy and inference speed. 
A total of 20 different model scales were tested using a 
carefully annotated plantation image dataset resized to 640 x 
640 pixels. Model performance was evaluated based on 
precision, recall, mean average precision (mAP), and inference 
time (milliseconds) on both GPU based systems and edge 
devices. Results showed that lightweight models such as 
YOLOv5s and YOLOv8s achieved mAP scores around 0.83 
with inference times below 340 ms, making them suitable for 
real 
time 
applications. 
Conversely, 
YOLOv9m 
and 
YOLOv11m achieved the highest accuracy with mAP scores 
exceeding 0.86, but their inference times were longer, 
surpassing 800 ms, making these models more appropriate for 
server based inspection applications. The largest models in 
each generation provided only slight improvements in accuracy 
but were accompanied by significant increases in inference 
time, highlighting a saturation point in the trade off between 
model complexity and performance. Consequently, this study 
offers a comprehensive performance map, serving as a 
practical guide for selecting appropriate YOLO based loose 
fruit detection models tailored to specific application 
requirements. The key contribution of this research lies in 
providing a scientific basis for choosing detection models that 
effectively balance accuracy and computational efficiency, 
thereby accelerating the integration of computer vision 
technologies into smart agricultural practices. 
Keywords— Loose Fruits Detection, YOLO, Loose Fruits 
Palm Oil, Real Time Object Detection, Computer Vision.  
 
I. 
INTRODUCTION  
Indonesia the worlds leading palm oil producer is under 
pressure to raise harvest efficiency while reducing field 
losses. Automated object detection of loose fruitlets, dried 
fronds, and stones is therefore a prerequisite for harvesting 
robots and intelligent monitoring systems [1], [2].

Over the past decade, artificial intelligence (AI) and deep 
learning have expanded rapidly in agronomy, yet their use 
remains concentrated on canopy segmentation or basic image 
classification tasks [3], [4]. A recent systematic review 
confirms that YOLO based methods dominate agricultural 
object detection, but persistent challenges include data 
scarcity, domain shift, and the need for real time inference on 
edge devices [5]. 
The phytopathology community likewise stresses the 
need for larger curated datasets and lightweight architectures 
that can bridge laboratory research with field deployment [6]. 
This cross disciplinary perspective underscores the urgency 
of evaluating multiple YOLO generations under standardized 
conditions in palm plantation scenarios to accelerate 
technology adoption. 
Most earlier palm oil detection studies evaluate only a 
single YOLO version or convolutional network, hindering a 
comprehensive appraisal of speed accuracy trade offs, 
particularly amid complex backgrounds and overlapping 
canopies [7]. Moreover, two stage pipelines for harvest 
readiness prediction 
still 
rely on 
a 
single model 
configuration, 
making 
them 
vulnerable 
to 
varying 
illumination [4]. 
The You Only Look Once (YOLO) family offers a fast 
single stage solution, and each generation introduces new 
backbones, attention mechanisms, and loss functions that 
boost accuracy. Nevertheless, no study has yet assessed the 
consistent performance of five successive YOLO generations 
(v5–v11) across model scales for palm related object 
detection. This gap is critical given evidence that the small 
variant of YOLOv11 can achieve real time operation in 
automotive applications [8] and that hybrid feature extractors 
can further raise accuracy [9]. 
Junos et al. [10] introduced YOLO-P, a DenseNet 
backboned, anchor tuned variant for robotic harvesting 
systems that reached a mean average precision (mAP) of 
98.7 %. Combined DenseNet with MBConv layers in a four 
scale YOLOv3-tiny, boosting the F1 score to 0.91 while 
keeping the model under 50 MB [11]. Two stage strategies 
have also emerged. Daud et al. [4] employed YOLOv5 to 
detect palm trees, followed by a separate fruitlet detector to 
predict harvest readiness, whereas Ang et al. [12] 
demonstrated that a Faster R-CNN with an Inception v2 
backbone can run on a Jetson TX2 with 94 % accuracy. At

canopy scale, Mubin et al. [7] used CNNs to distinguish 
young from mature palms in satellite imagery but noted 
accuracy loss in densely overlapping crowns. Liu et al. [13] 
detected palms from UAV orthomosaics using slide cropping 
and Faster R-CNN, highlighting challenges posed by object

Bibliometric analysis shows that YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 
dominate current agricultural applications, while newer 
generations (YOLOv8–v11) remain largely untested in this 
domain Badgujar et al., [5]. Additional constraints include 
small dataset sizes and non uniform evaluation protocols, 
which limit model generalizability Khan et al., [3]. From a 
technical standpoint, plant disease studies emphasize the 
need for extensive datasets and compact architectures to 
ensure field-ready solutions  Pacal et al., [6]. Consequently, a 
comparative analysis across YOLO generations, using 
consistent metrics, is necessary to map the speed accuracy 
frontier and to assess model robustness against the visual 
complexity of palm plantation imagery. 
The hypothesis of this study posits that medium sized 
variants (s/m) from each YOLO generation (v5, v7, v8, v9, 
v11) will lie on the Pareto frontier achieving an mAP ≥ 0.85 
at IoU 0.5 with inference latency ≤ 300 ms on 640-pixel-
resolution images. The aim is to perform a comprehensive 
performance evaluation of five YOLO generations on three 
critical object classes loose fruitlets, dried leaves, and stones 
using a uniformly annotated dataset and identical testing 
protocols. The study covers (1) acquisition and augmentation 
of field images, (2) training seven model scales per 
generation, (3) measuring accuracy, speed, and model size on 
desktop GPUs and edge devices, and (4) statistical analysis 
to determine the significance of performance differences. 
The novelty lies in providing the first thorough cross 
generation YOLO mapping in the palm oil domain and in 
supplying practical guidelines for model selection for 
computer vision applications in oil palm plantations.. 
II. 
METHODOLOGY 
A. Proposed System 
The diagram in Figure 1 illustrates the proposed system 
for detecting loose fruits in oil palm plantations using a deep 
learning approach with YOLO models. The system is 
divided into two main stages: Training Process and Testing 
Process. In the training phase, the process begins with data 
input, data labeling, and dataset splitting into training and 
testing sets. Then, data preprocessing is performed, which 
includes augmentation techniques such as flipping, resizing, 
brightness adjustment, and saturation adjustment to increase 
data variability. Once preprocessing is complete, the data is 
trained using various YOLO models (YOLOv5, YOLOv8, 
YOLOv9, YOLOv11). 
In the Testing Process, the trained models are used to 
detect loose fruits in the test dataset. This phase starts with 
data input and preprocessing similar to the training phase to 
maintain consistency. Object detection is then carried out 
using the exported YOLO models. The detection results are 
evaluated through a performance comparison to measure the 
effectiveness of each model in accurately detecting small 
objects (loose fruits). This evaluation aims to identify the 
best-performing model based on relevant performance 
metrics. The process concludes after the comparison of 
evaluation results is completed.

B. Input Data 
The dataset utilized in this research comprised both 
primary and secondary data. The primary data were directly 
collected from field sources and consisted of image samples 
of loose fruits palm oil captured at two camera heights, 
namely 0.5 m and 1 m. To ensure robustness, the loose fruits 
were arranged under different conditions, including fully 
visible, partially occluded by leaves, and stacked upon one 
another. 
To complement the primary dataset, secondary data were 
obtained from the Roboflow repository. These additional 
samples were employed to address class imbalance during 
model training. The secondary dataset contained images of 
dry leaves and stones, which were incorporated as 
representative classes of non fruit objects commonly found 
in palm oil plantations and exhibiting visual characteristics 
such as shape and color similar to palm oil loose fruits. 
labeling is completed, the data will be divided into three 
groups: training, validation, and testing. The total dataset 
consists of 597 images, which are split into 417 training 
images, 120 validation images, and 60 testing images.

C. Data Labelling 
Data labeling is a crucial stage in the process of training 
deep learning based object detection models, as the accuracy 
of the model is highly dependent on the quality of image 
annotations [5]. After data collection and input, the image 
data will be labeled which will be divided into three classes, 
dl, lf, and r. The dl class represents a dry leaf object that 
resembles the color of an oil palm stubble object. Class lf 
represents the oil palm stubble object which is the main 
object of the research. Class r represents a stone object that 
resembles the shape of an oil palm stubble object. 
D. Split Dataset 
After data labeling is completed, the data will be divided 
into three groups: training, validation, and testing. The total 
dataset consists of 597 images, which are split into 417 
training images, 120 validation images, and 60 testing 
images. 
E. Preprocessing 
After the dataset is shared, a pre-processing stage is 
performed, 
consisting 
of 
image 
resizing 
and 
data 
augmentation [14]. All images are resized to 640×640 in 
order to standardize the input dimensions and enhance the 
performance of the YOLO model. This step ensures that 
variations in the original image resolutions do not influence 
the training process and that the model receives uniform 
inputs across the dataset. The resizing procedure is applied 
consistently to the training, validation, and test sets to 
guarantee comparability of results. Furthermore, data 
augmentation is carried out to mitigate overfitting and 
improve the model’s generalization capability [15]. The 
augmentation 
techniques 
employed 
include 
flipping, 
brightness adjustment, saturation adjustment, and exposure 
adjustment, 
which 
simulate 
diverse 
environmental 
conditions. In addition, these transformations strengthen the 
robustness of the model by enabling it to recognize objects 
under different orientations, lighting variations, and visual 
contexts. 
F. Training Process 
The YOLO model was trained using the following 
hyperparameter settings: 500 epochs, a batch size of 32, the 
SGD optimizer, and an initial learning rate of 0.01. 
G. Testing Process 
The output of the YOLO training process is a model 
intended 
for 
real 
time 
detection 
implementation. 
Additionally, model evaluation is conducted to assess 
whether 
its performance is 
sufficient for practical 
deployment. The trained model is further tested on nine real 
time video samples captured at a height of 20 cm, which 
corresponds to the typical operating height of robots used for 
loose fruits palm oil detection. 
III. 
RESULT AND DISCUSSION 
This section presents the results of the performance 
evaluation of four generations of YOLO models, namely 
YOLOv5, YOLOv8, YOLOv9, and YOLOv11, in detecting 
loose fruits in oil palm plantation environments. The 
evaluation was carried out based on the Precision, Recall, 
mean Average Precision (mAP) metrics at the threshold of 
0.5 and 0.5:0.95, as well as the inference time in 
milliseconds. Each model variant was evaluated to illustrate 
the trade-off between accuracy and speed, which is an 
important consideration in model selection for both real time 
and non-real time applications. The results presented aim to 
provide a comprehensive understanding of the advantages 
and limitations of each model, as well as recommendations 
for its use based on the needs of applications in the field.

E. Comparative Analysis 
The evaluation of multiple YOLO model versions in this 
study reveals distinct trade offs between detection accuracy 
and inference speed. YOLOv5s, YOLOv7, and YOLOv8s 
emerged as the models offering the best compromise 
between speed and precision. These lightweight networks 
achieved real time performance on limited hardware while 
still maintaining reliable accuracy in detecting objects. In 
practical terms, such models proved particularly effective for 
tasks like identifying small objects in an oil palm plantation 
environment 
(e.g., 
loose 
fruits 
palm 
oil), 
where 
computational resources are constrained and prompt 
detection is required. 
Notably, the YOLOv8 series introduced architectural 
improvements that further enhanced inference efficiency 
with only a minor impact on accuracy. In our tests, the small 
and 
medium 
variants 
(YOLOv8s 
and 
YOLOv8m) 
demonstrated fast inference speeds comparable to earlier 
small models, yet delivered improved detection performance. 
This makes the YOLOv8 family, especially its smaller 
models, a versatile choice when an application demands a 
careful balance between speed and accuracy. 
On the other hand, the medium sized models from the 
newer YOLO versions, YOLOv9m and YOLOv11m, 
achieved the highest detection accuracy among all models

evaluated. These models excelled at detecting small and 
overlapping objects, as reflected by their higher mean 
Average Precision (mAP) compared to the YOLOv5 and 
YOLOv8 series. This superior performance can be attributed 
to the advanced architectures of YOLOv9 and YOLOv11, 
which provide improved feature extraction and finer object 
localization for small targets. However, this top tier accuracy 
came at the cost of longer inference times. The increased 
latency means that while YOLOv9m and YOLOv11m can 
detect challenging objects more reliably, they are more suited 
for deployment in server based environments or offline 
inspection systems rather than on resource limited devices in 
the field. 
Furthermore, the largest variants in each model series 
such as YOLOv5x, YOLOv8x, YOLOv9e, and YOLOv11x 
offered only marginal accuracy gains but imposed substantial 
computational 
burdens. 
Their 
inference 
times 
were 
significantly longer, making them impractical for any real 
time use case. This indicates a point of diminishing returns 
with increasing model size: beyond a certain complexity, the 
modest gains in accuracy are outweighed by sharply higher 
inference latency. These high complexity models are 
therefore better reserved for scenarios where detection speed 
is not critical and the utmost accuracy is prioritized. For 
example, they could be employed in batch processing on 
cloud servers or for detailed offline analysis, where slower 
inference is acceptable. 
In summary, the findings underscore the importance of 
selecting an object detection model aligned with the 
applications needs. For real time detection on edge devices 
with limited resources, lightweight models like YOLOv5s 
and YOLOv8s are ideal due to their fast inference and 
adequate accuracy. To maximize detection accuracy in more 
controlled settings (such as GPU equipped servers), medium 
sized models like YOLOv9m or YOLOv11m offer superior 
performance on difficult targets. Meanwhile, extremely large 
models (e.g., YOLOv5x, YOLOv8x, YOLOv11x) should 
only be considered when real time performance is 
unnecessary and the goal is to achieve the absolute highest 
possible accuracy. Overall, the choice of YOLO variant 
should be guided by whether speed, accuracy, or a balanced 
trade off is the primary requirement of the intended 
application. 
F. Examples of YOLO Models Detection Results 
The test data used in this study consisted of loose palm 
oil fruits stacked within a single area. This situation reflects 
actual field conditions in oil palm plantations, where loose 
fruits are not always evenly scattered on the ground but 
often accumulate due to simultaneous falls or obstruction by 
fronds. Such conditions pose challenges for detection 
models because the target objects are located in very close 
proximity, with partial occlusion, which may lead to 
misclassification. Therefore, testing under this scenario is 
essential to evaluate the robustness of the model against 
more complex field conditions compared to previous 
scenarios. 
Figure 2 presents a visual example of the detection 
results of the two models compared with the ground truth. 
The visualization indicates that YOLOv5s and YOLOv8s 
are generally capable of accurately recognizing clusters of 
loose fruits, as reflected by average mAP values above 
0.830 and 0.853, respectively. This visual evidence not only 
corroborates the quantitative findings but also reinforces 
that YOLOv5s and YOLOv8s demonstrate greater stability 
and reliability when faced with stacked fruit conditions, 
making them the most optimal models for real-world 
implementation in oil palm plantations.

IV. CONCLUSION  
This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the 
performance of loose fruits detection using four generations 
of YOLO models (YOLOv5, YOLOv8, YOLOv9, and 
YOLOv11) within the context of oil palm plantation 
applications. Based on the results obtained, lightweight 
models such as YOLOv5s and YOLOv8s offer the best 
balance between accuracy and inference speed, making them 
highly suitable for real time detection applications on edge 
devices with limited resources. Conversely, medium sized 
models like YOLOv9m and YOLOv11m demonstrate the 
highest accuracy in detecting small and overlapping objects 
but with higher inference times, making them more 
appropriate for server based or offline inspection systems 
where real time speed is not critical.

These findings emphasize that the selection of object 
detection models must be tailored to the specific needs of the 
application, in terms of both accuracy and processing speed. 
Large scale models such as YOLOv5x, YOLOv8x, 
YOLOv9e, 
and 
YOLOv11x 
yield 
only 
marginal 
improvements in accuracy at the cost of significantly longer 
inference times and substantial computational demands, 
making them suitable only for batch analysis or cloud-based 
scenarios with abundant resources. Accordingly, this study 
contributes significantly to the literature in the field of 
computer vision for the agricultural sector, particularly in 
providing data driven references for selecting optimal object 
detection models aligned with field implementation 
requirements. 
This work has several limitations, including the 
relatively small dataset (597 annotated images) that may not 
fully capture field variability such as illumination changes, 
seasonal factors, and occlusions, as well as the restricted 
evaluation on static images and limited video samples, 
which does not reflect long-term deployment stability. In 
addition, the study only compared YOLO series (v5, v8, v9, 
v11), without exploring transformer based or hybrid 
architectures that have shown promise for small object 
detection. Future research should therefore focus on 
expanding the dataset with more diverse conditions, 
integrating multi spectral or depth sensors for robustness, 
and investigating lightweight transformer based detectors, 
knowledge distillation, and model compression techniques 
to achieve real time accuracy on edge devices. Moreover, 
incorporating detection into a full autonomous harvesting 
pipeline would provide more practical insights into real 
world deployment
