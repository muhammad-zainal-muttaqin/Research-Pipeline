HIGHLIGHTS 
 An iOS-based app (BlueberryCounter) was developed for blueberry detection, counting, and maturity assessment. 
 The BlueberryCounter features a simple, user-friendly interface that integrates with YOLOv8-based fruit detectors. 
 Field testing validated the operational functionalities of the mobile app installed on a smartphone. 
 The BlueberryCounter offers a handy, useful tool for blueberry growers. 
ABSTRACT. Harvest maturity and yield estimation of blueberries are important for growers to optimize crop production 
and stay competitive. It is extremely labor-intensive and infeasible to assess fruit maturity and count fruit for yield estimation 
manually. The pervasive use of smartphones and the recent advancements in deep learning and edge computing open new 
opportunities for automated, inexpensive approaches to image-based blueberry detection and counting. This study presents 
a new, simple mobile application (app) developed using Swift in iOS (version 16), i.e., BlueberryCounter, which enables 
growers to assess fruit maturity and count rapidly. The app features a user-friendly interface and supports real-time blue-
berry detection, counting, and maturity estimation based on two YOLOv8-based fruit detectors: YOLOv8m (Fast) and 
YOLOv8l (Accurate), which offer users flexibility in choosing between speed and accuracy. A live window visualizes real-
time detection results alongside the detection logging statistics displayed. The fruit detectors deployed on the app were 
trained and evaluated on a blueberry canopy image dataset consisting of 17,809 annotated blueberry instances, including 
6,958 instances of ripe (“Blue”) and 10,851 unripe (“Unblue”) fruit. The in-season testing showed better accuracy than 
the cross-season testing, implying more efforts are needed to improve model robustness across different seasons. The oper-
ational functionalities of the app were verified on a smartphone in preliminary field testing. The BlueberryCounter, which 
will be made publicly available, promises to evolve into a useful tool on mobile devices for blueberry growers. 
Keywords. Artificial intelligence, Blueberry, Fruit counting, Mobile application, Precision horticulture.

significantly reduce pack-out rates (Cai et al., 2021). Accu-
rate crop maturity and yield estimation help growers sched-

Blueberry color transitions from green to deep blue or black 
as they mature. Harvesting generally begins when about 
20% of the fruit is blue, as the maturity advances with re-
peated two to five pickings. The first few harvests are often 
by hand, while machine harvesting can be considered when 
50% to 60% of the crop is blue (Michigan State University, 
2025; DeVetter et al., 2022)   . Manually counting individual 
blueberries of different colors for maturity assessment is te-
dious, labor-intensive, and unfeasible for canopy-level as-
sessment. In addition, this manual approach often introduces 
subjectivity and bias, resulting in inconsistent and poten-
tially inaccurate assessments that can compromise harvest 
timing and fruit quality. In contrast, the app standardizes the 
detection and evaluation process, offering an objective and 
efficient method for yield estimation and harvest decision-
making. Therefore, it would be greatly beneficial for grow-
ers if an automated or high-throughput monitoring tool, such 
as a mobile application, were available and capable of blue-
berry detection and maturity assessment, as this would facil-
itate harvest decision-making and yield estimation (Takeda 
et al., 2017; Gallardo et al., 2018). 
Computer vision technology offers a promising solution 
for automated fruit detection and counting in orchard condi-
tions. Considerable efforts have been reported using computer 
vision coupled with deep learning (DL) applied for large fruits 
(Koirala et al., 2019; Vasconez et al., 2020), such as apples, 
oranges, lemons, peaches, and mangoes (Koirala et al., 2019; 
Vasconez et al., 2020). However, the difficulty increases as 
the fruit size decreases, from mangoes and apples to blueber-
ries and grapes, where typically tens to hundreds of small fruit 
instances are present in a single canopy image. There are com-
paratively fewer studies on developing computer vision sys-
tems for small fruits. The complex occlusion among small 
fruits further reduces the predicted fruit count compared to the 
actual number, as seen in crops like grapes (Wang et al., 2024) 
and blueberries (Zhao et al., 2024b). Blueberry detection can 
be more challenging due to its smaller size, and it often re-
quires tree canopy-based counting rather than detecting fruit 
bunches. The detection and counting of blueberries present 
challenges due to the complexity of unstructured fruit cano-
pies where blueberries are often densely packed or sometimes 
occluded by foliage and branches, as well as the inherent dif-
ficulty of learning effective representations from small objects 
(Cheng et al., 2023). Counting is a fundamental task for as-
sessing maturity percentage, directly relevant to harvest deci-
sion-making. 
Recently, a handful of studies have been conducted on 
blueberry detection and maturity assessment using canopy 
images, with a focus on using YOLO (You Only Look Once) 
object detectors (Terven et al., 2023), given their optimized 
tradeoff between accuracy and speed. MacEachern et al. 
(2023) evaluated six variants of YOLO (You Only Look 
Once) detectors to detect the ripeness stage and estimate the 
yield of wild blueberries. YOLOv4 achieved the highest 
mAP (mean average precision) of 79.79% in detecting blue-
berries of three colors (green, red, blue) and 88.12% in two-
class (ripe vs. unripe) modeling. Liu et al. (2023) improved 
YOLOv5x for enhanced small object detection, achieving a 
mAP@50 of 78.3% in detecting three ripeness levels of 
blueberries. Li et al. (2023) reported using YOLOv8 to de-
tect blueberries from multi-view images and achieved 
mAP@50 of 77.3% by YOLOv8x. Very recently, Deng et al. 
(2024a) curated a new dataset of blueberry canopy images 
and evaluated both YOLOv8 and YOLOv9 for the detection 
of ripe and unripe fruit, achieving a mAP of nearly 93% by 
YOLOv8l (large) using high-resolution images. Despite 
these studies, the developed models were not implemented 
in resource-constrained edge devices such as smartphones 
for field testing and evaluation, which are important to de-
veloping cost-effective, real-time systems. Currently, there 
is a lack of computer vision-based, accessible tools such as 
smartphone applications incorporating detection models for 
blueberry detection and maturity estimation. 
As low-cost imaging devices, smartphones have seen 
widespread use in precision agriculture (Mendes et al., 
2020). There is a growing interest in developing 
smartphone-based applications (apps) that enable users to 
collect and process imagery and receive responsive results 
for informed decision-making. With the increase in compu-
ting power, smartphones allow the implementation of state-
of-the-art artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms for fruit de-
tection and counting, making AI-powered computer vision 
technology readily accessible to growers. Zhou et al. (2020) 
developed a kiwifruit detection app called KiwiDetector on 
an Android smartphone by implementing the single-shot 
multibox detector with the quantized MobileNetV2 (Sandler 
et al., 2018) as its backbone. The app achieved 90.8% mAP 
in detecting kiwifruit at a speed of nearly 10 frames per sec-
ond (FPS). Duncan et al. (2024) developed a mobile appli-
cation called Fruit Harvest Helper for apple yield estimation, 
highlighting its compatibility with Android and iOS 
smartphones. The app featured an intuitive, user-friendly in-
terface. However, it only worked for static images rather 
than real-time video streams and implemented traditional 
image processing instead of DL algorithms, producing a 
Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.6 in fruit counting. It is 
important to note that software programs for the two fruit 
detection applications are not publicly available, and their 
field performance has yet to be extensively validated, espe-
cially by growers. Currently, there are no smartphone apps 
specifically developed for blueberry detection and maturity 
estimation. 
To advance computer vision technology as a practical and 
accessible tool for blueberry growers, the overall objective 
of this research was to develop a new user-friendly, AI-pow-
ered iOS app, called BlueberryCounter, to assist in harvest 
maturity assessment and yield estimation. Considering that 
publicly accessible smartphone applications would stimulate 
community-wide efforts for further development and pro-
gress, the BlueberryCounter software programs will be made 
publicly available. Specific objectives of this work were 
twofold, i.e., to 1) design an iOS-based application (Blue-
berryCounter) with a user-friendly interface, equipped with 
trained YOLOv8 models for real-time blueberry detection, 
counting, and maturity estimation, and 2) evaluate the per-
formance of the BlueberryCounter in canopy image-based 
indoor testing and field testing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS 
The blueberry counting application considered in this 
study will meet several basic requirements. The application 
should be capable of detecting blueberries in given canopy 
images, distinguishing between mature and immature fruit, 
and reporting the fruit count and the percentage of mature 
fruit. The application is intended for field use throughout the 
harvest season. Two types of detection models are supported 
to cater to varied needs regarding detection efficiency and 
accuracy, one oriented for fast processing and the other for 
accuracy. For real-time performance, the detection models 
on mobile phones should achieve a rate of one or more FPS. 
The application is designed to be energy-efficient, demand-
ing minimal power to operate for hours on regular 
smartphones. In this regard, lightweight models and efficient 
resource management are vital to maintaining real-time, 
steady performance, ensuring that potential frame drops or 
latency do not interfere with blueberry detection or hurt the 
user experience. Moreover, a simple, friendly, and interac-
tive interface is important to user comprehension and tech-
nology assessment and adoption. 
In this study, iOS (iPhone Operating System) was chosen 
as the platform for application development because it offers 
a consistent, user-friendly experience across devices com-
pared to the diverse Android ecosystem. The design, layout, 
configurations, and models in iOS can be readily applied to 
different iPhone versions, benefiting a large user base. 
THE BLUEBERRYCOUNTER APPLICATION 
The mobile app, called BlueberryCounter, was developed 
in Xcode 15.3 using the programming language of Swift 
(version 5.1) on a Mac mini (24 GB RAM) with an Apple 
M2 chip running macOS 14.4.1 (Apple Inc., 2024) and sup-
ports iOS 16 and above. The app (exported in .ipa format 
with a file size of around 70 Mb) has already trained deep 
learning models (i.e., YOLOv8) deployed in the CoreML 
framework (version 7.2). Figure 1 shows the designed app 
interface. The BlueberryCounter consists of four main func-
tional sections, i.e., detector selection, logging, live display, 
and hyperparameter control, which are described below. 
The app offers two blueberry detector options, i.e., a fast 
detector (YOLOv8m) and an accurate detector (YOLOv8l), 
as shown in figure 1 as two optional tabs. The fast detector 
prioritizes speed with some trade-off in accuracy, while the 
accurate detector focuses on precision at the expense of 
speed. Different operational needs require the application to 
adapt to varying trade-offs between speed and accuracy. A 
quick detector would be desired in scenarios demanding 
quick feedback, e.g., when the software is run on a traveling 
vehicle for crop scouting. In the case of slow motion and de-
tailed assessment needed for localized bushes, detection ac-
curacy would take precedence. The flexibility of detector 
choices would be beneficial to the user’s experience. The de-
tector of interest can be readily launched by clicking the cor-
responding tab on the interface model selection menu, and 
the faster detector is set as the default fruit detector. The two 
detectors dictate two different video stream resolutions

Right below the model selection menu is the logging sec-
tion of the app, which displays real-time detection infor-
mation, including blueberry count, the percentage of ripe 
(blue) fruit, and inference speed in terms of FPS. This infor-
mation represents the metrics for real-time fruit detection, 
maturity, and model efficiency. The central region of the in-
terface shows a video frame feed with overlaid bounding 
boxes enclosing the detected blueberries. For iPhones, users 
may capture images at different spatial resolutions, depend-
ing on the camera model and settings. For visual effects, the 
bounding boxes are drawn with a thin boundary, coded with 
two distinctive colors, i.e., blue for ripe blueberries and red 
for unripe ones. The fruit count and the percentage of ripe 
fruit in the detected images are calculated based on the num-
ber of bounding boxes. The immediate detection results by 
the app allow users to assess the fruit maturity levels of blue-
berry bushes rapidly. The lower section of the interface pro-
vides two tunable thresholds for the intersection over union 
(IoU) and confidence, which control the spatial overlap of 
detected fruit instances and the confidence level of blueberry 
detections, respectively. Adjusting the two parameters is 
straightforward by dragging the sliders, enabling users to 
easily adjust detection sensitivity for various needs, such as 
using lower thresholds on shadowed, rainy days or higher 
confidence thresholds to exclude complex backgrounds (like 
blurry blueberries behind trees), without requiring technical 
expertise. The default settings of the IoU threshold and con-
fidence level are 0.45 and 0.25, respectively. These values 
are the defaults in YOLO prediction, so we have adopted 
them here. Overall, the design of the BlueberryCounter was 
inspired by the general object detection interface by Ultra-
lytics (2024) (publicly available for iPhone and iPad). 
The BlueberryCounter can be installed and run on a reg-
ular iPhone (with iOS version 16 or higher) without requir-
ing specialized hardware or extensive setup. The blueberry 
detection model files are automatically downloaded during 
the app installation. Upon installation, the app is immedi-
ately ready for use without additional configuration com-
plexities. 
BACKEND MODELS 
Dataset 
The dataset used for model development consisted of 
140 images captured by smartphones from two consecutive 
seasons, as described in Deng et al. (2024a), 134 images of 
Rabbiteye blueberries from mixed cultivars taken in May 
2022 in Mississippi, and additional six images of “Bluecrop” 
Northern Highbush canopies collected in August 2022 in 
Michigan. The images from Mississippi were captured be-
fore the peak harvest season, resulting in an extensive range 
of variation in fruit maturity. These images varied in spatial 
scale, from close-ups of individual branches to full bush can-
opies, under diverse natural lighting conditions, enhancing 
the robustness of the blueberry detection models. The dataset 
that was manually annotated had a total of 17,809 blueberry 
instances, including 6,958 instances of ripe (“Blue”) and 
10,851 unripe (“Unblue”) fruit, with an average of 128 blue-
berries per image, and the percentage of ripe blueberries 
ranging from 0% to 96%. More details on the dataset are 
given in Deng et al. (2024a). 
YOLOs 
The YOLO series models have become the most widely 
used real-time object detectors. Since its introduction in Jan-
uary 2023, YOLOv8 (Jocher et al., 2023) has shown out-
standing performance in high-speed object detection across 
various computer vision tasks (Terven et al., 2023). Imme-
diately built upon our prior work on blueberry detection by 
YOLO models (Deng et al., 2024a), the two detectors, i.e., 
YOLOv8m (Fast) and YOLOv8l (Accurate), were trained 
for deployment on the BlueberryCounter. Figure 2 illustrates 
the model development and deployment pipeline. 
As done in our prior work (Deng et al., 2024a), the dataset 
was randomly split into training (75%), validation (5%), and 
testing (20%) subsets, for training and testing YOLOv8m 
and YOLOv8l. Since our target is to run the YOLO models 
in the app, there is a trade-off between model size and input 
resolution to achieve both high accuracy and fast speed. For 
instance, YOLOv8m with a 1560×1560 resolution achieves 
approximately 9 FPS. Reducing the input size increases FPS 
more effectively than decreasing the model size and lower-
ing the resolution from 1560 to 1024 boosts FPS to over 20, 
while switching from YOLOv8m to YOLOv8n results in 
only a slight FPS gain of a few frames. With high accuracy 
as the primary goal and fast speed as the secondary goal, rel-
atively high resolutions were used in the study. For 
YOLOv8m, the input images (of 1080p resolution) were 
resized to 1560 × 1560 pixels, consistent with the resolution 
used for training YOLOv8m. Preliminary experiments found 
that 1560 × 1560 is a good trade-off, maintaining a moderate 
speed of around 9 FPS on iPhone 15 while offering higher 
accuracy than 1080p. This was held even when the 1080p 
images were directly upsampled to 1560 resolution without 
additional data augmentation. While for YOLOv8l, the input 
images (of 4K resolution) were resized to 3520 × 3520 pix-
els, a resolution empirically selected to balance detection ac-
curacy and real-time processing efficiency. This resizing

Both models were trained for 200 epochs, which was suf-

the BlueberryCounter, with INT8 quantization applied dur-
ing the conversion process. During inference in the app, the 
batch size was set to 1, meaning frames are to be fed to the 
model immediately without waiting for 4 or 8 frames to ac-
cumulate. 
Field Testing & Performance Metrics 
To verify the functionalities of the BlueberryCounter, the 
app was deployed on an iPhone 15 and used for field testing. 
The testing was conducted on-site in a Northern Highbush 
blueberry field at Michigan State University’s Horticulture 
Teaching and Research Center (HTRC, Holt, Mich.) in July 
2024. A set of 50 blueberry canopy images was utilized to 
build a separate test dataset, which was captured at the 
HTRC orchard twice a week, often on Mondays and Thurs-
days, over one month from June to July 2024, during the 
blueberry season near maturity. Likewise, these images were 
manually annotated with bounding boxes, resulting in 8,482 
blueberry instances, comprising 2,841 ripe (“Blue”) and 
5,641 unripe (“Unblue”) blueberries. The maturity of the 
blueberries in these images varied, with the blue fruit per-
centage ranging from 0.1% to 66.7%. In addition, function-
ality evaluation was conducted using the app to detect and 
count blueberries on blueberry trees in real time to assess 
user experience. The test indicates that the expected func-
tionalities were met, including real-time detection and fruit 
counting, moderate inference speed with decent accuracy, 
and a user-friendly interface with quick response. However, 
this test did not have quantitative performance metrics, as in 
the model performance evaluation, where detected blueberry 
images could be compared to manually counted results. 
There is still a need to develop a methodology to evaluate 
the app’s performance and user experience, such as conduct-
ing user surveys. 
The blueberry detection performance was evaluated on 
both the datasets acquired in the 2022 and 2024 seasons in 
terms of precision, recall, and mean average precision at 
50% IoU (i.e., mAP@50) for each of the blueberry classes 
(“Blue” and “Unblue”), as defined in Deng et al. (2024a). 
Moreover, the fruit count per image was determined by the 
total number of predicted bounding boxes, regardless of 
class, and the counting error was assessed using root mean 
square error (RMSE) by comparing predicted counts to 
ground truth (Deng et al., 2024a).   Additionally, the percent-
age of “Blue” fruit in each image serves as the estimate of 
the overall fruit maturity, which is the number of “Blue” fruit 
divided by the total number of detected fruit. The total fruit 
count and the percentage of “Blue” fruit are the most essen-
tial and easily understandable information for growers to 
make informed harvest decisions, hence these are displayed 
in the app.

Generally, both models attained moderate accuracy, with the

counting and blue fruit percentage estimation. Identifying 
more challenging blueberry bushes remains an open ques-
tion that needs further exploration. An in-depth study is re-
quired to develop a reliable method, which can be a potential 
research direction. The combination of fast and accurate de-
tectors appears to be a promising approach to maintaining 
high accuracy and speed. One possible method could involve 
using a confidence score as an indicator. If the average con-
fidence of the fast detector on certain blueberry bushes is 
low, switching to the accurate detector for further detection 
and combining the results of both detectors properly could 
improve overall performance. For instance, an object track-
ing strategy could be incorporated to select bounding boxes 
for subsequent frames from candidate bounding boxes de-
rived from fast and accurate detectors. 
DISCUSSION 
This study presents a new, simple iOS-based app, i.e., 
BlueberryCounter, for blueberry detection, counting, and 
maturity estimation to assist growers in orchard tasks such 
as harvest decision making and yield estimation. Currently, 
the model’s performance on in-season testing is compared 
favorably to those reported in previous blueberry detection 
studies, for instance, mAP@50 values below 90% in 
MacEachern et al. (2023) and even under 80% in Li et al. 
(2023). However, the cross-season results showed a decrease 
of about 7% in mAP@50. Such season-associated perfor-
mance discrepancies were also encountered in our earlier 
study on weed detection (Deng et al., 2024b),   which sug-
gests the presence of a domain gap (Koh et al., 2021) be-
tween the images acquired from the different seasons. 
Dedicated efforts are thus required to build robust models 
across different growth seasons in future research. For in-
stance, semi-supervised learning is a promising approach to 
leverage unlabeled images from a new domain, enhancing 
model robustness for domain adaptation (Yu et al., 2019; 
Deng and Lu, 2025). This is particularly useful for blueberry 
images, which often contain tens to hundreds of instances 
per image. 
The initial version of the BlueberryCounter still has room 
for improvement or upgrading. The app was designed to pro-
cess live video frames, but it does not allow externally up-
loaded images or videos for blueberry detection. Image-
based functionality is also crucial, as seen in the Fruit Har-
vest Helper app (Duncan et al., 2024), which allows users to 
view results directly from specific frames. However, they 
only used color and morph-based algorithms to detect fruit, 
whereas this study employed advanced YOLO models, 
achieving higher accuracy. Such a functionality can be a plus 
given the operation flexibility. On the other hand, the detec-
tion information logging capability of the app is still limited, 
although the app is not positioned as a dedicated data

The total fruit count and maturity status can be calculated

To better serve the needs of a broad range of mobile users, 
it would be highly desirable to make the app work on multi-
ple platforms in addition to iOS, which will require cross-
platform development efforts, as the Fruit Harvest Helper 
app (Duncan et al., 2024) can be installed on both iOS and 
Android devices. The app only produces detection results 
from specific, close-range views of blueberry bushes, similar 
to the Fruit Harvest Helper (Duncan et al., 2024) and Ki-
wiDetector (Zhou et al., 2020). As noted above, to enable 
plot- or orchard-scale decision-making by the mobile app, 
research is needed to develop algorithms enabling the aggre-
gation of blueberry detection results from a series of images 
for different blueberry bushes. To determine the blueberry 
count and maturity status of an entire bush canopy, it is nec-
essary to capture multiple images from different viewpoints 
and stitch images properly to generate a holistic view of the 
canopy, thus enabling comprehensive fruit detection. How-
ever, it remains an unresolved challenge to stitch multi-view 
canopy images and generate a unified view of specific 
bushes (Li et al., 2023). 
Moreover, the blueberry detectors have yet to be im-
proved in terms of accuracy and inference speed. In deploy-
ing AI models on edge devices, it is important to balance 
accuracy and efficiency, considering the constraints of com-
puting resources. Exploring model pruning or compression 
techniques (Wang and He, 2021;Lyu et al., 2024) could be 
beneficial for accelerating inference without compromising 
accuracy. The detector should achieve high accuracy to 
ensure meaningful results while maintaining fast perfor-
mance on edge devices. Integrating an attention-based mech-
anism into YOLO models offers a promising approach to 
accelerate inference without sacrificing accuracy (Zhao et 
al., 2024). Furthermore, for practical use, the detector must 
be robust against real-world challenges such as blurriness, 
occlusion, variability in fruit size, overlapping fruits, and 
fluctuating light conditions. Figure 4 shows examples where 
the YOLO models are still short of making accurate predic-
tions. The detector should also perform consistently across 
different environments, seasons, and growth stages of blue-
berries, ensuring reliable detection even in orchards with 
heavy canopy cover or mixed fruit ripeness. To handle com-
plex natural scenes, Zhu et al. (2024) proposed replacing the 
original feature fusion design in YOLO models with the 
more advanced BiFPN (Tan et al., 2020) to capture a broader 
range of features at different scales. Additionally, they re-
placed the default CNN layers in BiFPN with GhostConv 
(Han et al., 2020) to enhance feature robustness. Empiri-
cally, a well-performing blueberry detector can reliably 
achieve mAP@50 exceeding 95%, resulting in a fruit esti-
mation error of less than 3%. The primary methods for fruit 
estimation are direct counting of detected fruits (Deng et al., 
2024a ; Sapkota et al., 2024) or using a regression model to 
predict the fruit number (Gwak et al., 2024). Additionally, 
video-based tracking techniques can eliminate duplicated 
fruits and identify occluded ones across consecutive frames, 
which shows the potential to improve the accuracy of esti-
mating the total fruit count in the entire orchard (Zheng 
et al., 2024). 
Significant efforts are needed to develop and implement 
more powerful models while curating a substantially ex-
panded, more diverse dataset. The blueberry datasets cap-
tured in two seasons used in this study represent an initial 
step in evaluating the performance of blueberry detection 
models across different seasons. A larger, more diverse 
multi-season dataset is needed to conduct a thorough

improve overall productivity. 
CONCLUSION 
This study presents BlueberryCounter, an iOS mobile app 
for real-time blueberry counting and maturity detection us-
ing video streams. It utilizes YOLO models for onboard de-
tection, offering a cost-effective solution for monitoring 
orchards via iPhone (iOS v16 or higher). Features include 
model selection, hyperparameter adjustment, counting, ma-
turity estimation, and result visualization. Performance eval-
uations and field tests show that YOLOv8m and YOLOv8l 
models, trained on 2023 data, achieved mAP@0.5 scores of 
88.8% and 95.0%, with R2 values of 0.985 and 0.990, re-
spectively, for 2023 test set counting. For 2024 images, the 
models achieved mAP@0.5 scores of 81.5% and 88.7%, 
with R2 values of 0.911 and 0.947, highlighting a domain 
shift in cross-season detection. With further improvements, 
BlueberryCounter will become a valuable tool for blueberry 
growers and will be made publicly available.
