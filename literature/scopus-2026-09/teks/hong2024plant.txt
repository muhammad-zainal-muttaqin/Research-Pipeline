HIGHLIGHTS 
• Deep learning-based instance segmentation models were applied and evaluated for tomato fruit detection. 
• Mask R-CNN with vision transformer backbone showed the highest accuracy for tomato instance detection. 
• Size and weight estimation indexes were calculated using tomato region depth data from instance segmentation models. 
• Area-based index has higher accuracy for weight estimation than indexes based on weight and height information. 
ABSTRACT. The size and weight of fruits are crucial factors in yield prediction and determining harvesting time. Machine 
vision, including fruit detection, is a key technology in the automated monitoring and harvesting of fruits. In particular, 
deep learning-based fruit-detection methods have been actively applied. Estimation of fruit size after fruit detection requires 
depth information, which can be acquired using depth imaging. RGB-D cameras include color and depth information re-
quired for fruit detection and size estimation. In this study, the RGB-D imaging technique was used to estimate the size and 
weight of tomatoes. Furthermore, deep learning-based instance segmentation models, including Mask R-CNN, YOLACT, 
and RTMDet for tomato fruit detection, were trained and evaluated. The proposed method estimated the fruit width with a 
root mean square error (RMSE) of 4 mm, a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 4.28%, and a fruit height with an 
RMSE of 5.12 mm and a MAPE of 6.42%. Furthermore, the weight-prediction model based on the area index estimated the 
tomato fruit weight with an RMSE of 19.69 g and MAPE of 9.44%. Thus, the method can be used for accurate size and 
weight estimation and can be applied in growth monitoring and automated tomatoes harvesting. 
Keywords. Deep learning, Fruit sizing, Instance segmentation, RGB-D, Tomato.

Machine vision is a key technology in monitoring the 
growth of fruits and operating fruit-harvesting robots. In par-

including size, weight, defects, and maturity, the location of 
the fruit must be detected within the image. Before the ap-

ous fruits based on multiple features, including intensity, 
color, orientation, and edges.

methods for many vision applications. Deep neural networks 
can learn suitable features from data for different purposes 
and use more complex features that cannot be used in tradi-
tional methods. Since the introduction of AlexNet (Krizhev-
sky et al., 2017), deep neural network-based approaches 
have dominated state-of-the-art machine vision applications, 
including classification, object detection, and segmentation. 
Deep learning-based methods have been increasingly inves-
tigated for fruit detection. Sa et al. (2016) detected sweet 
peppers and rock melons using a Faster region-based convo-
lutional neural network (Faster R-CNN) (Ren et al., 2017) 
with a VGGNet (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2014) backbone. 
Koirala et al. (2019) detected mangoes based on Faster R-
CNN, and You Only Look Once (YOLO) (Redmon et al., 
2016). Additionally, Kang and Chen (2020) detected apples 
using a customized one-stage detector named LedNet. 
Object detectors, such as Faster R-CNN, Single-Shot 
MultiBox Detector (SSD) (Liu et al., 2016), and YOLO, pro-
duce outputs of the classes and bounding box coordinates of 
the objects. However, these box coordinate outputs cannot 
precisely represent the fruit area in the image. Recent studies 
have used instance segmentation models based on deep neu-
ral networks to overcome these limitations. Many previous 
studies have applied Mask R-CNN to segment instances of 
various fruits, including oranges (Ganesh et al., 2019), sweet 
peppers (López-Barrios et al., 2023), apples (Jia et al., 2020; 
Liu et al., 2021b), tomatoes (Minagawa and Kim, 2022), and 
cucumbers (Liu et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2023). Studies em-
ploying instance segmentation models have effectively ex-
tracted precise regions of fruits and utilized the information 
obtained from these regions.  
After detecting fruits in an image, depth information is 
necessary to calculate their sizes. Depth cameras based on 
time-of-flight or stereoscopic techniques are commonly used 
in machinery for agricultural applications (Neupane et al., 
2023). These depth cameras can provide the depth infor-
mation required for operating agricultural machinery, such 
as harvesting robots. In particular, consumer-grade depth 
cameras, such as Azure Kinect DK (Microsoft, Washington, 
USA) and Intel RealSense cameras (Intel RealSense Inc., 
California, USA), facilitate real-time depth estimation and 
RGB imaging at a low cost. 
Several studies have estimated the on-tree sizes of various 
fruits, such as apples (Gené-Mola et al., 2019; Freeman and 
Kantor, 2023), mangoes (Wang et al., 2017), and plums 
(Kim et al., 2022), using depth information from RGB-D im-
ages. These studies used RGBD imaging approaches for siz-
ing the width and height of fruits, but the volume or weight 
of fruits was not estimated under on-tree conditions. Several 
studies have focused on utilizing RGBD images or RGB im-
ages with known distance to measure the weight or volume 
of fruits and vegetables such as apples (Yamamoto et al., 
2018), tomatoes (Lee et al., 2020), cucumber (Huynh et al., 
2020), olive (Ponce et al., 2018, 2019), passion fruit (Gon-
zalez et al., 2017), carrot (Xie et al., 2023), and potato (Liu 
et al., 2021a). However, it should be noted that these studies 
predominantly imaged harvested fruits in controlled indoor 
environments. Estimating volume or weight using images of 
fruit placed on a stage with a controlled indoor environment 
  
differs from applying the same methods to on-tree or on-
plant fruit. Based on this research history, there appears to 
be a lack of studies on volume or weight estimation of on-
tree or on-plant fruit through instance segmentation. There-
fore, this study aimed to develop a technique that segments 
the region of on-plant fruit using RGBD images and subse-
quently estimates its weight using deep learning methods.  
In this study, the RGB-D imaging technique was used to 
estimate the size and weight of tomatoes. For the precise ex-
traction of the tomato-fruit area from images, instance seg-
mentation models based on deep neural networks were 
trained and evaluated. Previous studies on fruit detection 
based on instance segmentation used convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs) as the backbone of the Mask R-CNN; 
however, in this study, Mask R-CNN based on vision trans-
former backbone and real-time instance segmentation mod-
els was also trained and evaluated. Consequently, the fruit 
size and weight were estimated from the extracted tomato-
fruit area using the best-performing instance segmentation 
model, and the model performances were evaluated. Due to 
varying trends observed in the estimated size and weight of 
tomato fruits derived from depth information across differ-
ent dates, performances of estimation models were analyzed 
for each experimental date to assess the robustness of the 
methods. Furthermore, considering different regression 
trends by experimental dates, cross-validation was con-
ducted for weight estimation by splitting the data into train-
ing and validation sets based on dates to reconfirm the mod-
el's robustness and performance. 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
DATA ACQUISITION 
The tomatoes grown in a hydroponic greenhouse at the 
National Institute of Horticultural and Herbal Science, 
Wanju, Republic of Korea, were used for image acquisition. 
The cultivated tomato variety was “Dafnis,” and the tomato 
vines were suspended on an adjustable line while growing 
and cultivating. Samples were measured at various stages, 
including all six stages of tomato maturity (green, breaker, 
turning, pink, light red, and red) classified by the United 
States Department of Agriculture. 
RGB-D images were acquired using a consumer-grade 
RGB-D camera (D435i, Intel RealSense Inc., California, 
USA), which captures both RGB and depth images simulta-
neously using the RGB camera and the stereo vision of in-
frared cameras. The pixel resolution and field-of-view of the 
RGB-D images were 1280 × 720 and 69° × 42°, respectively. 
Images were obtained between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m. on the 
17th and 29th of June, the 19th and 26th of October, and the 
9th and 16th of November 2022. To train and evaluate the 
instance segmentation model for tomatoes, non-labeled to-
matoes in the greenhouse were randomly imaged within a 
distance of 0.3–0.7 m, and 533 images were collected. For 
the weight estimation of tomatoes using RGB imaging, 
60 target tomatoes were selected and labeled on each date. 
Two RGB-D images were obtained at a random distance of 
< 0.7 m for each labeled tomato. The camera direction was

INSTANCE SEGMENTATION MODELING 
To train the supervised machine-learning models, the 
ground truth of the data must be annotated. As our study 
aimed at the instance segmentation of tomatoes, annotating 
tomato-fruit masks in the image was necessary. The Python-
based annotation program Labelme (Wada, 2018) was used 
for mask annotation, and 2509 tomato-fruit masks were an-
notated. Dataset segregation is required to effectively train, 
optimize, and evaluate machine-learning models. In this 
study, the tomato image dataset for the instance segmenta-
tion model was segregated into training, validation, and test 
sets at a 3:1:1 ratio, as shown in table 1. Accordingly, the 
model was trained using the training dataset, and its structure 
and parameters were optimized by evaluating its perfor-
mance using the validation dataset that was not used in the 
training. After optimization, the performance of the final 
models was evaluated using the test dataset. 
Mask R-CNN is a model that adds another branch for pre-
dicting an object mask to the bounding box recognition 
branch of Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2017). Including this 
mask branch allows for simultaneous object detection and 
segmentation through instance segmentation (He et al., 
2017). Due to its intuitive and efficient design, Mask R-CNN 
is used as a meta-architecture for state-of-the-art studies in 
instance segmentation (Fang et al., 2022). Object detection 
or instance segmentation models with a two-stage 
architecture have limitations regarding inference speed. YO-
LACT, which stands for 'You Only Look At Coefficients,' is 
a real-time instance segmentation model that eliminates the 
need for an explicit localization step. Thus, compared with 
Mask R-CNN-based models, YOLACT achieves a higher 
speed by employing branches configured parallelly rather 
than sequentially (Bolya et al., 2019). The real-time model 
for object detection (RTMDet) is a state-of-the-art real-time 
object detection and instance segmentation model. It has a 
model architecture with compatible capacities in the back-
bone and neck using large-kernel depthwise convolutions as 
a basic building block for efficiency (Lyu et al., 2022). In 
this study, we trained and evaluated instance segmentation 
models based on Mask R-CNN, YOLACT, and RTMDet. 
Moreover, ResNet 101, ResNet 50 (He et al., 2016), and 
Swin-T (Liu et al., 2021c) were used as the backbones of the 
Mask R-CNN, and ResNet 101 was used as the backbone of 
YOLACT. Furthermore, the RTMDet-Ins-x and RTMDet-
Ins-tiny models were implemented, which employed CSP-
NeXt-x and CSPNeXt-tiny backbones, respectively. All 
models were trained using transfer learning from pre-trained 
models, and the Python programming language and 
mmDetection toolbox (Chen et al., 2019) were used for 
model training and evaluation. Validation was conducted 
every epoch during the training process. If the validation loss 
of the current epoch was lower than that of the previous 
epoch, the model was saved as an optimal model. Training 
was stopped when the validation loss did not decrease for 
more than 20 epochs. Training and evaluation were per-
formed on a computing system with QUADRO 6000 GPU 
(Nvidia, Santa Clara, CA, USA), Xeon Silver 4214R CPU, 
and 32 GB RAM (Intel Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

ESTIMATION OF TOMATO SIZE AND  
WEIGHT USING RGB-D IMAGES 
A depth image contains distance information of the pixels 
from the camera, providing distance information only from 
a single plane. Therefore, a single-depth image cannot rep-
resent the entire three-dimensional (3D) structure of an ob-
ject. Depth images captured in multiple directions are re-
quired to reconstruct the complete 3D structure of an object. 
However, images of growing tomatoes are difficult to cap-
ture from all directions because of obstacles such as 
branches and leaves. Additionally, 3D reconstruction from 
multiple images requires multiple capture and registration 
processes. Hence, our study aimed to develop a nondestruc-
tive and rapid prediction model for the size and weight of 
tomatoes using a single-view RGB-D image, despite the lim-
itations of a single-view depth image. 
The prediction of weight using 3D information can be 
aided by volume information; however, precise volume cal-
culation is not feasible with a single-view depth image. Fur-
thermore, because the shape of a tomato is often uneven, cal-
culating the volume using only the width and height can be 
inaccurate. Therefore, the projected area of the tomato was 
computed using the median depth information of the tomato-
fruit region extracted using the instance segmentation model, 
and the area information was used as the input for the 
weight-prediction model. Equations 3 and 4 show the calcu-
lations of fruit size and area based on lens theory (Wang et 
al., 2017; Kim et al., 2022). To mitigate the effect of depth 
noise, the size and area of each pixel were calculated using 
the median depth of the tomato-fruit region instead of the 
depth of individual pixels. For the fruit size, the length in the 
longitudinal direction of the mask was calculated as the 
width, and the length of a line perpendicular to the width line 
and passing through its center point was calculated as the 
height.

A simple linear regression model was employed for 
weight-prediction modeling, as shown in equation 5. Four 
input variables were used in the regression analysis. The cal-
culated indices were used as inputs for regression, and con-
sistent multiplication or division of the input variables by the 
same value does not affect the accuracy of the regression. 
Therefore, when assuming the tomato shape and calculating 
the indices based on the volume formula for the shape, coef-
ficients were excluded. The input variables were calculated 
in terms of the volume of tomatoes using the size and area 
obtained from the image. Two variables were computed as-
suming a sphere by cubing the width and height (width3 and 
height3). One variable was calculated assuming a spheroid 
with width2 multiplied by the height (width2 × height). The 
final variable was computed assuming a spheroid by multi-
plying the computed tomato-fruit area by the width (A × 
width). For accurate volume estimation, it is necessary to 
multiply a given area by the width perpendicular to that area. 
However, measuring the width perpendicular to an image 
plane using single-view images is challenging. Thus, assum-
ing a spheroid, the previously calculated width was multi-
plied by the area. Figure 1 shows illustrations of the input

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
INSTANCE SEGMENTATION MODELING 
Table 2 presents the evaluation results of the instance seg-
mentation models for the test dataset. In all the evaluation 
metrics, Mask R-CNN with a Swin-T backbone showed the 
highest AP among the models. It showed higher AP values 
than the other models by 4.4–9.1% in AP50, 9.3–10.5% in 
AP75, and 3.9–9.3% in AP50:5:95. These results show that 
the Swin-T-based Mask R-CNN model exhibits high perfor-
mance, especially in the localization of the mask, which is 
important for weight estimation. In contrast, YOLACT 
showed evaluation metrics similar to those of Mask R-CNN 
with CNN backbones (ResNet 101 and ResNet 50), with an 
inference time of 40 ms. RTMDet-Ins with a CSPNeXt-x 
backbone (RTMDet-Ins-x) showed higher AP75 and 
AP50:5:95 than the other models, except for Mask R-CNN 
with a Swin-T backbone. These results indicate that the 
RTMDet-Ins-x model has a higher mask localization accu-
racy than the other models. The inference time of RTMDet-
Ins-x in the original study on RTMDet was 5.31 ms (Lyu et 
al., 2022), whereas that in this study was 42 ms. Therefore, 
our RTMDet-Ins models have some limitations in terms of 
speed. If these limitations are addressed by optimizing the 
inference process and adjusting the model deployment op-
tions, RTMDet is expected to offer significant advantages in 
terms of time-to-performance. Figure 2 shows the detected 
tomato-fruit images of the Mask R-CNN with Swin-T back-
bone. Most false predictions occurred for small tomato fruits 
in images that were captured from a long distance. There-
fore, no false-negative predictions were obtained for toma-
toes during the size and weight estimations obtained at dis-
tances < 0.7 m. Based on these results, it was considered that 
valid segmentation is possible for tomato fruit at different 
maturity stages.  
SIZE AND WEIGHT MEASUREMENT OF TOMATOES 
Figure 3 shows the boxplot of the manually measured 
widths, heights, weights, and volumes of the tomato sam-
ples. The widths ranged from 53.78 to 91.27 mm, heights 
from 46.86 to 78.3 mm, weights from 78.29 to 339.26 g, and 
volumes from 8.008E-5 to 3.446E-4 m3. Figure 4 presents 
the scatter plots of the weights and volumes of the tomatoes. 
These two parameters exhibited a high correlation, with an 
R2 of 0.998. This result can be attributed to tomatoes being 
predominantly composed of water, whereas differences in 
other components have minimal influence on density. The 
indices calculated from the RGB-D images were based on 
the areas of the tomatoes, which were associated with the 
volumes. However, considering the consistent density ob-
served in the results shown in figure 4, the weight can be 
reasonably predicted using an index based on the fruit area. 
Furthermore, prediction modeling for weight and volume 
should achieve similar results because of the strong linearity 
between volume and weight. Therefore, only weight-predic-
tion modeling was performed in this study.

Mola et al. (2019) estimated the size of apples with an RMSE 
of 5.15 mm. 
In addition to the previously mentioned errors resulting 
from measurement angles, both width and height measure-
ments carry the potential of significant deviations in the ac-
tual length measurement. Factors such as irregular shapes or 
measurement positions can introduce errors into the actual 
values. The width can vary based on the measurement loca-
tion, and due to the characteristic of tomatoes where the ex-
tent of protrusion around the calyx varies, errors can occur 
depending on the measurement location for height. While 
this study has shown results for width and height estimation 
as previous studies and compared the results with them, es-
timation of volume and weight, which have a more precise 
definition as actual values, may be considered more reliable 
indicators.

Figure 6 shows the scatter plot and linear regression re-
sults for the four indices estimated from the RGB-D images 
and weights of tomatoes. Compared with the predicted 
height3, which had an R2 of 0.5819, the predicted width3 
showed a higher correlation with weight, with an R2 of 
0.7345. The main reasons for these results are that the size 
prediction of width was more accurate than that of height 
(table 3 and fig. 5), and tomatoes have a shape similar to a 
spheroid; therefore, the influence of width on volume is 
greater than that of height. The combined variables, width2 
× height with an R2 of 0.7806 and A × height with an R2 of 
0.7959, showed higher correlations with weight than the in-
dices calculated using only width or height. The A × width 
variable exhibited the strongest correlation with weight, in-
dicating that it provided the most accurate estimation. This 
is because tomatoes do not have a perfectly uniform ellipti-
cal shape. Therefore, precise calculation of the area of the 
fruit from the images using an instance segmentation model 
contributes to accurate weight estimation. Thus, in subse-
quent analyses, A × width was used as the input variable.

CONCLUSION 
This study used RGB-D images to predict the size and 
weight of on-plant tomatoes. Instance segmentation models 
based on various deep-learning architectures were trained 
and evaluated for the precise localization of tomatoes in im-
ages. Consequently, the best-performing instance segmenta-
tion model was used to extract the area of tomatoes and cal-
culate the index for size and weight prediction from the 
RGB-D images. The width and height calculated from the 
RGB-D images were compared to the measured values. Ac-
cordingly, the proposed method estimated the width with an 
RMSE of 4 mm and MAPE of 4.28% and the height with an 
RMSE of 5.12 mm and MAPE of 6.42%. Furthermore, the 
correlations between the calculated index and the measured 
weight of the tomatoes were analyzed for each experimental 
date, and a linear regression model for weight prediction was 
evaluated using cross-validation. The weight-prediction 
model exhibited an RMSE of 20.22 g and a MAPE of 9.23%. 
Previous studies have primarily focused on estimating the 
size, such as the width and height, of fruits. However, this 
study proposes a potential for size and weight estimation us-
ing RGB-D imaging and instance segmentation algorithms. 
The method proposed in this study can be applied to moni-
toring tomato growth and operating fruit-harvesting robots. 
The method can be utilized for managing the yield, size, and 
weight information of individual tomatoes during monitor-
ing and harvesting operations. Furthermore, it is anticipated 
to aid in determining the optimal harvesting time. However, 
maturity is a crucial factor in the harvesting of tomatoes. 
Therefore, further study considering maturity is necessary to 
determine the optimal harvesting time using vision technol-
ogy.
