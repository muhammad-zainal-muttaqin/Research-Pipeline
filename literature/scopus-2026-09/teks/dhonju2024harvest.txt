Abstract 
New technologies providing information on spatial distribution of fruit load in tree crops 
offer potential for improved orchard management operations.  One harvest management 
task is the distribution of empty fruit bins through the orchard prior to harvest.  This concept 
is explored for a mango orchard, with use of fruit load data derived from RGB-D cameras 
mounted to a ground vehicle-based platform for measurement of fruit size and fruit number 
some weeks before harvest, and a fruit growth model applied to forecast fruit mass 
distribution at harvest.  The system has inputs of cultivar parameters, fruit load distribution 
from machine vision mapping, average fruit size and bin size, and output of an in-field bin 
placement online map. A cumulative yield along each row is calculated from fruit count 
and mass distribution estimates, with bin placement tied to a user-defined bin capacity 
(nominally 400 kg). 
Keywords: field bin, fruit count, fruit size, machine vision, spatial location 
Introduction 
As for many tree fruit commodities, the harvest period for mango is usually relatively short, 
with a large quantity of fruit to be handled in this short harvest window.  This requires good 
organizational skills, involving appropriate harvest resourcing and the ability to manage 
casual labour effectively (Meurant et al., 1999). The Australian mango industry destalks 
and washes fruit in the field using mobile platforms termed ‘harvest aids’, with washed fruit 
placed into field bins. These bins have a capacity of around 800 m3 and a maximum load 
weight of 500 kg and are approximately 1300 by 1100 by 750 mm (length, width and height 
external dimensions). Optimal placement of empty bins across the orchards in preparation 
for harvest is one of the management tasks required in an efficient harvest operation. The 
placement of empty bins across an orchard ahead of harvest is currently based on a field 
worker estimate of tree fruit load. 
Several researchers and commercial providers have developed systems involving location 
of filled harvest bins as the basis of tree fruit yield maps for orchards. For example,

Ampatzidis et al. (2016) reports on a yield mapping system in which geolocation of cherry 
harvest bins was transmitted wirelessly to a cloud based server. For planning of empty bin 
placement, González-Araya et al. (2009) applied a capacitated p-median model (Hakimi, 
1964; Garey and Johnson, 1979) to locate empty harvest bins in apple orchards to 
minimize distance walked from tree to bin by harvest crews. This model is widely used in 
locating facilities in context of total demand and capacity, with application in the orchard 
use with input of tree planting density and expected yield.  González-Araya et al. (2017) 
extended this model with consideration of fruit maturity indicators per tree to estimate 
percentage of crop ready for harvest. Orchard-wide estimates for yield and the harvestable 
proportion of fruit were used, using previous year data. 
Recently, technologies have emerged for flower and fruit load mapping in orchard 
environments (Anderson et al., 2021b). One such technology is based on machine vision 
using RGB cameras mounted to a farm vehicle (Koirala et al., 2019). In context of mango 
yield forecast, machine vision-based estimates are typically acquired at up to five weeks 
before harvest, after the ‘fruit drop’ period is finished (Anderson et al., 2021a). The use of 
machine vision technologies in estimation of fruit size distribution of fruit on canopy has 
also been demonstrated (Neupane et al., 2022). However, fruit size continues increasing 
in the weeks before harvest. A heat unit model (Amaral et al., 2023a) can be used to 
predict optimal harvest time and a fruit growth model used to forecast fruit size distribution 
at harvest (Neupane et al., 2022; Amaral and Walsh, 2023). 
With use of these datasets, a spatial location plan for empty harvest bins can be produced. 
The current study proposes a system to produce such plans from the inputs of cultivar 
parameters, fruit load distribution from machine vision mapping, average fruit size and bin 
size, and is illustrated with data of fruit counts (Anderson et al., 2019) and fruit size (Amaral 
and Walsh, 2023) from the same orchard.

Site measurements 
Temperature at the orchard was monitored at 15 min intervals using a SensorHost 
(https://sensorhost.com) LoRa capable monitor for current season temperatures and a 10-
year temperature history of the site for forecast from the current date. Estimation of harvest 
date was based on a heat unit target of 1740 (Amaral et al., 2023b) from the asparagus 
stage of panicle development (with heat units calculated as the difference between the 
average of daily maximum and minimum temperatures minus a base temperature of 12 
C and flowering time). 
Fruit counts were estimated by a machine vision method. Machine vision data for the 
orchard was as described by Anderson et al. (2021a), with imaging of tree rows at 10 
frames per second from a vehicle driven at approximately 7 km/h.  Data output was in the 
form of cumulative fruit count per 3.5 m of tree row.

Discussion 
This exercise illustrates an application case for machine vision generated yield data for a 
tree fruit crop. The allocation of field bins into the orchard is a significant task occurring at 
a time within the season when human resources are stretched. In current practice, the 
task requires involvement a person able to judge on-tree fruit load density, thus to ‘eyeball’ 
bin placement.  The proposed automation of bin allocation involves a machine vision 
pipeline, with visualization in a web based geospatial web mapping application, delivered 
through an orchard farm management information system. The accuracy of this location 
plan is thus dependent on the accuracy of the count and sizing data.  
Conclusion 
The use of in-field machine vision for the collection of spatial information on fruit load 
information for an orchard is academically fascinating. However, the collection of such 
data requires an investment of resources. Farm adoption of such technology will only occur 
if there is sufficient ‘return on investment’. Such return is increased if there are more 
practical uses for the data. Bin placement is a potential use, additional to harvest 
scheduling and selective management (Anderson et al., 2019).

Discussion (from thesis examination) 
In practice, growers were not equipped to locate bins along inter-rows with the spatial 
accuracy required to match the suggested bin placements. The user request has 
subsequently morphed to an indication of the maximum number of bins required per row 
in a block, with that number of bins to be delivered to the end of every row. The practical 
considerations behind this change involve simplicity of operation for the bin-runner, with 
delivery of the same number of bins per row, and the practical consideration that it is more 
time efficient to deliver extra bins that are not used than to be short of bins during harvest.
