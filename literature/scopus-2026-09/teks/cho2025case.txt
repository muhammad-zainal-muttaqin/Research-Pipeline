ABSTRACT
This case study investigates the integration of a snapshot hyperspectral, field-portable imaging system for com-
prehensive fruit quality and yield assessment in agricultural orchards. The study introduces a novel framework
for segmenting, counting, sizing, and estimating the ripeness of orchard fruit using spatial-spectral data. By
combining hyperspectral imaging (HSI) with RGB data, we address the complexities of real-world agricultural
environments, including occlusion, shadowing, and motion blur.
Our proposed 1D-ConvNet + SAM2 model outperforms the RGB-only YOLOv8s approach, achieving a 25
percent improvement in recall by leveraging spectral data to detect partially occluded fruit. Using DepthAny-
thingV2 (DAv2), we estimate metric depth to localize fruit detections within a unified 3D coordinate system,
enabling unique tracking and accurate fruit counting across video frames. This capability supports yield es-
timation by providing precise counts and sizes of individual fruits in the orchard. Furthermore, segmentation
masks and point clouds derived from monocular depth estimation are employed for fruit sizing, with future work
focusing on improving handling of occlusions and segmentation errors.
In addition to spatial-spectral detection, we demonstrate the feasibility of non-destructive ripeness estimation
by correlating spectral features in the fruit’s skin with sugar content. An in-lab experiment using the Living
Optics hyperspectral camera achieved a Brix correlation with an R2 of 0.71, underscoring the potential of HSI
for assessing ripeness and fruit quality.
This research highlights the utility of snapshot hyperspectral imaging as an all-in-one solution for end-to-end
fruit quality and yield assessment in orchards. The dataset used in this study is publicly available at https:
//huggingface.co/datasets/LivingOptics/hyperspectral-orchard, encouraging further advancements in
agricultural hyperspectral imaging and computer vision.
1. INTRODUCTION
Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) has emerged as a transformative technology for solving complex challenges in fruit
quality and yield assessment, particularly in the context of precision agriculture. Unlike traditional imaging
techniques that rely on the visible spectrum’s three primary colors (red, green, and blue), HSI captures a wide
range of wavelengths across the electromagnetic spectrum, including visible, infrared, and ultraviolet regions.
This broad spectral coverage allows HSI to extract detailed spectral signatures, unique patterns that describe how
materials reflect, absorb, or emit light at different wavelengths.1,2 By leveraging these spectral signatures, HSI
can differentiate between materials and identify subtle biochemical and structural changes, even when objects
appear visually identical.3,4
HSI has shown significant promise in various fields, including material identification, medical diagnostics,
cultural heritage preservation, and environmental monitoring.5,6 In agriculture, it offers unique advantages for
tasks such as fruit detection, sizing, ripeness estimation, and yield prediction.7–9 However, these applications face
challenges, including the high dimensionality of spectral data, limited labeled training datasets, and variability
in spectral signatures caused by environmental factors such as occlusion, shadowing, and motion blur.10,11
Advancing the capabilities of HSI in real-world scenarios remains an active area of research, with a need for

This paper presents a case study on integrating a snapshot hyperspectral, field-portable imaging system to
solve fruit quality and yield assessment challenges in orchards. The study introduces a novel framework that
combines spatial and spectral information to enhance segmentation, tracking, sizing, and ripeness estimation
of orchard fruit in complex agricultural environments.
Specifically, we explore the use of spectral data to
complement RGB-based approaches, addressing limitations in traditional imaging systems. Our contributions
are as follows:
1. Establish Benchmarks for Spectral Sensing Technology: We define and develop standardized bench-
marks to assess the accuracy and effectiveness of spectral sensing technologies used in HSI for fruit data
analysis. This provides a clear framework for evaluating the performance of various HSI systems in tasks
such as detecting, classifying, and segmenting fruit.
2. Highlight the Benefits of Spectral Sensing: We explore the advantages of using spectral sensing
technologies in the context of fruit data analysis, improving the precision of fruit detection and classification,
enhancing the ability to track fruit growth, and providing valuable insights for agricultural applications.
3. Identify Efficient Feature Recognition Methods: We investigate efficient and effective methods for
feature recognition by comparing the performance of spectral-based methods with spatial-spectral-based
techniques. This demonstrates the potential of combining spectral and spatial information for improved
detection and classification of fruit in diverse environments.
4. Conduct Empirical Experiments: We perform a series of empirical experiments to compare the per-
formance of algorithms used in fruit data analysis, including tasks such as counting, tracking, sizing, and
ripeness estimation. These experiments utilize the same data and evaluation framework to ensure fairness
and consistency in evaluating different methods.

2. RELATED WORKS
2.1 Evolution of HSI Classification Techniques
The evolution of HSI classification has seen significant advancements, transitioning from traditional machine
learning techniques such as k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN),12 Support Vector Machines (SVM),13 and Random
Forests (RF)14 to more sophisticated methods. While these early approaches addressed high-dimensional spectral
data challenges, they were limited in handling the data’s complexity and scale.
In recent years, advanced
techniques like multinomial logistic regression15 and deep learning models—such as transformers,16 stacked
autoencoders (SAEs),17 and Convolutional Neural Networks (ConvNets)18—have gained prominence.
These
methods leverage spatial correlations between spectral pixels, significantly improving classification performance
when applied effectively.19
2.2 Spectral and Spatial-spectral Approaches
HSI classification approaches can be broadly categorised into spectral-based and spatial-spectral-based methods.
Spectral-based approaches focus solely on the spectral signatures of individual pixels, often overlooking spatial
dependencies.20 Although these methods capture detailed spectral information, they may lack the spatial con-
text necessary for higher accuracy.21 Spatial-spectral approaches, in contrast, integrate both spectral and spatial
features, yielding enhanced classification results. Among these, ConvNets have demonstrated exceptional per-
formance due to their ability to effectively combine spatial and spectral information, especially for agricultural
tasks such as fruit classification.22,23
2.3 Deep Learning and Recent Advances in HSI Classification
Recent advancements in deep learning have revolutionised HSI classification. A notable framework combining
spectral and spatial features with SAEs, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and logistic regression10 achieved
higher accuracy than traditional methods like SVM and PCA classifiers. This study highlighted the importance
of hierarchical feature extraction for improved classification, noting, however, that excessive network depth
could hinder performance. Reviews of deep learning techniques for HSI emphasised challenges related to limited
labelled datasets and spectral differences from traditional optical images,24 while underscoring the effectiveness
of 2D and 3D convolutional networks. Li et al.25 categorised deep learning approaches into spectral-feature,
spatial-feature, and spatial-spectral-feature networks, advocating for methods like reinforcement learning and
data augmentation to address data scarcity.
Proc. of SPIE Vol. 13373  1337304-3

2.4 HSI in Fruit Quality Assessment
HSI has emerged as a powerful tool for assessing fruit quality, enabling non-destructive evaluation of physical-
chemical properties, maturity stages, and decay detection. A comprehensive review identified challenges in image
processing, data analysis, and parameter optimisation, suggesting the integration of spectral and image data for
enhanced utility.26 Studies have also explored HSI’s potential for early decay detection in fruits like apples and
citrus, reporting promising results but acknowledging barriers such as large data sizes, high hardware costs, and
redundant data, which limit industrial adoption.27
2.5 Dimensionality Reduction and Quality Assessment Techniques
Dimensionality reduction techniques, such as PCA and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), have been exten-
sively employed to simplify HSI data processing. Applications include distinguishing banana maturity stages
under varying temperatures, where HSI effectively correlated spectral data with chemical transformations dur-
ing ripening, such as the conversion of starch into soluble sugars like glucose and fructose.28 HSI’s capabilities
in evaluating textural, biochemical, and safety attributes of fruits and vegetables have further highlighted its
non-destructive nature and real-time application potential, despite challenges like computational demands and
morphological calibration.29
2.6 Innovations in Fruit Ripeness, Maturity and Health Estimation
The assessment of fruit ripeness and maturity using HSI has gained traction due to its precision and non-
invasive nature. Studies have employed techniques like band selection and dimensionality reduction to classify
fruit maturity stages with high accuracy. For instance, blueberry maturity stages were classified with accuracies
exceeding 88% using methods such as pairwise class discriminability and hierarchical dimensionality reduction
alongside classifiers like k-NN, SVM, and AdaBoost.30 Similarly, persimmon ripeness was determined with 95.3%
accuracy through feature wavelength analysis and texture-based classification.31
HSI has also been used to automate ripeness evaluation in apples, combining spectral data with texture
features to detect surface lesions and other quality parameters.32
By capturing internal and external fruit
characteristics, these studies illustrate HSI’s potential to enhance ripeness assessment accuracy significantly,
paving the way for its integration into smart agricultural systems.
2.7 Methods and Challenges in Fruit Counting
Accurate fruit counting is vital for yield estimation, and various traditional and advanced methods have been
developed to address this need.
For instance, mango yield estimation using line-scan HSI on an unmanned
vehicle achieved determination coefficients of up to 0.83, outperforming RGB methods.33 Automated systems
for pineapple crown counting have achieved 94.4% accuracy using UAV-captured RGB images combined with
machine learning classifiers like artificial neural networks.34 Similarly, texture- and shape-based methods have
successfully counted mangoes on tree canopies, achieving counts within 16% of actual values under controlled
imaging conditions.35
2.8 Advances in Machine Learning for Fruit Counting
Machine learning has advanced fruit counting methods significantly. Studies comparing ConvNet architectures
for detecting avocados, lemons, and apples demonstrated that Faster R-CNN achieves superior accuracy, albeit
with higher computational demands, compared to single-shot detectors.36
ConvNets have also been used to
count clustered apples, achieving 97% accuracy by addressing challenges like occlusions and varying lighting
conditions.37 A low-cost green fruit counting system for orange trees reported a detection error of only 5% under
controlled conditions, showcasing the potential for accessible technology adoption.38
2.9 HSI for Fruit Detection and Size Estimation
HSI’s versatility extends to fruit detection and size estimation. Reviews of deep learning techniques for these
applications have highlighted efforts to overcome challenges like canopy occlusion and lighting variability.39
Studies assessing peaches’ internal and external quality attributes achieved high accuracy in weight prediction
and diameter estimation with minimal error margins.40 By integrating spectral and spatial data, HSI-based
methods for fruit size and count estimation have consistently outperformed manual approaches, demonstrating
its practical value in agriculture.41

2.10 Challenges in Yield Estimation Using HSI
Despite its potential, HSI faces significant challenges in yield estimation, including the misclassification of young
leaves and overlapping fruit due to spectral similarities and canopy occlusion.42 Environmental variables such
as inconsistent lighting, shadows, and debris further complicate data acquisition in outdoor conditions. Compu-
tational demands for processing high-dimensional data and the scalability of HSI systems remain key hurdles,
particularly in commercial agricultural applications.
The processing of high-dimensional hyperspectral data poses significant computational challenges. Traditional
techniques, such as linear unmixing, have been used to estimate vegetation abundance and correlate it with
yield by analyzing multidate imagery. While these methods achieve reasonable accuracy, they struggle with
nonlinear interactions and require extensive preprocessing to address errors caused by mixed spectral signatures
in heterogeneous canopies.43
Deep learning models, like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have shown promise for yield estima-
tion by combining spectral and spatial information. For example, a CNN-based approach integrating spectral
and RGB data achieved a prediction accuracy of 75.5% for corn yields, surpassing one-dimensional and two-
dimensional models.
However, the approach faced limitations, including small dataset sizes and a reliance
on preprocessing to optimize performance, emphasizing the need for larger datasets and robust preprocessing
pipelines for broader applicability.44
In orchard environments, ground-based HSI has been employed to estimate mango yields with results compa-
rable to RGB-based methods. Despite its advantages in detecting nuanced traits like disease and maturity, the
cost-effectiveness of HSI as a standalone tool for yield estimation remains questionable. Its practical scalability
is further hindered by labour-intensive ground truthing and the significant preprocessing demands of HSI data.33
Efforts to integrate hyperspectral data with machine vision systems for automated fruit detection across
growth stages have demonstrated potential. However, these systems face challenges in developing algorithms
that can handle variability in fruit appearance, such as differences in colour and size, across developmental stages
and environmental conditions. Furthermore, achieving the real-time processing speeds necessary for large-scale
agricultural operations remains an ongoing hurdle.45

4. DATASET PREPARATION
The dataset was acquired using a Living Optics hyperspectral camera,46 which captures detailed spectral data
spanning the visible to near-infrared (VIS-NIR) range, covering wavelengths from 440nm to 900nm. Each data
sample includes both standard RGB images with a resolution of 2048 × 2432 × 3 and hyperspectral (HS) data,
comprising 96 narrow spectral bands from 4384 spectral samples. For simplicity, we refer to the combined RGB
and spectral data as hyperspectral (HS) images.
4.1 Data Scope and Collection
This dataset was developed in collaboration with the UK’s largest orchard fruit producer and covers over 1,000
trees of multiple fruit varieties throughout the growing season (March to September). The dataset supports
various applications, including fruit detection, sizing, segmentation, and disease detection, by offering a compre-
hensive view of fruit development stages.
4.2 Data Annotation and Splitting
A subset of the dataset has been annotated, encompassing 44 unique raw files and 439 frames. To ensure robust
evaluation, an 8:2 train-test split is employed at the raw file level, so all frames from a given file are confined
to either the training or testing set. This approach minimizes the risk of data leakage and maintains dataset
integrity.
4.3 Class Distribution
The dataset includes three annotated fruit classes: Royal Gala Apple, Pear, and Cox Apple, with respective
instance counts of 3,785, 2,523, and 73, respectively. This substantial class imbalance necessitates careful data
preprocessing to address training challenges, particularly for the underrepresented Cox Apple class, which is
present exclusively in the training set.
4.4 Background Class and Imbalance
Unlabelled portions of each scene are mined for spectral samples to create a background class, which adds
substantial imbalance due to the high volume of background data compared to foreground spectra. Additional
details are provided in section 12.
4.5 Spectral Characteristics
The spectral profiles for each class, as shown in Figure 2, demonstrate unique characteristics. Royal Gala Apples
exhibit a pronounced peak between channels 42 and 62, associated with their strong red coloration.
Pears
exhibit spectral profiles similar to the background, primarily because of shared green hues. Lastly, the Cox
Apple’s profile indicates broad spectral responses across the visible range capturing changes akin to yellow-tone
identifiers reflective of its pale appearance.
4.6 Labeling Spectra
Spectral samples are extracted from HS images and assigned labels based on the segmentation mask at their
respective locations. This process produces two aligned arrays for each dataset split: a spectral array of shape
[N, 96], which represents the spectral samples, and a class array of shape [N, 1], containing the corresponding
class labels. Samples that fall outside labeled segmentation masks are assigned to the background class, which
is labeled as 0, thereby contributing to the imbalance within the dataset.

4.7.1 Reflectance Conversion
The average spectrum of the brightest 5% of pixels in each HS image is calculated to estimate the illuminant.
Each spectrum is then divided by this illuminant to convert radiance to pseudo-reflectance, mitigating lighting
variability between scenes. Figure 3 illustrates this process.
4.7.2 Sum Normalisation
Each spectrum is divided by its channel-wise sum to standardize the area under the curve to one. This step
reduces intra-class variance caused by variations in camera settings (gain, exposure) and environmental factors
like shadows. Examples are shown in Figure 4.
5. FRUIT DETECTION AND SEGMENTATION METHODOLOGY
5.1 Experimental Setup
The image dimensions vary by model type. The Spectral Segmentation Model leverages the full spectral resolution
of 4384 spectral points, while the Spatial-Spectral and YOLOv8s models operate on standardized images resized
to 960x960 pixels (cropped and half resolution).
This resolution limitation arises due to the Living Optics
Camera, which collects spectral samples only in the central 1920×1920 region due to its optical design.46 As a
result, only objects within this central area are labeled, leaving regions outside this area without ground truth
segmentation masks.
This study benchmarks the performance of seven models on spectral detection tasks with HS and RGB data.
These models, including two Spectral detection algorithms, four Spatial-Spectral segmentation methods, and one
RGB segmentation model, employ diverse strategies to optimize detection accuracy and computational efficiency
for HS and RGB analysis challenges.
Proc. of SPIE Vol. 13373  1337304-8

5.2 Spectral Detection Algorithms
This section details two spectral detection models used for hyperspectral data classification: a RF classifier as
a baseline and a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (1D-ConvNet) as a more advanced approach. Each model
leverages spectral information to predict class labels for individual spectra.
5.2.1 Random Forest
The RF classifier is implemented using the scikit-learn library, employing the OneVsRestClassifier framework
for multiclass classification. It predicts the most probable class for each spectrum. Custom hyperparameters,
such as a minimum of two samples per leaf, five samples per split, and 400 estimators, are used to configure the
model. Class weighting strategies are also applied to address the dataset’s imbalance, as discussed in section 12.
While the RF model achieves perfect accuracy on training data, it tends to overfit due to its decision trees
extending fully until leaf nodes are pure. Attempts to mitigate overfitting by limiting the maximum depth of trees
result in decreased test set performance. This outcome suggests that preprocessing steps, such as reflectance
conversion and sum normalization (subsection 4.7), have effectively produced well-separated classes, thereby
reducing the need for additional regularization.
5.2.2 1D Convolutional Classifier
The second method employs a 1D-ConvNet for spectral classification. A grid search is conducted to refine the
model’s architecture and hyperparameters, starting with a foundational structure. This baseline consists of a
single convolutional layer, a ReLU activation, batch normalization, a dropout layer to prevent overfitting, and a
fully connected output layer with four neurons. To optimize performance, various configurations of convolutional
and fully connected layers are tested during the grid search.
The final architecture identified through this process includes a single convolutional layer with 64 neurons,
followed by four fully connected layers that progressively narrow from 512 to 64 neurons. The output layer
applies Sigmoid and Softmax activations to compute class probabilities. Training is performed using the Adam
optimizer with early stopping based on validation F1 scores, and the model achieves its best results by balancing
robust spectral features with effective generalization.
The grid search explores two primary parameters to identify the optimal 1D-ConvNet architecture. The first
involves varying the number and size of convolutional layers, ranging from one to four layers, with neuron counts
set at 64, 128, 256, and 512. The second parameter involves the number and size of fully connected layers, tested
across configurations ranging from zero to four layers beyond the output layer. Both increasing and decreasing
layer-width patterns, such as [64, 128, 256, 512] and [512, 256, 128, 64], are considered. This combination results
in a total of 32 potential architectures for evaluation.
The grid search is conducted using the ”Background subsampling 1” method to address class imbalance.
Training is performed with a batch size of 4384, equivalent to one frame of spectral data, and optimized using
the Adam algorithm47 with default hyperparameters. An early stopping mechanism, triggered after 100 epochs
without improvement in the validation F1 score, is employed to prevent unnecessary computation. The final
architecture selected is the one achieving the highest validation F1 score. As is common in architecture searches,48
it is assumed that strong interim performance correlates with robust final results.
The selected architecture begins with a single convolutional layer comprising 64 neurons operating along
the spectral channels. This is followed by a ReLU activation and batch normalization to stabilize the feature
distributions. Beyond the convolutional block, the network contains four fully connected layers with progressively
narrowing sizes of 512, 256, 128, and 64 neurons. The output layer includes four neurons, utilizing Sigmoid and
Softmax activation functions to compute class probabilities.
A significant challenge in training the 1D-ConvNet arises from the pronounced imbalance in the orchard
dataset. This imbalance manifests in two ways: the overwhelming prevalence of background spectra compared
to foreground spectra and the uneven distribution of samples among the foreground classes. To counter these
issues, various techniques are employed to ensure that the model effectively learns to distinguish all classes,
despite the dataset’s inherent disparities.

5.3 Spatial-spectral Segmentation Algorithms
The spatial-spectral approach integrates spectral classification with spatial segmentation to enhance detection
capabilities in HSI. This method involves evaluating four combinations of spectral and spatial models: RF +
FastSAM, RF + SAM2, 1D-ConvNet + FastSAM, and 1D-ConvNet + SAM2. Each combination capitalizes on
the strengths of its spectral and spatial components to achieve optimal performance.
The spatial-spectral segmentation process operates in two stages.
Initially, spectra are classified using a
spectral classifier. Subsequently, the RGB image is over-segmented by a spatial segmentation algorithm. The
results from these steps are merged, mapping spectral classifications onto the segmentation masks to delineate
objects of interest.
Spectral classification relies on machine learning models to analyze HS data directly. The Random Forest
RF classifier identifies patterns using an ensemble of decision trees, allowing it to learn relationships within
the spectral inputs. In contrast, the 1D-ConvNet extracts hierarchical features using its deep learning architec-
ture, combining convolutional and fully connected layers. Despite differing methodologies, both classifiers are
fundamentally data-driven, leveraging the patterns within the HS dataset to make predictions.
For spatial segmentation, two distinct models are used: FastSAM and SAM2. FastSAM is optimized for com-
putational efficiency and speed, making it well-suited for real-time or large-scale applications. Conversely, SAM2
focuses on achieving higher accuracy by employing more resource-intensive spatial segmentation techniques,
prioritizing precision over speed.
Segments identified by either FastSAM or SAM2 are classified based on the most frequently occurring non-
background class within the segment, determined from the predictions of the N spectra it contains. The class
of each individual spectrum within a segment is assigned based on the highest classification probability output
by the spectral detector, whether RF or 1D-ConvNet.
This integrated approach leverages the strengths of spectral and spatial models, enabling effective detection
and segmentation of objects within HS images.

5.3.2 RGB Detection and Segmentation with a Fine-tuned YOLO model
The RGB detection and segmentation model utilises a fine-tuned YOLOv8s architecture, originally pre-trained
with Ultralytics weights, specifically adapted for segmentation tasks. The model underwent fine-tuning on the
orchard dataset to enhance its performance in predicting bounding boxes for objects.
To prepare the dataset for YOLOv8s, the RGB scene views were extracted from hyperspectral images, and
segmentation masks were converted into polygon corner coordinates outlining the labeled objects. These data
were exported in the YOLOv5 segmentation format, ensuring compatibility with the model.
The RGB scene views and segmentation masks were cropped to the central 1920x1920 region prior to data
export. This cropping was necessary for two primary reasons: first, the labeled data included segmentation
masks only for this region; second, the Living Optics Camera provides spectral samples exclusively within this
area. By standardizing the dataset in this manner, it became easier and fairer to compare the performance of
various detection and segmentation models.
The fine-tuning process was conducted using the Ultralytics fine-tuning framework. Key hyperparameters
included a maximum of 10,000 training epochs, with an early stopping patience of 200 epochs to prevent over-
fitting. The model was trained using 960x960 image dimensions, the Adam optimizer with default settings,
and a dropout rate of 40%. Default data augmentation parameters provided by the Ultralytics framework were
employed during training by calling the ‘YOLO.train()’ function.
6. PERFORMANCE COMPARISON
This research employs a comprehensive performance evaluation framework to rigorously analyse the capabili-
ties of various models in HSI and RGB detection and segmentation tasks. By examining both spectral and
spatial characteristics, the framework provides an in-depth understanding of each model’s strengths and limita-
tions, ensuring a nuanced evaluation of detection efficacy. The metrics used in the analysis facilitate a robust,
multidimensional assessment of the models’ performance.
Accuracy serves as the baseline measure, providing an overall indication of prediction correctness. However,
due to the limitations of accuracy in capturing nuanced performance, particularly in datasets with imbalanced
class distributions, additional metrics such as Precision and Recall are considered.
Precision evaluates the
model’s ability to correctly identify positive instances while minimising false positives. Recall, equivalent to the
true positive rate, assesses the model’s capability to detect all actual positive instances. These metrics, used in
tandem, offer a detailed view of detection capabilities.
The F1 score balances Precision and Recall, providing a single measure of performance. The F2 score, which
places greater emphasis on Recall, is particularly valuable in scenarios where minimising false negatives is critical.
This is especially important in spectral detection tasks, where undetected instances can have a significant impact
on performance.
For localisation evaluation, the framework incorporates IoU in two forms: Spectral IoU and Spatial IoU.
Spectral IoU assesses the effectiveness of spectral feature detection, particularly in the context of the Living Optics
Camera, which uses a sparse spectral sampling mask comprising 4,384 points across the scene. It compares the
predicted class area within this mask against the ground truth area. Spatial IoU, on the other hand, evaluates
localisation accuracy using dense RGB segmentation masks, offering insights into the geometric accuracy of
spatial predictions.
To complement IoU, mAP summarises precision across multiple thresholds, providing a
holistic measure of detection quality.
Additional evaluation metrics include G-Mean49 and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).50 G-Mean
reflects the balance between sensitivity and specificity, ensuring equitable performance across classes.
MCC
provides a comprehensive measure derived from all elements of the confusion matrix, making it particularly
suitable for imbalanced datasets.
Error patterns are further explored through False Positive Rate (FPR) and False Negative Rate (FNR). These
metrics, when considered alongside Recall, offer deeper insights into misclassification trends and their underlying
causes.

7. YIELD ESTIMATION VIA OBJECT TRACKING
With the completion of the spatial-spectral detector, we proceed to develop a tracking algorithm that builds
upon the best-performing spatial-spectral detector.
While counting the number of detections per frame is straightforward, determining the number of unique
detections across a sequence of frames is more challenging. Objects may not be detected on every frame, and
variations in both the scene and the camera’s movement make it difficult to identify previously detected objects.
To address this, we employ a tracking algorithm that estimates which detections correspond to unique objects
and which are simply repeated detections of the same object(s).
7.1 Method
Each detected fruit is assigned a set of 3D coordinates relative to the camera’s position in the first frame. This
requires estimating the distance between the camera and each detection, which involves generating a metric
depth image.
We utilise DepthAnythingV2 (DAv2)51,52 with the ‘vitl’ encoder and the ‘VKitti 2’ metric depth check-
point.53,54 Although these weights were designed for urban scenes in the VKitti 2 dataset and are not optimal
for orchard environments, fine-tuning DAv2 for this domain is beyond the scope of this study.

8. FRUIT SIZING
8.1 Method
To estimate the size of a fruit, we utilise the segmentation mask provided by the spatial-spectral segmentation
algorithm and the point cloud generated by DAv2.
By applying the segmentation mask to the point cloud of a detected fruit, we can estimate its height and
width.
This is done by selecting the four furthest points within the mask in the left, right, up, and down
directions in the imaging plane, and then computing the width as width = right −left and the height as
height = up −down.
This method does not account for the rotation of the fruit around the Z axis (which is orthogonal to the
imaging plane). However, it is computationally simple, and most pears tend to grow vertically due to the increase
Proc. of SPIE Vol. 13373  1337304-21

in mass in the lower half of the fruit. Apples, being roughly spherical, are not significantly affected by rotational
orientation.
8.1.1 Occlusion
A significant challenge in estimating the size of a detected object is occlusion. If an object is partially blocked
by an obstacle, such as a leaf, the measured extent of the object will be inaccurate.
To mitigate this effect, we apply an aspect ratio threshold for pears. Since pears are roughly twice as tall as
they are wide, any detected pear that does not meet this aspect ratio is likely to be either a false positive or an
occluded detection.
We utilise an aspect ratio threshold function, as shown in Equation 13, to filter out detections that are likely
to be of occluded pears. Specifically, a detection is considered a partial detection if the height is less than 1.7
times the width of the detection.

9. FRUIT RIPENESS ESTIMATION
The accurate estimation of fruit ripeness is essential for the agricultural supply chain, directly influencing harvest
timing, post-harvest handling, and consumer satisfaction. Ripeness determines critical fruit attributes such as
texture, flavor, sweetness, and shelf life, making it a key quality metric for high-value crops like apples and pears.
These attributes also influence market pricing and consumer purchasing decisions. Inconsistent ripeness across
a batch can lead to economic losses due to overripe or underripe fruits being rejected during quality inspections
or by end-users.
Traditional approaches to ripeness assessment involve destructive and resource-intensive techniques. These
include measuring sugar content, represented by total soluble solids (TSS) or Brix, as well as acidity levels such
as pH and titratable acidity. These methods typically require juicing the fruit, performing chemical titrations, or
using handheld refractometers. While effective, these techniques rely on labor-intensive sampling protocols and
require specialized laboratory infrastructure. For large-scale farming operations, the periodic sampling needed
to monitor ripeness across orchards over several weeks results in substantial operational costs.
Traditional methods face several challenges that limit their effectiveness in modern agricultural practices.
First, their destructive nature results in wasted produce, as sampled fruits cannot be sold or consumed. Moreover,
sampling bias often arises because only a small subset of fruits is tested, which may not accurately represent
ripeness variability across an orchard. The time-consuming nature of these processes further delays decision-
making, which is critical in large-scale operations where timing can impact both yield and quality. Additionally,
environmental factors such as temperature and humidity can influence the accuracy of measurements, reducing
reproducibility across different conditions. Finally, the dependence on manual intervention and laboratory setups
makes these methods incompatible with automation, a key component of modern precision agriculture.
These challenges underline the need for innovative approaches to ripeness estimation that are non-invasive,
scalable, and capable of providing real-time insights directly in the field. Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) offers a
solution that addresses these limitations by enabling detailed, non-destructive analysis of ripeness indicators.
9.1 Method
Hyperspectral imaging is a powerful technique that captures spectral information across a broad range of wave-
lengths, allowing for the detection of subtle biochemical and structural changes in the fruit’s skin that correlate
with ripeness. Unlike traditional methods, HSI provides a non-destructive means of assessment, enabling re-
peated measurements on the same sample without causing damage. This makes it ideal for longitudinal studies
of ripening. Additionally, HSI systems are highly adaptable and can be integrated into automated setups, offering
potential for high-throughput analysis in industrial or field environments. Recent advances in HSI technology,
such as compact sensors and portable configurations, further enhance its usability for in-field applications.
The spectral data collected through HSI can be processed using statistical and machine learning models to
predict ripeness indicators. In this study, partial least squares regression (PLSR) was used to estimate Brix
values, a key measure of sweetness and ripeness. Previous studies have shown that HSI combined with PLSR
can achieve coefficients of determination (R2) ranging from 0.2 to 0.7 and root mean square error (RMSE) values
between 0.3 and 1.5, depending on the dataset and experimental conditions. This demonstrates the potential of
HSI for ripeness estimation while highlighting the need for further refinement of the methodology.
Proc. of SPIE Vol. 13373  1337304-24

In our experiments, 40 Royal Gala apple samples were imaged using a Living Optics HSI camera in a
controlled laboratory setting. The imaging setup included broadband light sources arranged at a 45/0° geometry
to ensure consistent illumination. A white Tivet sheet was used to facilitate reflectance conversion. To evaluate
the robustness of the technique, two types of broadband light sources were employed—halogen and broadband
LED. The variation in radiance spectra caused by these lighting conditions is shown in Figure 12b. This setup
enabled us to assess the influence of lighting variations on the accuracy of ripeness predictions.
After imaging, the sugar content of the apples was measured using a handheld refractometer, which provides
an accuracy of ±0.2°Bx. Preprocessing steps included normalization to minimize the effects of surface curvature
and smoothing to reduce spectral noise. The spectral data were analyzed in radiance space rather than reflectance
space due to challenges in achieving reliable reflectance conversions in the presence of shadows.
While this
approach requires larger datasets to account for variability, it simplifies preprocessing and makes the model more
adaptable to real-world conditions. A linear PLSR model was trained and evaluated using a 50/50 dataset split,
correlating spectral features to measured Brix scores.
This decision to work in radiance space, while unconventional, reflects our focus on developing a methodology
that can be extended to field applications. Although this approach introduces the need for additional training
data, we hypothesize that it offers a more robust pathway for adapting the system to varying environmental con-
ditions. Future work will validate this hypothesis by testing the method under diverse lighting and environmental
scenarios.

10. CONCLUSION
We have presented a comprehensive case study on integrating a snapshot hyperspectral, field-portable imaging
system to solve critical challenges in orchard fruit quality assessment. Our work introduces a publicly available
dataset, benchmarking framework, and system for segmenting, counting, sizing, and estimating fruit ripeness.
The dataset, hosted at livingoptics.com/huggingface, is designed to inspire further advancements in hyperspectral
computer vision algorithms and foster collaborative innovation in this emerging field.
The results highlight the advantages of combining spectral classification with RGB-based segmentation al-
gorithms. Our proposed 1D-ConvNet + SAM2 with a similarity filter outperformed the RGB-only YOLOv8s
approach by nearly 25 percent in Recall, effectively addressing the challenges posed by occlusion, shadowing,
and motion blur in real-world agricultural settings. Unlike YOLOv8s, which struggled to detect over half of the
fruit due to its reliance on shape and limited color information, the Spatial-Spectral approach achieved over 70
percent detection accuracy. By leveraging hyperspectral data, our method demonstrated the ability to detect
partially occluded fruit, requiring only a small visible portion for successful identification.
Beyond fruit detection and segmentation, we developed a two-stage process that uniquely tracks individual
fruit across video frames and estimates their size. This approach enables accurate fruit count and size estimation,
key metrics for yield and quality assessment in orchards. Additionally, we demonstrated the feasibility of non-
destructive ripeness estimation using the Living Optics hyperspectral camera, achieving a Brix correlation with
an R2 of 0.71.
This study underscores the potential of snapshot hyperspectral imaging systems like the Living Optics HSI
camera to deliver end-to-end quality assessments of orchard fruit without requiring additional camera systems.
By addressing key agricultural challenges, our work paves the way for scalable, efficient, and automated solutions
to support modern precision agriculture.
11. FUTURE WORK
This research highlights the potential to further improve spatial-spectral detection, segmentation, counting, and
sizing methodologies within hyperspectral imaging systems. Several areas of improvement have been identified
that will serve as the basis for future work.
11.1 Spectral detection improvements
Future work will focus on refining the spectral detection algorithm to improve both accuracy and robustness.
A comprehensive search for optimal architectures and hyperparameters is essential to achieve the right balance
between complexity and performance. Employing Bayesian Optimised Hyperband48 will speed up this process
by eliminating poorly performing areas in the search space. Exploring alternative loss functions tailored for HS
data is also crucial to address imbalanced datasets and improve convergence. Increasing training data through
augmentation or more real-world data will help improve generalization and reduce overfitting. Additionally,
postprocessing techniques can be introduced to refine detections and minimize False Positives and False Negatives.
11.2 Object segmentation
Currently, segmentation relies on third-party models, and fine-tuning them on domain-specific data is an impor-
tant next step. However, long-term goals include developing a custom RGB segmentation algorithm optimized
for HS tasks, which would emphasize both speed and accuracy. Furthermore, creating a joint spectral and RGB
encoder to leverage both modalities directly, instead of treating them separately, could improve performance.
Proc. of SPIE Vol. 13373  1337304-26

11.3 Monocular depth
Depth estimation currently uses a third-party monocular depth model, and future work will focus on fine-tuning
this system to better align with the dataset’s characteristics. Developing a custom monocular depth estimation
algorithm tailored to HSI applications would offer better control over both accuracy and computational efficiency.
Integrating spectral data into the monocular depth algorithm may also enhance performance, as it would provide
more detailed feature embeddings. Improving the depth calibration process and automating it will be key to
ensuring precise measurements and minimizing calibration errors.
11.4 Tracking
Tracking improvements will focus on unifying the coordinate system used in HS analysis. Optimising ICP hyper-
parameters will enhance alignment and improve tracking accuracy across multiple frames. For faster processing,
replacing ICP with a non-point cloud-based approach like Scale-Invariant Feature Transform combined with
homography computation could significantly speed up the process. A key limitation of the current tracking algo-
rithm is its assumption of a static scene and a moving camera; future work should account for object movement
within the scene.
11.5 Sizing
The current sizing method relies on bounding box dimensions and can be enhanced by considering object rotation.
Future efforts will focus on developing algorithms to estimate the maximum length and width of an object by
analyzing its mask, irrespective of orientation. This will provide more accurate size estimates. Capturing ground
truth fruit sizes, or at least mean and standard deviation values, would also allow for a more empirical analysis
of sizing results.
11.6 Yield estimation
Obtaining ground truth counts of unique fruit would significantly improve the analysis quality in this study.
Additionally, combining the counts from the tracking system with size data could enable an estimate of the total
volume of fruit. Integrating spectral metrics to assess the health and quality of the fruit could further enhance
yield estimation, enabling classifications such as healthy, damaged, or diseased fruit.
11.7 Ripeness Extimation
This study represents an initial test to evaluate HSI’s capability in assessing fruit ripeness. While promising,
there is a need to expand the dataset in future studies to improve the robustness of predictive models and ensure
statistical reliability. Implementing this method in field conditions will be critical for evaluating its real-world
applicability, accounting for variables such as ambient lighting and environmental factors. Enhanced predictive
algorithms and calibration techniques will also be essential to accommodate diverse fruit varieties and ripening
stages, further refining the model’s accuracy and generalisability.

Preliminary experiments with the Random Forest classifier demonstrated the necessity of forming a background
class in the training data. This is due to two factors:
1. Random forests must assign a class to all inputs - leading to background spectra being assigned foreground
classes if no background class is utilised.
2. The subtle difference between foreground class spectra and background spectra mean that the confidence
score of the random forest is not wholly reliable for distinguishing between some foreground and background
spectra. Therefore we cannot easily suppress background spectra classified as foreground classes.

The background class is formed of all spectra which do not lie inside a mask from the foreground labelling.
The background class contains many more times the number of spectra that are found in all foreground
classes combined - for the training set 1,855,755 background spectra vs 51,285 foreground spectra. This biases
both any algorithm trained on the data to preferentially select the background class over all other classes if no
attempt is made to either balance the training data or the training loss.
In the case of the random forest, we have no notion of epochs of training or data batching, therefore if we
want to make use of all of the training data, we cannot easily remove background spectra to balance the number
of foreground vs background spectra.
Instead we have two possible directions to take to achieve a balanced training set:
1. Class weighting
2. Foreground data duplication
12.1 Class weighting
We can provide a set of weights to the random forest training algorithm to reduce the importance of correctly
classifying the background class vs the foreground class. These weights are calculated as being inversely pro-
portional to the frequency of the class. This can be done at either the dataset level or at the subsample level
and is directly supported in Scikit-learn by passing either ‘None’, ‘balanced’ or ‘balanced subsample’ to the
‘class weight’ argument of the random forest.
We can take a similar approach when training the 1D convolutional neural network, providing a set of class
weights which can be used to adjust the loss for each class so as to attempt to avoid biasing the model towards
the background class. We only calculate the weighting once, for the entire dataset, rather than on a batch by
batch basis as this would require reinitialising the loss function on each batch which would be prohibitively slow
during training. Though such an approach might yield better results by re-balancing the loss each batch.

We could choose to duplicate a random sample of the foreground spectra so that the number of background and
foreground spectra match as well as providing balancing across classes.
However, without a data augmentation method, this duplication isn’t helpful for training the random forest
as duplicated spectra will be classified by the same nodes, without the need for additional nodes to distinguish

random forest.
For the 1D convolutional classifier, directly duplicating the data is a more reasonable approach, even without
data augmentation as this simply biases the loss away from the background class by providing more frequent
weight updates calculated from the foreground class losses.

A third option for dataset balancing is available to the 1D convolutional classifier - background data sampling.
