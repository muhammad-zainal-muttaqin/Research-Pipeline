Abstract 
Binocular stereo vision is a low-cost and scalable 3D perception technology that shows 
strong potential in agricultural phenotyping and smart agriculture. By estimating depth 
from multi-view RGB images, it enables non-contact, high-precision sensing of crop struc-
ture, canopy morphology, growth dynamics, and livestock traits, providing essential sup-
port for digital and intelligent agricultural production. With recent advances in deep 
learning-based stereo matching, multimodal sensor fusion, and 3D reconstruction, its ro-
bustness and accuracy in complex ﬁeld environments have been signiﬁcantly improved. 
This paper systematically reviews recent progress in agricultural applications of binocular 
stereo vision, covering system architectures, traditional and deep learning-based stereo 
matching methods, point cloud reconstruction techniques, and emerging supervision 
strategies such as 3D Gaussian splatting. It further summarizes key applications, includ-
ing high-throughput phenotyping, fruit localization and robotic harvesting, weed detec-
tion and precision spraying, autonomous navigation, and livestock body condition assess-
ment, highlighting its role in multi-task agricultural perception systems. Finally, the paper 
discusses major challenges, including low-texture matching diﬃculty, occlusions in com-
plex environments, cross-domain generalization, real-time lightweight deployment, and 
limited dataset availability. Future directions are outlined in foundation model-based vis-
ual perception, self- and weakly supervised learning, multimodal fusion, and edge-eﬃ-
cient model design, aiming to support large-scale deployment in smart agriculture. 
Keywords: binocular stereo vision; agricultural 3D perception; deep learning; smart  
agriculture; multimodal fusion 
 
1. Introduction 
Plant phenotypes refer to the set of observable traits resulting from the interaction 
between genotype and environment. Accurate acquisition of crop phenotypic information 
is fundamental for understanding genotype–phenotype–environment interactions and 
serves as an essential basis for modern crop breeding, precision crop management, and 
intelligent agricultural decision-making [1–3]. Traditional phenotyping methods rely 
heavily on manual measurements, such as plant height estimation with rulers, leaf count-
ing, and destructive biomass sampling [4,5]. As shown in the study by Zou et al., manual

approaches typically depend on discrete sampling at isolated time points, making it diﬃ-
cult to capture continuous growth dynamics throughout the crop life cycle, which often 
leads to missing information at key developmental stages. 
Two-dimensional (2D) imaging technologies have partially alleviated the limitations 
of manual measurements. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and satellite remote sensing 
platforms enable large-scale and high-throughput data acquisition [7,8]. However, due to 
the inherent loss of depth information in 2D projections, the full three-dimensional struc-
ture of crops cannot be faithfully represented. Issues such as leaf overlap, organ occlusion, 
and viewpoint distortion remain diﬃcult to resolve [9–11]. For crops with complex can-
opy structures, such as maize, wheat, and rice, 2D images are insuﬃcient to accurately 
characterize key structural traits, including leaf area index, leaf angle distribution, and 
canopy volume, thereby limiting their applicability in ﬁne-grained phenotypic analysis 
[12,13]. 
In parallel with advances in sensing hardware, intelligent analysis methods have be-
come increasingly important for converting agricultural images and three-dimensional 
data into biologically and operationally meaningful information. Conventional machine-
learning methods rely on hand-crafted features and statistical classiﬁers, whereas recent 
deep learning approaches can automatically learn hierarchical representations for image 
classiﬁcation, object detection, semantic and instance segmentation, stereo matching, and 
pose estimation. For three-dimensional agricultural data, point-cloud ﬁltering, registra-
tion, segmentation, skeleton extraction, geometric modeling, and trait-estimation algo-
rithms are commonly used to characterize plant and animal structures. Multimodal sensor 
fusion and temporal modeling further integrate complementary information from RGB 
images, depth sensors, LiDAR, multispectral cameras, and sequential observations. To-
gether, these intelligent analysis methods support crop phenotyping, fruit localization, 
weed recognition, autonomous navigation, robotic harvesting, and livestock monitoring, 
thereby transforming raw sensor measurements into actionable information for smart ag-
riculture. 
From a global perspective, binocular stereo vision and intelligent analysis methods 
have attracted increasing attention in agricultural research and automation. Studies in 
North America, Europe, and East Asia have progressively moved from controlled labor-
atory experiments toward ﬁeld-based deployment and integrated perception systems for 
high-throughput phenotyping, robotic harvesting, precision spraying, autonomous ma-
chinery, and livestock monitoring. Regional research priorities vary according to crop 
types, production systems, labor availability, equipment infrastructure, and investment 
in agricultural digitalization. Meanwhile, the availability of low-cost stereo cameras, deep 
learning models, edge-computing platforms, and multimodal sensing systems has low-
ered the technical barriers to agricultural 3D perception. Nevertheless, global adoption 
remains uneven because of limited domain-speciﬁc datasets, substantial environmental 
variability, diﬀerences in farm scale and infrastructure, and the absence of standardized 
evaluation and deployment frameworks. These conditions indicate that future progress 
requires not only algorithmic innovation, but also internationally diverse datasets, cross-
region validation, aﬀordable hardware integration, and closer collaboration among re-
searchers, equipment manufacturers, and agricultural practitioners. 
The emergence of three-dimensional (3D) perception technologies has provided a 
promising pathway to overcome these limitations. Point clouds and mesh-based repre-
sentations preserve complete spatial geometric information, enabling organ-level mor-
phometric measurements and canopy-scale structural analysis [14,15]. Wu et al. con-

provide richer structural information and support non-destructive, continuous monitor-

Among existing 3D sensing technologies, binocular stereo vision occupies a unique 
position. Current crop 3D sensing approaches can be broadly categorized into active and 
passive methods [20–22]. Active methods, including structured light, time-of-ﬂight (Time 
of Flight) cameras, and LiDAR, obtain depth information by actively emitting signals 
[23,24]. Although these methods oﬀer high accuracy, they suﬀer from high hardware cost 
and limited adaptability to outdoor environments. In ﬁeld-based crop phenotyping, ToF 
cameras are prone to generating large invalid measurement regions under strong ambient 
illumination, while LiDAR, despite its high precision, remains expensive. Passive recon-
struction methods mainly include multi-view stereo (Multi-View Stereo), structure from 
motion (Structure from Motion), and binocular stereo vision, all of which rely solely on 
ambient light for 3D reconstruction [25–27]. 
Binocular stereo vision has become one of the most promising solutions for agricul-
tural 3D perception due to several advantages [28,29]. First, it is highly cost-eﬀective, re-
quiring only two standard RGB cameras, with hardware costs far lower than LiDAR sys-
tems [30]. Zhang et al. compared fruit tree canopy volume estimation between binocular 
vision and LiDAR in UAV-based precision spraying and reported that the binocular sys-
tem reduced cost by more than 80% while maintaining volume estimation errors within 
10% [31]. Second, binocular stereo vision provides a practical and cost-eﬀective solution 
for open-ﬁeld applications, but its performance remains sensitive to strong and spatially 
variable illumination. Direct sunlight, shadows, overexposure, and specular reﬂections 
may reduce photometric consistency between stereo image pairs and consequently de-
grade matching accuracy. In comparison, LiDAR is generally more robust to variations in 
ambient illumination, although intense solar radiation may still reduce its signal-to-noise 
ratio and eﬀective sensing range. Third, it provides high information density by generat-
ing dense depth maps, which are essential for ﬁne-grained organ-level phenotyping. Yang 
et al. combined binocular stereo vision with supervision signals derived from 3D Gauss-
ian splatting to generate dense depth maps for leafy vegetables, achieving an endpoint 
error (EPE) of only 0.91 pixels in leaf regions [32]. Fourth, the method beneﬁts from a 
mature algorithmic foundation, and recent advances in deep learning have further signif-
icantly improved stereo matching accuracy [33]. 
However, binocular stereo vision also faces several critical limitations in agricultural 
applications. The ﬁrst is the diﬃculty of stereo matching in low-texture and repetitive-
texture regions, as crop leaf surfaces often lack distinctive features while repetitive struc-
tures easily lead to mismatches. Wu et al. observed in oil-tea fruit harvesting robots that 
traditional stereo matching methods produce large holes in leaf regions, whereas im-
proved deep learning-based networks signiﬁcantly reduce 3D localization errors [16]. Oc-
clusion remains another major challenge. Zou et al. reported that severe occlusion in dense 
wheat canopies leads to substantial segmentation errors when directly applying cluster-
ing methods on reconstructed point clouds [6]. The trade-oﬀ between computational com-
plexity and real-time performance is also prominent. Yi et al., in a study on real-time vol-
ume estimation of four fruit types, found that lightweight models achieved higher frame 
rates but underperformed in occlusion handling and small-object detection compared to

more complex networks [34]. In addition, high-quality stereo matching datasets for agri-

large-scale datasets such as KITTI, existing agricultural datasets are small and lack diver-

To address these challenges, recent studies have made substantial progress from the 
perspectives of algorithm design, dataset construction, and system integration. Niu et al. 
proposed a stereo foundation model ﬁne-tuning strategy driven by 2D Gaussian splatting 
for maize ﬁeld scenarios [35]. Using multi-view reconstruction, they synthesized 3625 
high-ﬁdelity stereo pairs, and through a partial parameter freezing strategy, ﬁne-tuned 
only 45.7% of trainable parameters, reducing end-point error in disparity estimation to 
1.85 pixels. Wang et al. introduced the Plant Stereo dataset, providing a high-quality 
benchmark for plant 3D reconstruction [36]. Ajagekar et al. integrated vision foundation 
models such as Segment Anything with RGB-D cameras to build a phenotyping system 
for densely planted lettuce, enabling non-destructive and continuous monitoring of plant 
height, area, and volume at 10-min intervals, with correlation coeﬃcients exceeding 0.94 
compared to manual measurements [37]. 
This review provides a comprehensive narrative overview of advances in binocular 
stereo vision for crop phenotyping and smart agriculture, focusing on representative stud-
ies published between 2015 and 2025. This time window was selected because deep learn-
ing achieved a major breakthrough in stereo matching around 2015. A structured litera-
ture search was conducted using databases including Web of Science, Scopus, and Sci-
enceDirect, with keywords covering the intersection of binocular stereo vision, stereo 
matching, plant phenotyping, crops, and precision agriculture. Relevant studies were 
identiﬁed through the iterative screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts, with priority 
given to methodologically innovative works and those reporting quantitative perfor-
mance evaluations. While we aimed for broad coverage across application domains, we 
acknowledge that the review is narrative in nature and does not follow formal systematic 
review protocols (e.g., PRISMA). Given the interdisciplinary nature of the ﬁeld and the 
diversity of agricultural scenarios, research designs, and evaluation metrics, we consider 
this narrative approach most suitable for synthesizing current progress and identifying 
future research priorities. 
Overall, binocular stereo vision has evolved from early proof-of-concept studies to-
ward large-scale application-oriented research in agriculture, exhibiting trends toward 
higher accuracy, real-time processing, lightweight deployment, and improved generali-
zation. However, a signiﬁcant gap remains between algorithmic performance and large-
scale ﬁeld deployment, particularly in terms of robustness in complex environments, 
cross-domain generalization, and the lack of standardized evaluation frameworks. There-
fore, a systematic review of existing studies is essential for further advancing this ﬁeld. 
2. Fundamentals of Stereo Vision and Adaptation to  
Agricultural Environments 
Stereo vision reconstructs three-dimensional spatial structures from two-dimen-
sional images by simulating the human binocular vision mechanism [38,39]. It serves as a 
key technical approach for object localization and point cloud reconstruction in agricul-
tural scenes. Despite its strong potential in agricultural phenotyping, its practical deploy-
ment in complex ﬁeld environments remains challenging due to high model complexity, 
limited dataset availability, and the absence of uniﬁed evaluation standards. This section 
systematically analyzes the core components of binocular stereo vision and their suitabil-
ity for agricultural applications from a system-level perspective [40,41].

2.1. Construction and Computational Pipeline of Binocular Stereo Vision Systems 
The construction and computational pipeline of binocular stereo vision systems con-
sist of three tightly coupled stages: camera calibration, epipolar rectiﬁcation, and outdoor 
system design [42,43]. These stages jointly determine the ﬁnal accuracy of depth estima-
tion. 
Camera calibration is the foundational step in stereo vision systems, aiming to estab-
lish the mapping relationship between pixel coordinates and 3D world coordinates by 
estimating intrinsic and extrinsic camera parameters [44–46]. The checkerboard-based cal-
ibration method proposed by Zhang has been widely adopted in agricultural applications 
due to its high accuracy and robust implementation [31]. Zou et al. applied Zhang’s cali-
bration method with a ZED 2 stereo camera for ﬁeld wheat point cloud reconstruction, 
enabling eﬀective 3D reconstruction of crop scenes [6]. Multi-view calibration experi-
ments in leafy vegetable phenotyping have achieved a reprojection error below 0.18 pixels, 
providing a reliable geometric basis for subsequent stereo matching [32]. However, even 
with high-precision camera calibration, residual correspondence and disparity-estimation 
errors may still propagate into depth estimation and aﬀect the ﬁnal 3D reconstruction ac-
curacy [47]. In agricultural scenes, these errors can be further aggravated by low-texture 
surfaces, repetitive plant structures, occlusions, and illumination variations [48,49]. In a 
systematic review of 114 vision-based pose estimation studies, Menolotto et al. empha-
sized that calibration strategies should be adapted to deployment scenarios: more com-
plex calibration procedures are suitable for indoor ﬁxed setups, whereas outdoor mobile 
platforms require a balance between calibration eﬃciency and long-term stability, along 
with periodic recalibration mechanisms [50]. 
In calibrated stereo-vision systems, epipolar rectiﬁcation transforms the correspond-
ence search from a two-dimensional image-space problem into a one-dimensional search 
along corresponding epipolar lines, thereby reducing the search space and improving 
matching eﬃciency [51–53]. This is a general geometric principle of stereo vision rather 
than an agriculture-speciﬁc procedure. The accuracy of rectiﬁcation nevertheless directly 
aﬀects disparity estimation, because residual vertical misalignment may introduce corre-
spondence errors and systematic bias in the reconstructed depth. In agricultural applica-
tions, where stereo systems are often mounted on UAVs or mobile platforms, extrinsic 
parameters may drift due to vibration or mechanical shocks, making periodic recalibra-
tion essential for long-term operational stability. 
Outdoor system design requires careful consideration at the hardware level. Camera 
selection must balance resolution, frame rate, baseline distance, and ﬁeld of view [54]. 
Menolotto et al. systematically compared the suitability of diﬀerent camera conﬁgurations 
across zootechnical tasks [50]. Side-view RGB cameras are most suitable for gait and lame-
ness analysis, while top-view RGB setups provide an optimal trade-oﬀ for group behavior 
monitoring. In contrast, stereo vision and multi-view systems are more advantageous 
when absolute geometric accuracy, occlusion handling, or 3D pose reconstruction is re-
quired [54]. 
For UAV-based deployment, Zhang et al. mounted a ZED 2 stereo camera beneath a 
plant protection drone and employed an electronic three-axis stabilized gimbal to main-
tain a consistent vertical viewing angle despite ﬂight attitude variations [31]. The theoret-
ical model of UAV forward ﬂight imaging coverage and camera triggering timing pro-
posed in previous research is visualized in Figure 1, which illustrates the geometric rela-
tionship among ﬂight height, ﬁeld of view, image overlap, and camera moving interval.

2.2. Traditional Stereo Matching Algorithms 
Stereo matching estimates scene depth by identifying corresponding points between 
left and right images, computing disparities, and then reconstructing depth via triangula-
tion [55–57]. Traditional stereo matching methods can be broadly categorized into local 
methods, global methods, and semi-global methods. 
Local methods perform correspondence search within a pixel-wise or small window-
based neighborhood. They are computationally eﬃcient and highly parallelizable; how-
ever, they are prone to mismatches in low-texture, repetitive-texture, and occluded re-
gions. In addition, the use of ﬁxed-size windows limits their ability to balance accuracy 
and robustness adaptively. Global methods formulate stereo matching as an energy min-
imization problem and solve it using optimization techniques such as graph cuts and be-
lief propagation. Although they can integrate broader contextual information, their high 
computational cost makes them unsuitable for real-time applications. Semi-global meth-
ods aggregate matching costs along multiple directions, achieving a better trade-oﬀ be-
tween eﬃciency and accuracy [58,59]. 
In agricultural scenarios, the performance of traditional methods is constrained by 
multiple factors [60]. Yang et al. showed that semi-global methods produced endpoint 
errors as high as 24.74 pixels across the entire image and 10.87 pixels within leafy vegeta-
ble regions [32]. In maize stereo matching studies, Niu et al. observed that SGBM-gener-
ated disparity maps were highly fragmented in leaf regions, with blurred edges and nu-
merous holes, whereas deep learning-based methods reduced matching errors by more 
than one order of magnitude in vegetable regions [35]. These limitations primarily arise 
from three factors: the lack of distinctive texture information on crop leaf surfaces, illumi-
nation variations in ﬁeld environments that degrade visual consistency, and frequent oc-
clusions caused by overlapping leaves and stems, which result in monocular visibility in 
certain regions [61–64]. These inherent limitations of traditional methods have directly 
motivated the adoption of deep learning-based approaches.

2.3. Deep Learning-Based Stereo Matching and Lightweight Edge Deployment 
The introduction of deep learning has fundamentally transformed stereo matching, 
shifting it from hand-crafted feature engineering and heuristic design to a data-driven 
paradigm that learns feature representations and correspondence relationships automat-
ically [65]. Early deep learning-based stereo matching methods primarily used convolu-
tional neural networks to learn similarity measures or matching costs for stereo image 
patches [66]. Subsequent studies progressively developed end-to-end architectures that 
integrate feature extraction, cost-volume construction, cost aggregation, disparity estima-
tion, and reﬁnement within a uniﬁed framework [67,68]. 
Representative supervised learning models have evolved along multiple technical 
directions. PSMNet introduces spatial pyramid pooling and 3D convolutions to expand 
the receptive ﬁeld. GwcNet proposes group-wise correlation to balance eﬃciency and ac-
curacy. RAFT-Stereo employs a recurrent iterative reﬁnement strategy for disparity opti-
mization. CREStereo adopts a cascaded architecture with adaptive correlation to improve 
inference speed. In recent years, Transformer-based architectures have also been intro-
duced. Their self-attention mechanism enables global context modeling and mitigates the 
limited receptive ﬁeld of convolutional neural networks; however, the associated compu-
tational complexity poses signiﬁcant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained 
edge devices.

In agricultural applications, Yang et al. proposed the LVStereo network for leafy veg-
etable phenotyping, supervised by 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) [32]. Multi-view images 
were ﬁrst rendered into stereo training data using 3DGS, resulting in a leafy vegetable 
dataset containing 8060 stereo image pairs. The method introduces a multi-scale deform-
able convolution-based feature extraction network and a hybrid cost volume design. 
LVStereo achieved an endpoint error (EPE) of 2.32 pixels on the full image and 0.91 pixels 
within leafy regions, reaching sub-pixel accuracy for the ﬁrst time on this dataset and sub-
stantially outperforming traditional methods, which reported errors of up to 24.74 pixels. 
Similarly, a 2DGS-supervised framework has been developed for maize phenotyping 
[35]. By leveraging a 2D Gaussian splatting-driven stereo matching pipeline, ﬁeld-scale 
3D phenotyping of maize plants has been enabled. The mean absolute percentage errors 
(MAPE) for plant height, stem diameter, and leaf area were 3.82, 3.43, and 7.74%, respec-
tively, with all coeﬃcients of determination exceeding 0.95. 
In addition, YOLOv11n has been combined with binocular stereo vision for Camellia 
oleifera fruit harvesting [16]. Depth information obtained via stereo matching was con-
verted into 3D coordinates to support vision-based servo control for autonomous picking. 
The average localization errors along the three spatial axes were 1.70 mm, 1.34 mm, and 
1.27 mm, respectively, with a detection time of 58.21 ms, demonstrating the feasibility of 
deep learning-based stereo matching for real-time agricultural robotic operations. 
However, although supervised learning methods perform well on speciﬁc bench-
mark datasets, their direct deployment in agricultural scenarios often suﬀers from a pro-
nounced “domain shift” problem. Most training data are derived from urban road scenes 
or indoor environments, which diﬀer substantially from farmland conditions in terms of 
illumination, texture distribution, and object morphology. To address this issue, a range 
of domain adaptation strategies have been explored. Self-supervised learning leverages 
geometric consistency between stereo image pairs as a supervisory signal, eliminating the 
need for ground-truth annotations [69,70]. Domain adaptation methods typically rely on 
pretraining on synthetic data followed by ﬁne-tuning in the target domain, or design do-
main-invariant feature extractors to reduce the gap between source and target distribu-
tions [71–73]. Methods such as AdaStereo have made notable progress through output-
space self-supervision and feature-space adversarial learning. Nevertheless, the diversity

of agricultural environments—including variations in crop types, growth stages, illumi-
nation, and weather conditions—makes it diﬃcult for any single adaptation strategy to 
achieve comprehensive generalization. 
In recent years, the emergence of vision foundation models has provided new oppor-
tunities for improving stereo matching generalization. Foundation Stereo, proposed by 
NVIDIA in 2025, is pretrained on a large-scale synthetic dataset containing one million 
high-ﬁdelity stereo image pairs, aiming to achieve a strong zero-shot generalization capa-
bility. The model adopts a hybrid architecture, incorporating Depth Anything V2 as the 
core feature extractor to enhance geometric understanding, and introduces attention-
based cost aggregation along with ConvGRU-based iterative reﬁnement [74,75]. As noted 
in the review by Menolotto et al., vision foundation models demonstrate signiﬁcant po-
tential in agricultural vision tasks; however, model selection should follow a “task–cam-
era–keypoint alignment” principle rather than relying on a single model family [50]. In 
addition, limited cross-variety and cross-farm data remain a bottleneck. 
Lightweight design and edge deployment are essential for translating deep learning-
based stereo matching into practical agricultural applications. Agricultural machinery 
and UAV platforms are typically constrained by limited computational resources, power 
consumption, and thermal budgets, which further restrict model complexity. Wang et al. 
proposed a lightweight cattle body measurement method based on YOLOv8n-pose, 
achieving a 28.3 FPS inference speed on the Jetson Xavier NX platform [76]. A YOLO11-
SMMA model has been improved, reducing the parameter count to 1.58 M and achieving 
a runtime of 58.21 ms on embedded devices, thereby meeting real-time harvesting re-
quirements [16]. However, lightweight design often comes at the cost of reduced accuracy, 
and achieving an optimal balance between computational eﬃciency and perceptual per-
formance remains an open problem. For computation-intensive tasks such as stereo 
matching, model compression and eﬃcient architecture design are key research directions, 
but the high complexity of agricultural environments imposes strict requirements on ro-
bustness; excessive model simpliﬁcation may lead to severe performance degradation un-
der challenging conditions [77–79]. 
2.4. Agriculture-Speciﬁc Challenges and Adaptation Strategies 
Agricultural open-ﬁeld environments diﬀer fundamentally from structured indoor 
scenes and standardized autonomous driving roads. Multiple adverse factors coexist, 
forming major barriers to deploying stereo vision systems from laboratory settings to real 
farmland [80]. These challenges can be analyzed from ﬁve dimensions: illumination, tex-
ture, dynamics, occlusion, and environmental interference. 
Strong illumination and rapid lighting variations represent one of the most promi-
nent challenges. Field illumination can change by orders of magnitude within short peri-
ods, and specular reﬂections as well as shadows caused by direct sunlight signiﬁcantly 
disrupt stereo matching cost computation. Zou et al. explicitly reported that strong sun-
light exposure on wheat ears and camera sensors signiﬁcantly degrades the depth accu-
racy of the ZED2 system [6]. Zhang et al. further conﬁrmed in UAV-based spraying ex-
periments that outdoor illumination variability directly aﬀects the stability of stereo rang-
ing [31]. Mitigation strategies include hardware-level solutions such as high dynamic 
range (HDR) cameras, polarization ﬁlters, and active illumination, as well as algorithmic 
approaches based on data augmentation to improve robustness to lighting variations. 
Repetitive and low-texture surfaces are inherent characteristics of crop scenes. Most 
crop leaves exhibit smooth surfaces with limited distinctive features, making them among 
the most challenging cases for stereo matching. Yang et al. observed that low-texture re-
gions in leafy vegetables led to frequent matching failures in traditional algorithms, while 
Zou et al. reported increased ambiguity in wheat due to similar textures between ears and 

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7957 
10 of 34 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
leaves [6,32]. Deep learning methods can partially alleviate this issue by learning high-
level semantic representations; however, they depend heavily on large-scale annotated 
datasets, which remain scarce in agricultural domains. 
Dynamic disturbances, including wind-induced leaf motion and platform vibration, 
further degrade performance. Zhang et al. experimentally showed that increasing UAV 
ﬂight speed from 2 m/s to 6 m/s raises the relative error in regular object volume estima-
tion from 2.26% to 7.8%, while the coeﬃcient of variation increases from below 1% to over 
3%, clearly revealing a nonlinear relationship between motion conditions and measure-
ment accuracy [31]. Motion-related disturbances can be reduced at the hardware level 
through stabilized camera mounting, gimbal systems, and appropriate image-acquisition 
settings [81]. At the algorithmic level, temporal information from adjacent frames and mo-
tion-aware deblurring methods can be used to improve image consistency under camera 
or object motion [82]. 
Occlusion is particularly severe in dense planting systems, where overlapping leaves 
and stems result in large regions visible only from a single viewpoint, preventing reliable 
stereo correspondence. Menolotto et al. identiﬁed occlusion as a major bottleneck in multi-
object and multi-animal pose estimation tasks [50]. Common strategies for occlusion han-
dling include multi-view fusion to increase observational coverage, temporal modeling to 
exploit growth continuity, and learning-based approaches that explicitly model occlusion 
patterns to improve matching robustness [83]. Environmental interference includes con-
tamination from dust, dew, insects, and other factors aﬀecting either the camera lens or 
object appearance [84,85]. Zhang et al. noted that pesticide drift during UAV spraying 
may adhere to the camera lens and degrade image quality. In addition, temperature and 
humidity variations can aﬀect sensor performance and calibration stability [31]. 
From an algorithmic perspective, mitigation strategies can be summarized into four 
categories: data augmentation, which improves robustness through photometric and ge-
ometric transformations; multimodal fusion, which integrates RGB with depth, infrared, 
or thermal modalities to enhance resilience; temporal modeling, which leverages temporal 
consistency constraints in video sequences to reﬁne single-frame predictions; and domain 
adaptation with self-supervised learning, which reduces the discrepancy between train-
ing and deployment environments. 
Overall, the complexity of agricultural environments requires the systematic co-de-
sign of hardware conﬁguration, algorithm selection, and deployment strategies for stereo 
vision systems. As emphasized by Menolotto et al., vision system design should be guided 
by the underlying biological or agricultural problem, determining camera geometry, key 
point deﬁnition, and model selection accordingly, rather than merely pursuing the most 
advanced sensor technologies [50]. As consistently highlighted in the literature, although 
binocular stereo vision achieves high accuracy in agricultural localization, measurement, 
and recognition tasks, it still faces challenges such as model complexity, limited scene 
generalization, insuﬃcient datasets, and the absence of standardized evaluation protocols. 
Future development therefore requires not only continued algorithmic improvement but 
also parallel advances in dataset construction and evaluation frameworks. This represents 
a system-level engineering challenge rather than a problem that can be resolved through 
isolated technical breakthroughs. 
3. Binocular Stereo Vision–Driven Intelligent Applications  
in Agriculture 
Binocular stereo vision has enabled a wide range of intelligent agricultural applica-
tions due to its ability to acquire three-dimensional spatial information at relatively low 
hardware cost [86–89]. As highlighted in the systematic review by Menolotto et al., stereo

11 of 34 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
vision, RGB-D, and multi-view systems are particularly suitable for tasks requiring abso-
lute geometric accuracy, occlusion handling, or 3D pose reconstruction [50]. Following a 
hierarchical “crop–environment–equipment–livestock” framework, this section reviews 
key application scenarios, including high-throughput crop phenotyping, fruit localization 
and harvesting, weed detection and precision spraying, autonomous navigation of agri-

3.1. High-Throughput Crop 3D Phenotyping and Growth Monitoring 
Crop phenotypic information constitutes a fundamental data basis for high-through-
put breeding and precision crop management. Traditional manual measurements are sub-
jective and low-throughput, making them insuﬃcient to meet the demand for large-scale 
and high-frequency phenotypic data acquisition in modern breeding programs [93–95]. 
Owing to its non-contact operation, high eﬃciency, and capability for 3D reconstruction, 
binocular stereo vision has become an important tool for crop phenotyping. 
In leafy vegetable phenotyping, Yang et al. proposed a stereo matching network 
named LVStereo, supervised by 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS), for measuring plant height, 
leaf number, and leaf area in crops such as lettuce [32]. In this study, multi-view images 
were rendered into stereo training data using 3DGS, resulting in a leafy vegetable dataset 
containing 8060 stereo image pairs. A multi-scale deformable convolution-based feature 
extraction network was further designed. Experimental results show that LVStereo 
achieved an endpoint error of 2.32 pixels over the full image and 0.91 pixels within leafy 
regions. For phenotypic parameter estimation, the coeﬃcients of determination (R2) be-
tween predicted and manually measured values reached 0.9946 for plant height, 0.9577 
for leaf number, and 0.9775 for leaf area, respectively [96–98]. 
Ma et al. developed a track-driven greenhouse high-throughput phenotyping plat-
form and employed a multi-view stereo vision approach to acquire time-series point 
clouds of potted lettuce [99]. Through temporal point cloud registration, individual plant 
segmentation was achieved, with a vegetation-to-pot segmentation accuracy of 98.81%. 
The estimated plant height and canopy width achieved mean R2 values of 0.79 and 0.60, 
respectively, when compared with ground-truth measurements. Nevertheless, conven-
tional multi-view stereo pipelines fail to handle motion blur and inconsistent illumination 
in actual greenhouse acquisition. To address this limitation, Yang et al. designed LV-3DGS, 
a blur-robust 3D Gaussian splatting reconstruction framework, whose detailed workﬂow

Overall, the application of binocular stereo vision in crop phenotyping is evolving 
from single-instance static measurements toward time-series dynamic monitoring, and 
from single morphological descriptors toward multi-organ and multimodal integrated 
analysis [102–104]. However, three major challenges remain. First, the inﬂuence of illumi-

to biologically meaningful phenotypic traits still rely heavily on hand-crafted rules. Third, 
the generalization capability of models across cultivars and growth stages requires sys-

3.2. Fruit Stereo Localization and Autonomous Harvesting in Unstructured Environments 
Fruit harvesting is one of the most labor-intensive and automation-demanding pro-
cesses in agricultural production. Binocular stereo vision provides 3D spatial coordinates 
of fruits, enabling robotic systems to establish a complete perception loop of “perception–
localization–grasping [105,106]”. Unlike 2D detection, which only provides pixel-level lo-
calization, stereo vision enables depth perception, allowing robots to accurately plan 
grasping trajectories in unstructured orchard environments. In camellia fruit harvesting, 
Zhou et al. combined the YOLOv8x object detection network with binocular stereo vision 
to achieve 3D localization of picking points [17]. A knowledge transfer strategy was 
adopted, where the model was pretrained on an apple dataset and then transferred to 
camellia fruit detection via few-shot learning. With only 1012 annotated samples, 
YOLOv8x achieved an mAP50 of 0.96. The 3D coordinate estimation error was below 2.1 
cm along all three axes. Building on this, Wu et al. proposed a lightweight YOLO11-
SMMA model, reducing the parameter size to 1.58 M [16]. On an embedded platform, the 
inference time was 58.21 ms, and the average localization errors along the X, Y, and Z axes 
were 1.70 mm, 1.34 mm, and 1.27 mm, respectively. Integrated with a six-degree-of-free-
dom robotic arm, the system enabled vision-based servo control for automated camellia 
fruit harvesting, demonstrating the engineering feasibility of lightweight stereo vision so-
lutions in agricultural robotics [107–109]. 
For kiwifruit harvesting scenarios characterized by dense fruit clusters and severe 
branch occlusion, stereo vision combined with deep learning-based matching networks 
has been used to improve the 3D localization of calyx or fruit positions. To further enhance 
detection and pose estimation under occlusion, multi-view vision information has been 
fused with LiDAR point clouds. These methods typically use stereo disparity maps or

14 of 34 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
depth maps to guide noise suppression in LiDAR point clouds, eﬀectively removing spu-
rious points caused by occlusion from branches and leaves. As a result, they signiﬁcantly 
improve detection accuracy for occluded fruits. Experiments in real orchard environments 
show that such multi-sensor fusion approaches outperform conventional pipelines in both 
localization accuracy and pose estimation while also substantially the reducing ﬁltering 
time. 
Overall, research on fruit localization and autonomous harvesting exhibits three clear 
trends: a shift from 2D detection to 3D localization, from heavy models to lightweight 
architectures, and from single-sensor systems to multimodal fusion perception [110,111]. 
Nevertheless, harvesting success rates remain constrained by three core challenges: accu-
rate reconstruction of fruit geometry under severe occlusion, stability of depth estimation 
under varying illumination conditions, and end-to-end latency control from perception to 
grasping. Addressing these challenges requires improvements in stereo matching robust-
ness under occlusion and illumination changes, as well as further acceleration of vision–
control closed-loop systems. 
3.3. Stereo-Based Weed Recognition and Precision Site-Speciﬁc Spraying 
Weed competition is a major factor contributing to crop yield loss, potentially reduc-
ing yields by up to 34% if not properly managed [112–114]. Conventional uniform herbi-
cide application is not only ineﬃcient in chemical usage, but also leads to severe environ-
mental pollution and herbicide resistance. Binocular stereo vision provides a key percep-
tion foundation for precise site-speciﬁc spraying by enabling 3D discrimination between 
crops and weeds [115,116]. 
From a technical perspective, 3D perception in weed management is mainly utilized 
in two ways. First, it enhances crop–weed separation by leveraging spatial structure: crops 
typically exhibit regular row patterns, whereas weeds are randomly distributed [117,118]. 
Depth and spatial positioning information provided by stereo vision signiﬁcantly im-
proves this distinction. Second, 3D reconstruction enables the accurate spatial localization 
of weeds, guiding variable-rate sprayers or laser-based weeding systems for targeted re-
moval. 
In precision spraying systems, a WEED-YOLOv10-based targeted spraying platform 
has been validated in maize ﬁelds. The system performs real-time weed localization and 
controls spraying units for precise application. Field experiments show a targeting accu-
racy of 93.7%, spray coverage of 90.5%, a targeting deviation of 1.45 cm, and a detection 
speed of 20.1 cm/s. The variable-rate application system reduces overall application error 
to within 3% and achieves pesticide savings of 40–70%. 
For in-row weed recognition in robotic weeding systems, binocular stereo vision is 
used to distinguish weeds from crop plants based on 3D spatial information. A stereo 
vision-based in-row weed identiﬁcation method has been proposed for the eﬀective de-
tection of early-stage maize weeds. In orchard environments, a single-row tracking robot 
integrating stereo vision and LiDAR has also been developed, where deep learning-driven 
3D perception triggers lateral weeding mechanisms, improving operational eﬀectiveness 
in orchard inter-row management. 
At a broader level, 3D sensing technologies are reshaping precision weed manage-
ment. Existing reviews indicate that LiDAR–RGB fusion provides the most reliable out-
door 3D weed detection performance in dense canopies, while the combination of SfM 
and MVS achieves the lowest reconstruction error in dense planting scenarios [119,120]. 
However, these studies also highlight a critical limitation: model generalization across 
ﬁelds remains weak due to variations in crop species, soil conditions, canopy occlusion, 
and weed growth stages. Limited labeled datasets and high computational costs further

15 of 34 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
restrict ﬁeld deployment. This limitation equally applies to binocular stereo vision sys-
tems in weed management, where a single model struggles to generalize across diverse 
crops, growth stages, and ecological regions. 
Overall, stereo-based weed identiﬁcation and precision site-speciﬁc spraying have 
progressed from proof-of-concept studies to ﬁeld-scale validation, establishing an initial 
technical foundation for practical deployment. However, large-scale commercialization 
remains limited by several key bottlenecks. First, the 3D discrimination accuracy between 
crops and weeds is still insuﬃcient for precision weeding in high-value crops. Second, the 
trade-oﬀ between real-time processing capability and ﬁeld operation speed has not been 
fully resolved. Third, model generalization across regions and seasons remains inade-
quate and requires systematic improvement. 
3.4. Vision-Based Navigation of Agricultural Machinery and 3D Evaluation of  
Operational Quality 
Autonomous navigation of agricultural machinery is a core component of intelligent 
agriculture and directly aﬀects operational accuracy, eﬃciency, and safety. Traditional 
navigation systems primarily rely on Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) [121]. 
However, in high-density environments such as orchards, signal degradation and multi-
path eﬀects can severely reduce GNSS reliability. As a self-contained perception modality, 
binocular stereo vision provides an important complement for continuous navigation in 
GNSS-denied environments. 
In orchard sprayer navigation, researchers have developed deep learning-based ste-
reo vision navigation systems that integrate object detection models such as EﬃcientDet 
or YOLOv7 with stereo localization. These systems provide inter-row positioning and 
navigation guidance for air-assisted orchard sprayers. In ﬁeld tests conducted on a four-
row orchard road, the EﬃcientDet-based navigation algorithm achieved minimum aver-
age RMSE values of 0.24 m, 0.20 m, and 0.31 m at travel speeds of 1, 1.5, and 2 km/h, 
respectively. The system also integrates RGB-D-based collision avoidance modules, 
demonstrating operational safety under real-world conditions. 
For autonomous navigation of tractor–trailer systems, a two-stage stereo vision-
based 3D obstacle detection framework combined with Frenet-optimal trajectory plan-
ning has been proposed [122–124]. The system extracts 3D obstacle information from ste-
reo vision and performs trajectory optimization to enable autonomous path tracking of 
the trailer system. In rice transplanter navigation, a hybrid system integrating machine 
vision and GNSS has been developed for boundary detection and positioning, enabling 
continuous autonomous operation and automatic headland turning. 
In terms of operational quality assessment, binocular stereo vision has also been ap-
plied to real-time canopy volume estimation in spraying operations. Zhang et al. mounted 
a ZED 2 stereo camera beneath a plant protection UAV to perform online estimation of 
fruit tree canopy volume during ﬂight [31]. By combining canopy segmentation and sub-
grid-based volume computation, the system enables real-time canopy volume extraction. 
At a ﬂight speed of 2 m/s, the system-maintained volume estimation errors within 10% 
for regular targets, providing real-time decision support for variable-rate spraying. Fur-
ther analysis showed that when ﬂight speed increased from 2 m/s to 6 m/s, both measure-
ment error and variability increased signiﬁcantly, with the coeﬃcient of variation rising 
from below 1% to over 3%, revealing a nonlinear relationship between dynamic condi-
tions and 3D measurement accuracy. 
Overall, vision-based navigation of agricultural machinery is evolving from single-
row detection toward multi-task, multi-sensor integrated perception systems. Binocular 
stereo vision is shifting from an auxiliary positioning role to a core sensing component. 
Nevertheless, a gap remains between current navigation accuracy (decimeter-level) and

the requirements of precision agricultural operations (centimeter-level). In addition, sys-
tem reliability under adverse weather conditions such as rain, fog, and dust still requires 
further validation. Future research should focus on multi-source fusion navigation archi-
tectures integrating vision, inertial sensing, and GNSS, as well as robust perception algo-
rithms capable of operating under extreme illumination and weather conditions. 
3.5. Stereo-Based Livestock Phenotyping and 3D Behavioral and Body Condition Assessment 
Livestock body condition is a fundamental indicator in precision animal husbandry, 
directly inﬂuencing feeding strategies, reproductive management, and disease early 
warning [125,126]. Traditional manual scoring methods are subjective, labor-intensive, 
and unsuitable for large-scale, high-frequency monitoring. Binocular stereo vision and 3D 
point cloud reconstruction provide a non-contact solution for livestock body condition 
assessment. As noted by Menolotto et al., stereo vision, RGB-D, and multi-view systems 
are particularly valuable for tasks requiring absolute geometric accuracy, occlusion han-
dling, or 3D pose reconstruction, especially in body measurement and weight estimation 
[50]. 
In body weight estimation, binocular stereo vision has been applied to non-contact 
weighing of various livestock species. A non-contact yak body weight estimation method 
based on skeletal keypoint localization has been proposed [76]. YOLOv8 was used for yak 
detection and keypoint extraction (hip, ischial, shoulder, and hoof points), combined with 
stereo vision to compute body length, diagonal length, and withers height. Field data col-
lected from yaks on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau were used to train a Gaussian process re-
gression model, achieving a mean absolute error (MAE) of 25.4 kg and an R2 of 0.72 in 
cross-validation over 50 yaks. Based on this system, a Raspberry Pi-based intelligent in-
spection vehicle and a handheld device were further developed to enable real-time non-
contact weight estimation. 
For pig weight estimation, a LabVIEW-based binocular stereo vision system was ap-
plied to analyze body dimensions and live weight of 10 Landrace pigs aged 14 to 25 weeks 
[127]. Three-dimensional body measurements were extracted using stereo imaging, and 
regression models were established between body parameters and weight to enable non-
contact estimation. A review further compared image-based and point cloud-based ap-
proaches for pig weight estimation, highlighting diﬀerences in methodology and perfor-
mance. 
In cattle body condition scoring, computer vision models based on top-view images 
have been developed to automatically segment cattle bodies and detect keypoints associ-
ated with body condition assessment. Three-dimensional top-view imaging provides dis-
tinct advantages in estimating body condition score, growth development, and morpho-
logical traits. For sheep body condition evaluation, Cowie et al. proposed a video-based 
temperament prediction framework integrating computer vision, behavioral clustering, 
and machine learning [128]. Using arena test videos from 63 lactating ewes, kinematic 
features were extracted, and a random forest model achieved a classiﬁcation accuracy of 
77%. For sheep behavior recognition, Xue et al. proposed a DMS-Net dynamic multi-scale 
multimodal fusion network that integrates visual and skeletal modalities to classify stand-
ing, lying, kneeling, and sitting postures [129]. The model achieved an accuracy of 98.89% 
under complex housing conditions. 
In the ﬁeld of pose estimation and behavioral analysis, the review by Menolotto et al. 
systematically examined 114 vision-based pose estimation studies [50]. The authors re-
ported that side-view RGB imaging is most suitable for gait and lameness analysis, 
whereas top-view RGB conﬁgurations provide the best overall performance for group be-
havior monitoring, feeding analysis, tracking, and identity recognition tasks. However, 
the review also highlights several critical limitations that have constrained progress in this

from an auxiliary positioning tool to a core perception modality. In livestock monitoring, 
non-contact body condition assessment has demonstrated clear application value, yet 
large-scale commercial deployment remains limited. 
4. Datasets, Benchmarks, and Major Challenges 
High-quality datasets and standardized evaluation benchmarks are fundamental in-
frastructure for continuous progress in stereo vision algorithms. In the ﬁeld of autono-
mous driving, benchmark datasets such as KITTI, Scene Flow, and Middlebury have di-
rectly driven the paradigm shift from traditional stereo methods to deep learning-based 
approaches. In contrast, agricultural stereo vision research has long lacked comparable 
data infrastructure, which has become a key bottleneck limiting ﬁeld development 
[130,131]. Even when data are available, challenges such as illumination variability in 
open environments, real-time constraints, multimodal fusion requirements, and the inter-
pretability of safety-critical systems continue to hinder the translation from algorithmic 
research to ﬁeld deployment. This section therefore analyzes these challenges in a struc-
tured manner, starting from data foundations. 
4.1. Public Agricultural Stereo/RGB-D Datasets and Standardized Benchmarks 
The development of agricultural stereo vision datasets has consistently lagged be-
hind algorithmic advances, primarily due to several structural constraints [132–134]. Un-
like autonomous driving datasets, which can be collected continuously from vehicles in 
motion, agricultural data acquisition is constrained by crop growth cycles, which typically 
span several months or even a full year; missing a collection window often requires wait-
ing for the next season. Annotation is also signiﬁcantly more expensive: precise delinea-
tion of plant organs is far more time-consuming than bounding-box annotation for vehi-
cles or pedestrians, while obtaining ground-truth disparity at sub-pixel accuracy is prac-
tically infeasible through manual labeling. Together, these factors result in limited scale, 
diversity, and annotation precision in agricultural stereo datasets. 
Recently, researchers have begun to systematically address this gap. The PlantStereo 
dataset, which contains 812 stereo image pairs covering spinach, tomato, pepper, and 
pumpkin, has been proposed [36]. Ground-truth disparities were generated using a semi-
automatic pipeline based on depth images, enabling relatively high annotation accuracy. 
However, the dataset size remains limited for deep network training. In comparison, the 
Scene Flow dataset in autonomous driving contains more than 39,000 synthetic stereo 
pairs.

4.2. Robustness Under Variable Illumination and Adverse Weather Conditions 
Illumination conditions in agricultural environments exhibit an extremely wide dy-
namic range, spanning from tens of thousands of lux under direct midday sunlight to only 
a few hundred lux under overcast conditions. Such variations can occur within short time 
intervals, resulting in changes of up to two orders of magnitude in lighting intensity. 
Overexposed regions lead to the loss of texture information, while shadowed regions sig-
niﬁcantly reduce the discriminative power of matching features. In addition, changes in 
illumination direction alter the specular reﬂection characteristics of leaf surfaces, causing 
the same object to exhibit substantially diﬀerent appearances across time and viewpoints. 
Zou et al. explicitly reported a signiﬁcant degradation in depth estimation accuracy 
of the ZED 2 camera under strong sunlight in ﬁeld wheat experiments [6]. Similarly, Yang 
et al. observed that even under controlled greenhouse conditions, illumination variations 
could lead to a large number of matching failures in traditional stereo matching algo-
rithms [32]. To tackle the image blur problem induced by unstable lighting and motion 
during ﬁeld data acquisition, we designed a dedicated deblurring module, and its quali-
tative comparison results are illustrated in Figure 3. 
 
Figure 3. The visualization results of the deblurring module [32]. 
Adverse weather conditions further exacerbate illumination-related challenges. In 
rainy conditions, water droplets adhering to the camera lens can obstruct the ﬁeld of view 
and introduce refractive distortions [137–139]. In foggy environments, aerosol scattering 
leads to a signiﬁcant reduction in image contrast. In dusty conditions, particulate contam-
ination on the lens alters the light propagation path and degrades image quality. However, 
existing agricultural stereo datasets rarely include such extreme conditions, resulting in a 
lack of systematic evaluation of model performance under adverse weather. 
From an algorithmic perspective, current mitigation strategies mainly focus on three 
directions. First, data augmentation simulates diverse illumination conditions by apply-
ing transformations in brightness, contrast, and related photometric properties to training 
samples. Second, domain adaptation methods aim to reduce feature distribution discrep-
ancies across illumination conditions using adversarial training or self-supervised learn-
ing. Third, multimodal fusion combines RGB with infrared or thermal imaging, leverag-
ing their complementary responses to illumination variations to improve robustness. 
Pham et al. proposed the CattleFever system, which integrates RGB and thermal imaging 
for facial key point localization in cattle [103]. The system achieved an average precision 
(AP) of 94.37% using RGB images and 73.16% using thermal images, demonstrating the 
inherent robustness of thermal imaging to illumination changes. Nevertheless, each of 
these strategies has inherent limitations. Data augmentation cannot fully cover the space

4.3. Real-Time Constraints and Edge Computing Adaptation 
The deployment platforms of agricultural stereo vision systems—such as ﬁeld robots, 
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and vehicle-mounted devices—are typically subject to 
stringent computational resource constraints [140]. Compared with data-center-level 
GPUs, edge devices oﬀer signiﬁcantly lower computational power, limited energy budg-
ets, and restricted thermal dissipation capability. Stereo matching, however, is one of the 
most computationally intensive tasks in computer vision.

4.4. Multimodal Fusion and Synthetic Data-Driven Augmentation 
The limitations of single RGB modality in agricultural scenarios are becoming in-
creasingly evident. Variations in illumination, lack of texture, and severe occlusion make 

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7957 
23 of 34 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
it diﬃcult for pure vision-based approaches to maintain stable performance under all con-
ditions. Multimodal fusion and synthetic data-driven augmentation are widely regarded 
as two complementary strategies to address these limitations. 
In multimodal fusion, RGB-D cameras simultaneously provide color images and 
depth information, enabling algorithms to exploit both appearance and geometric cues. 
As noted by Menolotto et al., RGB-D, stereo vision, and multi-view systems are particu-
larly advantageous for tasks requiring absolute geometric accuracy, occlusion handling, 
or 3D pose reconstruction [50]. Xue et al. proposed DMS-Net, a vision–skeleton multi-
modal fusion framework for recognizing four sheep postures, achieving an accuracy of 
98.89% in complex housing environments [129]. More advanced fusion strategies include 
the integration of RGB with LiDAR, thermal imaging, or multispectral/hyperspectral data. 
However, multimodal fusion introduces signiﬁcant system-level engineering chal-
lenges, particularly in spatial calibration and temporal synchronization between sensors. 
Even millimeter-level calibration errors can lead to substantial deviations in 3D geometric 
reconstruction. In addition, the increased hardware cost and system complexity cannot be 
neglected, and sensor conﬁguration must be carefully balanced between information gain 
and implementation cost. 
Synthetic data provide an alternative solution to the scarcity of agricultural datasets. 
Yang et al. generated stereo training data using 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) from multi-
view images, constructing a leafy vegetable dataset containing 8060 stereo image pairs 
[32]. Niu et al. adopted a 2D Gaussian splatting (2DGS)-based approach for maize pheno-
typing, while Lin et al. developed the UE5-Forest dataset entirely within the Unreal En-
gine 5 simulation environment [35]. The key advantage of synthetic data lies in fully au-
tomated, zero-cost annotation, enabling the simultaneous generation of disparity, depth, 
surface normals, and semantic segmentation labels. However, the primary limitation is 
the domain gap between synthetic and real-world data, which must be mitigated through 
domain adaptation techniques. 
4.5. Explainability and Safety-Critical System Design 
As stereo vision systems transition from laboratory research to real-world agricul-
tural deployment, their role shifts from analytical tools to decision-making components, 
directly controlling robotic arms for harvesting, spraying nozzles, or autonomous agricul-
tural machinery. This transition makes system explainability and safety unavoidable en-
gineering concerns [141–143]. 
From the perspective of interpretability, the black-box nature of deep learning mod-
els is particularly problematic in agricultural applications [144,145]. When a stereo match-
ing network produces erroneous disparity estimates that lead to failed robotic grasping 
in orchards, it is necessary to trace the underlying source of failure. However, explaina-
bility research in stereo matching remains signiﬁcantly less developed than in image clas-
siﬁcation and object detection. Methods such as Grad-CAM provide limited interpretabil-
ity for dense prediction tasks. As noted by Menolotto et al., interpretability in pose esti-
mation is mainly achieved through skeleton overlay visualization, feature importance 
analysis, and 3D evidence representation; however, truly post hoc explainable AI remains 
limited, and explicit user-oriented XAI solutions are still the exception rather than the 
norm [50]. 
To mitigate the “black-box” nature of deep learning models in complex orchard en-
vironments, enhancing the visual interpretability of fruit feature perception through vis-
ualization techniques is essential. It has been observed that lightweight detection models 
often fail to focus accurately on target fruits under dense occlusion and background inter-
ference, leading to semantic drift in feature extraction. Representative detection visuali-

From the perspective of safety-critical system design, the deployment of agricultural 
stereo vision systems presents unique challenges. Unlike autonomous driving, agricul-
tural scenarios lack standardized safety regulations and technical protocols. The conse-
quences of vision system failure in harvesting robots diﬀer substantially from those in 
autonomous navigation machines, necessitating dedicated redundancy designs and fail-
safe mechanisms [146–148]. At present, safety design in agricultural stereo vision systems 
remains at an early stage, with most studies focusing on algorithmic accuracy while in-
suﬃcient attention is paid to system-level reliability and robustness. Future work should 
establish safety standards and testing protocols tailored to agricultural robotic vision sys-
tems, covering key aspects such as sensor fault detection, uncertainty quantiﬁcation in 
algorithms, and decision conﬁdence estimation. As emphasized by Menolotto et al., de-
ployment-oriented studies should go beyond reporting nominal frame rates and instead 
include critical information such as calibration burden, environmental robustness, 
runtime performance, edge hardware constraints, and integration with farm management 
systems [50]. 
Overall, agricultural stereo vision is undergoing a critical transition from an “algo-
rithm-driven” paradigm toward a “data–algorithm–system co-driven” framework. In 
terms of datasets and benchmarks, resources such as PlantStereo and AppleGrowthVision 
have partially ﬁlled the existing gaps; however, dataset scale, diversity, and standardiza-
tion remain insuﬃcient. Establishing a benchmark comparable to KITTI in autonomous 
driving remains an open challenge. In terms of illumination robustness, data augmenta-
tion, domain adaptation, and multimodal fusion provide partial solutions, but no method 
can reliably handle all illumination conditions. In terms of real-time performance, a sig-
niﬁcant gap remains between edge-device inference speed and real-world operational re-
quirements. For multimodal fusion, combinations such as RGB-D, RGB-LiDAR, and RGB-
thermal imaging have demonstrated performance gains, but at the cost of increased sys-
tem complexity and hardware overhead. In terms of explainability and safety, research is 
still in its early stages [149,150].

5. Challenges and Future Perspectives 
5.1. Key Findings Across Application Domains 
Based on a systematic review of binocular stereo vision fundamentals, algorithmic 
evolution, and agricultural applications, four key ﬁndings can be summarized. 
At the technical level, traditional stereo matching methods perform adequately in 
structured greenhouse environments but degrade signiﬁcantly in open-ﬁeld scenarios 
characterized by weak textures, strong illumination variations, and severe occlusions. In 
wheat density estimation, Zou et al. reported endpoint errors as high as 24.74 pixels using 
semi-global matching methods. In contrast, deep learning-based approaches have funda-
mentally improved performance. For example, Yang et al. achieved sub-pixel accuracy in 
leafy vegetable phenotyping using the 3DGS-supervised LVStereo network, reducing 
endpoint error in leaf regions to 0.91 pixels. This gap clearly demonstrates the superiority 
of data-driven methods in complex biological structures. 
At the data infrastructure level, agricultural stereo vision suﬀers from persistent data 
scarcity. PlantStereo contains only 812 stereo pairs, whereas Scene Flow in autonomous 
driving includes more than 39,000 pairs. The release of AppleGrowthVision (9317 stereo 

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 7957 
26 of 34 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
images across six growth stages and 31,084 annotated apple instances) represents a signif-
icant milestone in orchard-scale dataset construction. UE5-Forest exempliﬁes the frontier 
of synthetic data generation, producing 5520 stereo image pairs with pixel-perfect dispar-
ity in Unreal Engine 5. However, as highlighted by Menolotto et al. in a review of 114 
studies, inconsistent landmark deﬁnitions, fragmented datasets, and heterogeneous eval-
uation metrics remain major barriers to progress. 
At the application level, stereo vision has been widely adopted in crop phenotyping, 
fruit harvesting, weed control, agricultural machinery navigation, and livestock monitor-
ing. Each application imposes distinct requirements: phenotyping demands sub-millime-
ter accuracy and temporal consistency; harvesting emphasizes real-time and lightweight 
computation; weed management requires strong cross-domain generalization; navigation 
demands robustness; and livestock monitoring involves non-rigid object modeling. As 
emphasized by Menolotto et al., side-view RGB is most suitable for gait analysis, top-view 
RGB performs best for group behavior analysis, while stereo vision and multi-view sys-
tems are preferred when absolute geometric accuracy or 3D pose recovery is required. 
This highlights a key principle: performance depends on task–camera–keypoint align-
ment rather than reliance on a single model family. 
At the challenge level, signiﬁcant gaps remain between current systems and large-
scale deployment. No existing method can fully handle illumination variability. Although 
recent edge systems-on-chip have substantially improved computational capability, real-
time performance for computationally intensive stereo-matching networks remains de-
pendent on the speciﬁc hardware platform, model complexity, input resolution, and 
power conﬁguration. For example, a representative state-of-the-art stereo network 
achieved approximately 6.99 FPS on the Jetson Orin Nano Super. More powerful plat-
forms, such as the Jetson AGX Orin and Jetson Thor, provide greater computational ca-
pacity, but they also involve trade-oﬀs in power consumption, cost, and thermal dissipa-
tion. Multimodal fusion introduces additional calibration and system complexity, while 
research on explainability and safety is still in its infancy. 
5.2. Practical Roadmap from Research Prototypes to Deployment 
Based on these ﬁndings, a staged roadmap is proposed for transitioning agricultural 
stereo vision from research prototypes to real-world deployment. 
Stage I (0–3 years): Standardization and infrastructure development. 
The most urgent task is to establish standardized benchmarks for agricultural sub-
domains. As demonstrated by AppleGrowthVision, benchmark datasets can signiﬁcantly 
improve model performance (e.g., +7.69% F1-score for YOLOv8 and +31.06% for Faster R-
convolutional neural network). Future eﬀorts should focus on constructing benchmarks 
for orchard fruit detection, leafy vegetable phenotyping, and ﬁeld crop height estimation. 
Synthetic data pipelines such as UE5-Forest provide a scalable solution when real data are 
diﬃcult to acquire. Standardized reporting guidelines should also be established, includ-
ing calibration eﬀort, robustness evaluation, runtime, edge-device performance, and sys-
tem integration metrics. 
Stage II (3–5 years): Domain adaptation and lightweight modeling. 
Foundation Stereo demonstrates strong zero-shot generalization through large-scale 
pretraining on one million stereo pairs. Similarly, feature-adaptive strategies leveraging 
monocular priors have shown promise in reducing the sim-to-real gap. These approaches 
are particularly critical for agricultural scenarios. Future work should focus on domain-
adaptive ﬁne-tuning of foundation models for agriculture, combined with pruning, 
knowledge distillation, and quantization to enable real-time edge inference. 
Stage III (5–10 years): System integration and commercial validation.

https://doi.org/10.3390/app16167957 
Once accuracy and real-time requirements are satisﬁed, the primary challenge shifts 
to system-level integration, including closed-loop perception–control systems, hardware-
level multi-sensor synchronization, and integration with farm management platforms. 
End-to-end frameworks proposed by Ni et al. and Cowie et al. provide early examples of 
such systems. The next step is large-scale deployment and economic validation in com-
mercial farming environments. 
5.3. Perspective: Towards Fully Autonomous Self-Supervised Agricultural Perception Systems 
The following discussion presents our perspective on the long-term evolution of ag-
ricultural stereo vision toward fully autonomous, self-supervised systems. While 
grounded in the trends and gaps identiﬁed in the preceding sections, the proposals and 
projections made here are forward-looking and reﬂect our own research outlook, rather 
than a consolidated summary of existing experimental evidence. 
The ultimate goal of agricultural stereo vision is to develop fully autonomous, self-
supervised perception systems capable of continuous learning, adaptation, and perfor-
mance optimization without human intervention. 
Self-supervised learning is central to this vision. By exploiting stereo left–right con-
sistency, temporal continuity, and multi-view geometric constraints, supervision can be 
derived without manual annotations. This enables continuous learning from large-scale 
ﬁeld data across growth stages, weather conditions, and crop types. Methods based on 
3DGS-generated supervision and 2DGS-driven data synthesis have already demonstrated 
the feasibility of this direction. 
Vision foundation models represent another key pathway. The emergence of models 
such as Foundation Stereo suggests that large-scale pretraining can provide strong zero-
shot generalization capability. Future agricultural perception systems may no longer re-
quire retraining for each new scenario but instead adapt rapidly to new tasks and envi-
ronments. Synthetic environments such as UE5-Forest highlight cases where real-world 
depth acquisition is diﬃcult due to thin, overlapping structures that challenge LiDAR and 
structured light systems—precisely where synthetic data and foundation models oﬀer 
maximum value. 
Fully autonomous systems should further incorporate continual learning, automatic 
failure detection, adaptive recalibration, and cross-domain knowledge transfer. This re-
quires edge–cloud collaborative architectures, lifelong learning frameworks, and intuitive 
interfaces for non-expert users. 
Despite challenges such as extreme environmental diversity, long-tail distributions, 
and engineering reliability, recent progress—from hundreds to tens of thousands of stereo 
pairs, and from pixel-level errors to sub-pixel accuracy—demonstrates the feasibility of 
this trajectory. Ultimately, the future of agricultural stereo vision will not be determined 
by breakthroughs in individual algorithms, but by the co-evolution of data, algorithms, 
hardware, and system integration. When these components converge into a uniﬁed intel-
ligent system capable of perception, learning, and decision-making, stereo vision will 
evolve from a laboratory technique into a core productive force in modern agriculture.
