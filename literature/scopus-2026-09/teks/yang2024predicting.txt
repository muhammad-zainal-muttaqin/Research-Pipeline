A B S T R A C T  
Monitoring and modeling the growth of strawberries at the individual fruit level can open up new opportunities 
for yield prediction, fruit grading and supply chain optimization. However, existing strawberry growth models 
mainly focus on plot or plant level and can not simulate the growth of individual fruits, and existing computer 
vision (CV)-based studies primarily focus on instant tasks but lack the reasoning capabilities required for dy­
namic growth simulations. In this study, we developed a novel knowledge-guided CV framework, named KGCV- 
strawberry, to simulate the growth of strawberry fruits on an individual basis. We conducted two consecutive 
years of greenhouse experiments with intensive measurements to develop and test the framework. The KGCV- 
strawberry framework consists of two components: first, the fruit trait detector (acting as the “eye”) that in­
terprets bounding boxes and biophysical traits of individual fruits from raw images, and second, the fruit growth 
simulator (acting as the “brain”) that uses the estimated traits to predict fruit growth. We employed a hybrid 
training approach for KGCV-strawberry, where the fruit trait detector was trained by ground observations and 
the fruit growth simulator was trained by synthetic data generated by the S-shape fruit growth curves. The KGCV- 
strawberry is designed to be able to dynamically assimilate observations (e.g., image sequences) such that the 
fruit growth simulator infers growth curve parameters from fruit size sequences. We tested the KGCV-strawberry 
by ground fruit trait measurements, with a case study showing the RMSE of diameter estimation decreased by 74 
% as the sequence of observations expanded from 1 to 6. For the yield prediction task, we observed a reduction in 
the RMSE from 3.58 to 2.01 g and an increase in R2 from 0.25 to 0.73 as more images were assimilated into the 
framework. Additionally, the RMSE for predicting the remaining growing degree days (GDD) until maturity saw 
a significant reduction from 71.87 ◦C⋅day to 39.30 ◦C⋅day, accompanied by an increase in R2 from 0.11 to 0.61. 
Although the best prediction is achieved near maturity, the prediction accuracy is acceptable two weeks before 
fruit maturity. Additionally, we conducted a comparison between KGCV-Strawberry and a process-based model 
for predicting plant-level yields. KGCV-Strawberry exhibited superior performance in capturing yield dynamics 
for each harvest. These findings highlight the potential of applying this framework for precise management 
optimization of individual fruits in intelligent strawberry farming.   
1. Introduction 
Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa) is one of the most popular fruits in 
recent years. The production of fresh strawberries in the US was 1.33 
million tons in 2021 with a value of 3.42 billion dollars, making 
strawberry own the second largest noncitrus fruit market just behind 
grapes (NASS, 2022). Over the past few decades, the consumption of 
fresh strawberries in the US has more than quadrupled, increasing from 
2 lb per capita in 1980 to 8.5 lb in 2020 (Samtani et al., 2019; USDA, 
2021). However, meeting the growing consumer demand is a challenge 
due to the limited harvest window (e.g., June to July in the upper 
Midwest region) for traditional June-bearing strawberry cultivars. 
The appearance of day-neutral strawberry cultivars mitigated this 
situation by extending the fruiting season until fall (e.g., October in the 
upper Midwest region) (Petran et al., 2017). However, while day-neutral 
strawberries typically yield higher, the prolonged fruiting season also 
translates to increased maintenance and harvesting requirements, 
resulting in higher labor costs. With the swift advancement in computer 
vision (CV) and mechanical control technologies, labor costs may be 
greatly reduced by deploying the automated picking system (Khort

et al., 2019; Xiong et al., 2019). Such systems incorporate a robotized 
machine arm or gripper mounted on a track or vehicle. The onboard 
camera can capture the image of strawberries to assess their ripeness, 
and deep neural networks were used to guide the gripper to take action 
by detecting the bounding boxes or masks of the ripe fruits (Liming and 
Yanchao, 2010; Gao et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2019). Despite increased 
adoption of automation in the strawberry industry, most existing 
CV-based studies on strawberries primarily focus on instant tasks such as 
object detection (Hu et al., 2022), phenology detection (Wang et al., 
2022), diseases detection (Shin et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2020), or fruit 
volume estimation (Kirk et al., 2021). This is because the convolutional 
neural networks (CNN), which serve as the fundamental building blocks 
of CV algorithms, mimic human eye behavior in interpreting visual in­
formation but lack the reasoning capabilities required for dynamic crop 
growth models. Most of the previous studies overlooked the dynamic 
simulation of strawberry growth, which holds significant importance for 
predicting fruit-level yield and forecasting the optimal harvest time 
window. Given the short growth cycle and limited shelf life of straw­
berry fruits, timely forecasting of fruit condition, final yield, and 
maturity dates for individual fruits are crucial for optimizing manage­
ment and product distribution to maximize net profit (Hern´andez-Mar­
tínez et al., 2023). 
The origin of plant growth modeling dates back to 100 years ago 
when researchers tried to quantitatively describe plant growth via 
relative growth rate (RGR) (Blackman, 1919). Early crop models were 
developed by integrating soil models and canopy photosynthesis 
models, such as the early version of Wageningen crop models (de Wit, 
1978; Duncan et al., 1967). In the last 30 years, significant efforts have 
been made to simulate staple and cash crops, when the crop models, e.g., 
WOFOST (de Wit et al., 2019) and CERES (Timsina and Humphreys, 
2006), have evolved into more integrated so-called cropping system 
models, such as the DSSAT and APSIM (Jones et al., 2003; Holzworth 
et al., 2014). A cropping system model includes various crop modules 
but shares the same or similar modules for soil moisture and nutrient 
transfer. Additionally, models have been specifically designed for hor­
ticultural crops such as tomatoes and cucumbers under controlled 
environmental conditions (Jones et al., 1991; Marcelis et al., 1998; 
Katzin et al., 2022). However, there is a limited availability of models 
capable of simulating strawberry growth. For instance, Labadie et al. 
(2019) developed a model to simulate the phenology progress of 
strawberries; and Hopf et al. (2022) built a comprehensive process- 
based strawberry model, named CROPGRO-Strawberry, to simulate 
detailed processes such as leaf photosynthesis, carbon assimilation, and 
canopy size. Process-based strawberry models hold significant potential 
in simulating plant nutrient and water conditions, as well as providing 
in-season management advice to alleviate existing or potential stresses. 
Nevertheless, the conventional strawberry modeling framework en­
counters three challenges when it comes to achieving fully automated 
management and harvest planning: 1) lack of methods to simulate the 
growth of individual fruits; 2) lack of fruit-specific observations to 
initialize and constrain the simulations for individual fruits; and 3) lack 
of an efficient framework to use the vision information (i.e., image 
sequence) to improve the simulation in real-time. Consequently, there is 
a need to develop a framework that can address these three issues all at 
once, aiming to enhance the productivity and profitability of the 
strawberry industry by tracking and simulating the growth of millions of 
strawberry fruits. 
Knowledge-guided machine learning (KGML) is a promising path to 
fulfill the aforementioned needs by harnessing the strengths of both 
machine learning and dynamic models (Karpatne et al., 2022; Willard 
et al., 2022). Its strong feature learning capability (for detecting 
fruit-level traits from raw images), good interpretability (for simulating 
the fruit growth dynamic), and out-of-sample extrapolation ability 
(Shen et al., 2023), which positions it as a valuable tool for tackling the 
first two challenges outlined above. Its effectiveness and resilience have 
been demonstrated in various domains, such as hydrologic systems 
(Ghosh et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2022) and agroecosystems (Liu et al., 
2022). However, traditional methods rely on techniques such as 
network fine-tuning (Liu et al., 2022), domain adaptation (Ma et al., 
2021), and model parameter calibration (Zhou et al., 2021) to adapt the 
model to a new environment or a new crop type. These techniques need 
iterations to optimize the network weights or model parameters, which 
is computationally intensive and not suitable for large-scale fruit-level 
simulations. Recently, a KGML-based data assimilation framework 
developed by Yang et al. (2023) shed light on efficiently constraining 
model states using in-season and historical observations. The GPU-based 
tensor operation design makes this data assimilation framework more 
than thousands of times faster than the process-based model. However, 
previous applications of KGML predominantly rely on ground mea­
surements or derived variables (e.g., crop traits derived from remote 
sensing data) for model calibration or data assimilation, rather than 
directly incorporating raw image data. As a result, how to address the 
third challenge to provide a multi-neural networks-based framework for 
large-scale fruit-level simulation and decision-making is still an un­
solved issue. 
To disentangle these issues, we aim to establish an innovative 
knowledge-guided computer vision (KGCV)-based framework to 
dynamically simulate strawberry fruit growth using image sequences as 
input. The CV component of this framework serves as the “eye” to 
interpret image data, while the knowledge-guided component acts as the 
“brain” to analyze and reason the information provided by the CV 
component. Specifically, we employed a Faster-RCNN (Ren et al., 2015) 
network to detect the bounding box and main phenological stages of the 
strawberry fruits. Then a CNN module was used to estimate the fruit size 
and the percentile progress of the current phenological stage. To simu­
late fruit growth dynamics, we assumed the growth of strawberry fruit 
follows the pattern of the S-shape function (i.e., logistics/sigmoid 
function), and we trained a fruit growth network to learn possible 
growth trajectories of fruit traits under various scenarios. The parame­
ters of the fruit growth curve (i.e., one of the inputs of the fruit growth 
network) are directly inferred by a parameter learning network using 
the fruit trait sequence that is predicted by the “eye” of the KGCV- 
strawberry. The KGCV-strawberry leverages the superior visual inter­
pretation ability of CNN and the reasoning ability of KGML. The 
parameter-inference-based data assimilation scheme empowers the 
KGCV-strawberry to improve its prediction over time. Furthermore, the 
framework’s parameter inference design ensures its efficiency in con­
ducting large-scale simulations at the fruit level. 
2. Data and methodology 
2.1. Experiments setup 
Strawberry growth experiments were conducted at the Plant Growth 
Facility at the University of Minnesota Twin Cities from June to October 
2022 and 2023 (Fig. 1). A total of 149 pots of Cabrillo strawberry plants 
in 2022 and 180 pots of Albion strawberries were planted. We set up 
four levels of nitrogen (N) treatments, including 0 % N, 50 % N, 100 % 
N, and 150 % N, for 40 pots in 2022 and 60 pots in 2023 where the 100 
% N application treatment employed the conventional fertilizer rate 
based on the farmer’s experience (detailed in Table S1 in the Supple­
mentary material). The N treatments can increase the variation of plant 
traits which will facilitate model development. We water all the straw­
berries every three days to maintain the optimal soil moisture. For each 
N treatment, we harvested the ripened fruits every three days. Various 
data, such as images, microclimate data, and plant traits were collected 
during the experiments. Additionally, we collected images from outdoor 
strawberry fields on campus to enlarge our dataset for training the object 
detection neural network.

2.2. Climate data and plant traits measurement 
An Internet-of-thing (IoT) weather station (Folium, WayBeyond, NZ) 
was installed in the greenhouse to measure the air temperature, relative 
humidity, and downward shortwave radiation every 10 min. We 
calculated the growing degree day (GDD) to monitor the phenology 
progress of the strawberry fruits (McMaster and Wilhelm, 1997), 
GDD = (Tmax + Tmin)/2−Tbase
(1)  
where if Tmax < Tbase, then Tmax = Tbase; and if Tmin < Tbase, then Tmin =
Tbase. Similarly, if Tmax > Thigh, then Tmax = Thigh; and if Tmin > Thigh, then 
Tmin = Thigh. Tmax and Tmin refer to the maximum and minimum daily 
temperatures, respectively. Tbase and Thigh are two temperature thresh­
olds to determine GDD. The crops do not grow (i.e., GDD = 0) when it is 
below the base temperature (Tbase) due to cold stress, and the GDD re­
mains constant when the air temperature exceeds the upper- 
temperature threshold (Thigh) due to heat stress. In this study, the Thigh 
= 40 ◦C and the Tbase = 3 ◦C were used following Krüger et al. (2012). 
We conducted measurements of fruit characteristics, including 
length, diameter, and fresh weight, right after harvest. The fruits’ length 
and diameter (mm) were manually measured by a digital vernier caliper 
with 0.01 mm resolution. Furthermore, we measured fruit dry matter at 
three-day intervals throughout the season and the final aboveground 
biomass of the plants by subjecting samples to a 72-h drying process in 
an oven set at 65 ◦C. Tissue water content (%) was calculated as the 
moisture percentage of fresh weight. In each year, 20 fruits with five 
from each N treatment were randomly selected and tagged since their 
flowering stage to track the growth of individual fruits. For each tagged 
fruit, the fruit size was measured every three days and the corresponding 
photos were taken by camera. Once a tagged fruit was harvested, a new 
flower within the same N treatment would be randomly selected to 
continue the monitoring. Consequently, in experiment 2022, a sum of 
520 (i.e., 26 × 20) data points on fruit sizes were obtained, while in 
experiment 2023, 380 (i.e., 19 × 20) data points were collected. 
2.3. Image data collection 
To obtain images for fruit detection, we randomly selected potted 
greenhouse strawberries to capture images every three days, varying the 
focus lengths and lighting conditions for each capture. A total of 1477 
images (i.e., 1205 indoor images and 272 outdoor images) with more 
than 5000 strawberry objects were collected and labeled across different 
growth stages. For each fruit, the bounding box and development stage 
(including flowering, small green, green, white, turning red, red, and 
overripe) (Fig. 2) were manually labeled using the Labelme toolbox 
(https://github.com/wkentaro/labelme). 
For images of tagged fruits from the greenhouse experiment, each 
object was labeled with fruit diameter, fruit length, and decimal stage 
(DS) (Fig. 2b). Specifically, we divided each main fruit development 
stage into ten substages to build finer relationships between GDD and 
phenology. For example, the DS-1 in Fig. 2b means the fruit just 
completed the transition from the previous stage, and the DS-9 indicates 
the fruit is about to transit to the next stage.

2.4.1. Fruit trait detector 
Monitoring the ripeness and size of individual fruits is crucial for 
harvest scheduling as well as yield forecasts (Ilyas and Kim, 2021). 
Related works have been done by leveraging public datasets (Elhariri 
et al., 2023; Ilyas et al., 2023). However, the existing strawberry data­
sets may lack corresponding climate data, continuous fruit size mea­
surements for individual fruits, and sub-stage information demonstrated 
in Fig. 2b. The fruit trait detector is the eye of the KGCV-strawberry, 
which was trained on our exclusive dataset (Fig. 3). It includes a 
Faster-RCNN to detect the bounding box and main phenological stages 
of the strawberry fruits and a CNN to estimate the fruit size and the 
percentile progress of the current stage. Faster-RCNN is a state-of-the-art 
object detection network that is widely used in various applications (Ren 
et al., 2015). It is built on top of a CNN and uses a region proposal 
network (RPN) to generate potential bounding boxes of target objects in 
an image. The non-maximum suppression (NMS) technology was 
employed to suppress the overlapping bounding boxes. In practice we

found it is possible for the NMS to output several bounding boxes for a 
single object. This can occur when a strawberry undergoes a transition 
phase between two adjacent growth stages, or in cases where a straw­
berry is partially occluded. To tackle this issue, we first filtered out the 
NMS-produced bounding boxes with the classification confidence below 
0.5. In evaluation, we remove boxes that contain only partial object 
information if their Intersection over Union (IoU) with the corre­
sponding ground truth boxes is less than 0.5. Consequently, for each 
strawberry, we select the box with the highest confidence from the 
remaining boxes for model evaluation. 
Estimating fruit size solely based on the image patch cropped within 
the bounding box can be challenging due to the absence of references. 
However, it is still possible for the neural network to learn some abstract 
features from extra information for fruit size prediction (Ferrer-Ferrer 
et al., 2023). To include the surrounding reference substance (e.g., calyx 
stems, and leaves) for fruit size estimation, we used an extended 
bounding box (120 % for each side) to crop the original image. Because 
directly resizing the bounding box-cropped patches to a uniform size 
would result in image distortion, we opted to crop the image into a 
square patch that is 120 % of the length of the longest edge of the 
bounding box. These square patches, representing different objects, 
were subsequently resized to 256x256 dimensions (Fig. S1, the notation 
of S represents the Supplementary material). The cropped fruit objects 
were then fed into a DenseNet-121 network (Huang et al., 2017) for trait 
estimation. By expanding the bounding box, we can incorporate more 
information from the surrounding objects, which has the potential to 
serve as references. To interpret the features learned by DenseNet for 
estimating fruit size, we use the Grad-CAM method (Selvaraju et al., 
2017) to generate a feature heatmap by weighting the activation map of 
the last convolutional layer with the corresponding gradients. The hot­
spot area in the heatmap signifies the presence of important features. As 
depicted in Fig. S2, these hotspot areas encompass not only the fruit but 
also the calyxes and stems, illustrating that DenseNet can learn sur­
rounding information to enhance fruit size prediction. As a regressor, the 
DenseNet-121 estimated continuous fruit traits that include the fruit 
diameter (mm), the fruit length (mm), and the decimal phenological 
stages (0–70). The estimation of decimal phenological stages was con­
strained by the main phenological stages estimated by the Faster-RCNN 
classifier. Specifically, the predicted decimal phenological stages remain 
unchanged when the predictions of the classifier and the regressor 
belong to the same main stage. Otherwise, the outlier will be clamped 
within the range of the classifier predicted main stage. For example, if 
the regressors’ decimal estimate is 3.2 (the early white stage) and the 
main stage estimated by the classifiers’ estimate is 2 (the green stage), 
we clamp the decimal estimate within the main stage, which is 2.9 (the 
late green stage). Each estimation made at every timestep was recorded 
in a time series, which would be used to calibrate the fruit growth 
network. 
2.4.2. Fruit growth simulator 
2.4.2.1. Fruit growth modeling and synthetic data generation. RNN-based 
networks are typically employed for modeling complex dynamic sys­
tems. However, training an RNN without static parameters as input may 
fall short in capturing the diversity among episodes under various sce­
narios due to the lack of episode-specific parameters to guide the 
sequence learning process. In our context, we conducted an Observing 
System Simulation Experiment (OSSE) to demonstrate the importance of 
using fruit-specific parameters as input for fruit growth modeling 
(detailed in Supplementary material). The results show recurrent neural 
networks (RNN) only discern a general pattern when specific fruit 
growth parameters are absent for training (Fig. S3), whereas the RNN 
constrained by curve parameters can successfully capture the temporal 
patterns of individual fruits (Fig. S4). 
In order to obtain fruit-specific parameters, we employ the S-shape 
function to represent the fruit growth progress, with its curve parame­
ters serving as the fruit growth parameters. The S-shape function is a 
commonly employed model for illustrating the development of various 
aspects in both animal and plant entities, encompassing attributes like 
the dimensions and weight of organs and organisms. The adoption of S- 
shaped curves extends to attributes of fruits, such as diameter, length, 
and fresh/dry weight, as supported by previous studies (Nitsch, 1953; 
Muniz et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2020). We hypothesize the enlargement of 
strawberry fruit adheres to the Logistics function, a representative 
example of S-shaped curves, that uses four meaningful growth param­
eters to control the trajectory of the target variable as follows, 
f(x) =
y0 −s
1 + e−r(x−t0) + s
(2)  
where the f(x) starts from s (i.e., minimum value of the target variable, 
in this study we assume s = 4) and converges to y0 (i.e., maximum value 
of the target variable), with r denotes the increase rate of f(x). The center 
of symmetry of f(x) is x = t0, which corresponds to the maximum 
growth rate. 
Training a fruit growth simulator using only the observed fruit 
growth trajectories, which are limited in number, presents a significant 
challenge. To address this challenge, we proposed a method for gener­
ating synthetic data that incorporates knowledge into the network 
training process. We first plotted the ground truth trajectories from the 
2022 and 2023 greenhouse experiments (Fig. 4) and then retrieved the 
curve parameters of the upper and lower boundary curves by the trial- 
and-error method. The objective of establishing the upper and lower 
boundaries is to generate a synthetic dataset containing a variety of 
potential fruit growth trajectories. We randomly sampled the parame­
ters of the logistics function within the parameter ranges of the 
boundaries and selected 1,000 possible trajectories (Fig. 4b and d). Each 
trajectory represents a simulated episode of the fruit growth process. 
The parameter ranges used for sampling are y0,diameter ∈[21, 32] and 
y0,length ∈[22, 40]; the t0,diameter ∈[140, 410] and t0,length ∈[170, 400]; 
therdiameter ∈[0.009, 0.017] and rlength ∈[0.007, 0.014]. To generate the 
data pair to train the parameter-learning network, we implemented a 
“mask-out” strategy, from which we selected 200 fruit growth episodes 
for each generated curve. Specifically, we chose sample points along 
each trajectory to represent sparse observations, while masking out the 
remaining points. Each episode comprises a random selection of 1 to 8 
sparse observations, with the interval between consecutive observations 
being randomly sampled from 40 to 150 GDD. The label of each episode 
is the corresponding fruit growth parameters of the curve. We assembled 
a synthetic dataset comprising 200,000 episodes for the purpose of 
training the parameter network. To generate the training data for the 
fruit growth network, we discretized the previously generated trajec­
tories into pairs. Each pair included the initial fruit size, the experienced 
ΔGDD (ranging from 1 to 699), and the associated three curve param­
eters (i.e., y0, t0, and r) as inputs, and the final fruit size as the label. 
2.4.2.2. Parameter learning network and fruit growth network. The fruit 
growth simulator comprises two multilayer perceptron (MLP) networks: 
one for parameter learning and another for fruit growth simulation. We 
compared MLP with different structures for curve parameters estimation 
(Table S2) and the result showed that adding additional layers will not 
significantly improve the model performance. Consequently, we 
employed the 4-layer MLP for both networks which is relatively light­
weight and easy to train (architectural specifics are outlined in Table 1). 
The final layer of the parameter learning network estimates the three 
curve parameters y0, t0 and r, while the fruit growth network predicts 
the diameter and length. The parameter learning network and the fruit 
growth network were trained using the synthetic datasets generated in 
Section 2.4.2.1.

2.5. Networks training 
The flowchart depicted in Fig. 5 illustrates the training process for 
KGCV-Strawberry. Each of the framework’s four modules was trained 
independently, so no specific training order was required. Initially, we 
trained the detection network (i.e., Faster-RCNN) using all available 
images with labels of the bounding box and main phenological stage. 
Subsequently, the raw images were cropped into image patches by an­
notated fruit bounding boxes. The image patches were used to train the 
trait estimation network with labels of observed fruit size and decimal 
phenological stage. As described in Section 2.4.2.1, we randomly 
sampled 1,000 sets of curve parameters within the range of the upper 
and lower fruit growth boundaries to obtain 1,000 possible synthetic 
trajectories. We sampled 20,000 episodes of the sparse observations and 
the corresponding curve parameters from the 1,000 synthetic trajec­
tories to train the parameter learning network. To train the fruit growth 
network, we processed the synthetic trajectories into data pairs as 
described in Section 2.4.2.1 (refers to the input and output in Table. 1). 
To enhance the network’s training robustness, we also injected random 
noise ε into the input of the parameter-learning network and the fruit- 
growth network in every batch of training. The ε follows a Gaussian 
distribution of N(0, 0.02x) where the x is the input vector. 
Once all these modules have been effectively trained, KGCV- 
Strawberry is subjected to evaluation, which includes assessing its per­
formance based on observed fruit size, fruit fresh weight, and maturity 
time. In this study, all the neural networks were trained using the 
Pytorch platform and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. Specifically, for the 
fruit traits detector, we employed a detection network based on Faster- 
RCNN with a ResNet-50 backend. This network was trained for 50 
epochs with a batch size of 2, utilizing an SGD optimizer, which is 
commonly used to train a Faster-RCNN due to its robustness, with an 
initial learning rate of 0.005 and a momentum of 0.8. For fruit detection, 
we utilized a bounding box regression loss, and for phenology classifi­
cation, a classifier loss was employed. To avoid the optimizer getting 
stuck at the local optimum, we implemented a learning rate decay 
strategy where the learning rate was reduced by 5 % after each epoch. 
Regarding the trait estimation network, we incorporated various image 
transformations, including random horizontal flips, random cropping, 
random saturation adjustments, and random brightness adjustments, for 
each mini-batch during training to mitigate overfitting. The parameter 
learning network and fruit growth network were trained for 100 epochs 
using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, and the 
learning rate was reduced by 1 % after each epoch. 
2.6. Model evaluation 
Using a single metric may not always be enough to assess the per­
formance of a classification model due to imbalanced classes in the 
datasets and estimates. Consequently, we employed four metrics to 
evaluate the performance of the phenology detection task. These metrics 
include Accuracy, which is calculated as (TP + TN)/(TP + FP + FN +
TN); Precision, calculated as (TP)/(TP + FP); Recall, calculated as (TP)/ 
(TP + FN); and F1-score, calculated as (2TP)/(2TP + FP + FN). Here, TP 
represents true positive (i.e. both the prediction and label are positive), 
TN denotes true negative (i.e. both the prediction and label are nega­
tive), FP represents false positive (i.e. the prediction is positive and the 
label is negative), and FN represents false negative (i.e. the prediction is 
negative and the label is positive). In other words, the term “true” means 
the prediction matches the label, while “false” indicates the prediction 
and label is mismatched. We approach each class as a binary classifi­
cation problem to calculate metrics for a particular phenological stage, 
whereas predictions or labels at other stages are considered “negative”. 
For example, for the flowers class, there are only two classes labeled 
“flower” and “non-flower”. 
For the tasks of fruit traits estimation and fruit growth simulation, 
the square of the correlation coefficient (R2), and root-mean-square 
error (RMSE) were used. We selected 38 image sequences with lengths 
greater than six to evaluate the model performance for fruit-level yield 
maturity time prediction. For plant-level prediction with KGCV- 
Strawberry, we upscale the predicted fruit-level yield to plant-level by 
multiplying a ratio, which is calculated by the number of harvested 
tagged fruits divided by the total harvested fruits. The reason for using a 
ratio is we do not have image sequence data for all fruit in the green­
house. The upscaled plant-level yield was compared with the predictions 
of the uncalibrated and calibrated GROPGRO-Strawberry model. The 
GROPGRO-Strawberry is a process-based model that is included in the 
DSSAT model collection (Hopf et al., 2022). The climate inputs of 
GROPGRO-Strawberry are minimum and maximum daily air tempera­
ture, radiation, and relative humidity. There are no nitrogen modules in 
GROPGRO-Strawberry so the simulated strawberries were free from 
nitrogen stress. In the simulation, the irrigation was automatically 
triggered when the soil moisture was lower than 80 % of the field ca­
pacity. Regarding the model calibration, we employed particle swarm 
optimization (PSO) (Kennedy and Eberhart, 1995) to optimize 15 
strawberry cultivar parameters (detailed in Table S3) with 50 genera­
tions. To avoid the numerical optima of the calibrated parameter, we 
employed 20 ensembles for strawberry modeling based on the calibrated 
parameters. Specifically, we discretized the calibrated parameter “EM- 
FL” (i.e., controls the timing of the first harvest) from 50 % and 200 %. 
The source code and the complete dataset are available at https://g 
ithub.com/DigitalAgUMN/KGCV_Strawberry. 
3. Result 
3.1. Statistics of the greenhouse experiments 
The setting of nitrogen fertilization treatments increased the varia­
tion of plant traits that will facilitate model development. The growth of 
strawberry plants and fruits exhibited distinct responses to varying ni­
trogen application rates. We observed that increasing nitrogen appli­
cation typically resulted in greater aboveground biomass; however, this 
was not consistently reflected in fresh yield, fruit size, or fruit water 
content (Fig. S5). The result aligns with the findings of Wu et al. (2020), 
who claim that excessive application of nitrogen fertilizer may reduce 
yields. We also investigated the temporal pattern of strawberry pro­
ductivity (Fig. S6) and the harvest index (Fig. S7) for each treatment. 
The findings suggest that a nitrogen fertilizer rate of 30 kg N ha-1 is 
adequate, whereas higher nitrogen application levels do not lead to a 
significant productivity increase and instead result in a reduction of the 
harvest index. 
To predict fruit-level yield from estimated fruit size, we investigated 
the relationship between fresh weight and diameter, length, as well as 
the volume of the fruit cylinder (Fig. 6). We discovered a robust linear 
correlation between the volume of the fruit cylinder and the yield, and 
this correlation remained consistent across different nitrogen treatments 
and various fruit conditions, (i.e., normal, wizened, malformed, and 
overripe). As a result, the linear equation in Fig. 6c was used to estimate 
the fresh weight of the fruit. 
The average share of each development stage in the entire fruit 
lifespan is illustrated in Fig. 7. From the initial stage (small green) to the 
ripe stage, the shares for flower, small green, green, white, turning red, 
and red stages are 4.8 %, 32.7 %, 24.2 %, 15.6 %, 4.8 %, and 17.8 %, 
respectively. The small green stage, which begins after the withering and 
falling of the flowers, accounts for the largest portion of the growing 
degree days (GDD) in the overall fruit development process. The green 
stage and white stage have relatively lengthy durations during which the 
fruit undergoes rapid growth and accumulates dry matter. In contrast, 
the turning red stage is the shortest in duration, occurring as the fruit 
approaches its final size and utilizes nutrients from the plant to accu­
mulate glycogen. The short duration of the turning red stage also makes 
it a good time window to predict the maturity time.

3.3. Performance of fruit growth simulator 
3.3.1. Parameters estimation via assimilating historical data 
By capturing more images throughout the fruit growth process, the 
fruit trait detector can estimate a sequence of fruit sizes from the cor­
responding image time series. This sequence is then used as input for the 
parameter learning network, which predicts the parameters of the fruit 
growth curve. In Fig. 11, the performance of the parameter learning 
network is demonstrated by estimating three strawberry growth pa­
rameters from synthetic observation sequences of varying lengths. The 
estimates align well with the reference values for both the diameter 
growth curve, with the normalized root-mean-square-error (NRMSE) 
values lower than 0.17 and R2 higher than 0.66 (Fig. 11 a-c), and for the 
length growth curve, with NRMSE values lower than 0.14 and R2 higher 
than 0.73 (Fig. 11d–f). The growth curve could be updated as new ob­
servations become available to be assimilated into the parameter 
learning network, allowing for dynamic corrections to the forecasted 
future fruit status and estimated maturity time. Fig. 12 shows the growth 
curves rendered by the estimated parameters using observation se­
quences with different lengths. The results indicate that for shorter 
sequences, there is a notable discrepancy between the predicted curve 
and the reference, indicating higher uncertainty in predicting future 
fruit growth. However, as the sequence length increases, the estimated 
growth curve gradually approaches the reference, demonstrating the 
capability of the parameter learning network to assimilate updated 
observation sequences and improve prediction accuracy. 
3.3.2. Fruit growth prediction 
The parameter inferred from the parameter learning network is uti­
lized to drive the fruit growth network, enabling the transfer of fruit size 
from time t to t’ (as shown in Fig. 3 and Eq. (5)). Fig. 13 illustrates an 
example of how assimilating historical observation improves the fruit 
diameter prediction. In this case, the default (uncalibrated) growth 
curve did not align well with observations, and using it directly would 
result in an overestimation of future fruit size. The disparity between the 
growth curve and the actual fruit size does not substantially decrease 
when the length of the observation sequence is limited (Fig. 13a and b). 
However, as additional observations were assimilated, this disparity was 
significantly reduced, with the RMSE dropping from 3.84 to 0.98 mm 
after assimilating four observations. Despite the noisy and uncertain

3.3.3. Yield and maturity time prediction 
To further demonstrate the effectiveness of the data assimilation 
scheme of KGCV-strawberry, we investigated the performance of KGCV- 
strawberry in fruit-level yield prediction (Fig. 14) and plant-level yield 
prediction (Fig. 15). Predicting fruit-level yield using image sequences 
with a length of less than three poses a significant challenge, resulting in 
an RMSE ranging from 2.84 to 3.58 g. This is primarily due to the fact 
that the first three observations predominantly pertain to the flower and 
small green stages, characterized by a slow fruit growth rate. Conse­
quently, the growth curve inferred from these initial three images may 
exhibit substantial uncertainty, as there were insufficient observations 
to constrain the upper bounds of the curve. As more images were 
assimilated into the KGCV-strawberry, the performance of yield pre­
diction increased, with the R2 increasing from 0.25 to 0.73, and the 
RMSE decreasing from 3.58 to 2.01 g/fruit. Regarding the plant-level 
yield prediction, we compared the performance of KGCV-Strawberry 
with the process-based model CROPGRO-Strawberry. Fig. 15a 
illustrates model predictions for the cumulative harvested fruit fresh 
weight. It can be seen that the CROPGRO-Strawberry with the default 
parameters underestimated the cumulative yield due to the slower 
phenology development. Although calibration of cultivar parameters 
allowed CROPGRO-Strawberry’s predicted trend to align with ground 
truth (Fig. 15a), the process-based model failed to capture the variability 
of fresh yield across each harvest (Fig. 15b). By contrast, the KGCV- 
Strawberry outperformed the CROPGRO-Strawberry in capturing the 
yield dynamics for each harvest event. This capability stems from KGCV- 
Strawberry’s adeptness at leveraging information from image sequences 
to depict fruit growth dynamics on an individual basis, while the plant- 
level process-based model primarily focuses on final cumulative yield, 
thereby oversimplifying the harvesting dynamics for multi-harvest crops 
like strawberries. 
Additionally, we conducted experiments on fruit maturity time 
prediction (Fig. 16). The results show that assimilating image sequences 
with a length greater than three substantially enhanced the accuracy of 
maturity time prediction. In this case, the RMSE decreased from 
71.87 ◦C⋅day to 39.30 ◦C⋅day and the R2 increased from 0.11 to 0.61.

(Du et al., 2023). One of the possible solutions is to fuse the information

images, multispectral images, and hyperspectral images (Gao et al.,

Additionally, an early warning system can be built upon the data 
fusing technology and these multi-source fruit-level temporal data. For 
example, calculate the pollination rate of a bench or individual plants, 
where a low pollination rate means a part of the flowers did not turn into 
fruits, indicating a low marketable yield. In such cases, amendment 
measures such as introducing pollinators (e.g., bees), improving air 
circulation, and optimizing temperature and humidity can be imple­
mented. Additionally, the CV technology can detect leaf-level water 
stress and nitrogen stress in plants (Zhou et al., 2021; Sethy et al., 2020), 
providing crucial information for dynamic decision-making regarding 
irrigation and fertilization. Furthermore, CV can be used to identify 
plant diseases and pests (Shin et al., 2021), assisting in determining the 
appropriate type and dosage of pesticides and antiseptics for precise 
disease and pest control. Therefore, enhancing the proposed 
KGCV-strawberry framework to incorporate a decision-making system 
based on plant stress monitoring, disease detection, and pest control 
shows great promise as a future research direction. 
The proposed framework has the capability to estimate the harvest 
date of individual berries, providing an opportunity to optimize the 
harvest schedule and minimize the occurrence of overripe strawberries 
resulting from untimely harvesting. Moreover, this information holds 
value in predicting marketable yield and arranging transportation lo­
gistics. In temperature-uncontrolled greenhouses, the progression of 
phenology can be influenced by sudden cold snaps or heat waves. 
Strawberry fruits cultivated in cooler environments often exhibit higher 
levels of fructose, glucose, total carbohydrates, and larger fruit sizes 
(Wang and Camp, 2000). Consequently, instead of relying solely on the 
traditional fruit grading method based on the final size observed at 
harvest, early fruit grading that considers fruit growth patterns (such as 
growth rate and duration) holds the potential for classifying the quality 
of individual fruits. 
We employed the classic Faster-RCNN for the object detection task. 
The Faster-RCNN is easy to train and it already produced a commend­
able classification accuracy of 0.95 in our case. While we do not ensure 
the best performance for the employed Faster-RCNN because newer 
networks may marginally surpass it in a certain scenario, achieving this 
improvement necessitates additional training data and computational 
resources. We opted not to develop more intricate modules in this study, 
as our main objective is to present a comprehensive framework and 
paradigm to enhance the utility of image sequence data, rather than 
focusing on refining algorithms for specific tasks like object detection. 
Moreover, the modules within the framework can be easily substituted 
by end-users. Another limitation of this study is we utilized two 2D 
Logistic functions to independently model the diameter and length of the 
fruit using GDD. However, there are interconnections between the 
diameter and length of the fruit that are not independent, and other 
environmental factors, such as radiation, CO2 concentration, etc., may 
influence the fruit growth process. For healthy fruits of the same variety, 
the ratio of diameter to length could remain relatively stable (Roussos 
et al., 2009). Therefore, in future research, it is necessary to explore the 
use of a higher-dimensional Logistic function to incorporate additional 
constraints into the modeling of fruit growth. It is worth noting that the 
temporal pattern of strawberry growth may deviate from the “S” shaped 
curve when the plants experience various stresses, potentially affecting 
the growth rate and altering the trajectory of fruit growth. In such cases, 
it becomes essential to investigate non-linear models that incorporate 
knowledge-guided constraints to minimize structural errors in simu­
lating strawberry fruit growth. 
5. Conclusion 
We developed a framework called KGCV-strawberry to simulate the 
growth of strawberry fruits on an individual basis. Two modules, 
including the fruit trait detector (i.e., the eyes) and the fruit growth 
simulator (i.e., the brain), were designed to detect the present fruit size 
and phenological stage as well as provide a dynamic prediction of the 
future fruit traits and estimate maturity time. We conducted intensive 
greenhouse experiments with four different nitrogen treatments to 
support the model establishment and evaluation. We tested the fruit trait 
detector for object detection, fruit size, and phenology estimation, and 
the estimates show high consistency with the observations, with the fruit 
detected rate of 92.8 % and the mean accuracy, precision, recall, and F1- 
score for all phenological stages of 0.95, 0.83, 0.81, and 0.82, respec­
tively. For the fruit growth simulator, the performance of the parameter 
learning network is evaluated by a numerical experiment with synthetic 
data. The results demonstrated that the parameter learning network was 
capable of inferring plausible parameters from observation sequences of 
varying lengths. Furthermore, we examined the combined use of the 
parameter learning network and the fruit growth network for predicting 
fruit growth and estimating maturity time, and the predicted yield was 
compared with the process-based model CROPGRO-Strawberry. As 
more images were assimilated, the RMSE for yield prediction decreased 
from 3.58 to 2.01 g/fruit, and the RMSE for maturity time prediction 
decreased from 71.87 ◦C⋅day to 39.30 ◦C⋅day. The KGCV-Strawberry is 
capable of capturing the yield dynamics for each harvest, while the 
process-based model produced a high predictive error for each harvest 
separately. The results indicate the potential of applying this framework 
for precise management optimization of individual fruits in intelligent 
strawberry farming.
