A B S T R A C T
Orchards and perennial tree crops are economically and nutritionally vital components of global agriculture, yet 
their heterogeneous canopy architectures, long production cycles, and complex management requirements pose 
persistent challenges for reliable, scalable, and repeatable monitoring in support of precision management. Light 
Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) provides three-dimensional information on canopy architecture, tree structure, 
terrain, and orchard infrastructure, making it a key technology for perennial crop sensing. This review synthe­
sizes LiDAR research in orchards and perennial tree crops from three perspectives: bibliometric development, 
sensor and platform evolution, and data-processing workflows. A curated corpus of 288 publications covering 24 
crop types was analyzed to characterize temporal trends, crop representation, platform adoption, retrieved pa­
rameters, and publication patterns. The results show rapid growth after 2015, driven by UAV-based mapping, 
mobile laser scanning, compact multi-beam sensors, and increasing demand for structure-aware crop monitoring, 
while many tropical and subtropical perennial crops remain underrepresented. LiDAR systems are reviewed 
according to dimensionality, ranging principle, backscatter sampling, structural configuration, deployment 
platform, measurement attributes, field-condition robustness, and task-specific suitability. Processing workflows 
are then examined, including preprocessing, registration and SLAM, noise filtering, ground classification, seg­
mentation, machine learning, deep learning, data fusion, and open-source software ecosystems. As the technical 
component of a two-part review series, this review emphasizes bibliometric trends, sensor technologies, and 
algorithmic workflows, complementing a companion review focused on LiDAR applications, implementation 
challenges, and management-oriented opportunities in orchard systems. Particular attention is given to orchard- 
specific bottlenecks, including canopy occlusion and beam attenuation, non-random missing data, repetitive-row 
registration drift, wind-induced canopy motion, radiometric inconsistency, limited model transferability, and 
computational constraints for real-time deployment. By integrating bibliometric evidence, sensor-system anal­
ysis, and algorithmic evaluation, this review identifies key priorities for future LiDAR in orchard research: 
standardized metadata and quality-control reporting, task-specific validation, occlusion-aware modeling, 
radiometric normalization, multi-platform fusion, transferable learning, and uncertainty-aware digital orchard 
modeling. Overall, LiDAR is positioned as a methodological foundation for reproducible phenotyping, precision 
orchard management, robotic perception, and data-driven perennial crop production.

Orchards and perennial tree crops, encompassing high-value fruits 
such as apple, citrus, and mango; nuts such as almond, walnut, and 
pistachio; and industrial crops such as coffee, cacao, and oil palm, are 
vital components of global agriculture due to their economic importance 
and long cultivation cycles (Kreitzman et al., 2020; Longchamps et al., 
2022). In this review, orchards primarily denote intensively managed 
fruit and nut tree production systems, whereas perennial tree crops are 
adopted as a broader term encompassing both orchard systems and

cycles, heterogeneous canopy architectures, diverse planting geome­
tries, and heightened vulnerability to abiotic stresses, pests, and dis­
eases, creating persistent demands for precise and scalable crop 
monitoring (Trentin et al., 2024; Vashisth et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 
2021). From a sensing perspective, these same characteristics also create 
a technically challenging environment: complex vertical structure, 
crown overlap, under-canopy occlusion, seasonal phenological varia­
tion, and repetitive row patterns can all affect data acquisition, geo­
metric reconstruction, and trait extraction (Liu et al., 2026a; Rivera 
et al., 2023). Accordingly, precision agriculture in orchards and peren­
nial tree crops requires not only sensing technologies capable of repre­
senting orchard structure, but also robust technical frameworks that can 
convert heterogeneous observations into reliable and reproducible in­
formation for analysis and management.
Among the available sensing technologies, Light Detection and 
Ranging (LiDAR) has emerged as a particularly valuable tool for 
perennial tree cropping systems. Unlike passive optical sensors, which 
are constrained by illumination conditions and spectral mixing, LiDAR 
emits laser pulses and records their returns to generate three- 
dimensional point clouds of vegetation architecture (Guo et al., 2023; 
Tao et al., 2025). This capability enables the reconstruction of under- 
canopy features and the estimation of key biophysical parameters, 
including canopy height, crown volume, foliage density, leaf area index 
(LAI), biomass, and fruit-related traits (Penzel and Tsoulias, 2022; Riv­
era et al., 2023; Scalisi et al., 2021). Collectively, these attributes sup­
port diverse applications, ranging from inventory mapping and 
phenotyping (Barbosa Júnior et al., 2024; Rui et al., 2024) to variable- 
rate spraying (Partel et al., 2021), robotic navigation (Chen et al., 
2025b), and yield sensing and forecasting (Longchamps et al., 2022). 
However, the value of LiDAR in orchard research is not determined by 
sensor deployment alone. It depends on the interaction among sensor 
characteristics, platform configuration, acquisition geometry, point- 
cloud quality, registration and alignment, ground normalization, seg­
mentation, trait retrieval, and downstream analytical models. Conse­
quently, the expanding use of LiDAR in perennial cropping systems has 
produced not only a growing application literature, but also an 
increasingly complex technical landscape in which hardware choices, 
platform design, and algorithmic workflows jointly shape measurement 
reliability, operational feasibility, and cross-study comparability.
The technical evolution of LiDAR has unfolded over several decades. 
LiDAR emerged in the early 1960s following the invention of the laser 
(Maiman, 1960). Its first routine applications were in atmospheric sci­
ence, where it was employed for aerosol and cloud profiling (Collis, 
1970; Fiocco and Smullin, 1963), and it was subsequently applied to 
spaceborne altimetry with the Apollo-15 laser altimeter in 1971 (Sun 
et al., 2013). During the 1970s to 1990s, experimental airborne laser 
profilers were developed to investigate vegetation structure, while 
large-scale ecosystem applications matured as georeferencing and 
waveform modeling became more established in the 1990s (Lefsky et al., 
2002). In agriculture, research in the mid-1990s employed laser-induced 
fluorescence LiDAR to monitor vegetation physiological status and 
stress, though not specifically in orchards (Edner et al., 1994). The first 
explicit orchard-targeted deployments appeared when Walklate et al. 
(1997) introduced short-range LiDAR to characterize top fruit can­
opies for pesticide spraying. In parallel, canopy quantification was 
advanced through the use of laser and ultrasonic ranging for citrus 
canopy volume estimation (Tumbo et al., 2002), as well as dedicated 
laser scanners for citrus canopy traits (Wei and Salyani, 2004). Further 
developments included airborne LiDAR for orchard tree inventory (Jang 
et al., 2008), tractor-mounted two-dimensional LiDAR for row-wise 
three-dimensional reconstruction (Rosell et al., 2009), and tripod- 
based terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) in olive orchards to derive can­
opy height, crown size, volume, and structural indices (Moorthy et al., 
2011). More recently, spaceborne LiDAR has been extended to 
perennial tree crops, with Ice, Cloud, and land Elevation Satellite-2 
(ICESat-2) footprints enabling regional oil palm height modeling 
(Zang et al., 2023). Together, these developments demonstrate the rapid 
diffusion of LiDAR from forestry to agriculture, and specifically to or­
chards and perennial cropping systems. At the same time, this expansion 
has generated a fragmented methodological landscape spanning diverse 
sensor architectures, deployment platforms, point-cloud characteristics, 
and analytical requirements, creating a need for a more integrated 
technical synthesis.
Despite this expansion, the technical foundations of LiDAR have not 
yet been synthesized in a sufficiently integrated and crop-system- 
specific manner. Existing reviews have provided valuable perspectives 
on agricultural remote sensing, orchard sensing applications, and broad 
LiDAR use in precision agriculture, but they have generally placed less 
emphasis on the combined evolution of bibliometric trends, sensing- 
system design, and end-to-end algorithmic workflows in perennial tree 
crops (Farhan et al., 2024; Lin, 2015; Rivera et al., 2023; Tao et al., 
2025). Several gaps remain particularly important. First, the rapid 
growth of the field has not been fully mapped in terms of crop repre­
sentation, platform adoption, sensor preferences, and thematic devel­
opment across orchard and plantation systems. Second, LiDAR sensor 
classes and deployment platforms, including emerging solid-state and 
photon-counting systems, are often discussed descriptively, but less 
frequently evaluated in relation to orchard-specific observability chal­
lenges such as canopy occlusion, crown density, row geometry, survey 
scale, and under-canopy accessibility. Third, data-processing methods 
are commonly presented as isolated techniques rather than as interde­
pendent workflows in which preprocessing, registration, ground classi­
fication, segmentation, machine learning, and validation jointly 
determine the reliability of derived outputs. Fourth, important technical 
bottlenecks, 
including 
cross-platform 
inconsistency, 
phenology- 
dependent variability, radiometric limitations, point-cloud registration 
in repetitive row environments, and the limited transferability of data- 
driven models across crop architectures, remain insufficiently synthe­
sized in relation to practical study design and deployment. Addressing 
these gaps is necessary not only for understanding the development of 
LiDAR in orchard research, but also for identifying where technical 
standardization, benchmarking, and validation guidance are most ur­
gently needed.
This review addresses these needs by providing a technical and 
methodological synthesis of LiDAR research in orchards and perennial 
tree crops from three complementary perspectives: bibliometric evi­
dence, sensing systems, and algorithmic workflows. First, drawing on a 
structured bibliometric analysis of 288 selected publications spanning 
more than two decades, we quantify temporal trends, crop distributions, 
platform preferences, retrieval targets, publication venues, and thematic 
shifts in the literature. Second, we examine LiDAR sensors and deploy­
ment platforms by classifying systems according to dimensionality, 
ranging principles, backscatter sampling, structural configurations, and 
deployment modes, while interpreting their relevance to orchard-scale 
observability, survey extent, and operational constraints. Third, we 
synthesize data-processing and analytical workflows, covering pre­
processing, registration and alignment, noise filtering, ground classifi­
cation, segmentation, machine learning, deep learning, and data fusion, 
with emphasis on the technical dependencies and bottlenecks that affect 
robustness, reproducibility, and practical deployment. Beyond orga­
nizing the literature, this review aims to clarify methodological matu­
rity, identify major technical bottlenecks, and highlight validation 
priorities for future orchard LiDAR studies. A companion review pro­
vides a complementary application-centered synthesis of LiDAR-enabled 
orchard management, implementation challenges, and forward-looking 
opportunities (Liu et al., 2026b), whereas the present review focuses on 
bibliometric patterns, sensing technologies, processing workflows, and 
methodological foundations. Ultimately, this review positions LiDAR 
not merely as a collection of emerging sensing tools, but as an evolving 
technical ecosystem whose progress depends on the coordinated

2. Bibliometric Analysis
2.1. PRISMA-style Literature Identification, Screening, and Bibliometric 
Coding
To improve transparency and reproducibility, the bibliometric 
corpus was assembled using a structured literature identification and 
screening procedure following PRISMA-style reporting principles. The 
procedure included database searching, record consolidation, de- 
duplication, title–abstract screening, eligibility assessment, and final 
inclusion for bibliometric coding. Because this review was designed as a 
bibliometric and technical synthesis rather than a quantitative meta- 
analysis of intervention effects, a formal risk-of-bias assessment was 
not conducted. Instead, quality control focused on transparent search 
criteria, explicit eligibility rules, de-duplication, manual verification of 
ambiguous records, and consistent coding of study attributes.
2.1.1. Literature Search Scope and Information Sources
The review targeted peer-reviewed and scholarly publications that 
applied LiDAR to orchards or perennial tree crops for precision 
agriculture-relevant purposes. A structured literature search was con­
ducted across three major bibliographic sources that provide broad and 
complementary coverage of agricultural engineering, remote sensing, 
geospatial science, and crop sensing research: Web of Science Core 
Collection, Scopus, and CAB Abstracts. The search had no starting-year 
restriction and included records available up to August 1, 2025. Records 
indexed in at least one of the three sources were considered for 
screening. The scope was restricted to orchard systems and perennial 
tree crops, including fruit trees, nut trees, and long-lived woody plan­
tation crops. Studies on annual crops, herbaceous crops, conventional 
forestry without perennial crop relevance, or purely methodological 
LiDAR research without a crop-specific application were excluded.
2.1.2. Search Strategy and Crop Taxonomy
To capture LiDAR applications across diverse orchard and perennial 
tree crop systems, Boolean search queries were constructed by 
combining LiDAR-related terms with common and scientific crop names. 
LiDAR-related terms included: 
“LiDAR” OR “lidar” OR “light detection and ranging” OR “laser 
scanning” OR “laser scanner” OR (“TLS” AND laser) OR (“MLS” AND 
laser) OR (“ALS” AND laser)
These terms were paired with crop names using the Boolean operator 
AND. Common and scientific names within each crop group were con­
nected using OR. In Web of Science, searches were conducted using the 
Topic field, which includes title, abstract, author keywords, and Key­
words Plus. In Scopus, searches were conducted within title, abstract, 
and keywords using TITLE-ABS-KEY(). In CAB Abstracts, equivalent 
Boolean searches were conducted in title, abstract, and keyword/subject 
fields where available. The search covered 33 predefined focal crops 
organized into six agronomic groups based on crop type, production 
system, and agronomic use (Table 1). The crop groups were used to 
structure crop-specific searches, whereas final bibliometric counts were 
de-duplicated at the publication level.

2.1.3. Screening and Eligibility Criteria
All records were screened using predefined inclusion and exclusion 
criteria. Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria:
1) The study explicitly used LiDAR, laser scanning, laser ranging, or a 
LiDAR-derived point cloud.
2) The study focused on orchards, fruit trees, nut trees, or perennial 
woody crop systems.
3) The study addressed a precision agriculture-relevant task, such as 
tree inventory, canopy structure characterization, growth monitoring, 
phenotyping, yield estimation, flower or fruit quantification, health or 
stress assessment, navigation, variable-rate management, or orchard 
mapping.
4) The study provided sufficient information to identify the crop 
type, LiDAR platform or sensor category, and at least one retrieved 
parameter, application, or processing workflow.
Records were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:
1) The study did not involve LiDAR or laser scanning.
2) The study relied only on RGB imagery, multispectral imagery, 
hyperspectral imagery, photogrammetry, ultrasonic sensing, or other 
non-LiDAR technologies.
3) The study focused exclusively on annual or herbaceous crops.
4) The study focused on conventional forestry without direct rele­
vance to orchard or perennial tree crop systems.
5) The record was non-scholarly, inaccessible, duplicated, or lacked 
sufficient bibliographic information for reliable coding.
6) The same study appeared multiple times across databases or crop- 
search categories.

2.1.5. Export and Record Management
Bibliographic metadata were exported separately for each crop- 
specific search and database to preserve search provenance. Exported 
fields included title, authors, publication year, venue, abstract, author 
keywords, affiliations, country or region, DOI, database source, and 
references where available. After merging and de-duplication, metadata 
were standardized using a common schema, including column-name 
unification, DOI normalization, spelling harmonization, and crop-label 
standardization.
2.1.6. Data Extraction and Coding
Each included publication was coded using a predefined data dic­
tionary. The coded variables included publication year, crop type, crop 
group, database source, journal or conference venue, geographic region 
where available, LiDAR platform, LiDAR dimensionality, sensor type or 
model where reported, deployment mode, retrieved biophysical or 
operational parameters, application domain, and major methodological 
keywords. Multi-label coding was used when a study reported multiple 
platforms, crops, parameters, or applications. For example, a study 
combining UAV-based LiDAR and terrestrial laser scanning was assigned 
to both platform categories. Similarly, a study retrieving both canopy 
height and canopy volume contributed to both parameter categories. 
Ambiguous platform descriptions, sensor information, or application 
categories were resolved by consulting the methods section of the 
original publication.

2.2. Results and Discussion
The PRISMA-style screening process resulted in a final corpus of 288 
eligible publications on LiDAR applications in orchards and perennial 
tree crops. These publications covered 24 crop types and provided the 
basis for analyzing temporal trends, crop representation, platform 
adoption, LiDAR dimensionality, sensor models, retrieved parameters, 
keywords, and publication venues. This section interprets the descrip­
tive statistics with emphasis on their agronomic relevance and meth­
odological implications for LiDAR-enabled precision management.
2.2.1. Temporal Dynamics of the Field
The earliest LiDAR applications in orchards and perennial tree crops 
emerged in the late 1990s, with only sporadic studies published before 
2005 (Fig. 2). A clear upward trajectory became evident around 2010, 
and annual publications increased substantially after 2015, coinciding 
with the rapid diffusion of UAV-based LiDAR systems and the avail­
ability of relatively low-cost multi-beam LiDAR sensors. The cumulative 
publication curve demonstrates a sustained exponential growth pattern, 
indicating that LiDAR research in perennial crop systems has evolved 
into an established and rapidly expanding field. The domain has crossed 
a “viability threshold”, with LiDAR increasingly recognized as a stan­
dard instrument for structure-aware crop management and agricultural 
automation. Continued growth is expected, supported by advances in 
compact three-dimensional sensors with reduced size, weight, power, 
and cost, as well as by the development of solid-state and frequency- 
modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) devices.
2.2.2. Crop Distribution
The bibliographic survey covered 33 crop species, of which 24 were 
represented in the final corpus (Fig. 3). The most frequently studied 
crops were apple (21.8%), oil palm (13.0%), pear (8.1%), olive (7.4%), 
and citrus (7.4%). Collectively, these crops accounted for more than half 
of the included studies. Nut trees such as almond, walnut, and pecan, as 
well as tropical and subtropical crops including mango, avocado, cacao, 
and coffee, received moderate representation. By contrast, crops such as 
cashew, lychee, hazelnut, guava, pistachio, apricot, and pomegranate 
were only rarely investigated, while rambutan, quince, papaya, durian, 
and several other tropical fruits had no eligible LiDAR studies in the final 
corpus. This distribution reflects both the economic prominence of 
certain crops and the availability of structured orchard or plantation 
environments suitable for LiDAR experimentation. At the same time, it 
reveals a clear geographic and crop-system imbalance. Many tropical 
and subtropical perennial crops have complex, dense, and heteroge­
neous canopies, yet remain underrepresented in LiDAR research. These 
crops represent important opportunities for future studies focused on 
canopy structure, fruit-bearing surfaces, biomass, light interception, and 
management-relevant structural indicators.
2.2.3. Platforms in Practice
Platform usage is dominated by mobile or vehicle-mounted laser 
scanning (MLS), which account for 59.4% of reported studies, followed 
by terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) at 20.3% and airborne laser scanning 
(ALS) at 16.9% (including UAV-based mapping), while simultaneous 
localization and mapping (SLAM)-assisted laser scanning (SLS) remains 
niche at 3.4% (Fig. 4). These results indicate that ground-based and 
mobile systems constitute the backbone of orchard-focused LiDAR 
research, although ALS has rapidly gained prominence in recent years. 
Row-structured orchards are particularly well-suited to drive-through 
data collection. MLS platforms mounted on tractors or robotic vehicles 
provide repeatable trajectories for temporal monitoring and operate 
effectively under canopy conditions where GNSS signals are degraded, 
but SLAM algorithms can be applied. TLS is widely employed for high- 
resolution structural studies, phenotyping, and validation tasks. In 
contrast, ALS supports inventory efforts at larger scales, ranging from 
individual blocks to entire estates, enabling tree counts, height estima­
tion, and gap detection. Importantly, ALS complements ground-based 
platforms by capturing crown extents that are otherwise occluded in 
terrestrial perspectives. The most effective observability arises from 
combining platforms across scales. For example, TLS and MLS offer 
detailed information on crown interiors, while ALS provides complete

2.2.4. LiDAR Types Classified by Spatial Dimensionality
3D LiDAR dominated the literature, representing about 67.4% of 
studies, reflecting its capability for canopy reconstruction, crown 
delineation, structural trait retrieval, and navigation-relevant environ­
mental perception (Fig. 5). 2D LiDAR, comprising 30.3% of cases, 
remained important for row-structured mapping, local navigation, and 
under-canopy sensing, particularly in early orchard studies and low-cost 
robotic applications. 1D LiDAR was rare (2.3%) and largely limited to 
proximity detection, range triggering, or simple distance measurement 
tasks. The distribution illustrates a clear transition from early planar or 
profile-based sensing toward full three-dimensional characterization of 
orchard architecture. This transition is methodologically important 
because 3D LiDAR provides richer sampling geometry, supports more 
complete canopy representation, and enables point-cloud-based work­
flows for segmentation, registration, and trait extraction. Nevertheless, 
2D systems remain useful where the target task is simple, cost-sensitive, 
or operationally constrained.
2.2.5. Manufacturers and Sensor Models
The distribution of LiDAR manufacturers and sensor models illus­
trates a technological transition from early reliance on legacy two- 
dimensional planar scanners, such as the SICK LMS series (SICK AG, 
Waldkirch, Germany), toward wider adoption of multi-beam three- 
dimensional sensors, including the Velodyne VLP-16 (Velodyne LiDAR, 
San Jose, CA, USA) and the RIEGL VZ-400i (RIEGL, Horn, Austria) 
(Figs. 6 and 7). This transition has been accompanied by reductions in 
sensor cost, size, and deployment complexity, enabling broader use in 
agricultural robotics, UAV platforms, and mobile mapping systems. 
Model-level statistics (Fig. 7) further confirm the shift from planar 
scanning to volumetric canopy sampling. The transition from 2D to 3D 
modalities has improved not only point density but also sampling ge­
ometry, reducing angular aliasing in crown measurements and sup­
porting more reliable retrieval of crown-level metrics such as canopy 
volume, porosity, height, and obstacle structure. These developments 
have strengthened the role of LiDAR in both crop phenotyping and ro­
botic perception.

and volume (Fig. 8). These structural metrics were reported in 54 and 51 
studies, respectively. Functional parameters such as leaf area (26 
studies) and fruit yield (17 studies) were less common but are increas­
ingly represented. Navigation and obstacle detection were reported in 9 
studies, highlighting LiDAR’s additional role in supporting automation

This section reviews LiDAR sensors and their deployment platforms, 
emphasizing how different design choices affect data characteristics, 
measurement outputs, and application domains. The discussion is 
organized along five principal dimensions: dimensionality, ranging

each providing a distinct perspective on how LiDAR systems operate and 
are adapted to orchard applications. In addition to these classification 
dimensions, this section discusses field-condition constraints and prac­

affected by environmental conditions, canopy architecture, acquisition

3.2. Ranging Principles
LiDAR systems can be categorized according to their underlying 
ranging principles, which define how the system determines the distance 
between the sensor and the target. Broadly, this is achieved either by 
measuring the time-of-flight (ToF) of laser pulses or by analyzing the 
phase shift between emitted and received signals (Shan and Toth, 2018). 
A third category leverages photon-counting detectors to measure the 
arrival time of individual photons, while a fourth relies on coherent 
detection of a beat frequency between emitted and received signals. 
These distinct ranging mechanisms underpin four major types of LiDAR 
systems: pulse-based (linear-mode), phase-based, Geiger-mode (or 
photon-counting), and frequency-modulated continuous-wave LiDAR.
3.2.1. Pulse-based (Linear-mode) LiDAR
Pulse-based LiDAR systems operate by emitting discrete laser pulses 
and measuring the time interval between emission and detection of the 
returned signal. The distance is then calculated using the speed of light 
in combination with system calibration parameters. This time-of-flight 
approach is one of the most established and widely adopted methods 
in LiDAR technology, owing to its maturity, long effective range, strong 
adaptability to diverse environments, and resilience to ambient noise. It 
has become the standard technique for airborne topographic mapping 
and terrain measurement, and is also widely applied in autonomous 
driving for environmental perception and in SLAM-based mobile 
mapping.
In practice, airborne systems are typically employed for regional- 
scale high-precision terrain mapping, vehicle-mounted systems are 
used for traffic scene perception, and compact sensors are increasingly 
utilized in under-canopy or orchard environments for mobile mapping 
(Rivera et al., 2023; Southee et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2023). The point 
density and coverage efficiency of these systems are primarily deter­
mined by pulse repetition frequency, scanning geometry, and platform 
dynamics, while radiometric quality and geometric accuracy depend on 
factors such as pulse width, receiver bandwidth, beam divergence, and 
errors in orientation and calibration (Shan and Toth, 2018). In orchard 
and forestry applications, the multiple-return capability of pulse-based 
systems enables partial penetration of dense canopies, which facili­
tates the construction of digital terrain models and the characterization 
of vertical canopy structure (Ru et al., 2023). These features make pulse- 
based LiDAR particularly valuable for retrieving parameters such as tree 
height, crown volume, and canopy density, thereby supporting precision 
agricultural management.
3.2.2. Phase-based LiDAR
Phase-based LiDAR employs a continuous-wave laser that is sinu­
soidally modulated. Distance is inferred by measuring the phase shift 
between the emitted and received signals. This method enables high- 
accuracy range measurement, often at the millimeter level, making it 
particularly suitable for indoor mapping, high-resolution crop recon­
struction, and industrial metrology (Li et al., 2023). Commercial ex­
amples include scanners produced by FARO and Zoller+Fr¨ohlich. 
Despite its accuracy, its typical limitations include a relatively short 
effective range and higher sensitivity to stray light as well as surface 
reflectance characteristics, which necessitate trade-offs between acqui­
sition geometry and target material properties.
3.2.3. Geiger-mode (Photon-counting) LiDAR
Geiger-mode LiDAR systems employ avalanche photodiode (APD) 
arrays operating in Geiger mode to detect single-photon returns. Unlike 
conventional LiDAR systems that estimate distance based on the 
strength and timing of the return signal, Geiger-mode systems register 
only the arrival time of individual photons. This principle enables ultra- 
high sensitivity, facilitating efficient data acquisition from high-altitude 
platforms with minimal return energy (Degnan, 2002). Such charac­
teristics make Geiger-mode LiDAR particularly suitable for large-scale, 
high-resolution remote sensing applications, including global alti­
metric measurement and vegetation structure characterization, with 
NASA’s ICESat-2 ATLAS system serving as a representative example (Li 
et al., 2020; Neumann et al., 2019). However, these systems are 
intrinsically more sensitive to background noise and therefore require 
advanced signal processing algorithms to effectively filter noise and 
accurately reconstruct three-dimensional information. In commercial 
single-photon systems, ranging accuracy may be reduced relative to 
conventional linear-mode LiDAR under conditions of complex surface 
materials or large incidence angles, necessitating compensation through 
system calibration and post-processing (Degnan, 2002; Stoker et al., 
2016).
3.2.4. Frequency-modulated Continuous-wave LiDAR
Frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) LiDAR estimates 
distance by transmitting a continuously chirped laser signal and 
measuring the coherent beat frequency between the emitted and 
received optical signals (Wu et al., 2024). In contrast to conventional 
direct time-of-flight LiDAR, FMCW LiDAR generally relies on coherent 
detection, in which the returned signal is mixed with a local oscillator to 
extract frequency-domain information related to target range. With 
appropriate modulation schemes, especially triangular chirps, the beat 
frequencies can encode both range and Doppler-induced radial velocity, 
allowing simultaneous distance and velocity estimation from the 
returned signal (Poulton et al., 2017; Bianconi et al., 2025). This capa­
bility is relevant to agricultural robotics and dynamic orchard envi­
ronments because radial velocity information may help separate static 
canopy structure from moving branches, workers, vehicles, or machin­
ery. Doppler velocity measurements from FMCW LiDAR have been 
shown to improve LiDAR-only odometry and motion estimation in 
geometrically challenging environments (Wu et al., 2023), suggesting 
potential relevance to orchard navigation, where pulse-based multi- 
beam LiDAR is already widely used for mapping, obstacle avoidance, 
and autonomous robot guidance (Jiang and Ahamed, 2023; Li et al., 
2025). However, FMCW LiDAR remains uncommon in orchard studies 
because of its higher system complexity, coherence and modulation 
requirements, cost and integration challenges, limited agricultural 
validation, and the need for workflows that can exploit both range and 
velocity information (Riemensberger et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2024; 
Bianconi et al., 2025). Thus, it should be viewed as a promising but still 
emerging technology for motion-aware orchard perception.

3.3. Backscatter Sampling and Measurement Attributes
LiDAR backscatter can be digitized either as discrete returns, which 
record a few peaks per emitted pulse, or as the entire analog echo 
waveform. These sampling modes determine the information available 
for ranging, radiometry, target decomposition, and downstream anal­
ysis, and they also influence storage formats and processing workflows 
(Mallet and Bretar, 2008; Wehr and Lohr, 1999). From a data-output 
perspective, LiDAR measurements should be considered not only as 
point coordinates but also as attribute-rich observations. Depending on 
the system, available outputs may include three-dimensional co­
ordinates, intensity, return number, number of returns, scan angle, 
timestamp, scanner channel, waveform amplitude, echo width, back­
scatter cross section, and, for some emerging FMCW systems, radial 
velocity. These attributes determine whether LiDAR data can support 
simple geometric reconstruction, radiometric interpretation, vertical 
canopy 
profiling, 
motion-aware 
perception, 
or 
multi-temporal 
monitoring.
3.3.1. Discrete Return LiDAR
The receiver detects local maxima in the backscattered signal and 
records one or more discrete returns per emitted pulse, typically with 
per-return attributes such as 3D coordinates, intensity, return number, 
number of returns, scan angle, and timestamp where available (Wehr 
and Lohr, 1999; Wagner et al., 2006). Because discrete-return point 
clouds are compact, computationally efficient, and broadly supported by 
standard formats such as LAS 1.4/1.5 (ASPRS, 2025) and common point- 
cloud software, they remain widely used in orchard applications, 
including canopy height estimation, crown delineation, tree inventory, 
row mapping, terrain normalization, and mobile robotic perception 
(Hadas et al., 2019; Rosell et al., 2009; Rosell and Sanz, 2012; Under­
wood et al., 2016).
However, discrete-return data simplify complex echo structures into 
a limited number of detected peaks, which can reduce sensitivity to fine 
branches, small canopy gaps, internal foliage layers, and partially 
occluded lower-canopy structures (Liu et al., 2026a). Intensity can 
provide supplementary information for target discrimination, registra­
tion, or classification, but raw intensity is affected by range, incidence 
angle, target reflectance, atmospheric attenuation, receiver gain, 
wavelength, and sensor-specific calibration; therefore, cross-sensor, 
cross-platform, multi-date, or cross-block intensity comparisons 
require normalization and should not be interpreted as direct biological 
measurements without calibration (H¨ofle and Pfeifer, 2007; Kaasalainen 
et al., 2011a).
Return number and number of returns are especially useful in or­
chard environments because they provide indirect information on can­
opy penetration and vertical complexity. First returns are generally 
associated with upper canopy surfaces, whereas later returns may 
represent internal branches, lower foliage, trunks, or ground surfaces. 
Nevertheless, return structure is affected by beam divergence, pulse 
width, footprint size, canopy density, scan angle, and receiver sensi­
tivity. Return-based metrics should therefore be interpreted together 
with acquisition geometry and sensor specifications.
3.3.2. Full Waveform LiDAR
The receiver samples the analog echo as a function of time. Subse­
quent deconvolution, peak detection, and Gaussian or physically based 
decomposition yield multiple echoes, echo widths, amplitudes, and in 
some systems, an estimate of backscatter cross section (Mallet and 
Bretar, 2008; Wagner et al., 2006). The principal advantages are richer 
information on vertical canopy structure and improved echo detection 
in complex vegetation, together with the possibility of radiometric 
modeling after system and atmospheric correction. These characteristics 
make full-waveform LiDAR especially valuable for estimating canopy 
vertical distribution, gap fraction, biomass, foliage arrangement, and 
structure-to-function relationships. The limitations include larger data 
volumes, greater computational demands, and a stronger dependence on 
rigorous radiometric calibration (Pirotti, 2011).
In orchard applications, full-waveform LiDAR is most useful when 
the objective is to characterize vertical canopy organization or energy 
attenuation rather than only tree height or crown boundary. However, 
waveform processing remains less standardized than discrete-return 
processing, and waveform-derived traits can be difficult to compare 
across sensors unless waveform decomposition, calibration, and vali­
dation procedures are clearly documented.
3.4. Structural Configuration
LiDAR systems can also be systematically classified according to 
their structural configuration, which refers to the physical design and 
the degree of mechanical movement in the beam steering mechanism. 
This classification is particularly relevant in real-world deployment, as it 
directly affects factors such as durability, cost, scalability, form factor, 
and suitability for dynamic environments (Jaster et al., 2023). Based on 
the mechanical complexity, LiDAR systems are typically categorized 
into three types: mechanical LiDAR, hybrid (or semi-solid-state) LiDAR, 
and solid-state LiDAR.
3.4.1. Mechanical LiDAR
Mechanical LiDAR systems use rotating components, either the 
entire sensor head or a spinning mirror, to direct laser pulses across the 
surrounding environment. This design allows the potential for a full 360- 
degree horizontal field of view and is well established in applications 
requiring long-range and high-resolution scanning. Commercial prod­
ucts include Hesai XT series (Hesai Technology, Shanghai, China), 
Ouster OS (Velodyne VLP) (Ouster, San Francisco, CA, USA), and RIEGL 
VUX (RIEGL, Horn, Austria). Mechanical LiDAR systems typically offer 
ranges up to hundreds of meters, comprehensive scanning coverage, and 
a mature ecosystem with strong software support. However, the use of 
moving parts increases the risk of mechanical failure, limits robustness 
under vibration, and elevates system cost and size, making them less 
ideal for compact or consumer-grade applications (Jaster et al., 2023).
3.4.2. Hybrid (semi-solid-state) LiDAR
Hybrid or semi-solid-state LiDAR represents a transitional category 
that reduces mechanical complexity while retaining some level of beam 
steering flexibility. These systems often use Micro Electro Mechanical 
Systems (MEMS) mirrors, optical wedges, or small oscillating compo­
nents to scan the laser beam (Royo and Ballesta-Garcia, 2019). The 
reduced reliance on bulky rotating parts leads to a smaller form factor, 
lower power consumption, and enhanced durability, making them 
well-suited for automotive-grade deployment. Commercial products 
include Hesai QT series (Hesai Technology, Shanghai, China), Livox 
Horizon (Livox, Shenzhen, China), and RoboSense M1 (RoboSense, 
Shenzhen, China). Although hybrid LiDARs generally do not achieve a 
full 360-degree field of view, they strike a balance between perfor­
mance, cost, and scalability, making them a practical choice for 
forward-facing scanning in autonomous systems.
3.4.3. Solid-state LiDAR
Solid-state LiDAR systems eliminate all mechanical or moving parts 
in the scanning process, relying instead on optical phased arrays, flash 
LiDAR sensor arrays, or electro-optic beam steering technologies (Liang 
et al., 2024). These systems are designed for mass production, 
combining high robustness with compact size, low power consumption, 
and rapid scanning capabilities. Two main types of solid-state LiDAR 
exist: a) Flash LiDAR captures an entire scene in a single pulse using a 
two-dimensional detector array, similar to a camera with depth 
perception; and b) Optical phased array-based LiDAR electronically 
steers the beam using interference patterns across a waveguide array. 
Such systems are increasingly adopted in consumer electronics, robotics, 
and next-generation autonomous vehicles, where compactness and

3.5. LiDAR Platforms
Platform selection influences every subsequent decision in a LiDAR 
workflow, affecting coverage and revisit interval, attainable resolution 
and accuracy, operational cost and logistics, as well as the range of 
ecological or agronomic variables that can be retrieved (Coops et al., 
2021; Goodbody et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2025). In orchard systems, no 
single platform dominates. Ground-based systems provide millimeter to 
centimeter structural detail within plots (Calders et al., 2020; Liu et al., 
2025). Airborne systems on crewed aircraft and uncrewed aircraft 
achieve a balance between area and resolution for stand-to-farm scale 
mapping (Donager et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2026a). Spaceborne systems 
deliver a globally consistent sampling of canopy structure and can be 
fused with airborne and ground observations to support inventory, 
habitat modelling, and precision management (Neumann et al., 2019).

sponding point cloud densities is illustrated in Fig. 11, complementing

3.5.1. Ground LiDAR
Terrestrial LiDAR (TLS, fixed-station): Static TLS provides highly 
detailed and geometrically rigorous reconstructions of tree architecture 
in a non-destructive manner. In orchard and forest plots, it can resolve 
stem form, scaffold branches, foliage distribution, and even occluded 
understory with centimeter to millimeter precision, thereby supporting 
trait-level phenotyping, canopy light interception modelling, and the 
calibration and validation of airborne and spaceborne products 
(O’Sullivan et al., 2021). Practical considerations include the need for 
multi-scan registration, which can be performed with or without artifi­
cial targets, as well as shadowing behind dense foliage and relatively 
long field acquisition times compared with mobile platforms. Never­
theless, TLS is widely regarded as the gold standard for providing 
structural ground truth in biomechanical studies and radiative transfer 
modelling (Disney et al., 2019).
Mobile Scan LiDAR (MLS, vehicle and backpack): Kinematic MLS 
systems, which are commonly mounted on a vehicle or robotic plat­
forms, minimize setup requirements and enable rapid data acquisition 
along orchard rows or farm roads. With modern GNSS/IMU integration 
and loop-closure strip adjustment, they can typically achieve 
centimeter-level relative consistency, although absolute accuracy re­
mains dependent on ground control and trajectory quality (Muhojoki 
et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024c). To address challenges in GNSS- 
degraded orchard environments, SLAM techniques are increasingly 
being incorporated into MLS platforms, including backpack and hand­
held units, thereby enhancing their capability for understory and under- 
canopy mapping (Zhang et al., 2020). By exploiting geometric loop 
closures together with tree and ground primitives, LiDAR–inertial SLAM 
can mitigate GNSS outages and the aliasing effects of repetitive row 
patterns. Continuous-time and factor-graph formulations have become 
increasingly robust in orchard-like settings, providing drift-bounded 
local maps that support trunk detection, diameter at breast height esti­
mation, and crown segmentation (Teng et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 
2024b).
3.5.2. Airborne LiDAR
Airplane-based LiDAR (ALS): Airplane-based ALS flown on crewed 
aircraft remains a widely adopted backbone for regional terrain and 
canopy mapping because it provides consistent calibration, high geo­
metric quality, and efficient coverage (Goodbody et al., 2021). National 
programs such as the USGS 3D Elevation Program specify a minimum 
Quality Level 2 with an aggregate nominal pulse density of at least 2 
points per square meter and a vertical accuracy of about 10 cm RMSE in 
the z direction, enabling wall-to-wall digital elevation models, canopy 
height metrics, and repeat inventories over large extents (USGS, 2025). 
In orchard applications, ALS is valued for its high point cloud quality 
and ability to provide consistent canopy height and cover metrics across 
entire production regions. Compared with TLS and MLS, which focus on 
plot or block-level structural detail, ALS delivers seamless large-scale 
coverage, making it particularly effective for integrating orchard 
blocks into regional inventories and for benchmarking canopy condi­
tions at farm to landscape scales (Donager et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2020).
UAV-based LiDAR (ALS): UAV-based ALS bridges the gap between 
plot-level detail and regional mapping, occupying the farm-to-estate

3.5.3. Spaceborne LiDAR
GEDI (ISS): NASA’s Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation 
(GEDI) provides globally consistent large footprint waveform measure­
ments, with footprints of approximately 25 m in diameter, a spacing of 
about 60 m along track, and a separation of roughly 600 m across track 
between beams, collected simultaneously across eight laser tracks 
(Dubayah et al., 2020). GEDI’s relative height (RH) metrics and Level 4A 
biomass models enable the estimation of forest structure, growth, and 
aboveground biomass at stand to landscape scales. These products are 
frequently fused with airborne LiDAR to generate wall-to-wall canopy 
height and biomass maps or to support change detection analyses 
(Guerra-Hern´andez and Pascual, 2021).
ICESat-2 (ATLAS): The Advanced Topographic Laser Altimeter 
System (ATLAS) on board ICESat-2 is a photon counting LiDAR that 
splits a single 532-nanometre laser into six beams arranged as three pairs 
of strong and weak beams (Markus et al., 2017). The instrument samples 
the Earth’s surface approximately every 0.7 m along track, and the beam 
geometry is specifically designed to support accurate slope recovery. 
On-orbit spot diameters have been reported to be close to 11 m, 
compared with the design specification of about 17 m for the 85 percent 
energy enclosure (Markus et al., 2017). The vegetation product ATL08 
provides along track measurements of terrain and canopy height, which 
are widely used as a sparse sampling layer to calibrate and validate 
airborne LiDAR maps or to extend structural models in regions with 
limited data availability (Neuenschwander and Pitts, 2019).
Overall, platform selection determines the balance among point 
density, viewing geometry, canopy penetration, spatial coverage, revisit 
frequency, and operational cost. Ground systems provide detailed 
under-canopy and trunk-level information but are sensitive to occlusion, 
trajectory quality, and row accessibility. UAV and airborne systems 
provide efficient canopy-scale coverage but may under-sample lower 
canopy and trunk structure. Satellite LiDAR provides regional context 
but is generally unsuitable for individual-tree orchard management. 
Therefore, platform choice should be matched to the target trait, 
required spatial resolution, and validation standard rather than selected 
solely by sensor availability.
3.6. Field-condition Constraints and Environmental Robustness
LiDAR performance in orchards is strongly affected by environ­
mental and field conditions, including precipitation, fog, dust, strong 
background illumination, high humidity, wind, and canopy motion. 
These factors influence signal transmission, target backscatter, return 
intensity, valid point density, noise level, and registration stability. 
Because LiDAR surveys in orchards are usually conducted in open-field 
environments, environmental robustness should be considered an inte­
gral component of system performance rather than an external source of 
random error.
Precipitation, fog, mist, and airborne particles can degrade LiDAR 
measurements through atmospheric attenuation, scattering, and false or 
spurious returns. Rain can reduce return intensity, decrease valid point 
counts, shorten effective detection range, and generate returns from 
raindrops, while fog and mist can scatter laser energy along the beam 
path and produce false returns before the target surface (Kutila et al., 
2018; Haider et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023). In orchards, these effects 
may be compounded by canopy occlusion and by changes in leaf-surface 
moisture after rainfall, which can make intensity-based interpretation 
less stable. Dust and aerosols generated by tractor operation, harvesting, 
mowing, spraying, or movement on unpaved roads can similarly intro­
duce near-field noise, attenuate canopy returns, and contaminate optical 
windows. These issues are especially relevant for ground and mobile 
platforms operating close to the canopy. Practical mitigation includes

Background illumination and acquisition timing also affect LiDAR 
data interpretation, particularly when intensity is used. The influence of 
sunlight depends on wavelength, detector design, optical filtering, 
receiver sensitivity, and modulation strategy (H¨ofle and Pfeifer, 2007; 
Sanchiz-Viel et al., 2020). Although active LiDAR is generally less sen­
sitive to ambient illumination than passive imaging sensors, strong 
background light can still reduce signal-to-noise ratio for some receiver 
designs. Therefore, consistent acquisition timing and radiometric 
caution are recommended for multi-temporal studies, especially when 
LiDAR intensity is used as a proxy for canopy or fruit properties. More 
generally, intensity values should be interpreted with caution because 
they are influenced not only by target properties but also by range, 
incidence angle, atmospheric conditions, and sensor-specific calibration 
(H¨ofle and Pfeifer, 2007; Kaasalainen et al., 2011a,b; Sanchiz-Viel et al., 
2020).
Wind-induced canopy motion is particularly important in orchards 
because leaves, branches, and fruit can move during scanning. For static 
terrestrial laser scanning, wind violates the assumption that the scene 
remains unchanged during acquisition, leading to blurred canopy 
boundaries, duplicated branches, misalignment between overlapping 
scans, and distorted merged point clouds (Albert et al., 2025). For mo­
bile LiDAR and laser-scanning-based SLAM, non-rigid canopy motion 
can reduce feature stability, introduce local misregistration, generate 
ghost points, bias canopy volume estimates, and degrade loop closure. 
Similar issues are relevant to fruit detection and canopy characterization 
because moving branches and leaves alter visibility and occlusion pat­
terns; for example, forced air flow has been used in apple orchards to 
intentionally modify occlusion conditions for LiDAR-based fruit detec­
tion and canopy geometric characterization (Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020). 
Therefore, wind speed or qualitative wind condition should be reported 
when canopy traits, crown boundaries, fruit visibility, or multi-temporal 
structural change are evaluated.
These field-condition effects have direct implications for validation 
and operational deployment. LiDAR studies should report weather 
conditions, canopy moisture status, wind conditions, platform speed, 
sensor cleaning procedures, acquisition timing, and filtering thresholds 
used to remove environmental noise. Robustness should be evaluated 
not only by accuracy under ideal conditions but also by performance 
degradation under realistic orchard field conditions. This is particularly 
important for studies intended to support operational phenotyping, 
autonomous navigation, variable-rate management, or repeated moni­
toring across seasons and orchard blocks.
3.7. Key Implications for Sensor Selection in Orchard LiDAR Studies
Sensor selection in orchard and perennial tree crop studies should be 
guided by the intended application, spatial scale, target structural 
attribute, and validation requirement rather than by nominal sensor 
class alone. The most appropriate system is therefore not necessarily the 
highest-density or most advanced sensor, but the configuration that can 
provide sufficient structural information, repeatability, operational 
feasibility, and validation quality for the target decision or research 
objective.
In general, short-range 2D or mobile 3D LiDAR is suitable for canopy- 
wall profiling, variable-rate spraying, and robotic navigation because 
these applications require row-wise geometry, canopy envelope infor­
mation, obstacle perception, or real-time sensing rather than complete 
tree reconstruction (Rosell et al., 2009; Rosell and Sanz, 2012; Under­
wood et al., 2016). TLS remains valuable for detailed structural recon­
struction and reference validation, especially for trunk, branch, and 
lower-canopy traits, but its scalability is limited. UAV-based LiDAR is 
increasingly suitable for orchard-scale tree height, canopy cover, crown 
volume, inventory, and gap detection, although it may be biased toward 
upper-canopy surfaces and often requires complementary field or TLS 
validation (Estornell et al., 2020; Hadas et al., 2019). Airborne and 
spaceborne LiDAR can extend structural monitoring to estate, regional, 
or landscape scales, but lower spatial resolution, sparse sampling, geo­
location uncertainty, and mixed-footprint effects limit their use for 
direct individual-tree management (Guerra-Hern´andez and Pascual, 
2021; Zhu et al., 2023).
Based on these application- and scale-dependent trade-offs, Table 4
summarizes sensor-selection guidance according to the type of structural 
information, spatial coverage, and operational readiness required for 
orchard and perennial tree crop applications. The table emphasizes that 
LiDAR configuration should be selected according to the required 
sensing task rather than treated as a simple comparison among plat­
forms. In practice, many orchard studies may require complementary 
configurations, such as UAV-based LiDAR for block-scale canopy map­
ping combined with TLS or MLS for reference validation, lower-canopy 
characterization, or algorithm benchmarking.
From a technology-readiness perspective, terrestrial LiDAR is mature 
as a reference and validation tool, but its operational scalability is 
limited. Mobile LiDAR is relatively mature for canopy profiling, tree-row 
mapping, obstacle detection, and sprayer control because it operates at 
short range and can be integrated with agricultural machinery. UAV- 
based LiDAR is increasingly mature for orchard-scale canopy height, 
crown volume, inventory, and gap detection, provided that flight plan­
ning, calibration, trajectory processing, and ground control are carefully 
implemented. Spaceborne LiDAR is mature for broad vegetation- 
structure monitoring but remains low in readiness for individual-tree 
orchard management. Full-waveform trait retrieval, photon-counting 
LiDAR for crop-specific management, and FMCW LiDAR for agricul­
tural robotics remain promising but require additional orchard-specific 
validation across crops, seasons, platforms, and commercial production 
conditions.
3.8. Perspective of Sensor Development
Driven by rapid commercialization in autonomous driving, LiDAR 
systems are entering a phase characterized by miniaturization, reduced 
cost, enhanced performance, and architectural diversification across 
operating wavelengths and scanning modalities (Holzhüter et al., 2023; 
Liang et al., 2024). Recent automotive LiDAR commercialization further 
indicates a rapid increase in angular resolution and spatial sampling 
density, with several high-channel sensors moving toward image-level 
or near-image-level perception. Although these systems were devel­
oped primarily for advanced driver-assistance and autonomous driving 
rather than agriculture, their mass-production trajectory suggests that 
compact, high-resolution LiDAR architectures may become increasingly 
accessible for orchard robotics, canopy phenotyping, and machinery 
perception. These trends are directly enabling the next generation of 
LiDAR sensors for agriculture, particularly in orchards and perennial 
cropping systems, where compact, robust, and high-resolution devices 
are essential for under-canopy measurements, frequent monitoring, and 
large-scale deployment. In this section, we discuss three key dimensions 
of this evolution: sensor miniaturization and photonic integration, new 
beam-steering modalities, and wavelength diversification with associ­
ated performance improvements.
Sensor miniaturization and photonic integration. Advances in 
silicon photonics and photonic–electronic integration are driving sig­
nificant reductions in the size, weight, power, and cost (SWaP-C) of 
LiDAR sensors. Silicon-based integrated LiDAR systems, in which pho­
tonic integrated circuits are co-designed with electronic drivers and 
receivers, enable on-chip beam steering, detection, and control, thereby 
advancing toward chip-scale coherent and direct-detection architectures 
(Hu et al., 2023; Lukashchuk et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2024). Notably, a 
recent demonstration of a photonic–electronic frequency-modulated

compact and stable coherent sensing (Lukashchuk et al., 2024).
New beam steering modalities. The market is shifting away from 
spinning mechanical scanners toward MEMS mirrors, photonic- 
integrated optical phased arrays (OPAs), and compact prism or 
dispersion-based steering (Wang et al., 2024). These approaches reduce 
large moving assemblies, thereby improving robustness and lifetime, 
while retaining wide field-of-view options through hybrid solid-state 
and fully solid-state implementations (Holzhüter et al., 2023).
Wavelength diversification and performance improvement. 
Commercial LiDARs commonly operate around 905 nm, 940 nm, 1064 
nm, and 1550 nm. The selection reflects a balance among eye safety, 
atmospheric transmission, and the availability of sources and detectors 
(Holzhüter et al., 2023). An increasing shift from 905 nm to 1550 nm is 
evident, as the latter permits higher eye-safe emission power and sup­
ports longer detection ranges. At 1550 nm, the maturity of fiber and 
III–V laser sources pairs naturally with InGaAs/InP APD or SPAD re­
ceivers, enabling both extended-range direct detection and coherent 
FMCW architecture (Holzhüter et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024; Lukash­
chuk et al., 2024).
Looking forward, LiDAR sensor development should be evaluated 
not only by increased point density or reduced sensor cost, but also by 
the reliability of measurement attributes, adverse-condition robustness, 
calibration transparency, and task-specific validation. These priorities 
are essential for translating LiDAR from experimental canopy mapping 
to operational orchard monitoring, robotic perception, variable-rate 
management, and digital orchard modeling.
4. LiDAR Data Processing Workflow and Algorithms
4.1. LiDAR Data Processing Workflow
A standard end-to-end workflow in orchard studies proceeds from 
data acquisition to preprocessing, registration or trajectory refinement, 
ground classification, height normalization, segmentation, feature 
extraction, and application-specific modeling (Calders et al., 2020; 
Rivera et al., 2023; Vandendaele et al., 2022). However, LiDAR data 
processing should not be treated as a generic point-cloud pipeline. The 
workflow is strongly conditioned by the sensor and platform configu­
ration discussed in Section 3. Airborne laser scanning provides regional 
coverage with systematic viewing geometry but may under-sample 
trunks and lower canopy layers. UAV-based LiDAR provides high- 
density canopy observations and flexible revisit capability but is sensi­
tive to trajectory error, strip misalignment, wind, and heterogeneous 
scan angles. Terrestrial laser scanning yields dense local point clouds 
and is valuable for validation, but occlusion and multi-scan registration 
remain critical. Mobile and handheld laser scanning inside orchard rows 
can efficiently capture trunks, canopy walls, trellis systems, and under- 
canopy structure, but these platforms are especially sensitive to trajec­
tory drift, repetitive row geometry, and motion artifacts.
Preprocessing typically addresses metadata normalization, removal 
of spikes and outliers, initial denoising, ground classification for height 
normalization, preliminary radiometric checks on intensity, and scale- 
aware downsampling. Harmonization of spatial and temporal base­
lines is essential. Practitioners should choose appropriate geodetic and 
vertical datums, enforce tight time synchronization among LiDAR, 
GNSS, and inertial measurement unit (IMU), and estimate a smooth 
trajectory together with boresight and lever-arm parameters (Guo et al., 
2023). Strip adjustment reduces residual inter-strip misalignment and is 
particularly valuable for UAV missions over orchards and plantations; 
DEM-based point-to-plane alignment followed by pose-graph optimiza­
tion has proven effective (Chen et al., 2021).
Quality control can be structured as a task-dependent checklist. First, 
confirm reference consistency, including coordinate reference system, 
vertical datum, time stamps, and sensor metadata. Second, calibrate or 
validate boresight and lever arm parameters and assess range bias. 
Third, evaluate trajectory quality with dilution of precision, loop clo­
sures, strip overlap, and trajectory residuals. Fourth, diagnose

4.2. Point Cloud Registration and Alignment
Registration aligns multi-strip, multi-station, or multi-temporal point 
clouds into a common coordinate frame. In orchard and perennial tree 
crop studies, registration is not only a geometric operation but also an 
important source of trait uncertainty, can bias tree height, crown width, 
canopy volume, gap detection, and multi-temporal change analysis. 
Registration is especially challenging in orchards because tree rows 
often contain repeated geometric patterns, dense foliage may obscure 
stable trunk or ground features, and wind-induced canopy motion can 
violate the rigid-scene assumption used by many scan-matching 
algorithms.
Registration algorithms can be grouped into several practical fam­
ilies. First, ICP variants refine local alignment by minimizing residuals 
between corresponding points, planes, or local covariance structures 
(Besl and McKay, 1992). Point-to-point ICP is simple and representation- 
independent but requires good initialization and sufficient overlap. 
Point-to-plane ICP generally converges faster on locally planar surfaces 
and is useful for strip refinement where normals are reliable. General­
ized ICP extends this family by modeling local covariance in both point 
clouds and often improves convergence on rough terrain (Segal et al., 
2009). Second, Normal Distributions Transform (NDT) represents local 
point distributions as Gaussian models within voxels and can provide a 
wider basin of convergence than ICP in some scenarios (Biber and 
Strasser, 2003; Magnusson, 2009). Third, primitive-assisted or 
feature-based alignment uses stable orchard structures, such as trunks, 
trellis posts, ground surfaces, and row lines, as anchors for coarse 
initialization or refinement, often with RANSAC-based fitting when 
repeatable geometric features are available (Fischler and Bolles, 1981; 
Schnabel et al., 2007). These method families, together with 
SLAM-based trajectory estimation for mobile mapping, are summarized 
in Table 5.
For mobile and handheld LiDAR, registration is closely linked to 
SLAM. Laser-scanning-based SLAM estimates the platform trajectory 
while building a local or global map (Zhang and Singh, 2014; Shan and 
Toth, 2018). In orchards, SLAM drift is commonly caused by weak GNSS 
reception, repetitive row geometry, insufficient cross-row geometric 
diversity, smooth canopy walls, moving foliage, and limited loop- 
closure opportunities (Jiang and Ahamed, 2023; Wang et al., 2024). 
Wind-induced non-rigid branch movement further violates the rigid- 
scene assumption used by many scan-matching algorithms (Albert 
et al., 2025). Under these conditions, foliage points may become un­
stable correspondences, causing local misregistration, ghosting, map 
thickening, or biased canopy volume. Practical countermeasures include 
using trunk, pole, terrain, and row-line primitives as stable features; 
incorporating GNSS/IMU constraints where available; adding cross-row 
or headland passes to improve loop closure; down-weighting foliage 
during registration; using robust loss functions; and evaluating residuals 
on independent check patches rather than only within optimized 
correspondences.

4.3. Noise Filtering
Point clouds acquired in orchard environments often exhibit isolated 
outliers from multipath or mixed returns, density gradients with range, 
and motion blur caused by wind or platform dynamics. Statistical outlier 
removal eliminates points whose mean neighbor distance exceeds a 
threshold set as a multiple of the neighborhood standard deviation, 
while radius-based criteria require a minimum number of neighbors 
within a search radius calibrated to local point spacing. Both filters are 
widely implemented in the Point Cloud Library (Rusu and Cousins, 
2011). Edge-preserving smoothing adapts bilateral filtering to unorga­
nized point sets, ensuring that along-normal smoothing does not prop­
agate across sharp bark edges; anisotropic formulations further stabilize 
ridges and stems (Digne and de Franchis, 2017). Moving Least Squares 
reconstructs a local polynomial surface with robust weighting, offering 
effective smoothing for bark and crown envelopes, although large 
window sizes risk over-shrinking thin branches; the point-set surface 
framework provides theoretical stability guidance (Alexa et al., 2003). 
Parameterization should be matched to the data scale and to the voxel 
size selected for uniformization.
In mobile scanning, wind-induced non-rigid motion introduces 
additional challenges, which require conservative smoothing and short 
temporal windows. Practitioners are advised to retain raw data for 
auditability and to tune filters on small validation patches, with 
particular attention to preserving edge fidelity on stems and trellis 
infrastructure (Rusu and Cousins, 2011). Common filtering methods and 
their orchard-specific implications are summarized in Table 6.
In orchard point clouds, filtering should not aim only to maximize 
visual smoothness. Excessive smoothing can remove thin branches, 
sparse canopy gaps, fruit-bearing surfaces, or trunk edges that are bio­
logically important. Therefore, filtering parameters should be evaluated 
against the intended trait. For example, canopy volume estimation may 
tolerate moderate smoothing of outer crown envelopes, whereas trunk 
detection, branch reconstruction, and leaf–wood separation require 
stronger edge preservation. A recommended practice is to tune filters on 
small annotated validation patches and report both noise-removal rate 
and feature-preservation behavior.
4.4. Ground Point Classification
Ground filtering is fundamental to the generation of digital elevation 
models, height normalization, and biomass estimation. The Progressive 
Morphological Filter (PMF) progressively enlarges the window size 
while applying elevation difference thresholds, and is particularly 
effective in gentle to moderately undulating terrain (Zhang et al., 2003). 
The Cloth Simulation Filter (CSF) inverts the point cloud and simulates a 
falling cloth with adjustable grid resolution and rigidity. It has demon­
strated robustness across varying vegetation densities and is relatively 
straightforward to parameterize (Zhang et al., 2016). Multiscale Cur­
vature Classification (MCC) identifies non-ground points based on pos­
itive curvature across multiple scales and is well suited for rugged, 
forested terrain (Evans and Hudak, 2007). The Improved Simple 
Morphological Filter (ISMF) integrates progressive window growth and 
slope factors with inpainting to mitigate voids, yielding competitive 
performance in mixed terrain (Pingel et al., 2013).
Parameter selection should reflect both topography and sampling 
characteristics. For the PMF, a starting window of approximately 3 to 5 
m with an incremental step of 1 to 2 m and slope-aware elevation 
thresholds is common (Zhang et al., 2003). For the CSF, the cloth grid 
resolution should be set close to the local point spacing, with reduced 
rigidity in rugged terrain, and iterations continued until convergence 
while avoiding over-smoothing of micro-relief features (Zhang et al., 
2016). MCC requires careful selection of scales to balance sensitivity 
to shrubs against the preservation of narrow berms (Evans and Hudak, 
2007). The ISMF benefits from slope-aware tuning to prevent terraced 
step artifacts, and evaluation using independent check patches is rec­
ommended, with both digital elevation model error and misclassifica­
tion rates reported (Pingel et al., 2013). Key characteristics, parameter 
considerations, and comparative strengths of representative ground 
filtering algorithms are summarized in Table 7.
In orchards, ground classification is complicated by tree rows, raised 
beds, berms, irrigation structures, leaf litter, weeds, and low branches. 
Misclassification of low canopy as ground can compress normalized tree 
height and underestimate lower-canopy density, whereas omission of 
true ground points can bias digital terrain models and canopy height 
models. Ground filtering should therefore be evaluated not only by DEM 
error but also by downstream effects on tree height, crown base height, 
canopy volume, and row-scale terrain normalization. This is particularly 
important for orchards with raised beds, terracing, drainage ditches, or 
sloped terrain.
4.5. Point Cloud Segmentation and Classification
Segmentation divides a point cloud into geometrically coherent 
units, whereas classification assigns semantic labels such as ground, 
trunk, canopy, fruit, trellis, machinery, or other infrastructure. The 
development of LiDAR data segmentation has broadly evolved from 
rule-based and geometry-driven approaches toward feature-based ma­
chine learning and, more recently, deep learning. Early approaches 
commonly used voxelization, morphological filtering, canopy height 
models, local maxima, region growing, Euclidean clustering, and 
primitive fitting (Rosell et al., 2009; Rusu and Cousins, 2011; Schnabel 
et al., 2007; Qiu et al., 2024). These methods remain useful because they 
are interpretable, require limited labeled data, and can be tuned for 
specific orchard geometries. A comparative overview of representative 
segmentation and classification approaches is provided in Table 8.
Geometry-driven methods are especially effective when orchard 
structure is regular and class definitions are simple. Region growing 
based on smoothness merges neighboring points when surface normal 
differences and curvature remain below predefined thresholds, whereas 
Euclidean or density-based clustering separates objects after ground 
removal and is useful for isolating trunks, posts, or separated crowns 
(Rusu and Cousins, 2011; Rabbani et al., 2006). Supervoxel over- 
segmentation produces boundary-aware units that can reduce labeling 
cost and serve as input for machine learning; voxel cloud connectivity 
segmentation is a representative approach that uses voxel relationships

Deep learning architectures for point clouds can be organized by 
their input representation and feature-learning strategy. Point-based 
models operate directly on unordered point sets. PointNet introduced 
permutation-invariant point processing, whereas PointNet++ extended 
this framework through hierarchical local grouping and multi-scale 
feature learning, making it better suited to irregularly sampled canopy 
geometry (Qi et al., 2017a; Qi et al., 2017b). Graph-based models, such 
as Dynamic Graph CNN, use local neighborhood graphs and edge 
convolution to capture relational structure among nearby points (Wang

learnable convolutional kernels in Euclidean space and have shown 
strong performance for complex outdoor scenes (Thomas et al., 2019; 
Thomas et al., 2024). Efficient large-scale models, such as RandLA-Net, 
use random sampling and local feature aggregation to reduce memory 
and computational burden for large point clouds (Hu et al., 2020).

For orchard applications, model selection should be guided by task 
requirements rather than by architectural novelty alone. Tree-level 
crown segmentation requires models capable of representing crown 
boundaries, canopy gradients, and multi-scale structural variation, 
whereas trunk, trellis, and scaffold-branch segmentation often depends 
on preserving local geometric continuity. Large orchard blocks further 
require tiling, sparse convolution, efficient sampling, or streaming 
strategies to manage memory and computational cost. In contrast, ro­
botic navigation and machinery perception prioritize near-real-time 
inference and robustness to dust, vibration, dynamic canopy motion, 
workers, vehicles, and machinery. Across these applications, cross- 
season, cross-platform, and cross-cultivar validation is more informa­

Deep learning in orchard LiDAR data should be evaluated using more 
than overall accuracy. For semantic segmentation, precision, recall, F1- 
score, mean intersection over union, and class-specific accuracy are 
more informative than overall accuracy when classes are imbalanced. 
For instance segmentation or object detection, instance-level detection 
metrics, boundary accuracy, and count error are often more relevant 
than point-level accuracy alone. For operational use, runtime, GPU 
memory demand, failure rate, and robustness under cross-field, cross- 
season, and cross-platform testing should also be reported. These con­
siderations are especially important for small or underrepresented 
classes such as trunks, fruit, trellis wires, dead branches, or machinery 
obstacles.
Data fusion can further improve trait retrieval and classification by 
combining the complementary strengths of different sensing modalities. 
LiDAR provides three-dimensional geometry, canopy structure, and 
spatial context, whereas RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, thermal, and 
stress-index sensors provide complementary information on color, 
reflectance, temperature, water status, disease symptoms, and stress 
responses (Iheaturu et al., 2024; Man et al., 2014; Savinelli et al., 2024). 
Fusion can occur at the point level, feature level, decision level, or model 
level. Point-level fusion attaches image-derived or spectral attributes 
directly to LiDAR points. Feature-level fusion combines geometric and 
spectral descriptors before classification or regression. Decision-level 
fusion combines outputs from independently trained models. Model- 
level fusion integrates multimodal streams within a unified neural 
network. In orchard studies, fusion is particularly relevant for fruit 
detection, disease and stress monitoring, vigor mapping, cultivar or 
species discrimination, and yield estimation. However, fusion also in­
troduces co-registration error, scale mismatch, illumination depen­
dence, sensor-specific noise, and temporal inconsistency. Therefore, 
fusion studies should report registration accuracy between modalities, 
acquisition time difference, spatial resolution, calibration procedures, 
and whether the model was validated across dates, orchards, or crop 
types.

4.7. Software and Open-source Ecosystem
Although this chapter emphasizes algorithms rather than tools, 
reproducibility in point cloud research is enhanced by pipeline-oriented 
processing, version control, explicit parameter provenance, and diag­
nostic artifacts at each stage. Community libraries such as the Point 
Cloud Library provide efficient, well-documented implementations of 
filters, registration methods, feature extractors, and segmentation al­
gorithms, thereby enabling rigorous comparison under controlled con­
ditions and facilitating batch processing for large orchard datasets (Rusu 
and Cousins, 2011). More broadly, open-source point cloud libraries 
span a continuum from general-purpose toolkits (e.g., PCL, Open3D, 
PDAL) to lightweight utilities (e.g., pyntcloud, PyVista) and domain- 
specific packages (e.g., lidR for forestry, GDAL for geospatial abstrac­
tion). Table 10 summarizes representative open-source libraries for 
point cloud processing, highlighting their implementation languages, 
primary functions, and application domains.
In addition to open source libraries, a number of specialized software 
platforms provide comprehensive graphical interfaces and production- 
oriented workflows for point cloud processing. These tools typically 
integrate modules for ground filtering, surface modeling, feature 
extraction, visualization, and data management, with varying degrees of 
automation and scalability. Commercial and open-source software differ 
in accessibility, extensibility, and licensing, but together they form an

4.8. Algorithmic Challenges, Validation Priorities, and Practical 
Roadmap
Despite substantial progress in LiDAR data processing, point clouds 
still present several persistent algorithmic and operational bottlenecks. 
These bottlenecks arise from the interaction among acquisition geome­
try, canopy architecture, environmental conditions, and downstream 
modeling assumptions.
4.8.1. Occlusion, Beam Attenuation, and Observability Bias
Canopy occlusion and beam attenuation remain fundamental 
observability constraints in orchard LiDAR data analysis. They are not 
only descriptive field problems but also sampling and observability 
problems. As laser beams pass through foliage, branches, fruit, and 
woody structures, the probability of reaching deeper canopy layers de­
creases with path length, canopy density, scan angle, footprint size, and 
wavelength-dependent reflectance. A useful conceptual model is the 
Beer–Lambert type attenuation relationship, in which transmitted en­
ergy or gap probability decreases exponentially with cumulative plant 
area or optical path length (Martens et al., 1993; Armston et al., 2013). 
Although the assumptions of random foliage distribution and homoge­
neous optical media are simplified for structured orchards, this

From an algorithmic perspective, occlusion produces non-random 
missing data. Upper canopy surfaces, outer crown envelopes, and 
exposed row sides are overrepresented, whereas shaded inner crowns, 
trunk bases, and lower foliage may be under-sampled (B´eland et al., 
2011; Soma et al., 2020). This sampling bias affects canopy height 
models, crown volume, leaf area estimation, biomass modeling, fruit 
visibility analysis, and health assessment. Algorithms that assume uni­
form point density may therefore overestimate canopy compactness 
from aerial data or underestimate inner-canopy structure from single- 
sided mobile scans. Similar occlusion-related limitations have been re­
ported in terrestrial LiDAR-based leaf area distribution and leaf area 
density estimation, where unobserved or poorly sampled voxels require 
explicit correction or modeling.
Several strategies can reduce occlusion-induced bias. Multi-azimuth 
scanning improves visibility of crown sides and inner canopy gaps. 
Combining UAV and ground LiDAR provides complementary top-view 
and side-view perspectives (Liu et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2025). Voxel- 
based occupancy models can represent both observed and unobserved 
space rather than treating missing points as empty space (Hornung et al., 
2013). Ray-tracing approaches can explicitly model beam paths through 
three-dimensional canopy representations and support occlusion-aware 
metrics such as gap probability, path-length distribution, sampling 
depth, and visibility-weighted point density (Hancock et al., 2014; Lowe 
et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024a). For trait retrieval, model validation 
should report not only global accuracy but also bias by canopy height 
layer, tree density, row position, scan side, and phenological stage.
This issue is particularly important for structure-to-function 
modeling. If light interception, leaf area, biomass, or fruit load are 
inferred from point clouds, the model should distinguish between bio­
logical absence and sensor non-observation. In other words, a canopy 
gap observed by LiDAR is not necessarily equivalent to a true foliage gap 
unless the beam path and viewing geometry are considered. Future or­
chard LiDAR workflows should therefore integrate occlusion-aware 
sampling diagnostics before estimating functional traits.
4.8.2. Registration Drift and Non-rigid Canopy Motion
Registration and laser-scanning-based SLAM remain unstable under 
repetitive row geometry, weak GNSS reception, and wind-induced 
canopy motion. Regular orchard rows provide operational regularity 
but may reduce geometric distinctiveness for scan matching. Wind- 
induced non-rigid branch movement violates the rigid-scene assump­
tion used by many registration and SLAM algorithms, causing local 
misregistration, ghosting, map thickening, and biased canopy volume 
estimates. Real-time LiDAR odometry and mapping methods address 
motion estimation and mapping simultaneously, but trajectory drift, 
scan-to-map correspondence instability, and loop-closure limitations 
remain important concerns in geometrically repetitive field environ­
ments (Zhang and Singh, 2014). Future pipelines should combine 
LiDAR-inertial odometry, GNSS constraints where available, loop 
closure, trunk and terrain primitives, row-line features, robust loss 
functions, and automated residual diagnostics. Registration accuracy 
should be evaluated using independent check patches or stable orchard 
structures rather than only optimized internal residuals.
4.8.3. Radiometric Inconsistency and Model Transferability
Radiometric inconsistency and limited model transferability 
constrain trait retrieval and semantic interpretation. Intensity and 
waveform attributes are affected by range, incidence angle, target 
reflectance, atmospheric condition, receiver gain, wavelength, and 
sensor-specific processing; therefore, intensity values generally require 
correction or calibration before being used as comparable radiometric 
variables (Kaasalainen et al., 2009; Kashani et al., 2015; Sanchiz-Viel 
et al., 2020). Without normalization, intensity-based traits can be 
confounded with acquisition geometry or sensor settings. Similarly, 
segmentation and classification models may fail across cultivars, 
phenological stages, platforms, and orchard architectures when trained 
on limited local datasets. Rule-based methods remain interpretable but 
require crop-specific tuning; classical machine learning depends on 
engineered features; and deep learning requires labeled data, compu­
tational resources, and robust external validation. PointNet and 
PointNet++ demonstrated direct learning from unordered point sets 
and hierarchical local feature extraction, while RandLA-Net further 
addressed large-scale semantic segmentation through efficient random 
sampling and local feature aggregation (Hu et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2017a; 
Qi et al., 2017b). Future studies should prioritize cross-field, cross-sea­
son, cross-cultivar, and cross-platform validation, together with class- 
specific metrics for underrepresented structures such as trunks, trellis 
wires, fruit, dead branches, and canopy gaps.
4.8.4. Computational Scalability and Real-time Feasibility
Computational scalability and real-time feasibility remain unre­
solved for operational orchard deployment. Dense TLS, MLS, UAV, and 
multi-temporal LiDAR datasets can exceed the memory and processing 
capacity of conventional desktop workflows (Zhang and Singh, 2014; Hu 
et al., 2020). Real-time applications such as variable-rate spraying, ro­
botic navigation, pruning support, and in-field scouting require low- 
latency processing, robust downsampling, streaming algorithms, and 
efficient onboard inference. Therefore, future evaluations should report 
not only accuracy but also runtime, memory consumption, sensor-to- 
decision latency, and failure modes under realistic field conditions. 
Efficient large-scale point cloud networks and LiDAR odometry frame­
works provide useful methodological directions, but their orchard- 
specific reliability still requires validation under canopy occlusion, 
dust, wind, repetitive rows, and variable point density.
4.8.5. Digital Orchard Modeling and Uncertainty Propagation
LiDAR-enabled digital orchard modeling requires a more explicit 
technical framework. A digital orchard should include at least four 
layers: a geometric layer representing terrain, trees, trunks, crowns, 
rows, and infrastructure; a semantic layer assigning biologically and 
operationally meaningful labels; a temporal layer maintaining tree 
identities and structural changes across time; and an uncertainty layer 
documenting registration error, occlusion, trait-estimation uncertainty, 
and model confidence. Digital twin research emphasizes the integration 
of physical entities, virtual representations, data connections, and life­
cycle updating, but these principles must be translated into orchard- 
specific sensing, modeling, and validation workflows (Tao et al., 2018; 
Jones et al., 2020). Without these layers, digital twin terminology risks 
remaining conceptual rather than reproducible.
4.8.6. Practical Roadmap
A practical roadmap can be organized into three stages. In the near 
term, LiDAR in orchard studies should standardize metadata reporting, 
quality-control diagnostics, registration validation, ground-filtering 
evaluation, and task-specific segmentation metrics. In the medium 
term, algorithms should shift from isolated processing steps toward in­
tegrated workflows that jointly account for acquisition geometry, oc­
clusion, radiometry, phenology, and platform-specific uncertainty. In 
the long term, LiDAR data analysis should move toward transferable and 
uncertainty-aware systems, including self-supervised pretraining on 
large unlabeled orchard point clouds, active learning for efficient 
annotation, multimodal fusion, persistent tree-level digital orchard 
models, and real-time decision-support pipelines for robots, sprayers, 
and UAV platforms.
5. Conclusions
This review provides an integrated synthesis of LiDAR research in 
orchards and perennial tree crops by linking bibliometric trends, sensor

and platform development, and data-processing workflows. The analysis 
of 288 publications across 24 crop types shows that the field has 
expanded rapidly since 2015, driven by UAV-based mapping, mobile 
laser scanning, compact multi-beam sensors, and increasing demand for 
three-dimensional crop monitoring. At the same time, the literature 
remains unevenly distributed across crop systems, with apple, oil palm, 
pear, olive, and citrus receiving most attention, while many tropical and 
subtropical perennial crops remain underrepresented. This imbalance 
indicates a need for broader validation across diverse canopy architec­
tures, planting systems, production regions, and environmental 
conditions.
The review also shows that LiDAR performance depends on the co­
ordinated interaction among sensors, platforms, acquisition design, al­
gorithms, and validation protocols. Terrestrial and mobile LiDAR 
provide detailed under-canopy and row-scale observations, UAV and 
airborne LiDAR enable orchard- and estate-scale structural mapping, 
and spaceborne LiDAR offers regional context for large perennial sys­
tems. However, each platform introduces distinct constraints in viewing 
geometry, point density, canopy penetration, environmental robustness, 
registration accuracy, and computational demand. Processing work­
flows have advanced from geometry-based methods toward machine 
learning, deep learning, data fusion, and open-source ecosystems, but 
persistent bottlenecks remain. Canopy occlusion and beam attenuation 
create non-random missing data; registration and laser-scanning-based 
SLAM are challenged by repetitive row geometry and wind-induced 
canopy motion; radiometric inconsistency limits intensity- and 
waveform-based interpretation; and data-driven models still require 
stronger cross-field, cross-season, cross-cultivar, and cross-platform 
validation.
Future LiDAR research should move beyond isolated sensor dem­
onstrations toward reproducible, task-driven, and uncertainty-aware 
workflows. Priority directions include standardized metadata report­
ing, quality-control diagnostics, task-specific validation metrics, 
occlusion-aware modeling, radiometric normalization, multi-platform 
fusion, transferable learning, and computationally efficient pipelines 
for real-time field deployment. Longer-term progress will depend on 
digital orchard systems that integrate geometric, semantic, temporal, 
and uncertainty layers, allowing tree-level structural information to be 
updated over time and linked to management decisions. In this context, 
LiDAR should be viewed not only as a three-dimensional sensing tool, 
but also as a methodological foundation for reproducible phenotyping, 
precision orchard management, robotic perception, and data-driven 
perennial crop production.
