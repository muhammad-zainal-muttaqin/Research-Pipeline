Yield estimation can provide critical information for high-value crop growers. It helps them to plan and coordinate 
the logistics of harvesting operations. Most existing yield estimation techniques are based on manual sampling 
and statistical approaches, which are complex, labor-intensive, and time-consuming. In recent years, advances 
in image processing and the ubiquitous use of autonomous platforms, such as unmanned aerial platforms, have 
provided an opportunity for faster and more accurate yield estimation techniques for diﬀerent crops. A new rapid, 
eﬃcient, and accurate image processing algorithm was proposed in this study. The image segmentation technique 
was able to separate mature citrus trees and natural background in CIE (Commission International Eclairage) 
L ∗ a ∗ b ∗ (i.e., Lab) color space. The eﬀect of camera distance to object, aﬀected resolution, and detection accuracy 
were evaluated and discussed. The results showed that the distance between 1.524 meters (5 feet) and 2.134 
meters (7 feet) could provide high detection accuracy. The number of correctly counted fruit reached 91.69% 
at the average processing time of 1.1 s per picture for 132 pictures. A correlation coeﬃcient (R 2 ) of 0.98 was 
obtained between the citrus counting algorithm and counting performed through human observation of 66 sample 
trees. The comparative analysis of the proposed method showed a higher accuracy rate and robustness compared 
to other similar studies. 
1. Introduction 
In modern agriculture, estimation and mapping of yield prior to har- 
vesting are important at the various stages during the production cycle. 
The ability to predict yield means that farmers can schedule their har- 
vesting logistics, crop storage, and sales in advance. Currently, the most 
commonly used technique for yield estimation of fruit trees is based on 
fruit sampling and a statistical approach. Fruit sampling is often done 
over a few individual trees and extrapolated over the entire orchard. 
Many researchers have attempted to improve traditional methods, such 
as [1] , who classiﬁed the tree rows based on vegetative indices to de- 
velop multi-level manual sampling techniques to estimate fruit counts. 
However, their approach requires high labor input. Ineﬃciency, labor 
cost, and time consumption are the essential drawbacks of conventional 
techniques. An intelligent remote imaging system was designed and 
tested by [2] to predict yield based on both direct sampling of the fruit 
count and aerial observations. Their result showed that advanced types 
of equipment are required to accelerate the yield estimation and reduce 
the cost.

With the rapid development of machine vision technology, these 
systems can be real-time, cost-eﬀective, reliable, and rapid [3] . Some 
researchers have studied automatic fruit detection methods to detect 
and count fruits on the tree. Koirala et al. [4] estimated mango fruit 
load by machine vision with deep learning. Bresilla et al. [5] used deep 
convolution neural networks architecture based on single-stage detec- 
tors to detect and count fruits (apple and pear) within the tree canopy. 
Gan et al. [6] detected immature green citrus using color and thermal 
images. Wan et al. [7] developed a methodology for fresh tomato matu- 
rity detection using computer vision. Choi et al. [8] implemented red, 
green, and blue (RGB), near-infrared (NIR), and depth sensors in an out- 
door machine vision system to determine the number of immature citrus 
in tree canopies. Sa et al. [9] performed a fruit detection system to detect 
seven fruits using a novel multi-modal Faster R-CNN model. Qiang et al. 
[10] used a multi-class support vector machine (SVM) to simultaneously 
segment the fruits 50 and branches of citrus fruit. The speed, accuracy, 
and robustness of these methods in the machine vision technology that 
is used are essential for fruit yield estimation [11] . Based on the achieve- 
ments of these works, improving a fruit detection method can also aid 
to improve the accuracy of fruit yield estimation and mapping.

Methods that have previously been studied about fruit detection in- 
clude diﬀerent types of sensors and systems as well as various image 
processing techniques and soft computation methods [12 , 13] . Most of 
the image processing steps involved in fruit detection are very complex 
[15] . Even the soft computing methods are carried out after feature ex- 
traction to improve the detection accuracy [9] . 
The most advanced computation methods in fruit detection were the 
following: support vector machine [16] , artiﬁcial neural network algo- 
rithm [17 , 18] , and deep learning [14] , all of which had a good con- 
tribution. However, they increase the system complexity and require 
suﬃcient accurate training samples to deliver reliable results. 
Image processing under visible light is the most common practice 
for in-ﬁeld fruit recognition [19] . Other practices have employed vari- 
ous types of thermal imaging cameras, color cameras, and hyperspectral 
cameras to collect images of fruit [20 , 21] . The variability of lighting 
conditions in orchards with the natural sunlight also can aﬀect the in- 
tensity of light reﬂection, which will cause false fruit detection [22] . 
The fruit color, geometry, and texture are three important features in 
image processing. These features help to distinguish fruit from leaves, 
branches, and other background objects in the orchard environment. 
Linker et al. [23] and [24] used shape-color-based analysis and reported 
a respective accuracy of 85% and 89%. Thus, for superior accuracy in 
fruit detection, multiple features (color, shape, texture, and reﬂectance) 
should be included to overcome challenges such as clustering, occlusion, 
and variable lighting conditions. Earlier, [25] applied color, shape, and 
size features comprehensively to segment oranges in tree canopy im- 
ages. Later, [26] presented an automatic fruit recognition system for 
classifying and identifying fruit types by exploiting the fruit shape and 
color to identify each image feature. 
The essential part of estimating the yield is to detect the surface- 
located fruit and separate them from the confusion background. Dif- 
ferent color spaces were applied to images for diﬀerent types of fruit 
detection. Dorj, et al. [15] developed a new algorithm for automat- 
ing fruit detection by converting red-green-blue (RGB) color space to 
hue-saturation-value (HSV) color space. As a result, they reported that 
a correlation coeﬃcient (R 2 ) of 0.93 was obtained between the cit- 
rus counting algorithm and counting performed through human obser- 
vation. However, their image segmentation procedure was very com- 
plicated because it included threshold color detection, fruit segmenta- 
tion, noise ﬁltering, morphological operation, labeling, feature extrac- 
tion, and classiﬁcation. Recently, [27 , 28] constructed a multi-elliptical 
boundary model in Cr-Cb coordinates to detect citrus fruit and tree 
trunks in natural light environments. Their experimental results showed 
that the correct and false-positive percentages in fruit identiﬁcation 
from images were improved to 90.8 and decreased to 11.2%, respec- 
tively. However, image processing is still time-consuming. 
In some studies, the combination of several color components in one 
or several color spaces was also used to segment images [29] . The meth- 
ods were mainly used to extract weighted additions and subtractions by 
extracting multiple color components in the selected color space with- 
out the noise deletion, ﬁltering, and thresholding steps. However, it is 
hard to get the trade-oﬀbetween accuracy and computational eﬃciency. 
While the proposed ensemble technique can improve the results of the 
constituent methods, it also accumulates the cost of all of them. 
Citrus is one of the many fruits grown in California, and it is impor- 
tant to the state economy. As it ripens, the color of citrus (not including 
limes) fruit becomes diﬀerent from that of the background color; thus, 
we hypothesized that the color feature would be easy to segment from 
the background elements such as leaves, trunk, and sky. Therefore, it 
may be possible for the image segmentation, the detection of citrus can 
be based on the color image algorithm in a diﬀerent color space. In this 
study, a new algorithm was developed to detect and count the citrus 
fruits by employing rapid image processing without removing the back- 
ground and the noise. Due to the advantages of minimizing data process- 
ing and faster operation, the proposed algorithm can also be suitable for 
use in a real-time machine vision and harvesting robot.

2. Material and methods 
2.1. Materials 
One hundred thirty-three images were collected at the height of 145 
cm and a constant distance of 213 cm perpendicular to each tree row us- 
ing an iPhone XR (Apple Inc., USA) digital camera phone. The resolution 
of the images was 4032 ×3024 pixels (approximately 5.5 Megapixels), 
in the auto-focus mode in natural sunlight background, without opti- 
cal or digital zoom and ﬂashlight, and all of them were stored in JPG 
format. Tests were performed on two varieties (mandarin, orange) of 
citrus trees during the ripening period. The fruit images were acquired 
during September and November in 2018 at the Shiraz Ranch orchards 
(the row between latitude: 37°20 ′ 42.6"N, longitude: 121°06 ′ 48.4"W; and 
latitude: 37°20 ′ 53.5"N, longitude: 121°06 ′ 51.3"W) located in Newman, 
California. As light is one of the main factors aﬀecting the image quality, 
a light intensity meter was used to measure the light near the trees and 
near the camera under natural outdoor daylight, including cloudy and 
sunny days. The following hardware was used: Intel Core i5 Processor 
Laptop, Windows 64-bit operating system, and 4 GB RAM. Moreover, 
MATLAB software (version 2018a, Mathworks Inc., USA) was used for 
image processing and modeling in this research. 
2.2. Methods 
The most diﬃcult part of fruit recognition is removing the “clut- 
ter objects ”, which include leaves, branches, sky, and soil, from the 
background, and it has proven to be a challenging task in past stud- 
ies. Therefore, a new algorithm for rapid image processing with fewer 
steps and complexity was oﬀered to accomplish an acceptable accu- 
racy. This algorithm was simpliﬁed, as shown in Fig. 1 . Unlike the 
other methods, this algorithm does not include complex steps such as 
noise ﬁltering, morphological operation, labeling, and feature extraction 
[ 7 , 15 , 22 , 26 , 30 , 31 ]. 
2.2.1. Color space selection 
In order to attain a better rate of fruit detection, it is important to 
choose appropriate color space when a color image is segmented. There 
is a signiﬁcant diﬀerence between gray levels of diﬀerent color compo- 
nents when the same image is processed under diﬀerent color spaces. 
The fruit segmentation can be easier if the diﬀerence in the gray level 
between the target and the background becomes greater. Therefore, it 
is crucial to ﬁnd a color component that can distinguish the target from

The color of mature citrus fruit ranges from yellow to orange de- 
pending on the variety, and so the fruit color is diﬀerent from the back- 
ground color. Therefore, color feature image segmentation methods are 
commonly used. Considering that images were captured under natural 
conditions, image segmentation can be easily aﬀected by changes in ex- 
ternal light. Therefore, an image segmentation algorithm based on both 
color and shape was selected in image processing.

2.3. Circle Hough transform method 
After the fruits are separated from the background by image process- 
ing, the next step is to detect and count the number of fruits. The citrus 
fruit usually has a round shape, so the circle Hough transform (CHT) 
[38 , 39] is applied to detect the circles from objects in the processed 
images. 
The CHT is a basic technique used in digital image processing for de- 
tecting circular objects. It is a specialization of the Hough transform. The 
purpose of this technique is to ﬁnd circles in an imperfect image input. 
The CHT is a process of converting a circle in a two-dimensional image 
space into a point in a three-dimensional parameter space determined by 
the radius and center horizontal axis and the vertical axis of the circle. 
Therefore, a circle determined by any three points on the circumference 
should correspond to a point the three-dimensional parameter after the 
Hough transformation.

2.4. Distance in image processing 
Distance from the camera to the object has a signiﬁcant eﬀect on 
the size of the object its detectable features from the captured image 
[40] . The closer the shooting distance is to the fruit tree, the larger the 
size of the fruit in the picture. Conversely, the farther away from the 
fruit tree, the smaller the size of the fruit being photographed. In or- 
der to improve the processing speed, we use a similar code to process 
all the pictures. In the program, the maximum and minimum values of 
RADIUS_RANGE must be given in the function imfindcircles(A, 
RADIUS_RANGE) . According to the typical size of citrus fruit, the pa- 
rameter range of RADIUS_RANGE is selected as [8 20], and the sensitiv- 
ity is ﬁxed to 0.93. Core program is as follows: 
[centersBright,radiiBright,metricBright] = 
imfindcircles(rgb,[820], ...’ObjectPolarity’, 
’bright’,’Sensitivity’,0.93,’EdgeThreshold’,0.1) .

3. Image preprocessing and acquisition 
3.1. Image acquisition 
The images of trees for this experiment were taken from Shi- 
raz Ranch, located in Newman, California. Sixty-six fruit trees with 
diﬀerent row spacing and plant spacing were selected for measure- 
ment. These 66 trees were selected at the same row between latitude:

37°20 ′ 42.6"N, longitude: 121°06 ′ 48.4"W; and latitude: 37°20 ′ 53.5"N, 
longitude: 121°06 ′ 51.3"W to ease the access and adjust the similar and 
more precise camera to object distance for the entire experiment and 
reduce the error. The average row spacing of fruit trees was 5.46 m, 
and the average plant spacing was 4.5 m. Images of both sides (the east 
and west sides) of the 66 fruit trees were captured, thus a total of 132 
pictures were used for the image processing task. According to the pre- 
vious discussion, images taken between 1.5 m and 2.1 m had the highest 
accuracy rate. Considering that if the camera were to be installed on an 
automated robot system, 2.1 m is a suitable distance between the cam- 
era and the fruit tree. Variation of canopy shape and number of fruits per 
tree on both sides of each tree made a suﬃcient number of diﬀerent sam- 
ples for this experiment. On each side, the number of 10 images per tree 
at diﬀerent distances between 0.3 and 3m with the increment of 0.3m 
was token. The number of fruits in each captured image and detected by 
the human eye are counted manually. After that, these numbers were 
compared with the number of fruits counted by the machine using the 
introduced rapid image processing technique in the natural background. 
The linear least-squares analysis was used to ﬁgure out and compare the 
result of counting the fruits by the human eye and the machine on both 
sides of the trees. Fig. 3 shows the conﬁguration of the task of collecting 
tree images. 
The two images shown in Fig. 4 are representative images of the high 
and low-yield citrus trees about ten days before harvest. 
The most complex and challenging step for digital image process- 
ing is the segmentation of the desired characteristic. This becomes an

3.2. Color space transformation 
The image in Fig. 4 (a) shows that blue sky, green leaves, and 
branches are present in the background of the image. Before the orig- 
inal RGB image is converted to “HSV ” and “Lab ” color space, the RGB 
image was processed to the new RGB image, where the “B ” component 
is equal to zero. This is because the blue color background usually can 
be only the color of the sky. Next, the RGB image was converted to HSV 
and Lab color space, and nine components (R, G, B, H, S, V, L ∗ , a ∗ , and 
b ∗ ) were separately extracted from each image, as shown in Fig. 5 . 
By comparing the images of RGB, HSV, and Lab, it was decided which 
image is the most suitable for automatic detection by the machine, as 
shown in Table 2 . 
By comparing the image in diﬀerent color spaces in Table 2 , it was 
found that the images in RGB and HSV color space cannot be elimi- 
nated easily by the machine. However, only in Lab color space can the 
background be thoroughly removed, which can be done easily by the 
machine. Thus, the Lab color space was selected as the best color space 
for background removal and mature fruit recognition. Since the L ∗ com- 
ponent and b ∗ component were blank in the Lab color space, the a ∗ 
component was selected for detection and counting the mature citrus 
fruits.

4.6. Mapping of estimated citrus fruit yield 
The distribution of detected citrus fruits in Table A.1 was mapped 
using MATLAB software on the base map of the orchard and was shown 
in Fig. 9 . It is important to mention that the trees used in this experiment 
were planted on a raised part of the orchard with uneven land. The 
results clearly shows that the higher yield was from the bottom of the 
hill where the trees got plenty of irrigated water, whereas less yield was 
obtained from trees located on the crest of the hill that absorbed less 
water compared to the trees at the lower altitude. It was also clear that 
the trees planted at the lower altitude were larger compared with those 
at the top of the hill, which were smaller. 
5. Conclusion 
A new algorithm was developed to detect and count citrus fruits. The 
yield obtained from the orchards was computed by using image process- 
ing. The result was also compared with human vision-based counting 
under natural lighting conditions. An R 2 of 0.98 was obtained for lin- 
ear regression of the citrus counting algorithm and counting performed 
through human observation results for 66 sampled trees. Moreover, an 
accuracy of 91.8% and an average elapsed time of image processing was 
1.1 s were achieved. It was found that the combination of an image pro- 
cessing method based on the Lab color space method with color space 
and the Hough circle transform can identify the citrus in the natural 
environment. The advantages of this method are a fast recognition pro-
