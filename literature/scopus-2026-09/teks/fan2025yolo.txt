A B S T R A C T
The rapid detection and counting of immature pomegranates plays a crucial role in estimating pomegranate 
yield. Existing deep learning models, however, often encounter difficulties in simultaneously optimizing 
detection speed and accuracy. This study proposes a novel, lightweight, and efficient algorithm for the detection 
of unripe pomegranates based on YOLOv8. First, the number of channels in the Neck component was reduced, 
resulting in a model size that is 49.56% of the baseline model. Additionally, the Partial Depthwise Convolution 
(PDWC) module with constant number of channels is introduced as a new feature extraction module to further 
enhance detection speed. Subsequently, Depthwise Separable Convolution (DWSConv) and Ghost Convolution 
(GhostConv) were employed for downsampling, thereby mitigating the potential loss of detailed information 
during feature extraction. In order to alleviate the accuracy loss introduced by lightweighting process, a Triplet 
Attention mechanism is introduced to improve the model capacity to identify small targets. The experimental 
results of the improved model, YOLO-PDGT, indicate that detection speed is significantly enhanced by this model 
while a high level of accuracy is also maintained. The model shrank about 82.3% in size while frames per second 
(FPS) improved by 67.64% and mAP@0.5 improved by 0.2%. The count accuracy of the model outperformed 
baseline with a R2 of 0.96. Compared to existing models, the model in this study achieves better detection speed 
and model size while maintaining high accuracy, hitting the balance between speed and accuracy, and thus 
providing a powerful technical solution for real-time monitoring of pomegranate orchards.
1. Introduction
Pomegranate is a nutrient-dense fruit renowned for its high vitamin 
content and potent antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. Evidence 
shows that pomegranates may help lower blood pressure and reduce the 
risk of serious diseases like cancer and diabetes [29]. Within the context 
of pomegranate cultivation, timely detection and counting of unripe 
fruits are critical for improving production efficiency [33]. Knowing the 
number of fruits and their growth stages helps create specific manage­
ment plans and yield forecasts [5,22,40]. This can lead to better use of 
resources and enhance the sustainability of farming [38]. At present, the 
methods for monitoring and counting pomegranate fruits rely on manual 
inspection. These methods are often inefficient and inaccurate due to 
factors such as worker fatigue or lapses in concentration [7]. Automated 
detection and counting of pomegranate fruits not only ensures the ac­
curacy of detection, but also improves resource utilisation and reduces 
costs. Therefore, automating pomegranate fruit inspection has become 
an inevitable trend in the development of pomegranate industrialization 
[35].
With the rapid advancements of machine vision and artificial intel­
ligence [34,36], target detection technology has become an essential 
tool for in the agricultural field. The combination of drones and image 
processing technology can achieve real-time monitoring of fruit growth 
conditions, including fruit size, colour, and ripeness analysis [12,24]. 
The integration of multimodal and multitask approaches with machine 
learning further promotes its applications in the agricultural field 
[1,37]. By combining image data and environmental data with deep 
learning algorithms, it is possible to predict fruit yield more accurately 
and optimize planting management. Gopi et al. implemented crop 
recommendation and yield prediction based on information such as 
area, irrigation type, and temperature with machine learning [10], 
while Kang et al. achieved tomato leaf disease detection and counting

Traditional target detection algorithms typically rely on the extrac­
tion of manually designed features, such as texture, shape, colour, and 
size. Then input the features into a classifier to achieve target detection 
and classification. For instance, Patel et al. enhanced feature extraction 
methods for fruit target detection by extracting intensity, colour, edge, 
and orientation features derived from fruit images [23]. Addressing the 
issue of target occlusion, Zhang et al. acquired pomegranate images that 
included depth information and applied Support Vector Machines (SVM) 
based on colour and shape features for fruit counting [38]. However, the 
fruit images used in the above studies were pre-processed and were not 
intended for real-time detection tasks in natural environments. Tradi­
tional target detection algorithms, which rely on manually designed 
features, are difficult to effectively deal with complex situations such as 
changing light, high colour similarity between target and background, 
and small feature size.
Comparatively, deep learning methodologies have transformed 
target detection by enabling the automatic learning of feature repre­
sentations, thus eliminating the need for manual feature extraction al­
gorithms. It not only enhances detection accuracy but also improves 
robustness [15].
Currently, the mainstream deep learning approaches for target 
detection are typically classified into one-stage and two-stage detection 
networks. Among them, two-stage detection networks utilise a sequen­
tial approach. This involves an initial identification of target objects 
through the generation of candidate region proposals, followed by a 
detailed classification and recognition process of these proposals. Such 
as R-CNN [9], Faster R-CNN [8], Mask R-CNN [11], G-RCNN [25]. Yu 
et al. implemented the improved PointNet combined with 3D point 
cloud clustering of pomegranate for detection and segmentation of 
pomegranate fruit [35]. Ferrer-Ferrer et al. adds a regression branch 
based on Mask R-CNN, and the improved model enables both target 
detection and fruit size estimation [41]. For the task of small fruit 
detection, Tu et al. developed a multi-scale feature extractor based on 
Faster R-CNN by combining the colour and depth information of the 
image, which significantly improves the precision and recall [27].
Two-stage networks are favoured for their high accuracy in target 
detection tasks, but their large number of parameters also implies a large 
consumption of computational resources, which usually leads to a 
reduction in detection speed. Relatively speaking, one-stage detection 
networks such as SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) [17] and the 
YOLO family of models [26] is faster because no candidate frames are 
generated and targets are detected and classified directly. Among them, 
YOLO is widely used in real-time detection tasks in agriculture as a 
representative of one-stage detection networks [4,16,20].
In recent years, lightweighting of target detection models has 
become a research theme for many researchers due to the requirements 
of computational resources for field equipment and the need for model 
detection speed for real-time detection tasks [18]. For small and 
medium-sized fruit detection tasks, Mao et al. designed a lighter model 
based on PicoDet-S by reducing the number of partial channels and 
using ACON-C activation and CIoU loss function to compensate for 
partial performance [21]. Although these studies have compensated for 
the loss of accuracy by means such as introducing new loss functions and 
attention mechanisms, the compensation effect is limited and may be 
unacceptable in tasks that require high detection accuracy. Wang et al. 
used layer pruning and channel pruning to compress the YOLOv5s 
model, reducing the volume by 71 % and the mAP of the model by 1.57 
% [32]. In the pomegranate detection task, Wang et al. compressed the 
model size of YOLOv8s to 2.2 MB by using ShuffleNetv2 as the backbone 
network, introduced deep separable convolution and other methods, 
and mAP decreased by 1.2 % [30]. Similar lightweighting approaches 
rely on specific model structures and are difficult to directly migrate to 
other models or tasks and lack generalizability.
In the practical application of pomegranate detection, the large 
number and widespread distribution of small targets necessitate a 
particular emphasis on enhancing detection speed. While previous 
studies have offered valuable insights for significantly improving the 
performance of deep learning models, the inherent complexities of or­
chard backgrounds present additional challenges. First, the high simi­
larity between the colour of the unripe pomegranate and the background 
makes it easy for the model to misidentify the green background as the 
target. Second, the occlusion of the fruit by the pomegranate branches 
and leaves, as well as the occlusion problem between the fruits, un­
doubtedly exacerbates the complexity of detection. In addition, the 
image contains a large number of small targets whose feature informa­
tion is relatively scarce, which further increases the difficulty of detec­
tion. Consequently, the core issue to be addressed is how to enhance 
detection speed while simultaneously ensuring accuracy in identifying 
pomegranates.
To address the challenges identified in the detection of unripe 
pomegranates, we proposed a novel model named YOLO-PDGT, which is 
based on YOLOv8s network. This model is specifically tailored to facil­
itate the rapid and accurate detection and counting of unripe pome­
granates within orchard environments. The key contributions of this 
paper are outlined as follows: 
(1) A rich dataset of unripe pomegranates has been established, 
providing a foundation for future target detection of 
pomegranates.
(2) A lightweight pomegranate detection network based on YOLOv8s 
achieves a 67.64 % increase in FPS and an 82.3 % reduction in 
model parameters, while exceeding the baseline model in detec­
tion accuracy.
(3) Comparative evaluations indicate that the YOLO-PDGT model 
significantly outperforms existing detection models, laying the 
foundation for rapid detection in pomegranate orchards.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Construction of datasets
2.1.1. Data sources
The dataset constructed in this study initially comprises a total of 
1,387 images of pomegranates, all captured in the Lintong Pomegranate 
Industrial Park located in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, China (latitude 
34.36◦N, longitude 109.21◦E). In practical applications, the row spacing 
of pomegranate trees typically ranges from 2 to 4 m. Therefore, a 
working distance of 1.5 m is optimal, as it allows the robot to navigate 
flexibly between the trees while avoiding collisions with neighbouring 
trees or obstacles. Consequently, we primarily employed smartphones to 
capture images at this distance. To further enhance the model’s gener­
alization ability by effectively capturing both the overall characteristics 
and fine details of pomegranates, we collected additional images at 
varying distances, incorporating both close-up and distant perspectives. 
The shoot took place between 10am and 8 pm, covering all stages of the 
day as the light changed, including soft mornings and evenings, and 
sunny midday. A variety of angles was also considered, and images of 
pomegranates were taken not only from the front and from the side, but 
also from the sunlit direction and from the backlight, respectively. 
Additionally, the dataset incorporates images taken in different weather 
conditions—sunny, overcast, and cloudy, thus broadening its diversity 
and improving its applicability in varied detection scenarios. Fig. 1 il­
lustrates the pomegranate images captured under these diverse shooting 
conditions.
In addition, Labelme, a sophisticated image annotation tool, was 
employed to delineate the pomegranate’s location within each image, 
subsequently generating a JSON-formatted file that encapsulates the 
coordinates of the target detection frame. Finally, we utilized a simple 
Python script to convert the label files into TXT format, facilitating 
subsequent model training.

2.1.2. Data augmentation
To further increase the sample diversity and mitigate the risk of 
model overfitting, this study implemented a series of data augmentation 
techniques on the collected images, incorporating both offline and on­
line strategies. In order to enhance the robustness of the model to 
different light conditions and improve the detection ability of the model 
to occluded targets, the original images brightness were enhanced to 1.3 
times of the original, and random Cutout processing is performed, as 
shown in Fig. 2.
Among them, brightness processing enriches the pomegranates in 
the dataset under different light intensities, thus allowing the model to 
be more adaptable to the light variations that may be encountered in 
practical applications. The Random Cutout technique is a method of 
simulating the effect of occlusion by occluding random areas in the 
image. In this study, 50 regions of each image each comprising 0.2 % of 
the image size are randomly selected and their grey values are adjusted 
to random numbers between 0–200. Following augmentation, the 
expanded dataset was randomly divided into training and validation 
subsets at a ratio of 7:3, ensuring a well-balanced approach for robust 
model training and evaluation. This methodology not only enhances the 
diversity of the dataset but also strengthens the model’s capability to 
accurately detect pomegranates under various real-world conditions.
The final pomegranate dataset comprises a total of 2,774 images, 
encompassing 29,300 target instances. For the purposes of this study, 
targets are classified based on their size relative to the total image area: 
those with a bounding box occupying less than 0.5 % of the image area 
are categorised as small targets, while those exceeding this threshold are 
classified as large targets. As illustrated in Fig. 3, our analysis reveals 
that the dataset contains 21,513 small targets, which represents 73.4 % 
of the overall target instances.
During the training phase, this study employed two advanced online 
data augmentation techniques: Mosaic4 and Mixup, each utilised at a 
ratio of 0.5. The methodologies are depicted in Fig. 4. Given the high 
proportion of small targets in the dataset, Mosaic4 augmentation was 
specifically implemented to enhance the representation of these targets.

2.2. Improved model
As illustrated in Fig. 5, the YOLOv8 algorithm [28] inherits the core 
advantage of its predecessor YOLO family of algorithms in the field of 
target detection, i.e., it achieves a significant improvement in detection 
accuracy while maintaining high speed. The key enhancements include 
the integration of advanced network structures and optimization algo­
rithms, which significantly enhance the model’s capability to manage 
complex scenes. Additionally, YOLOv8 employs multi-scale feature 
fusion technology, which improves the capability of the model to detect 
objects of different sizes, thus increasing overall detection accuracy. 
Furthermore, the integration of an innovative attention mechanism al­
lows the model to focus on critical regions of the target, thereby 
improving its robustness in diverse conditions.
YOLOv8s represents the compact variant of the YOLOv8 architec­
ture, optimised for scenarios requiring rapid detection of numerous 
targets. Although the YOLOv8s is not the smallest model in the family, 
we chose this version, which offers a good balance between accuracy 
and speed, as the baseline model for the challenge of our target detection 
task. Additionally, considering the unique characteristics of the imma­
ture pomegranate detection task: where the targets are small in size, 
numerous in quantity, and have colours similar to the background, thus 
imposing high demands on detection speed. This study has developed 
the YOLO-PDGT model based on the lightweight design of YOLOv8s. 
The model significantly enhances detection speed while ensuring that 
the detection accuracy remains higher than that of the baseline model. 
(1) Firstly, in order to streamline the model’s architecture and 
accelerate processing times, the number of channels in the Neck 
component has been reduced to one quarter of the original count, 
while the number of channels within the Spatial Pyramid Pooling 
Fast (SPPF) module has been compressed to one half.
(2) Subsequently, this study proposes a more efficient feature 
extraction module PDWC module based on partial depthwise 
separable convolution (PDWConv), which replaces the conven­
tional C2f module in the network.
(3) To further optimise the efficiency of the network, the ordinary 
convolution in the Backbone is replaced with DWSConv, and the 
downsampling module in the Neck is replaced with GhostConv.
(4) Finally, to address the challenges of detecting small targets with 
colours similar to the background and to mitigate potential ac­
curacy losses due to the lightweight design, Triplet Attention has 
been integrated into the fifth and tenth layers of the network, 
respectively.
As illustrated in Fig. 6, the lightweight network architecture pro­
posed in this study comprises three components: Backbone, Neck, and 
Head. The Backbone is tasked with extracting features from the input 
image and generating feature maps at three different scales. The Back­
bone is used to extract features from the input image and outputs feature 
maps at three different scales.
In this process, the PDWC module is used as a lightweight feature 
extraction module, where the second PDWC module loops twice. The 
Neck part improves the detection of targets at different scales by fusing 
feature maps at different scales output from the Backbone, improving 
the detection of targets at different scales. The downsampling of the 
Backbone section and the Neck section uses DWSConv and GhostConv, 
respectively. In addition, the Triplet Attention mechanism is added to 
the fifth and tenth layers of the network, respectively, to enhance the 
model’s ability to detect small targets. Additionally, a 1 × 1

2.2.1. Reduction in the number of channels
In the target detection task for unripe pomegranates, it is essential to 
refine the components of YOLO architecture to achieve optimal perfor­
mance, especially given the task’s focus on a single class. In the YOLO 
framework, the Backbone segment is tasked with extracting critical 
features from the input image, while the Neck segment efficiently in­
tegrates these features. The detection head subsequently decouples these 
integrated features to produce the final output results.
By reducing the number of channels in the Neck, the computational 
complexity of the model can be reduced, and the number of floating- 
point operations (FLOPs) can be reduced, thus increasing the effi­
ciency of the model’s operation. Pre-experimental results similarly show 
that the efficiency of the model can be significantly improved by 
adjusting the number of channels in the Neck part of the network. In 
contrast, directly reducing the number of channels in the Backbone part 
leads to a significant decrease in detection accuracy. Therefore, an 
optimization strategy was adopted: the number of channels in the Neck 
section was reduced to a quarter of its original size, while the number of 
channels in the SPPF module was halved. Such adjustments not only 
effectively reduce the computational burden on the model but also 
maintain high detection performance.
2.2.2. PDWC module
The size of unripe pomegranates is usually small, and multiple 
pomegranate targets may appear in a single image simultaneously, 
which leads to a decrease in detection speed and makes it difficult to 
meet the dual requirements of fast target identification and high accu­
racy in real-time detection. To tackle this issue, Chen et al. introduced 
the concept of Partial Convolution (PConv) to address channel redun­
dancy in convolutional neural networks [3]. This method reduces the 
computational burden of the model by performing convolution opera­
tions selectively on a subset of the input channels, while maintaining the 
integrity of the unchanged channels. Based on this concept, Liu et al. 
further proposed PDWConv, which replaces the standard convolution in 
PConv with a depthwise convolution (DWConv), as shown in Fig. 7, 
which further reduces the computational amount of the model [18].

2.2.3. Lightweight downsampling module
Most targets in our dataset are small in size and are classified as small 
targets. In convolutional neural networks, the presence of down­
sampling operations is known to result in the loss of critical detail, which 
adversely affects the detection performance for small-sized targets. To 
address the issue of information loss during the convolutional process, 
our approach emphasises the preservation of spatial detail in the Back­
bone architecture. In this study, we propose substituting of conventional 
convolution-based downsampling modules with a depth-separable 
convolution module, as illustrated in Fig. 9. DWSConv comprises two 
distinct stages: depthwise convolution and pointwise convolution. The 
depthwise convolution processes each input channel independently, 
thereby preserving the spatial information inherent in the input data.

Subsequently, pointwise convolution aggregates these spatial features

By implementing DWSConv in place of standard downsampling 
techniques, the capability to detect small and medium-sized targets is 
enhanced, meanwhile optimizing computational efficiency. The inte­
gration of depth-separable convolution modules is expected to signifi­
cantly bolster detection speed without compromising the integrity of 
spatial information, thereby contributing to improved performance in 
real-time target identification tasks.
In contrast to the Backbone component, the Neck component has 
been optimised in its initial stages to retain a greater amount of feature 
information within a limited number of channels. To maximise the 
utilization of feature information in the Neck, this study proposes the 
substitution of standard convolution operations with GhostConv. As 
illustrated in Fig. 10, the operation of GhostConv begins by generating 
intrinsic feature mappings through a 1 × 1 convolutional layer, which 
compresses the input feature map across the channel dimension. Sub­
sequently, these feature maps undergo a series of simple linear trans­
formations to produce additional feature maps, thereby increasing 
feature diversity while preserving critical detail information. Finally, the 
newly generated feature maps are concatenated with the original feature 
maps to form the final output feature maps. This method stands in 
contrast to conventional convolution, which typically relies on the 
addition of extra convolutional layers to enhance feature extraction. By 
leveraging linear transformations to generate additional feature maps 
from existing ones, GhostConv significantly reduces computational 
overhead and enhances model efficiency. The implementation of 
GhostConv within the Neck component aims to optimise feature reten­
tion and diversity, ultimately contributing to improved accuracy in 
target detection tasks.
2.2.4. Triplet Attention
The orchard environment presents significant complexities, as the 
intricate arrangement of branches and leaves in pomegranate trees often 
obstructs visibility. This foliage can conceal the fruits themselves, while 
the small size of immature pomegranate fruits leads to a high density of 
small targets within captured images. To address these challenges, we 
embedded the Triplet module at the 5th and 10th layers of the network, 
respectively. The module at the 5th layer focuses on enhancing the 
detailed features of the target, which is particularly important for the 
identification of small-sized targets. Meanwhile, the module at the 10th 
layer emphasizes improving high-level semantic features, enhancing the 
model’s understanding of the overall structure and classification char­
acteristics of the targets, thereby effectively detecting occluded targets.
As illustrated in Fig. 11, the Triplet Attention mechanism is a light­
weight yet effective approach for computing attention weights by 
capturing cross-dimensional interactions through a three-branch struc­
ture. Triplet Attention consists of three branches, each dedicated to

dimensions (height (H) or width (W)) and the channel dimension (C) of 
the input tensor. Given an input tensor of shape C × H × W, each branch 
focuses on enhancing the model’s sensitivity to different aspects of the 
feature representation.

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Coefficient of Determination

3. Experimental results
3.1. Target detection results
The similarity of the colour features of unripe pomegranates to the 
background, coupled with the occlusion between the branches and 
fruits, resulted in a significant reduction of the target feature area, which 
undoubtedly increased the difficulty of target detection. In addition, the 
challenge is further exacerbated by the fact that the small size of the 
target itself is not sufficiently well characterised. Detection accuracy is a 
crucial measure when evaluating model performance. The YOLO-PDGT 
model trained on our pomegranate dataset achieves 93.4 %, 92.6 %, 
97.2 %, and 92.9 % of P, R, mAP@0.5 and F1, respectively.
To further demonstrate the performance of our model in small target 
detection, we randomly selected 600 images from the validation set, 
which contained a total of 9306 ground truth bounding boxes. Among 
these, there were 6646 small targets with an area ratio of less than 0.5 %. 
The validation results for YOLOv8s and YOLO-PDGT are shown in 
Table 2. The improved model recognized a total of 6539 small targets, 
and after eliminating false positives, the mAP was 96.1 %, which is an 
increase of 0.7 % compared to the original model. Due to the inherent 
characteristics of small targets being less distinct, both models exhibited 
lower precision in detecting small targets compared to their overall 
detection accuracy.
Fig. 12 demonstrates the results of the YOLO-PDGT for pomegranate 
fruit detection under different light conditions, angles and distances. 
Specifically, Fig. 12(a) through Fig. 12(d) demonstrate the model’s ef­
ficacy in well-lit environments, where it successfully identifies pome­
granates. However, in low-light conditions, the reduced colour contrast 
between the fruit and surrounding leaves, particularly when the fruit is 
partially occluded, significantly hampers target identification, resulting 
in lower confidence levels in detection. Furthermore, Fig. 12(e) and (f) 
depict detection outcomes for pomegranate images taken from different 
shooting angles, confirming that our model exhibits strong adaptability 
and robustness to variations in angle. Detection results from images 
captured at close and far distances are represented in Fig. 12(g) and 
Fig. 12(h), respectively. The model excels in close-range detection, 
successfully identifying most small targets even when distant shots lead 
to a reduction in target size. Notably, Fig. 12(i) illustrates the model’s 
capability to accurately identify heavily occluded pomegranate fruits, 
maintaining effective detection even with over 80 % occlusion. In 
summary, the YOLO-PDGT model demonstrates significant adaptability 
and accuracy in the recognition of pomegranate fruits, affirming its 
potential for effective deployment in complex and varied environmental 
conditions.
3.2. Counting experimental results
To evaluate the counting capability of the YOLO-PDGT model, we 
conducted a test using a randomly selected dataset of 200 pomegranate 
images from the validation set. The aim was to assess the model’s per­
formance by accurately counting the number of pomegranate fruits 
present in these images. In this experiment, we plotted the count 
regression curves and counted errors for both the baseline and improved 
models, as illustrated in Fig. 13. The left panel of the figure presents a 
linear regression plot depicting the relationship between the counts 
inferred by the model and the manually counted values. Here, the blue 
line represents the regression curve predicted by the improved model. 
The right panel features a histogram detailing counting errors, where the 
x-axis indicates the model’s counting error while the y-axis reflects the 
number of samples corresponding to each error range.
The results demonstrated that the improved model achieved a co­
efficient of determination R2 of 0.96, surpassing the baseline model by 
0.1. Additionally, the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for the improved 
model was 0.11 lower than that of the baseline model. These metrics 
collectively indicate that our improved YOLO-PDGT model delivers 
enhanced accuracy in counting unripe pomegranate fruits and out­
performs the baseline model.
3.3. Ablation experiment
In this study, we aim to develop a lightweight algorithm for the 
detection of unripe pomegranates by enhancing the baseline network, 
YOLOv8s. Our approach involves several targeted modifications: 
reducing the number of channels in the Neck (Model 1), designing a new 
feature extraction module, PDWC, based on PDWConv to replace the C2f 
module in the Backbone network (Model 2), employing DWConv and 
Ghost Conv for downsampling (Model 3), and incorporating a Triplet 
Attention mechanism to create the YOLO-PDGT model. In addition, 
Model 4, Model 5, and Model 6 represent the baseline model with only 
the PDWC module added, the baseline model using DWConv and Ghost 
Conv as downsampling modules, and the baseline model with only the 
Triplet Attention mechanism added, respectively. As the P-R curve 
shown in Fig. 14, after the two initial lightweight operations, the pre­
cision (P) and recall (R) values of Model 2 both decreased significantly. 
However, the subsequent two-step improvements effectively eliminated 
this impact, and the final model demonstrated excellent performance in 
terms of the balance between precision and recall.
To elucidate the impact of each enhancement, we conducted ablation 
experiments under consistent training environments and hyper­
parameters. YOLOv8s served as the baseline model, to which each 
improvement was sequentially added. As presented in Table 3, reducing 
the number of channels in the Neck resulted in a significant decrease in 
both the FLOPs and model size, achieving 50.70 % and 49.56 % of the 
original YOLOv8s, respectively. Concurrently, the FPS performance 
improved by 10.3 %. Notably, the mAP@0.5 decreased by only 0.2 %, 
indicating that the original model possesses some redundancy in the 
Neck section, and thus the optimised channel count is more suitable for 
pomegranate fruit detection.
Following Model 1, the introduction of the PDWC feature extraction 
module in Model 2 further reduced the FLOPs and model size to 30.07 % 
and 30.97 % of YOLOv8s, respectively, while enhancing FPS by 18.91 % 
compared to the original model. However, this modification resulted in 
a decrease in the model’s feature extraction capabilities, as evidenced by 
a 0.6 % reduction in mAP@0.5 and a 1.5 % decrease in F1 score. When 
we add only the PDWC module to the YOLOv8s model, the mAP@0.5 of 
Model 4 decreases by 0.04 %, while the model’s FPS increases by 11.08 
%. Taking into account the reduced parameter count and increased 
processing speed brought by the PDWC module, we believe that this is, 
in fact, an optimization of the model’s overall performance.
Subsequently, we replaced the downsampling modules in the Back­
bone with DWSConv modules and substituted normal convolutions in 
the Neck with GhostConv modules to yield Model 3. With the reduced 
channel count in the Neck from the previous step, we employed distinct 
lightweight methods for the downsampling in both the Backbone and 
Neck. The resulting FLOPs and model size for Model 3 were reduced to 
19.93 % and 19.03 % of YOLOv8s, respectively, with an impressive 
28.02 % increase in FPS. Importantly, this step not only enhanced 
detection speed but also improved both mAP@0.5 and F1 relative to

4. Discussion
4.1. Comparison of different mosaic augmentation strategies
In this study, we employed Mosaic data augmentation to enhance the 
model’s detection capability for small targets. Specifically, two config­
urations were utilised: Mosaic4, which combines four randomly selected 
images, and Mosaic9, which incorporates nine images. During the 
experimentation with these Mosaic augmentation techniques, we 
observed that variations in the augmentation ratio had a relatively 
minor effect on model performance. Consequently, we set the augmen­
tation ratios at 0.5 and 1 for our experiments.
The results presented in Table 5 indicate that, when maintaining a 
consistent number of image splices, an increase in the Mosaic augmen­
tation ratio from 0.5 to 1.0 resulted in a decline in the F1 score for both 
augmentation methods; specifically, the F1 scores for Mosaic4 and 
Mosaic9 decreased by 0.1 % and 0.2 %, respectively. Additionally, when 
the Mosaic ratio was held constant, a transition from four to nine image 
splices produced a similar trend, with corresponding F1 score reductions 
of 0.1 % and 0.2 % for the experimental groups with augmentation ratios 
of 0.5 and 1.0.
These findings suggest that while Mosaic data augmentation is 
beneficial for improving detection performance, the degree of 
augmentation and the number of images combined may have dimin­
ishing returns, necessitating careful consideration in practical applica­
tions for optimal results.
The bounding box loss function curves for various data augmentation 
strategies are presented in Fig. 16, where the x-axis denotes the number 
of training rounds, and the y-axis represents the bounding box regres­
sion loss for the training set. The data augmentation technique employed 
here is Mosaic4, and it is observed that the model achieves optimal 
performance at an augmentation ratio of 0.5. This enhancement can be 
attributed to the balanced application of Mosaic augmentation, which 
effectively increases the representation of small targets within the 
dataset, thereby enriching its diversity and improving the model’s 
generalization capabilities. However, it is noteworthy that in datasets 
with a pre-existing abundance of small targets, excessive Mosaic 
augmentation can inadvertently reduce the apparent size of these tar­
gets. This reduction complicates the detection process for the model, 
ultimately leading to a decline in its generalization ability. Thus, an 
optimal augmentation strategy is crucial, as it must balance the intro­
duction of new examples while preserving the detectability of existing 
targets.
4.2. Lightweight convolution discussion
To verify the effectiveness of the proposed lightweight down­
sampling method, we conducted comparative analyses of various 
lightweight convolutional techniques applied within the Backbone and 
Neck components of the model. The results are summarised in Table 6. 
Notably, while DWSConv presents a lighter alternative with reduced 
FLOPs compared to GhostConv, replacing all downsampling modules 
with DWSConv resulted in a 0.5 % decrease in mAP@0.5. This decline 
can be attributed to DWSConv’s diminished capacity for inter-channel 
information interaction.
In contrast, substituting all downsampling modules with GhostConv 
not only decreased the overall parameter count but also enhanced both 
mAP and F1 scores relative to standard convolution. A comparison of 
overall performance between the second and fourth groups indicates 
that employing GhostConv in the Neck while integrating DWSConv in 
the Backbone represents the most efficient configuration.
This paper posits that, when the network features enough channels, 
compromising some inter-channel information interaction may yield 
improvements in model speed and efficiency. Conversely, in scenarios 
where the number of channels is limited, the application of GhostConv- 
which is designed to maximise feature extraction from constrained 
computational resources-becomes more advantageous. Thus, selecting 
the appropriate convolutional strategy is contingent upon the specific 
architecture and resource availability of the model.

5. Conclusion
In this study, a lightweight pomegranate detection algorithm based 
on YOLOv8s is proposed for the needs of real-time monitoring and 
automatic counting of pomegranate orchards. By constructing diverse 
pomegranate datasets and applying data augmentation techniques, we 
enabled the model to achieve good generalisation ability under different 
environmental conditions. In terms of model design, we reduced the 
computational effort by adjusting the number of Neck channels, using a 
new feature extraction module PDWC, introducing DWSConv and 
GhostConv to reduce the loss of detail information, and adding the 
Triplet Attention mechanism in the key layer to enhance the small target 
detection capability. The experimental results show that the improved 
model maintains higher detection accuracy than the baseline model 
while reducing the number of parameters by 82.3 % and improving FPS 
by 67.64 %. Compared with the existing models, the model proposed in 
this study shows superiority in both speed and accuracy, effectively 
realizing the fast and efficient detection of pomegranate fruits.
However, this study utilized task-specific down-sampling methods, 
which may limit generalizability. Due to experimental conditions and 
the growth duration of pomegranates, we have not yet completed 
deployment testing in real-world scenarios. In the future, we plan to 
research more compatible optimization methods and focus on deploying 
them on robots or mobile devices to achieve dynamic detection in real- 
world scenarios.
