A B S T R A C T
Mangoes hold significant economic and cultural importance in India and globally, making accurate yield 
prediction crucial for optimizing domestic consumption, enhancing international trade, and supporting farmers 
in decision-making. Traditional yield estimation methods, such as manual counting, are labor-intensive, error-
prone, and impractical for large-scale orchards, necessitating automated solutions. This study presents a novel 
time-series vision dataset for mango yield prediction, capturing images from 12 trees across eight spatial 
orientations (SW, S, SE, E, NE, N, NW, W) over 2.5 months to analyze fruit growth and flowering patterns. 
A high-resolution image dataset was created and annotated using a semi-automatic pipeline integrating YOLO 
for object detection and SAM for precise segmentation, significantly reducing manual annotation efforts. 
Benchmarking was conducted using state-of-the-art deep learning models for segmentation (DeepLabV3+, 
PSPNet, SegFormer, Swin-S, YOLO+SAM) and detection (Mask R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, DETR, YOLO). For flower 
segmentation, Swin-S achieved the best results with 67.35 IoU, followed closely by SegFormer with 66.44 
IoU, while for fruitlet segmentation, YOLO+SAM obtained the highest IoU of 78.97. In detection tasks, YOLO 
achieved the best performance for both flowers and fruitlets, with 63.8 mAP50 and 80.5 mAP50, respectively. 
Additionally, segmentation is identified as a suitable approach for flower feature extraction in yield prediction 
models, with SegFormer emerging as a strong choice due to its lower computational cost. Furthermore, this 
study discusses the relationship between wind direction patterns and the flower-to-fruit conversion ratio, 
challenging previous research that attributed yield variations solely to canopy structure differences with respect 
to orientation.
1. Introduction
Agriculture plays a pivotal role in the global economy, contributing 
significantly to food security, employment, and trade. Among various 
crops, mangoes hold a vital position due to their economic and cultural 
importance. As one of the most widely consumed fruits worldwide, 
mangoes are a key component in both domestic markets and interna-
tional trade, particularly for tropical and subtropical countries (Khalifa 
and Abobatta, 2023). In India, one of the largest mango producers 
worldwide (Thakor, 2019), the fruit is integral to the livelihood of 
millions of farmers. However, accurate yield prediction remains a 
critical challenge that can directly impact farmers’ economic decisions 
and market planning (Borrero and Mariscal, 2022). Traditional yield 
estimation methods, such as manual counting or rough approxima-
tions, are labor-intensive, error-prone, and unsuitable for large-scale 
orchards. Vision-based approaches, leveraging the growing accessibility 
of smartphones and cost-effective imaging devices, offer a scalable and

Afsar et al., 2024). These approaches target tasks like counting mangoes 
to estimate yields, but they focus mostly on the fruiting stage and 
leave other factors, such as flowers, less explored. For example, Payne 
et al. (2013, 2014) employed thresholding methods to count fruits, 
while Gutiérrez et al. (2019) used hyperspectral images collected via an 
Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV) combined with a CNN-based method 
for fruit counting.
Flowering is critical in early yield prediction and has been studied 
less. Also, the flower segmentation dataset mentioned in the Table 
1 is collected at night using specific artificial light illumination and 
traditional machine learning methods like color thresholding and SVM 
are used for flower segmentation (Wang et al., 2016). While these ap-
proaches offered some success, they are not robust in orchard environ-
ments where lighting and background conditions can vary. Advanced 
methods like deep learning have not been fully explored for flower 
segmentation, creating an important gap in this area.
Environmental factors such as wind direction and speed are crucial 
in mango yield prediction. These factors directly impact flowering, 
pollination, and fruit retention, ultimately affecting the yield (Bediako-
Kyeremeh et al., 2024). To the best of our knowledge, while some 
existing datasets include weather information (Kulkarni et al., 2023), 
there is currently no publicly available dataset that combines weather 
data and images specifically for mango yield prediction. This gap in 
data limits the ability to develop more accurate models that consider 
the influence of environmental conditions on mango production.
To address these limitations, this work presents the MangoSense 
dataset, a novel time-series vision dataset capturing high-resolution 
images of mango trees over a two-and-a-half-month period. The dataset 
covers multiple tree orientations (SW, S, SE, E, NE, N, NW, W) and 
includes raw images (8000 × 6000 pixels) collected during eight visits 
across the mango season, covering both flowering and fruitlet stages. A 
semi-automatic annotation pipeline, combining YOLO-based detection 
and the Segment Anything Model (SAM), was developed to annotate the 
dataset for segmentation tasks. State-of-the-art models for segmentation 
and detection are benchmarked to establish baseline performance, with 
the goal of identifying effective feature extractors for future mango 
yield prediction models. Additionally, the correlation between wind di-
rection parameters and flower-to-fruit conversion is studied to explore 
the influence of environmental factors on mango development.
2. MangoSense dataset
This section details the dataset collection, annotation, and statistical 
analysis. It covers the image acquisition process, the semi-automatic 
segmentation pipeline, dataset characteristics, and the integration of 
weather data.
2.1. Data collection
The dataset was collected on mango trees with varying orien-
tations and growth stages to accomplish the yield prediction task. 
Twelve mango trees located in Hyderabad (17.430207808540793, 
78.31961657176475), India, were selected for the study. For each tree, 
eight high-resolution images (representing the flowering to fruiting 
process, arranged in a circular format) with a resolution of 8000 × 6000 
pixels were captured during each visit from eight distinct directional 
viewpoints: South-West (SW), South (S), South-East (SE), East (E), 
North-East (NE), North (N), North-West (NW), and West (W). This 
approach ensured comprehensive coverage of the tree canopy.
Data collection lasted for two and half months(15th February to 
30th April 2024), with eight scheduled visits. In total, 8 visits × 12 trees 
× 8 viewpoints resulted in 768 images collected using a 48 MP mobile 
phone RGB camera. This temporal distribution allowed the dataset to 
account for tree flowering and fruit development variations(see Fig. 1 
for a visual representation of the time-series data across visits). The 
visits were scheduled for the morning (7:30 AM to 11:00 AM IST) and 
evening (5:30 PM to 7:00 PM IST). The dataset contains two varieties 
of mango: Banginapalli and Rasalu. Except for tree number 6, which 
is of the Rasalu variety, all other trees are of the Banginapalli variety. 
Additionally, during the final visit (Visit-08), the fruit count for each 
of the 12 trees was manually recorded. For accuracy, the fruit count 
was calculated twice per tree, and the average was taken. This ground-
truth data can be useful for future development and validation of yield 
prediction models.
2.2. Semi-automatic segmentation annotation workflow
As shown in Fig. 2 semiautomatic pipeline was developed to pro-
duce accurate and efficient segmentation annotations. Due to the high 
resolution of the images, each 8000 × 6000 image was divided into 
patches. Images were divided into 1000 × 1000 size 48 patches based 
on the model choice. Initially, a 2% subset of the annotated images 
was used to fine-tune the YOLOv8 (Jocher et al., 2023) model for the 
bounding box detection task. These patches were processed through 
the fine-tuned YOLOv8 model to create bounding boxes, which were 
reorganized into appropriate prompts for the Segment Anything Model 
(SAM) (Kirillov et al., 2023a). SAM operated in a zero-shot mode 
to generate polygonal segmentation for each patch, ensuring high 
accuracy.
Polygons were aggregated based on patch locations in the original 
image, with a threshold-based algorithm applied to merge and connect 
polygons on patch boundaries. These aggregated annotations were 
converted to COCO 1.0 format and saved as a .json file. The .json file 
was then loaded into the CVAT annotation tool (CVAT.ai Corporation, 
2023) for review and correction. The final annotations, after review, 
were exported in COCO format and were used to fine-tune the YOLOv8 
model for subsequent visits. The exact process was done for all eight 
visits. This pipeline significantly reduced manual annotation effort and 
ensured the annotations were precise and accurate.

2.3. Dataset statistics
For the segmentation and detection model, the dataset consists of 
images with dimensions of 8000 × 6000 pixels. Due to the input size 
constraints of most models and resource limitations, the dataset was 
divided into patches of 500 × 500 pixels. This patch size is chosen 
as the nearest divisible size that balances model input requirements 
and computational resources, resulting in 147,456 patches derived 
from the original images. Out of these 147,456 patches, only 40,424 
patches contain annotations, and the remaining patches consist solely 
of background (sky, leaves, soil, etc.).
Fig. 3 provides a detailed analysis of the annotated patches in the 
dataset. In Fig. 3(a), the total number of annotated pixels for each 
class is shown. Specifically, the flower class occupies 295,879,615 
pixels, while the fruitlet class occupies 175,175,608 pixels, representing 
approximately 4.66% of the total dataset pixel area. This indicates a 
sparsity of annotated regions within the dataset. Fig. 3(b) illustrates 
the number of patches containing annotations for each class. Notably, 
23,541 patches include the flower class, and 25,380 patches include 
the fruitlet class, highlighting the distribution of these annotations 
across the dataset. Finally, Fig. 3(c) presents a histogram depicting the 
percentage of the patch area occupied by annotations. The distribution 
shows that most patches contain a small proportion of annotated re-
gions, and only a few patches have higher annotation coverage. This 
shows the sparsity of annotations and provides insight into the relative 
density of the annotated regions within individual patches.
2.4. Weather data
The Weather dataset was collected to complement the image dataset 
for the same period (15 February to 30 April 2024) from a nearby 
weather station located at the Rajiv Gandhi International Airport, 
Shamshabad, Hyderabad (17.240420200356564, 78.429336450661). 
This dataset includes weather parameters such as temperature, hu-
midity, dew point, precipitation, wind speed, wind direction, solar 
radiation, cloud cover, etc. The information was sourced from the 
Visual Crossing web page (Visual Crossing Corporation, 2025). The 
dataset covers hourly records. By integrating these weather data, the 
study helps to increase the utility of the data set to examine the 
growth dynamics of flowers and fruitlets under various environmental 
conditions by capturing the impact of climatic parameters on yield 
prediction.

3.1. Semantic segmentation
3.1.1. Task description
Semantic segmentation is a fundamental computer vision task that 
involves assigning a class label to every pixel in an image. In the 
MangoSense dataset, this entails classifying pixels into two categories: 
flower and fruitlet. Using annotated ground truth data, models are 
trained to predict these classes for each pixel.
3.1.2. Baselines
The following models were benchmarked for the segmentation task:
• DeepLabV3+ (Chen et al., 2018): A CNN-based model with an 
encoder–decoder architecture and spatial pyramid pooling for 
multi-scale feature extraction.
• PSPNet (Zhao et al., 2017): A CNN model that employs pyramid 
pooling to capture global context for effective segmentation.
• SegFormer (Xie et al., 2021): A lightweight transformer-based 
model optimized for semantic segmentation.
• Swin-S (Liu et al., 2021): A hierarchical transformer that utilizes 
shifted window attention for improved computation and feature 
representation.
• YOLO-seg (Khanam and Hussain, 2024): An extension of the 
YOLO architecture with a segmentation head that predicts object 
masks along with bounding boxes.
• YOLO+SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023b): A hybrid model combin-
ing YOLO’s object detection with SAM’s pixel-level segmentation 
capabilities.
3.1.3. Metrics
The models were evaluated using the following metrics:
• Class-wise IoU: IoU (Intersection over Union) measures the over-
lap between the predicted and ground truth regions for an object. 
Class-wise IoU is the IoU computed separately for each class 
(flower and fruitlet).
• mIoU: The average IoU calculated across all classes.
3.1.4. Training details
For training segmentation models, including DeepLabV3+, PSPNet, 
SegFormer, and Swin-S, the MMSegmentation library (MMSegmenta-
tion Contributors, 2020) was utilized. Pretrained weights for these 
models, trained on the ADE20K dataset (Zhou et al., 2019), were di-
rectly taken from the MMSegmentation model zoo. ADE20K was chosen 
due to the similarity of its pre-trained model resolution (512 × 512) to 
the patch size (500 × 500) used in our dataset. These pretrained models 
were subsequently fine-tuned on our custom dataset to adapt them to 
the specific task of segmenting mango flowers and fruitlets. In these 
models, only two classes, namely flower and fruitlet, were considered 
for segmentation, while the background class was excluded during the 
training phase. However, for validation, the background was included 
to ensure that the model’s performance is evaluated under real-world 
conditions where background images are present in the data.
For the hybrid YOLO + SAM model, we used the Ultralytics YOLO 
library in conjunction with the SAM library for segmentation. YOLO 
was fine-tuned on our dataset to detect mango flowers and fruitlets, 
while SAM was used in a zero-shot manner to provide pixel-level 
segmentation using the bounding box prompt generated by YOLO.
3.2. Detection
3.2.1. Task description
Detection involves identifying objects in an image by drawing a 
bounding box around them and assigning a class label. In the Man-
goSense dataset, this means detecting flowers and fruitlets along with 
their bounding box coordinates.
3.2.2. Baselines
The following models were benchmarked for the detection task.
• Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2016): A two-stage CNN-based model 
that first generates region proposals and then refines them for 
object classification and bounding box prediction.
• Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2018): An extension of Faster R-CNN that 
adds a branch for predicting segmentation masks for each object, 
in addition to bounding boxes.
• DETR (Carion et al., 2020): Transformer based decoder architec-
ture with CNN-backbone (Resnet-50).
• YOLO(You Only Look Once): A CNN-based, real-time, single-pass 
object detection model that predicts both bounding boxes and 
class labels for all objects in one step.
3.2.3. Metrics
• mAP50: The mean Average Precision (mAP) at a fixed IoU thresh-
old of 50%, meaning a detection is considered correct if it over-
laps at least 50% with the ground truth.
• mAP (mAP50-95): The mean of Average Precision calculated 
across multiple IoU thresholds ranging from 50% to 95% (in steps 
of 5%).
3.2.4. Training details
MMdetection (MMDetection Contributors, 2018) library is used for 
the training of the Faster RCNN, Mask R-CNN and DETR-R50. For 
YOLOv8 and YOLO11 ultralytics library is used. All the models were 
pre-trained on the COCO dataset (Lin et al., 2015).

4. Results
This section presents benchmarking results for segmentation and 
detection models, analyzing their performance under different training 
data constraints. It also investigates the impact of weather conditions, 
particularly wind direction, on mango yield variations across different 
canopy orientations.
4.1. Segmentation results
The benchmarking results are shown in Table 2 for the 70% train 
and 30% test data. Transformer-based models, such as SegFormer and 
Swin-S, demonstrated superior performance, with Swin-S achieving the 
highest mIoU among non-hybrid models. Hybrid approach YOLO+SAM 
(ViT-H) achieved the best IoU for the fruitlet class, highlighting the 
advantages of combining detection and segmentation. Larger input 
image sizes improved performance for the flower class. Larger SAM 
models further enhanced performance in hybrid models.
The benchmarking results presented in Table 3 evaluate the segmen-
tation models under extreme data constraints, using 1% and 10% train 
data. Even with such limited training data, transformer-based models 
maintained their superiority over CNN-based models. For the 1% train 
data scenario, the performance gap between CNN and transformer 
models remained consistent at 3%, similar to the trend observed with 
70% train data. Hybrid approach YOLO+SAM achieved the best IoU for 
the fruitlet with only 2% drop compare to 70% train data.
For the 10% train data scenario, transformer-based models contin-
ued to outperform CNN-based models. However, the performance gap 
between them reduced to 2%, indicating that CNNs benefit more from 
the increased training data. Meanwhile, YOLO+SAM and YOLO11l-seg 
get the best IoU score for the fruitlet class but there is a significant 
difference for the flower class IoU score with respect to other models. 
For fruitlet class YOLO+SAM get the better IoU score for 10% train data 
compared to 70% train data.

4.2. Detection results
The benchmarking results for detection are presented in Table 4 for 
the 70% train and 30% test data. YOLO models demonstrate superior 
performance compared to Mask R-CNN, Faster R-CNN and transformer 
decoder-based (DETR) approaches. An increase in image patch size 
leads to improved results. Additionally, YOLO models achieve higher 
accuracy with fewer parameters than other methods. Furthermore, 
increasing the number of parameters within the same YOLO version 
enhances its performance. As the patch size increases, the number of 
edges used to divide the image into smaller parts is reduced. Conse-
quently, fewer flowers and fruitlets are split across multiple patches, 
making it easier for YOLO models to detect complete objects rather 
than fragmented ones. This leads to improved detection performance. 
As a result, when the patch size was increased from 500 × 500 to 
1000 × 1000, the mAP score also improved.

4.3. Weather and image dataset analysis
The existing study, ‘‘Terminal Branch Density and Fruit Produc-
tion in the Canopy of High-Yield Mango Orchards’’, attributes the 
variation in mango yield across different canopy directions solely to 
tree-specific factors such as branch density (Ferraz et al., 2020). While 
canopy structure undoubtedly influences light availability and fruit-
bearing capacity, the study fails to consider critical environmental 
parameters like wind direction and speed directly affecting pollina-
tion(a key determinant of fruit set) (Bediako-Kyeremeh et al., 2024). 
The study was conducted from February to November 2016 in three 
experimental orchards at Ibacém Farm, located in Curacao County 
(−8.983078957374664, −39.88333330341969), Bahia State, Brazil. 
Fig. 5 presents the wind direction data recorded for Curacao from 
February to November 2016 (The wind direction data is collected 
from the weatherspark website (WeatherSpark, 2016)). The figure 
utilizes color coding to represent different wind directions, with green 
indicating winds predominantly blowing from east to west. This wind 
pattern significantly enhances pollinator activity and pollen transfer on 
the west side of the tree while limiting these processes on the east 
side, leading to higher yields in the west. Neglecting these environ-
mental factors creates an incomplete hypothesis, as the relationship 
between canopy structure and yield cannot be fully understood without 
considering how wind interacts with the pollination process.

Our study conducted in Hyderabad, India, further emphasizes the 
significance of wind direction in explaining directional variations in 
mango yield. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the flower count by direction is 
based on data from Visit-01, while Fig. 6(b) presents the fruit count by 
direction from Visit-08. The flower-to-fruit conversion ratio, calculated 
as fruit count divided by flower count for each direction, is depicted 
in Fig. 6(d). Additionally, Fig. 6(c) shows the wind direction frequency 
for each corresponding direction.
By comparing the wind direction frequency and the flower-to-fruit 
conversion ratio, we observe that directions with higher wind fre-
quencies generally exhibit lower conversion ratios. For instance, the 
East (90◦) and South-East (135◦) directions exhibit the highest wind 
frequencies, which correspond with relatively lower flower-to-fruit con-
version ratios, such as 8.11% for the East and 11.31% for the South-
East. This pattern can be explained by the mechanism of wind-driven 
pollination: as winds predominantly originate from the east and south-
east, they first impact the east-facing side of the tree canopy. The 
airflow then travels through the canopy towards the west-facing side, 
enhancing the dispersal of pollen and increasing pollinator move-
ment across the western canopy (Kumari et al., 2014). Consequently, 
the West-facing, South-West-facing, and North-West-facing sides of the 
trees receive greater benefits in terms of pollination success, leading 
to higher fruit set rates and therefore higher flower-to-fruit conversion 
ratios: 18.87% for the West, 13.71% for the South-West, and 13.56% 
for the North-West. This directional advantage created by prevailing 
wind patterns highlights the critical role of environmental factors in 
shaping yield outcomes within the orchard.
While the general trend supports our hypothesis, slight deviations 
exist. For example, despite the southeast having the highest wind fre-
quency, its conversion ratio is not the lowest. These discrepancies may

and should be an integral part of future research efforts.
5. Discussion
From the detection and segmentation results, object detection is not 
suitable for extracting flower-related information. This is due to signif-
icant occlusion and the difficulty of distinguishing individual flowers, 
even in manual annotation. Instead, segmentation provides a more 
effective solution by estimating the flower area within the tree. Meth-
ods like measuring the average flower size (in pixels) and calculating 
the flower-to-leaf pixel area ratio provide meaningful insights. These 
metrics serve as valuable inputs for yield prediction models, helping to 
quantify flower distribution and density. For fruitlets, both detection 
and segmentation can be used to extract relevant information for yield 
estimation. The segmentation results indicate that transformer-based 
models(swin and segformer) are the most suitable choice for flower 
segmentation and YOLO+SAM model is suitable for the fruitlet seg-
mentation. In applications where computational resources are limited, 
lightweight models such as SegFormer offer a practical alternative with 
a slight trade-off in IoU performance for flower segmentation. For 
fruitlet detection, YOLO-based models remain the most reliable choice 
with the advantage of low computing resource requirement and higher 
mAP scores.
This dataset can serve as a valuable resource for training detection 
and segmentation models for agricultural applications. For instance, it 
can be used to fine-tune pretrained models for segmenting or detecting 
mango fruits, as well as other fruit types. Additionally, the use of eight 
images captured from different angles facilitates 3D modeling, which 
serves as a crucial step in producing a reliable yield prediction, espe-
cially since data on individual fruit counts is available. It is important 
to note that traditional image stitching is not feasible in this context 
due to the non-planar and non-panoramic nature of the tree structure. 
The MangoSense dataset is collected under natural conditions, making 
it well-suited for training models that need to generalize across real-
world agricultural environments. Furthermore, the inclusion of weather 
data allows for deeper analysis, helping to identify correlations between 
environmental conditions and yield patterns, which can improve the 
yield prediction models.
6. Conclusion
This study introduces a novel time-series vision dataset for mango 
yield estimation, addressing the challenges of traditional manual count-
ing methods. Captured from 12 mango trees across eight orientations 
over 2.5 months, the dataset consists of high-resolution images an-
notated using a semi-automated pipeline that uses YOLO and SAM 
models, significantly reducing annotation effort. Benchmarking results 
highlight the effectiveness of deep learning models for segmentation 
and detection for the flower and fruitlet classes. Additionally, this 
study explains the influence of wind direction on yield variability, 
challenging previous assumptions that yield differences solely due to 
canopy structure.
Although the dataset contains a sufficient number of images (40K) 
for fine-tuning segmentation and detection models, it is based on only 
12 trees spanning two mango varieties. For training a more robust 
and generalizable yield prediction model, a larger and more diverse 
dataset is required. Additionally, the current dataset represents a single 
fruiting season; incorporating data from multiple seasons would enable 
the models to better capture seasonal variations and improve predictive 
performance. The data was collected using a mobile phone, which 
is suitable for small- to medium-sized orchards. However, for large-
scale orchards, an automated data acquisition process using UAVs 
(Unmanned Aerial Vehicles) or UGVs (Unmanned Ground Vehicles) 
would be necessary to ensure scalability and efficiency. Furthermore, to 
develop high-fidelity 3D models of individual trees, a greater number 
of images per tree would be beneficial. While this work discusses the 
choice of feature extractors for downstream tasks, the development of 
a complete yield prediction model remains an important area for future 
work.
