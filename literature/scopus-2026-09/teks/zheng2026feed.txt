Feed-forward 3D reconstruction with point-cloud 
representations: From DUSt3R to VGGT and beyond
Zeyi ZHENG, Xiong YANG, Ran YI*, Lizhuang MA
School of Computer Science, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai 200240, China
Received 28 November 2025; Revised 12 March 2026; Accepted 26 March 2026
Abstract: This survey reviews feed-forward, point-cloud 3D reconstruction methods from DUSt3R to VGGT and 
their recent variants. Here, feed-forward primarily refers to predicting dense geometry and, when applicable, 
camera poses through learned network inference, without relying on classical per-scene SfM + MVS optimization 
as the main inference mechanism. We first formalize the reconstruction task in pose-aware and pose-free settings, 
and contrast feed-forward point-map regression with classical Structure-from-Motion and Multi-View Stereo 
pipelines. Building on this, we organize existing methods into three stages: early pairwise models typified by 
DUSt3R, DUSt3R-style extensions that enhance multi-view consistency, streaming, efficiency, and dynamic-scene 
handling, and large unified transformers such as VGGT that process tens to hundreds of views jointly, while noting 
differences in their inference paradigms. We analyze these models along shared axes, including scene 
representation, correspondence reasoning, pose regression, fusion strategies, and the role of large-scale training 
data. We summarize widely used 3D datasets and evaluation metrics, and provide a case study on the DTU 
benchmark for multi-view depth and point map estimation, highlighting accuracy-efficiency trade-offs between 
optimization-based and feed-forward approaches. Finally, we discuss open challenges in data scarcity, sparse-view 
reconstruction, non-Lambertian structures, dynamic scenes, long-context processing, and resource-efficient 
deployment, and outline future directions that combine feed-forward architectures with differentiable rendering, 
generative priors, and safety mechanisms to enable scalable and trustworthy 3D reconstruction systems.
Keywords: Feed-forward 3D reconstruction; Point cloud; Point-map; Vision Transformer; Multi-view depth 
estimation; Dynamic scene reconstruction; Virtual reality

1 Introduction
1.1 Background & motivation
3D reconstruction supports AR/VR, robotics, and autonomous driving by estimating camera poses and dense 
scene geometry from images. Classical optimization pipelines—Structure-from-Motion (SfM) followed by 
Multi-View Stereo (MVS) —remain strong in accuracy but rely on complex workflows and per-scene 
optimization, which leads to high latency and limited scalability; they also struggle with wide baselines, low 
texture, non-Lambertian surfaces, and occlusions [1–3]. Recent feed-forward methods (e.g., DUSt3R [1] and 
VGGT [2]) use learned inference to predict poses and dense geometry without treating classical per-scene 
optimization as the primary reconstruction mechanism. Many representative methods remain close to single-
pass prediction, while others augment it with lightweight alignment, memory, or test-time adaptation. 
Together, they combine global correspondence reasoning with Transformer backbones [3] and confidence-
guided fusion to achieve faster inference with competitive accuracy. These developments call for a 
systematic review of DUSt3R®VGGT models, their common design patterns, and their practical 
limitations, which we discuss in the following sections.
1.2 Scope
This survey covers feed-forward 3D reconstruction methods from DUSt3R (CVPR 2024) to VGGT (CVPR 
2025) and subsequent VGGT-family variants published up to October 2025. Our focus is on models that 
(i) predict dense geometry and, when applicable, camera poses primarily through learned feed-forward 
inference rather than classical per-scene iterative SfM + MVS optimization, and (ii) use explicit point-cloud/
pointmap representations for geometry.
We include two-view and multi-view settings, and distinguish strictly single-pass models from methods 
that additionally rely on lightweight alignment, persistent state, or limited test-time adaptation at inference 
time. For dynamic scenes, we include methods whose core geometry prediction remains feed-forward, while 
explicitly noting when temporal state or inference-time adaptation is introduced. Out of scope are 
optimization-based pipelines (SfM + MVS) and rendering-oriented methods (e.g., NeRF [4], 3D Gaussian 
splatting [5]) whose primary objective is view synthesis; when geometry-grounded renderable systems are 
briefly mentioned, they are treated only as downstream extensions or boundary cases rather than the main 
focus of this survey. Datasets, metrics, and evaluation protocols used for comparison are defined in 
Section 2.4.
1.3 Contributions
This survey makes the following contributions.
• Problem clarification. We formalize feed-forward 3D reconstruction in pose-aware and pose-free 
settings, and clearly define the pointmap/point-cloud-centric scope from DUSt3R to VGGT and their 
variants.
• Method taxonomy. We organize recent methods into three stages: early pairwise DUSt3R, DUSt3R-
style extensions (multi-view, streaming, efficiency, dynamic scenes), and unified VGGT-based frameworks.
• Unified comparison. We summarize representative 3D datasets and metrics, and adopt a DTU-based 
protocol to compare classical SfM + MVS and feed-forward models on multi-view depth and point map 
estimation.
• Insights and outlook. We distill common design patterns and discuss open problems in data scarcity, 
sparse views, challenging materials, long-context processing, deployment efficiency, and safety.

2 Preliminaries
2.1 Task formulation
We consider 3D reconstruction from an image set I ={Ii}N
i = 1, where the goal is to predict per-pixel scene 
geometry—depth maps or pixel-aligned pointmaps for one or multiple views. Inputs may be pose-aware 
(camera intrinsics/extrinsics are provided) or pose-free (camera parameters are estimated at test time). 
Outputs are pixelwise depth or 3D points, which can be transformed via extrinsics into a global point cloud 
for downstream use. In this survey, we use feed-forward to denote methods whose primary reconstruction 
signal is produced by learned network inference rather than classical per-scene SfM + MVS optimization. 
We further distinguish strictly single-pass models from methods that additionally introduce lightweight 
alignment, persistent state, or limited test-time adaptation during inference.
2.2 Traditional pipelines
As shown in Figure 1, classical 3D reconstruction follows a two-stage, optimization-based pipeline: SfM 
first estimates camera intrinsics/extrinsics and a sparse 3D structure from feature matching, geometric 
verification (e.g., RANSAC [6]), and global or incremental bundle adjustment (BA) [7]; MVS then computes 
dense depth for each view by multi-view photometric/geometry consistency and fuses the results into a point 
cloud or surface (e.g., [8]). These methods remain the solution of choice for applications that demand very 
high accuracy and strict geometric consistency. However, they rely on per-scene optimization and complex 
toolchains, which raise end-to-end latency and deployment complexity; they are also sensitive to wide 
baselines, low or repetitive texture, non-Lambertian materials, and occlusions, where errors can accumulate 
across stages.

2.3 Neural reconstruction evolution
Early learning-based methods such as MVSNet [9], DPSNet [10], and CasMVSNet [11] introduced learned 
features and cost volumes on top of the traditional pipeline, improving matching quality and depth 
estimation under moderate baselines. Compared with purely hand-crafted MVS, these methods learned 
stronger image representations and more robust matching costs, but they still largely operated in a pose-
aware setting and inherited the core logic of classical Multi-View Stereo: camera geometry was assumed to 
be known or pre-estimated, and scene structure was recovered through staged cost-volume construction, 
regularization, and depth regression. Although explicitly constructed cost volumes offer strong 
controllability and interpretability, these systems remain modular and sequential: feature extraction, cost-
volume aggregation, depth/pose estimation, and post-fusion are implemented as separate stages, and many 
methods still rely on explicit multi-view aggregation or test-time refinement. Consequently, latency and 
memory usage remain high, and the robustness issues of traditional pipelines are not fundamentally 
resolved. In this sense, MVSNet-style methods can be viewed as learned extensions of the classical SfM + 
MVS pipeline rather than a departure from it. Their main limitation is not only computational cost, but also 
the fact that multi-view reasoning remains tightly tied to explicit geometric conditioning and staged depth 
inference.
The emergence of feed-forward models, exemplified by DUSt3R, VGGT, and their variants, marks a shift 
from optimization-centered reconstruction to primarily feed-forward inference. In these models, Vision 
Transformers [3] enable global correspondence reasoning across views without making classical per-scene 
SfM + MVS optimization the dominant inference procedure. The key methodological shift is that recent 
pointmap-based models move away from pose-conditioned cost-volume inference toward more direct 
geometric prediction: DUSt3R regresses pointmaps from unposed image pairs and recovers pose and 
correspondences from the predicted geometry, while later multi-view models strengthen scene-level 
consistency and broader cross-view reasoning. VGGT pushes this trend further by processing many views 
jointly within a unified transformer backbone, thereby integrating geometry, pose, and tracking into a single 
multi-view prediction framework. This evolution improves the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency 
and simplifies deployment, making these methods suitable for real-time or large-batch processing in AR/VR 
and robotics.
2.4 Datasets & evaluation metrics
Datasets. Feed-forward 3D reconstruction models are highly data-driven, and their performance depends 
strongly on the scale, diversity, and quality of the training data. However, compared with large-scale 2D 
image datasets, existing 3D benchmarks are still relatively limited in number, coverage, and annotation 
richness. In this survey, we therefore consider widely used indoor/outdoor and object/scene datasets, 
combining real-world and synthetic sources [12] to probe robustness across texture, lighting, motion, and 
viewpoint changes; their main characteristics are summarized in Table 1 [13–33]. For quantitative experiments 
in Section 4, we focus on the DTU dataset [19], which provides calibrated multi-view scans and high-
quality ground-truth geometry, and is one of the standard benchmarks for multi-view depth and point map 
evaluation.

3 Methodology review
3.1 Stage I: early feed-forward models—DUSt3R
Overview & architecture. As shown in Figure 1, DUSt3R [1] formulates pairwise reconstruction from 
uncalibrated, unposed image pairs as pointmap regression: given (I1I2 ), a weight-sharing ViT encoder and 
dual-branch Transformer decoder (self-attention per view, cross-attention across views) predict two pixel-
aligned pointmaps in the first-view frame together with a dense confidence map. These outputs directly 
provide per-pixel 3D geometry; camera intrinsics/extrinsics, depth, and correspondences are then recovered 
from the pointmaps via standard estimators or the global alignment step, without bundle adjustment or test-
time refinement. Moreover, the Transformer backbone can encode strong priors from large-scale training 
data, giving DUSt3R a natural advantage in challenging cases such as sparse viewpoints, where classical 
matching-based pipelines often fail.
From pairs to multi-view. For N > 2 images, DUSt3R performs a lightweight global 3D alignment over 
the connectivity graph: per-pair pointmaps with confidences are aligned to a common world frame using a 
rigid transform and scale per edge. The objective minimizes confidence-weighted 3D point distances rather 
than 2D reprojection errors. It is optimized by simple gradient descent, converging in seconds on a GPU to 
yield globally consistent pointmaps and poses, without global bundle adjustment. This design preserves the 
flexibility of pairwise prediction, but as the number of views grows, pair enumeration and post-hoc 
alignment can become a bottleneck for both efficiency and global scene-level consistency.
3.2 Stage II: 3R enhanced feature aggregation
Rationale. While DUSt3R demonstrates that pairwise pointmap regression can replace multi-stage 
optimization, it still aggregates information pairwise with post-fusion, which accumulates errors across 
views, and treats pose and geometry largely in a serial manner. This motivates a broad family of DUSt3R-
style extensions that improve representation quality, multi-view consistency, streaming capability, 
efficiency, and adaptation to more challenging settings.
Direction. Subsequent feed-forward methods address these issues by (i) raising representation quality for 
stronger correspondences, (ii) cross-view reasoning via multi-view fusion or global 3D alignment inside the 
network, and (iii) leveraging geometric regularization to stabilize aggregation. Beyond core accuracy, they 
introduce specializations for multi-view inputs and streaming/long-context operation, efficiency-oriented 
designs (distillation, sparse attention), and task/domain adaptations (e.g., autonomous driving, relocalization). 
We emphasize that the following Stage II taxonomy is not strictly exclusive: it groups methods by their 
primary functional emphasis, and several representative methods naturally span multiple categories. As 
illustrated in Figure 2, the following subsections organize these developments by two-view strengthening, 
multi-view/global consistency, streaming/long-context, efficiency, and dynamic-scene handling.
3.2.1 Two-view strengthening: reconstruction quality

Specifically, MASt3R [35] augments the DUSt3R backbone with a dense descriptor head that outputs per-

reconstruction. Building on these predictors, MASt3R-SfM [36] forms a training-free SfM pipeline that uses 
MASt3R features for scalable retrieval and replaces RANSAC with a global 3D matching objective plus a 
light 2D reprojection refinement, achieving near-linear complexity and robustness under few-view or pure-
rotation cases.
3.2.2 Multi-view/global consistency
This line of work goes beyond pairwise fusion by enforcing cross-view coherence either through multi-view 
fusion inside the network or via explicit global 3D alignment/registration, so that all inputs share a 
consistent world frame without heavy BA. The goal is to reduce error accumulation across views and 
improve scene-level consistency under larger multi-view inputs.
Specifically, MV-DUSt3R+ [37] ingests many views in a single pass with multi-view decoder blocks and 
cross-reference-view attention, removing the second-stage alignment and delivering large accuracy/runtime 
gains for sparse inputs. MUSt3R [38] extends DUSt3R from pairwise prediction to a symmetric multi-view 
architecture that predicts all views in a shared coordinate frame, replacing asymmetric pairwise decoding 
with joint cross-view reasoning and introducing memory mechanisms to improve scalability beyond 
quadratic pair enumeration. Regist3R [39] adopts an incremental strategy: given a reference image and its 
world-frame pointmap, it predicts each target view’s pointmap in the same frame and schedules views with 
a lightweight graph policy, enabling scalable autoregressive reconstruction across large, unordered 
collections. Pow3R [40] injects versatile priors at test time—intrinsics, relative pose, and sparse/dense depth
—through lightweight conditioning, enabling high-resolution sliding-window inference, and accelerating 
pose via dual-coordinate pointmaps with Procrustes alignment [41].
3.2.3 Streaming/long-context
This thread targets online operation and long sequences, emphasizing constant-memory updates, stable 
poses across thousands of frames, and real-time throughput. Compared with early strictly single-pass feed-
forward models, methods in this category often introduce persistent state, recurrent updates, or lightweight 
test-time adaptation to maintain consistency over long sequences. SLAM3R [42] integrates a sliding-
window image-to-points module with a local-to-world registration network to incrementally fuse local 
pointmaps into a globally consistent scene without explicitly solving camera parameters. Reloc3r [43] 
complements reconstruction with a large-scale trained relative pose regression backbone and a minimalist

3.2.4 Efficiency
This thread targets throughput and memory, aiming at scaling feed-forward reconstruction to hundreds or 
thousands of images with seconds-level latency. Fast3R [46] processes many unposed views in one forward 
pass via a ViT encoder and an all-to-all fusion transformer. It eliminates pairwise enumeration and global 
alignment, scaling to 1000 + images while improving pose and geometry accuracy at much higher speed. 
Light3R-SfM [47] replaces optimization-based global alignment with a latent global alignment attention 
module and builds a sparse shortest-path scene graph for decoding, achieving competitive accuracy with 
major runtime gains. Spann3R [48] enables online, incremental reconstruction by regressing each frame’s 
pointmap directly in a global coordinate system using an external spatial memory.
3.2.5 Dynamic scenes
Dynamic scenes introduce additional ambiguity for feed-forward 3D reconstruction: object motion violates 
the rigid-scene assumption behind pairwise pointmaps, breaks epipolar consistency, and makes it harder to 
jointly estimate geometry and camera motion from videos. Recent DUSt3R-style methods have started to 
address this setting while preserving the pointmap/point-cloud representation and mostly feed-forward 
inference.
At the representation level, recent work such as dynamic point maps (DPM) [49] reflects an important 
extension from static pointmaps to dynamic or 4D pointmaps. Dynamic point maps explicitly models both 
viewpoint and time, providing a more natural representation for jointly capturing geometry, motion, and 
time-varying visibility in dynamic scenes.
From the perspective of inference paradigm, most works adapt DUSt3R by finetuning on dynamic video 
datasets with additional priors. MonST3R [50] extends the original pointmap formulation to per-timestep 
geometry on dynamic videos and uses small-scale supervised finetuning for video depth and pose. Align3R 
[51] couples a monocular depth estimator with a DUSt3R backbone, using pairwise pointmaps to align per-
frame depth and recover temporally consistent video depth and camera trajectories. D2USt3R [52] keeps the 
D2USt3R architecture but introduces motion-aware supervision to regress 4D pointmaps that align static and 
dynamic regions jointly. Point3R [53] focuses on streaming reconstruction and maintains an explicit spatial 
memory of 3D pointers, which can integrate information from dynamic sequences in a single pass. Driv3R 
[54] targets at large-scale autonomous driving scenes: it predicts per-frame pointmaps from multi-view 
camera rigs, augments DUSt3R with a temporal–spatial memory pool and a 4D flow predictor for dynamic-
object focusing, and aligns per-frame pointmaps into a global coordinate frame in an optimization-free 
manner, enabling real-time dense 4D reconstruction in driving scenarios. In contrast, Easi3R [55] follows a 
training-free paradigm: it analyzes and reweights DUSt3R’s attention maps at inference time to decouple 
camera and object motion, obtaining dynamic masks, camera poses, and 4D pointmaps without additional 
finetuning. These methods therefore differ in inference behavior: MonST3R, Align3R, and D2USt3R remain 
closer to feed-forward per-frame prediction, whereas Point3R and Driv3R rely more explicitly on spatial or

3.3 Stage III: advanced frameworks—VGGT
From DUSt3R to VGGT. Although DUSt3R is the first widely adopted model to demonstrate that a purely 
data-driven, feed-forward paradigm can dramatically improve 3D reconstruction, its pairwise design makes 
it difficult to train on and reason about an entire scene jointly. During training, the model only observes 
image pairs; even if multi-view fusion is performed at test time, this fusion happens outside the network, so 
the backbone itself never sees the full scene context. Incorporating fusion inside training would require 
keeping many views and pointmaps in memory simultaneously, leading to huge GPU cost. In practice, this 
also creates a characteristic trade-off: DUSt3R remains flexible for sparse or modular pairwise reasoning, 
but its computation and post-hoc alignment cost grow unfavorably as the number of views increases.
Unlike DUSt3R’s pairwise encoder-decoder with explicit cross-attention and a post-hoc global alignment 
stage, VGGT [2] is a single, large transformer that ingests images at once and predicts cameras (intrinsics/
extrinsics), depth maps, point maps, and dense tracking features in a single forward pass, as shown in Figure 
1. The backbone alternates frame-wise and global self-attention layers (without cross-attention), balancing 
per-frame normalization with cross-view information exchange. Special camera tokens and register tokens 
are appended per frame, with the first frame marked by distinct learnable tokens so that all dense outputs are 
expressed in the first-camera coordinate frame; dense heads (a DPT head [3]) map image tokens to depth, 
point maps, uncertainties, and tracking features, while a dedicated camera head predicts intrinsics and 
extrinsics.
This design uses minimal hand-crafted 3D inductive biases, relying instead on scale and supervision from 
diverse 3D-annotated data. Optional BA can further refine results, but the feed-forward predictions are 
already competitive and typically run in sub-second for tens to hundreds of views. Compared with DUSt3R, 
VGGT shifts more of the multi-view reasoning into the backbone itself, which improves scene-level 
consistency and unifies geometry, pose, and tracking predictions, but also comes with a different systems 
trade-off: stronger joint reasoning at the cost of higher memory pressure for large-scale inputs and long 
sequences. Building on VGGT, recent variants target architecture, efficiency, and deployability in 
complementary ways, which are summarized in Figure 3.

extrinsics and voxelized Gaussians, delivering fast, pose-free novel-view synthesis.
4 Experiments
We evaluate representative methods on four aspects: (i) multi-view depth estimation on DTU, (ii) point map 
estimation on DTU [19], (iii) runtime comparison under different input lengths, and (iv) pose-related 
evaluation on ScanNet [14]. Since feed-forward reconstruction is highly data-driven, we focus on the DTU 
benchmark, which provides calibrated multi-view scans and high-quality ground-truth geometry for 
controlled comparison, while using ScanNet to complement the geometry analysis with pose-related 
evaluation under a multi-frame reconstruction setting.

5 Analysis and discussion
From the DTU tables and the qualitative figure above, the DTU results highlight the benefits of a unified 
feed-forward architecture such as VGGT over both traditional SfM + MVS pipelines and earlier DUSt3R-
style models. Classical pipelines decompose reconstruction into sparse SfM, dense MVS, and post-fusion, 
which leads to high end-to-end latency and complex engineering. DUSt3R already simplifies this workflow 
by replacing multi-stage optimization with pairwise pointmap regression followed by a lightweight global 
alignment, but its pairwise design restricts the model to local context during training, and multi-view 
consistency is only enforced post-hoc.
In contrast, VGGT treats multi-view reconstruction as a single feed-forward prediction problem: a single 
transformer ingests all views jointly and outputs cameras, depth, and pointmaps in one pass. Empirically, 
this translates into higher Overall scores on DTU, strong pose-related performance on ScanNet [14], and 
more favorable runtime scaling as the number of input frames increases. The runtime comparison shows that 
unified models such as VGGT and π3 scale substantially better than pairwise DUSt3R-style reconstruction, 
whereas CUT3R is most advantageous in settings closer to its intended streaming usage. Meanwhile, the 
ScanNet pose evaluation suggests that stronger scene-level aggregation benefits not only depth and 
pointmap prediction but also the recovery of consistent camera geometry. At the same time, these 
comparisons also reveal a more nuanced systems trade-off: DUSt3R-style methods remain flexible under 
sparse or modular settings, whereas VGGT-style models push more reasoning into the backbone itself, 
improving scene-level consistency at the cost of higher memory pressure for larger inputs and longer 
sequences.
5.1 Existing challenges
Despite the progress of DUSt3R-VGGT style models, several challenges remain. First, current feed-forward 
reconstruction is strongly limited by data: large-scale 3D datasets with accurate poses, depth, semantics, and 
material labels are still scarce compared to 2D image collections. As a result, models are often trained on a 
relatively small set of benchmarks or rely on pseudo ground truth from SfM + MVS, which constrains 
diversity and may propagate biases and noise from the teacher pipelines.
Second, sparse-view reconstruction and novel-view synthesis remain difficult. Most pointmap-based 
models are discriminative: they estimate geometry only for observed views and do not explicitly model the 
full radiance field or shape distribution. Under very sparse viewpoints, the network cannot reliably infer 
occluded or back-facing geometry, and there is no principled way to hallucinate plausible structure for 
completely unseen regions, in contrast to generative NeRF- or diffusion-based approaches.
Third, non-ideal materials and complex radiometry are still poorly handled. Semi-transparent and 
reflective objects exhibit strong view-dependent effects that violate simple Lambertian assumptions; thin-
shell structures (wires, fences, foliage) are close to or below pixel resolution; and very distant content such 
as sky or horizon lies effectively at infinity. These factors challenge both the pointmap representation and 
standard depth-based losses, leading to incomplete geometry, floaters, or over-smoothed reconstructions in 
such regions.
5.2 Future directions
Looking ahead, a major frontier for feed-forward 3D reconstruction lies in extending current models to 
dynamic scenes and in scaling training beyond the limited pool of curated 3D datasets. Truly annotated 3D

data remain orders of magnitude scarcer than 2D images and videos; in practice, many recent models 
already exhaust most publicly available 3D benchmarks plus sizable proprietary collections, yet are still far 
from saturating Internet-scale data. Bridging this gap will likely require tighter integration between feed-
forward architectures and differentiable rendering, so that rich 3D representations can be learned from 2D-
level supervision at scale.
Another emerging trend is the interaction with generative models. On the one hand, generative models of 
3D or 4D content can provide high-quality synthetic datasets with controllable coverage of viewpoints, 
materials, and motion, alleviating some of the data scarcity for supervised training. On the other hand, 
generative priors can be used at inference time to hallucinate geometry for unseen viewpoints or occluded 
regions, complementing the purely discriminative nature of current pointmap-based models.
Efficiency and deployment are another key axis. Current VGGT-style architectures rely on dense per-pixel 
pointmap prediction and full attention over tens of frames, which leads to high memory usage and energy 
cost, and limits training to relatively small view counts (e.g., 24 images per batch). For mobile and edge 
devices such as VR, lighter-weight representations and tokenization schemes are needed, such as 
hierarchical or patch-wise 3D outputs, sparse or low-rank attention.
Finally, safety and security considerations will become increasingly important as feed-forward 3D 
reconstruction and view synthesis mature. High-quality 3D reconstructions and 4D scene models can be 
abused for realistic forgeries, privacy-invasive content, or misleading digital twins. Future work should 
therefore also address provenance tracking, robust watermarking for generated content, and mechanisms to 
detect or limit malicious use, ensuring that advances in DUSt3R/VGGT-style models translate into 
trustworthy and responsible 3D systems.
