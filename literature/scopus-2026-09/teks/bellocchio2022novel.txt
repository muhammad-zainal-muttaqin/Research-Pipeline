A B S T R A C T
Autonomous systems have been established as a ground-breaking technology in agriculture, particularly
for resource optimization and labor savings. However, even those solutions that are limited to monitoring
activities, such as yield estimation, rely on costly robotic platforms equipped with a series of range devices (e.g.,
LIDAR and GPS-RTK). Recently, vision-based strategies have gained considerable attention as a less expensive
and more efficient alternative, capable to be on par with or even surpass approaches that benefit from range
sensors. Nonetheless, they exploit deep learning methodologies, which require burdensome labeling procedures
to perform training. To address these shortcomings, we present a novel approach that performs yield estimation
requiring only a monocular camera and needs a limited amount of supervision information. It detects, locates
and maps fruits and tree canopies to estimate the total yield of a specific crop. To keep the image labeling effort
to a minimum, we propose a weakly-supervision paradigm that only requires a simple binary label encoding
the presence or the absence of fruits in the training images. Our approach does not make any assumptions on
the underlying platform, i.e., it can be used by collecting images either with a hand-held camera or with an
autonomous robot. Therefore, we are able to considerably reduce the deployment time, the energy and the
cost of the overall yield estimation system. At the same time, we keep the performance comparable to both
vision-based fully supervised baselines (which require costly labeling operations) and classical systems that
rely on more expensive and power-demanding sensors.
1. Introduction
In the last few years, autonomous systems have become increasingly
pervasive in agricultural applications. Their use has brought several
benefits and allowed the growers to (i) enhance monitoring and control
of their cultivation, (ii) optimize the yield operations and ease the
maintenance procedures and (iii) save resources and minimize human
efforts (Zermas et al., 2020; Sulistyo et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2011;
Lambert et al., 2014; Tong et al., 2021; de Araújo et al., 2015). Among
the different key operations in agricultural scenarios, yield estimation,
whose objective is to measure the total yield of a given crop is certainly
one of those that benefited the most from these solutions. Their advan-
tages are particularly evident in contexts with very wide cultivations
or in scenarios where human intervention is limited or completely
prohibited (e.g., during the current COVID-19 pandemic outbreak).
Most of the State-of-the-Art (SotA) yield estimation systems employ
robotic platforms mainly equipped with three types of sensors: range
sensors (e.g., LIDAR and sonar sensors), localization sensors (e.g., GPS
and GPS-RTK) and vision systems (e.g., monocular, multi-spectral and
stereo cameras).

approaches and may not be accessible to small and medium farms with
limited financial resources.
For these reasons, the interest in vision-based strategies, that only
need low-cost cameras, has rapidly increased in recent years. The
impressive results achieved with Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are cur-
rently empowering autonomous systems with high-level reasoning and
vision capabilities, making possible the effective application to contexts
that only a decade ago were considered to be non-resolvable (Tateno
et al., 2017; Ran et al., 2017). Even with just a single camera, it is today
possible to detect fruits, track them across frames and localize the cam-
era to avoid multiple counts of the same object and estimate the yield
of the entire orchard. Nonetheless, the best performing DNN-based
approaches require a huge amount of labeled data (images or videos)

estimation system that benefits from the advantages of vision sensors
and, at the same time, exploits a weakly supervised paradigm to heavily
reduce the effort needed to train the DNNs (see Fig. 1).
Differently from state-of-the-art methods, our approach is a com-

the position of the detected fruits using only a monocular camera and
a minimal amount of supervision.
As we discuss in the following sections, all the modules in our
system are designed to keep to a minimum the annotation efforts, by
either exploiting weak supervision that requires simple and easy-to-
compute labels or by relying on publicly available pre-trained models
that do not need further optimization or fine-tuning (e.g., depth and
optical flow estimation modules). Furthermore, our approach requires
only a monocular camera and does not make any assumption on the
underlying platform: the sensor can be either mounted on ground
or aerial robots, or hand-held by a human operator. To the best of
our knowledge, this is the first attempt to perform vision-based yield
estimation exploiting the weakly supervised paradigm.

2. Related work
The importance of autonomous systems in precision farming has ex-
ponentially grown in the last few years (Thayer et al., 2020; Berenstein
and Edan, 2018; Williams et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2020). For the
yield estimation task, alongside range sensors (e.g., LIDAR or lasers),
there is increasing adoption of vision-based methods (mostly based
on CNNs) that are very effective also in unstructured or partially
unstructured environments. A modern vision-based yield estimation
approach is a complex system capable to detect, track and map the
fruits collecting streams of images. Such a system consists of different
functional modules, among which the most relevant is certainly the
fruit detection module. Its purpose is to detect and locate the fruits
within a single image. In this section, we first provide an overview of
the methods used for fruit localization and then we analyze how these
methodologies are employed for yield estimation.
2.1. Object detection and counting for agriculture applications
Fully supervised CNN architectures for detection and counting
have achieved impressive results in a wide variety of datasets and
tasks (Zhang et al., 2015, 2017; Khan et al., 2016; Bargoti and Un-
derwood, 2017a). While their effectiveness is evident and extensively
demonstrated, the need for massive amounts of data and supervision
labels limits their practical applicability. For these reasons, weakly
supervised methods have emerged as a viable and more convenient al-
ternative: their objective is to guarantee performance comparable with
fully-supervised baselines while relying on less informative and easier
to acquire labels. A diagram depicting the fundamental difference
between fully and weakly supervised methods is shown in Fig. 2(a).
In the following paragraphs, we first describe fully-supervised meth-
ods used for fruit detection and counting, and then we outline the
weakly-supervised approaches.
2.1.1. Fully supervised methods
Fruit detection and counting methods can be divided into detection-
based,
segmentation-based
and
regression-based
approaches
(see Fig. 2(a)). Detection-based strategies rely on CNNs that are op-
timized to predict the location of fruits in the image. Counting is then
achieved by simply enumerating the number of detected fruits. To
perform optimization, the training images need to be annotated with
ground truth bounding boxes indicating the category and the region of
the image containing the object. These strategies have been successfully
used in Bargoti and Underwood (2017a), Mai et al. (2020) and Mu et al.
(2020). All of them rely on FasterRCNN (Ren et al., 2015) to perform
detection. In particular, in Bargoti and Underwood (2017a) the model is
fine-tuned to detect apples, almonds and mangoes, while in Mu et al.
(2020) the authors focus on tomatoes. Instead, in Mai et al. (2020),
FasterRCNN (Ren et al., 2015) is extended to incorporate a multiple
classifier fusion strategy and trained to detect oranges. Similarly, in Liu
et al. (2020) the authors also consider tomatoes, however, they take
advantage of the YOLO (Redmon et al., 2016) architecture.
As an alternative, CNN-based segmentation methods, which perform
classification at the pixel level, could be used to measure the number

impressive, the collection of the supervision information needed for
training is burdensome and time-consuming. This is particularly evi-
dent for detection and segmentation-based solutions since they require
highly informative labels, i.e., bounding-boxes or pixel-wise annota-
tions. On the other hand, while the labeling effort for regression
methods is lower, their output does not contain information to local-
ize the fruits, which is crucial to track them across frames to avoid
redundant counts.
2.1.2. Weakly supervised methods
Weakly supervised approaches aim to ease the burden of sample
annotation by relying on less informative yet easy-to-acquire labels.
Since, in this case, the optimization is not supervised with quantities
that explicitly measure the desired output variable, it is common to
exploit coherency losses or auxiliary tasks. An example of this process,
inspired by the work presented in Bellocchio et al. (2019), is depicted
in Fig. 2(b). In this case, weak supervision information (i.e., pres-
ence/absence labels) is first used to train a simple presence/absence
classifier, whose goal is to predict whether the input image contains
fruits or not. Its binary predictions are then combined with the counts
regressed at multiple scales to impose different consistency losses and
train the object count regression network.
In the literature, there are several weakly supervised approaches
used for object recognition, detection, semantic segmentation and in-
stance segmentation (Durand et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2018; Oquab
et al., 2014; Bilen et al., 2015, 2014). Promising works that benefit
from the weakly supervised paradigm have also been proposed for
agricultural applications. In Marino et al. (2019) a CNN is trained with
image-wise classification labels to locate several types of potato defects
within the images. For fruit counting, the approach proposed in Belloc-
chio et al. (2019), which we summarized in the previous paragraph
and in Fig. 2(b), has been extended in Bellocchio et al. (2020), where
the authors propose a quasi unsupervised framework that combines
a weakly supervised counting network and an unsupervised domain
adaptation method. The authors demonstrate that with this strategy
it is not necessary to acquire new labels (not even weak ones) when
the application domain changes (i.e., the model is used to count a fruit
category different from those in the training set).
Despite the interesting results achieved, most of the work previously
mentioned are not specifically designed for agricultural tasks. On the
other hand, those that address yield estimation (i.e., (Bellocchio et al.,
2019) and Bellocchio et al. (2020)) are not designed to locate fruits,
thus, there are no straightforward ways to match them across frames
and distinguish between new and already tracked elements.
2.2. Yield estimation systems
The works discussed previously focus solely on the detection and
counting of fruits in a single image. In this section, we summarize the
approaches that integrate the counting module into a complete yield
estimation system capable to track and locate fruits. These solutions
mostly rely on images acquired by vision sensors mounted on a robotic
platform, in some cases with the support of additional sensors such as
GPS/GPS-RTK or laser and LIDAR range sensors.
Estimating the yield of an entire orchard raises numerous challenges
that cannot be addressed by using only the fruit detection module, in
particular:
• Redundant counts in sequential images: the same fruit may
appear in multiple sequential frames. To avoid redundant counts
of the same fruit, it is necessary to distinguish between new and
old ones.
• Occlusions: Fruits that are visible from a certain point of view
might become occluded by foliage or other elements as the cam-
era moves. It is necessary to recognize those fruits once they
become visible again and avoid considering them as newly ob-
served.
• Redundant counts from multiple points of view: The same
fruit can be observed from different points of view (e.g., from two
sides of the same cultivation row). Since multiple observations
might not be sequential, tracking the fruits across consecutive
frames is not sufficient. Instead, fruit positions with respect to the
trees and the orchard need to be memorized to recognize them
when observed in the future.

Most of these issues can be resolved by using methodologies already
adopted for other applications, such as camera pose estimation, local-
ization and mapping. In Nuske et al. (2014) the authors extract hand-
crafted features for fruit detection, i.e., Radial Symmetry Transform
and Invariant Maximal Detector, from RGB stereo images collected by
an Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV). These visual cues are used to
localize the camera (and, as a consequence, the UGV) and detect fruits
in grape orchards. Conversely, the strategy proposed in Roy and Isler
(2016) uses a combination of Structure from Motion and SIFT/SURF
features to predict apple sizes and perform counting.
Since it is well known that hand-crafted features are not robust to
image non-idealities, CNN models, which provide more robust descrip-
tors, have become more popular even in agricultural contexts. In Stein
et al. (2016) the authors present a yield estimation framework that
combines the information from auxiliary sensors and the visual features
extracted by a CNN. The proposed strategy is composed of a fruit
detector based on FasterRCNN (Ren et al., 2015), a LIDAR sensor and
a GPS-RTK coupled with an inertial unit. Fruit matching, occlusions
and multi-view errors are avoided by estimating the robot pose and
by 3D positioning the detected fruits. Bargoti and Underwood (2017b)
presents a solution that combines semantic segmentation maps, com-
puted with a CNN architecture and a Multi Layer Perceptron, with GPS
data.
In the last years, researchers have focused on strategies that rely
only on monocular cameras and remove the need for range and differ-
ential positioning sensors. Besides the reduction of the costs, this allows
extending the applicability of those systems also in indoor scenarios.
Following this direction, Nellithimaru and Kantor (2019) uses only
RGB images collected by a UGV for yield estimation within grape
orchards. The authors take advantage of MaskRCNN (He et al., 2017)
to perform fruit segmentation. Redundant counts and occlusions are
avoided by localizing the robot pose and the fruit locations with a
vision-based SLAM method. Multiple-counting errors can be avoided
also using Structure-from-Motion, as in Liu et al. (2018, 2019). To per-
form yield estimation in a mango orchard, the former uses a semantic
segmentation CNN while the latter relies on an object detector one. In
both cases, occlusions and redundant counts are avoided by combining
a Kalman-based object tracker with a semantic Structure-from-Motion
algorithm.
More recently, other approaches try also to perform yield estimation
with hyperspectral images, such as in Gutiérrez et al. (2019), where this
sensor is used to estimate the yield in large mango orchards.
2.3. Contributions
All the weakly supervised approaches described in Section 2.1.2 are
designed to regress the number of fruits in a single image. They do not
provide strategies to locate and track fruits across consecutive frames.
Therefore, they cannot distinguish between fruits already observed in
the past or new instances, making it impossible to estimate the yield of
a cultivation.
On the other hand, the systems presented in Section 2.2 can track
and triangulate into a global 3D map the fruits detected in the images,
which allows them to estimate the total yield. However, their fruit de-
tectors have some important drawbacks. Approaches such Nuske et al.
(2014) and Roy and Isler (2016) employ hand-crafted features or design
heuristic functions that directly process RGB image intensities, which
results in systems that are very specific to the fruit species and that lack
robustness to non-ideal conditions. Conversely, the strategies proposed
in Stein et al. (2016), Bargoti and Underwood (2017b), Nellithimaru
and Kantor (2019) and Liu et al. (2018, 2019) exploit the more robust
CNN detector. However, they require full supervision to train them and,
thus, do not exploit the weakly supervised paradigm. As a consequence,
the data collection process for their optimization is burdensome and
time-consuming, and limits their practical applicability.
The approach proposed in this work aims to overcome the afore-
mentioned issues. In the following, we summarize our contributions:
• In contrast to the yield estimation systems discussed in Section 2.2
(i.e., Nuske et al., 2014; Roy and Isler, 2016; Stein et al., 2016;
Bargoti and Underwood, 2017b; Nellithimaru and Kantor, 2019;
Liu et al., 2018, 2019), we propose a fruit detection approach
that benefits from the representational power of CNNs, and,
at the same time, exploits the weakly supervised paradigm. In
particular, it is designed to require only binary labels encoding
the presence or absence of fruits in images for the training phase.
These annotations are considerably easier and faster to obtain
than bounding boxes or pixel-wise semantic segmentation masks
and do not require the intervention of expert operators.
• Our system requires only a monocular camera to operate, avoid-
ing expensive range sensors and the need for complex sensor-rig
setups.
• Differently from the weakly supervised approaches presented
in Marino et al. (2019) and Bellocchio et al. (2019, 2020), the
proposed fruit detection module is integrated into a complete
yield estimation system capable to: avoid redundant counts across
sequential frames; handle occlusions; estimate the depth of the
detected fruits.
• To recognize and avoid multiple counts of the same fruit from
different points of view, a 3D global map with fruits and cam-
era poses is incrementally built and refined with a graph-based
optimization framework.
3. Proposed approach
In this section, we describe our proposed weakly-supervised yield
estimation system. Our framework is composed of several functional
blocks (see Fig. 3):
1. Fruit detection module, composed of the Weakly-Supervised
Fruit
Localization
Network
(WS-FLNet)
described
in
Sections 3.1.1 and 3.1.2. It is responsible to localize fruits in
the image. In particular, it predicts pixel-wise maps indicating
the presence and the position of the fruits. To train this module,
we do not require pixel-level annotations, instead, we use a
binary label that indicates the presence or the absence of fruits
within the image. Specifically, we annotate an image with label
0 if it does not contain any fruit and with label 1 when one
or more fruits are present, regardless of their number. This
module outputs the set of detected fruits described by their 2D
coordinates in the image plane.
2. 2D fruit tracking module (Section 3.1.3). Fruits are tracked
on the image plane across frames by combining the detected
elements with a Kalman filter-based multi-object tracker. To
refine the fruit position estimates during the filter measurement
update, we also compute optical flow maps (see FlowNet2 (Ilg
et al., 2017) block in Fig. 3) encoding the element displacement
between consecutive frames. The output of this module consists
of a set of tracked fruits (up to the current time step) that are
individually described by the 2D pixel position and velocity in
the image plane.
3. Tree
canopy/trunk
detector
and
tracking
module
(Section 3.2). To associate fruit counts to trees, a specific ro-
bust tree detection module is required. This is achieved with
a tree canopy/trunk detector. As for the fruits, tree tracking is
performed by a Kalman filter. This module provides the image
regions containing the tree canopy and trunks tracked up to the
current time step.
4. 3D fruit positioning module (Section 3.3). To avoid the re-
counts of the same fruit from multiple points of view, fruits
are 3D positioned with respect to the associated tree trunk. The
projection of a detected element in the image plane into its 3D
world coordinates is computed by estimating its depth using
another dedicated functional block implemented by a CNN (see

It is important to emphasize that the training of the fruit detection
module is weakly supervised, while depth maps and optical flows
are estimated by using publicly available modules without fine-tuning
them. Therefore, the sole annotations needed to optimize these modules
are the presence–absence labels of the images. On the other hand, the
trunk/canopy detector module is fully supervised with ground-truth
bounding boxes. Nonetheless, the time needed to acquire these labels is
negligible compared to the annotation effort that a fully supervised fruit
detection module would require and, hence, it does not significantly
impact the overall deployment cost (quantitative evaluation on the
labeling time are provided in Section 4.3). This is even more evident
if we consider that trunks and canopies of different fruit species share
similar shapes. Therefore, once the trunk/canopy detector is trained
with a reasonable variety and amount of samples, it is not necessary
to retrain or fine-tune it when the approach is deployed on different
agricultural domains.
3.1. Weakly-supervised fruit detection and tracking
The proposed fruit detection module takes inspiration from the work
presented in Bellocchio et al. (2019), with the significant difference
that in the present study the weak supervision paradigm is exploited
to detect and localize fruits, while the aim of the previous study is
to simply regress their number. Fruit localization is crucial for yield
estimation, since it makes it possible to track and match fruits across
frames to avoid redundant counts, and to build a global fruit map,
fundamental to analyze the yield distribution in the orchard.
For this purpose, we design a dedicated CNN that outputs high-
resolution maps encoding the 2D position of the fruits within the
image.
3.1.1. Network architecture
The architecture has an encoder–decoder structure and stems from
the popular U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) network (see Fig. 4 and
Table 1 for layer details). It is composed of a contractive (encoder)
and an expansive (decoder) path, which are symmetric. Both of them
are characterized by convolutional, pooling and un-pooling layers.
The encoder exploits the residual blocks introduced in the popular
Resnet architecture (He et al., 2016) and follows the structure of
Resnet50, while the decoder response maps are computed through a
series of up-convolutions and concatenations with the high-resolution
features extracted by the encoder. This allows adding high-level con-
textual information to the final prediction, which is crucial to ro-
bustly detect fruits, particularly in those cases where occlusions and
background-clutter arise. We name this network Weakly-Supervised
Fruit Localization Network (WS-FLNet).
More specifically, the first stage of the encoder features a convo-
lutional layer with a 7 × 7 kernel and 64 feature channels, a batch
normalization layer and ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation, fol-
lowed by a 3 × 3 max-pooling. The rest of the encoder is built with
a sequence of stages with residual blocks. In each residual block, the
response maps from the previous one are processed by three convo-
lutional layers (with 1 × 1, 3 × 3 and 1 × 1 kernels, respectively),
each followed by a batch normalization layer and a ReLU activation.
As typical in the residual paradigm (He et al., 2016), skip connections
are used to add the block input to the feature maps computed by the
three convolutional layers. The first stage computes 256 feature maps
and is followed by a 3 × 3 max-pooling layer. The number of filters is
doubled after each one of the remaining three stages, resulting in 2048
filter responses at the end of the encoder.
The decoder is based on a sequence of blocks that first up-sample
the feature maps and process them with a 3 × 3 up-convolution halving
the number of filters. Afterward, the feature maps are concatenated
with the associated responses from the encoder path and then fed into
a 3 × 3 convolutional layer, followed by a final ReLU activation. Each
output of the last four blocks is fed into a 1 × 1 convolutional layer
computing 8 response maps at four different scales (i.e., 1∕8, 1∕4, 1∕2
and 1∕1 of the input resolution). After optimization, the 8 maps should
have high values in those image regions that contain fruits, thus, we
name them fruit response maps.

3.2. Canopy/trunk detection and tracking
The detection of the canopies is necessary to associate fruits to trees,
while trunk detection is used to distinguish foreground and background
fruits, as discussed in the next section. As we mentioned before, the
canopy/trunk detector is the sole part of the system that we decided
to train with fully supervised labels. We choose this approach for two
main reasons: (i) the effort needed to acquire bounding box labels to
train the canopy/trunk detector is negligible compared to that one
required by a fully supervised fruit detector; (ii) the same canopy/trunk
detector can be used in different contexts and orchards without re-
training or fine-tuning, since trees of different fruit species share similar
shapes and characteristics.
The model used for the detection of canopies and trunks is based on
the YOLO-v3 architecture presented in Redmon and Farhadi (2018).
During the training phase, we use the weights trained on the COCO
dataset, as in Redmon and Farhadi (2018), and fine-tune them on
the images gathered in the orchards annotated with bounding boxes
indicating canopies and trunks. The detection candidates computed by

3.3. Depth-estimation and 3D fruit positioning
To compute the 3D coordinates of the detected fruits we benefit
from a depth estimation network. Fruit positioning is crucial to obtain
a detailed estimation of the yield, particularly if we need to gain
insights about the distribution of the fruits on different portions of the
orchard. Furthermore, it allows distinguishing between foreground and
background fruits with respect to the tree trunk. In this way, we can
exclude from the count background fruits, which are more affected
by occlusions and harder to track, and try to detect them from a
better point of view at a later stage of the exploration (or acquired
when navigating on the other side of the cultivation row). Also, the
estimation of the detected fruit depth and the foreground/background
distinction is crucial to avoid redundant counts from multiple points of
view.
To perform depth estimation we rely on the DiverseDepth CNN
architecture described in Yin et al. (2020). As for FlowNet2 (Ilg et al.,

4.2. Datasets
We use two datasets for the yield estimation experiments. The first
one is the ACFR-Mangoes dataset presented in Stein et al. (2016)
and contains images of mango orchards collected with an Unmanned
Ground Vehicle (UGV). The second one is the ISAR-Almonds dataset
proposed in Bellocchio et al. (2019, 2020), and is gathered by flying a
MAV over an orchard with almond trees.
4.2.1. ACFR-mangoes
The UGV used to navigate in the orchard is developed by the
Australian Centre of Field Robotics and is equipped with RGB cam-
eras, LIDAR and GPS/RTK sensors. Images are taken with a Prosilica
GT3300C camera with a Kowa LM8CX lens at 5 Hz and with a reso-
lution of 1648 × 1236 pixels (see Fig. 6 for some examples). The site
used for data collection is a mango orchard composed of 10 rows of
trees. Each tree is observed by two video sequences taken from the two
opposite sides of each cultivation row. The images are characterized by
good illumination conditions that are stable across the sequence. On the
other hand, fruits are often occluded by foliage and tree trunks, which
increases the difficulties for the tracking module.
For comparison purposes, we choose 18 trees having different sizes
and fruit numbers among all the 10 rows as the test set. The ground
truth number of fruits is manually associated during the harvesting

4.2.2. ISAR-Almonds
The ISAR-Almonds dataset (Bellocchio et al., 2019, 2020) is gath-
ered by flying a DJI Phantom 4 drone over an orchard with almond
trees (see Fig. 7). In contrast to the ACFR-Mangoes dataset (Stein et al.,
2016), the image sequences exhibit significant view-point variations
from one frame to another due to the frequent attitude changes of the
MAV. This condition increases the complexity of this dataset and has a
considerable impact on the performance of the approaches we compare,
as shown in Section 4.6.2.
To train WS-FLNet and the other baselines, we separately take 17
photos at 5472 × 3078 pixels and crop them into smaller tiles of
300 × 300 pixels. We select and annotate 1057 tiles for the training
set and 99 tiles for the validation set.
To train the canopy/trunk detector and evaluate the yield estima-
tion performance, we use the video sequences of the drone flights.
The video sequences have a resolution of 4096 × 2160 pixels and a
frequency of 25 frames per second. The canopy/trunk detector opti-
mization is supervised by selecting 1080 frames among the sequences
(980 of which are used for training and 100 for module evaluation
(see Section 4.6.3) and annotating them with bounding box labels
of canopies and trunks. Tests are performed on the video streams
associated with 6 trees, gathered from two opposite sides of the cul-
tivation row. In this case, since there are no ground-truth counts from
harvesting operations, we manually annotate all the visible fruits within
the videos and separately consider the two sides of each tree during the
experimental evaluation.
As for the ACFR-Mangoes dataset, we annotate 70 images of one
sequence assigning unique IDs to fruits and canopies and matching
them across frames.

To analyze the time needed for the manual annotations, we sepa-
rately consider the fruit and canopy/trunk detector labeling process. As
we detailed before, we divide images into tiles and label each of them.
The human operator time required to determine the presence/absence
of fruits in a tile is at most 1–2 s since we only need to determine
whether the image contains at least one fruit and no information
about the fruit positions is required. Conversely, if we rely on a fully
supervised fruit detector, the bounding box annotation time depends
on the number of fruits in the tile and takes, on average, 1 min. As an
instance, annotating all the 17 high-resolution images from the ISAR-
Almonds dataset (Bellocchio et al., 2019, 2020) (which are divided into
80 tiles each) requires more than 22 h in the fully supervised case and
only 45 min in our weakly-supervised setting. Thus, we are able to
reduce the labeling effort by approximately 30 times, while keeping
the performance comparable to those obtained with fully supervised
solutions (as shown in Section 4.6).
To optimize the trunk/canopy detector, we rely on a fully-supervised
strategy. However, this procedure is deemed not excessively time
consuming since we only need to annotate at most 2 canopies and
2 trunks per image. In fact, this process takes 10 s per image on
average, requiring at most 3 h of time to annotate 1080 frames selected
from the ISARLab Almond sequences for the canopy/trunk detector
training. This implies that the annotation time, in this case, is about
12% of the time requested to annotate fruits by the fully supervised
approach. Furthermore, different fruit species share similar canopy and
trunks shapes, and, hence, there is no need to re-train the canopy/trunk
detector for each new orchard. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume
that the effect of the annotation required by the trunk/canopy detector
is negligible compared to that required by the fruit detection module.
4.4. Implementation details
The different CNN architectures used in our system are imple-
mented by using the Pytorch framework. Training is performed only
for WS-FLNet and the YOLO-based canopy/trunk detector, while for
FlowNet and DiverseNet we use the publicly available weights with no
fine-tuning procedures.
For WS-FLNet we use a Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizer,
setting the learning rate to 0.001 and the batch size to 32 images.
Training is performed for 100 epochs and takes 5 h for each dataset, on
average. Similarly, the canopy/trunk detector, based on the YOLO-v3
architecture, is optimized with SGD by setting the learning rate and the
batch size to 0.01 and 16, respectively. In this case, the learning phase
lasts 150 epochs and takes 8 h.

4.5. Baselines
To highlight the advantages introduced by the proposed approach,
we compare it against different baselines.
On the ACFR-Mangoes dataset (Stein et al., 2016), the comparison
is performed against the fully-supervised method proposed in Liu et al.
(2019). Specifically, this approach relies on a vision-based monocular
yield estimation system that is based on a FasterRCNN object detec-
tor (Ren et al., 2015) coupled with a semantic structure-from-motion
module.
We also perform comparisons against the two versions of the
method introduced by Stein et al. (2016). This yield estimation system
combines a FasterRCNN object detector (Ren et al., 2015) with a LIDAR
sensor to perform fruit detection and localization, while the camera
pose is estimated by using a GPS/RTK system. The first version, namely
the Multi-View-Suite (MVS), uses all the images of the video sequences
associated with the target tree. The second version, i.e., Dual View
Suite (DVS), uses only the two images taken from the two sides of the
cultivation row containing the target tree.
Since the code implementations of the previous baselines are not
publicly available, in the ISAR-Almonds dataset (Bellocchio et al., 2019,
2020) we decided to compare our approach against other two baselines:
1. The fully supervised yield estimation baseline used in Bellocchio
et al. (2020). At each step, it uses a canopy detector based on
FasterRCNN (Ren et al., 2015) with a ResNet101 backbone (He
et al., 2016) to process the image. The portions containing
the detected canopies are then divided into smaller tiles of
300 × 300 pixels and processed by the fully-supervised fruit
counting CNN described in Bargoti and Underwood (2017a) and
based on FasterRCNN (Ren et al., 2015). The total fruit count
is then computed by summing the estimated counts of each
tile. Observation of the same canopy tree across consecutive
frames are associated with a Kalman-based multi-object tracker.
The final yield for each tree is obtained by averaging the five
highest counts estimated in the image stream. We refer to this
baseline as YE-FasterRCNN (Bargoti and Underwood, 2017a)
(Yield Estimation with Faster RCNN).
2. The weakly supervised version of the previous yield estimation
baseline. The processing pipeline is similar to the previous one,
except for the fruit counting module that is based on the weakly-
supervised strategy introduced in Bellocchio et al. (2019). In the
following, we refer to this approach as YE-WSCOUNT (Belloc-
chio et al., 2019) (Yield Estimation with WSCOUNT).
The FasterRCNN (Ren et al., 2015) canopy detectors of these two base-
lines are trained on the same split used for our canopy/trunk detector.
On the other hand, the fully-supervised and weakly-supervised fruit
counting networks are optimized on the set used for WS-FLNet (see
Section 4.2).
4.6. Results
The following section discusses the qualitative and the quantitative
results of the experiments run on the ISAR-Almonds (Bellocchio et al.,
2019, 2020) and the ACFR-Mangoes (Stein et al., 2016) test sets.
4.6.1. ACFR-mangoes
The experimental results for the ACFR-Mangoes dataset (Stein et al.,
2016) are reported in Table 3 and some qualitative outputs of our
system are provided in Fig. 8. In terms of RMSE, MAE and MAEP, the
best results are achieved by MVS. This is expected since this approach
combines a fully supervised fruit detector, which is more robust to
occlusions and background clutter, and a 3D localization method based
on a LIDAR and a GPS-RTK system. The latter allows a precise robot
positioning, crucial to avoid redundant counts and understand when
new fruits are observed.
Although the performance achieved by Liu et al. (2019) (i.e., the
MAE, the RMSE and the MAEP are 27.66, 35.16 and 15.52%, respec-
tively) is slightly worse than those of MVS, they are still comparable.
This is impressive since the overall processing pipeline from Liu et al.
(2019) is based solely on a monocular camera. The performance drop
can be mostly explained by noticing that, in this case, the 3D positions
of both camera and fruits are estimated with a structure-from-motion

supervised baselines (i.e., the MAE, the RMSE and the MAEP are 58.61,

tree, we observe that, compared to the fully supervised solutions, our
method underestimates the true count, on average. This is mainly
related to poor count estimates on fruits that are grouped and partially
overlapped. In these situations, the fruit response maps exhibit a single
cluster of pixels with high values and, hence, the local maxima search
procedure will, most likely, identify only one fruit instead of multiple
ones. On the other hand, fully supervised approaches are generally
more effective in handling these cases since they have been explicitly
trained to distinguish fruits in groups by providing bounding box
labels. Nonetheless, it is important to stress that this comparison is
not fair since fully supervised strategies exploit labels that specifically
measure the fruit locations, while our approach relies on annotations
that are significantly less informative. Our aim here is to show that,
even without using highly informative labels, the performance is still
satisfactory and comparable with those from MVS (Stein et al., 2016)
and Liu et al. (2019). This is remarkable, considering that with our
weakly-supervised solutions we are able to considerably reduce the
labeling effort, as discussed in Section 4.3. In some cases, our approach
is even able to outperform the other baselines, such as for the yield
estimation associated with tree 18. There are some situations, e.g., tree
6, where our method performs worse. We observed that this is mainly
related to the inaccurate depth map estimates from the DiverseDepth
network caused most likely by the poor image conditions (i.e., bad
lighting conditions).
Interestingly, our approach considerably outperforms the DVS vari-
ant from Stein et al. (2016), which is fully supervised. This can be
explained by noticing that this approach estimates the yield by only
using the two images captured on opposite sides of the cultivation row,
which are not sufficient to detect and count all the fruits within a
tree. Conversely, although trained with weak supervision signals, our
approach is able to provide reliable fruit detections and handles more
robustly occlusions by exploiting the 3D position optimization and the
fruit trackers.
It should also be noticed that all the approaches underestimate the
ground truth yield for all the trees. This can be motivated by observing
that, for this dataset, the actual yield is computed during the harvesting
operations, when all the fruits in each tree can effectively be counted.
Conversely, during image acquisition, many fruits are severely occluded
by foliage or other elements and, in practice, they are never observed.
Thus, it is basically impossible for yield estimation approaches to detect
them.

4.6.2. ISAR-almonds
For the tests on the ISAR-Almonds dataset (Bellocchio et al., 2019,
2020), we report the results and the qualitative analysis in Table 4
and Fig. 10, respectively. When discussing the performance, in this
case, we make a distinction between the results obtained on the two
video sequences from the opposite sides of the cultivation rows that
contain the tree in the test set. We choose to proceed in this way since,
differently from the ACFR-Mangoes dataset (Stein et al., 2016), in this
case, the ground truth yield is not computed during the harvesting
operations. Instead, images are manually inspected to count the actual
fruit number of a specific tree that can be observed from each of the
two sides of the cultivation row. In this way, we are able to identify
possible shortcomings or challenges related to the particular point of
view from which we are observing the tree.
Differently from the more complex baselines we considered in the
previous section, YE-FasterRCNN (Bargoti and Underwood, 2017a)
does not explicitly handle redundant counts and occlusions by relying
on 3D positioning or fruit tracking. Nevertheless, its fully supervised
nature allows it to obtain the best performances in terms of MAE, RMSE
and MAEP. This confirms the tendency observed in the ACFR-Mangoes
dataset (Stein et al., 2016): the fully supervised approach guarantees,
on average, the lowest yield estimation errors.
On the sequence gathered from side 1 of the cultivation row, our
approach is able to provide yield estimates comparable to those from
YE-FasterRCNN (Bargoti and Underwood, 2017a) . This is remarkable,
since this dataset is considerably more challenging than the ACFR-
Mangoes (Stein et al., 2016), due to UAV motion dynamics, non-ideal
light conditions and varying distance from the trees. These difficulties
are even more severe when side 2 of the row is observed, as shown in
the last row of Fig. 10. In this latter case, we experience a performance
drop compared to YE-FasterRCNN (Bargoti and Underwood, 2017a) .
Nevertheless, it is more interesting to analyze the comparison
against the other weakly-supervised baseline, i.e., YE-WSCOUNT (Bel-
locchio et al., 2019). Table 4 shows that we always outperform it,
particularly on the ‘‘side 1’’ experiments, where our approach achieves
an RMSE of 97.79, a significantly lower error than 124.07 scored by
YE-WSCOUNT (Bellocchio et al., 2019). This proves that the strategies
we introduce to handle occlusions and redundant counts (which are
not considered in YE-WSCOUNT (Bellocchio et al., 2019)) are decisive
when estimating the total yield.
Finally, as in the previous section, we show in Fig. 11 the global
map computed on the ‘‘side 1’’ sequence. In this case, the trajectory
is not linear, since the drone continuously changes its height and the
distance from the trees during the navigation. It is also interesting to
observe that, in this case, the number of fruits varies greatly between
different trees, with some of them containing very few almonds (e.g.,
see tree 5).

detectors are, instead, evaluated on the test sets of the train-test splits
used to optimize them (see Sections 4.2.1 and 4.2.2 ).

the most crucial.
By analyzing the performance of the trackers, we can observe that
the MOTA is always negative: this implies that the errors made by
the tracker exceed the total number of fruits/canopies in the scene.
The MOTP for the canopy tracker is between 0.201 and 0.369. This
low values are expected since the estimated tree bounding boxes are
heavily affected by the camera point-of-view and the foliage shape at
the contours of the canopy. When analyzing the trackers’ performance
two aspects should, however, kept in mind. First, with respect to
other tracking scenarios (e.g., as the people ones considered in Milan
et al. (2016)), fruit tracking is more challenging due to the significant
presence of occlusions and background clutter, and the high similarity
between different fruit instances. Second, the core of our approach
is the weakly supervised detector (WS-FLNet) and it can be easily
integrated with other more precise trackers, if needed.
Finally, the canopy/trunk detectors achieve good accuracies, with
F1 scores above 0.80 and the mAP between 87.56% and 96.46%.
It is important to mention that we could not evaluate the depth and
the 3D fruit map estimation modules since ground truth information
about pixel depths and 3D fruit positions are not available for the
dataset we considered.
4.6.4. Discussion on yield estimation performance
Yield estimation differs in scope and usage according to the market
and the business application scenario. In particular, it is convenient
to investigate which estimation errors could be acceptable for the
almond and mangoes business. Historically, the production of almonds
is mainly located in countries between 30◦–40◦of latitude, (Reinecke,
1941), while the mango fruit is an important product in tropical
regions (Rasmikayati et al., 2019). Due to the countries’ diversities, it
is necessary to identify an upper bound value for the error percentage
of the yield estimation.
In general, underestimating and overestimating the yield does not
have the same impact on the company’s business. The former usually
affects only the allocated resources for the production and sale and does
not significantly compromise the planned market strategies. On the
contrary, overestimates may expose the business to risks similar to crop
loss. Therefore, the maximum value for the MAEP can be determined
considering harvest loss scenarios, which mimics the production over-
estimation. For mangoes, typical yields fluctuations and post-harvest
losses vary between the range ±11 −30% (Rasmikayati et al., 2019;
Savaria et al., 0000). Instead, variations with peaks of 43% were
observed for almond production between 1986 and 2021 (United States
Department of Agriculture, 2020). Following the above considerations,
it is reasonable to set the upper-bound value for the MAEP to 30%,
while it is probably acceptable to have an underestimation of up to
40%.

method exploiting weak supervision. It achieves an MAEP of slightly
more than 30%, close to the upper-bound value we identified. In
addition, as discussed in Section 4.6.1 and shown in Table 3, in the
majority of cases, errors are related to underestimations, which has
less impact on the business. Considering also that, contrary to the

burden for the training phase, we believe that it could become an
important milestone for the development of efficient and fast-to-deploy
yield estimation systems.

discussed in the previous sections, this scenario is more challenging and

approach is able to outperform the other weakly supervised baseline
and, on the side 1 experiment, it is close to the underestimation bound
(40%), a promising result considering the very difficult context.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced a monocular visual-based yield esti-
mation framework, capable of tracking and mapping fruits and trees
using only a single camera. We are able to significantly reduce the
labeling effort needed by other fully supervised baselines by exploiting
a weakly-supervised paradigm to optimize the fruit detector. Specif-
ically, it only requires presence/absence labels during the training
phase, which are considerably less burdensome to acquire than highly
informative bounding boxes. As a result, the effort required to deploy
vision-based solutions for yield estimation is heavily reduced, thus
making possible the applications of these strategies to a wider set
of real-life problems. Furthermore, we design a processing pipeline
that includes tracking, depth estimation and global map optimization
modules to handle redundant counts from multiple and consecutive
views and occlusions.
We demonstrated through different experiments on two challeng-
ing datasets that our method is capable to achieve yield estimation
performance comparable to state-of-the-art baselines that rely on fully
supervised strategies (requiring expensive annotation procedures) or
involve expensive sensors. In addition, our approach outperforms the
weakly supervised baseline, confirming that it is an easy-to-deploy yet
reliable solution for yield estimation. Future work will focus on the
robustness of the method for more challenging scenarios and fruits,
including species with reduced dimensions and difficult to distinguish
from the background foliage, such as olives. We will also investigate do-
main adaptation techniques to further reduce the need to acquire labels
and try to completely eliminate the necessity to perform optimization
or model tuning for each new scenario the approach is deployed into.
