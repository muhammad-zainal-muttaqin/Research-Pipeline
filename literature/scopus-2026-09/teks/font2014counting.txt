a b s t r a c t
This paper presents an automatic method for counting red grapes from high-resolution images of
vineyards taken under artiﬁcial lighting at night. The proposed method is based on detecting the specular
reﬂection peaks from the spherical surface of the grapes. These intensity peaks are detected by means of a
morphological peak detector based on the deﬁnition of one central point and several radial points. The
morphological condition applied is that the intensity of the central point must be higher than all the
radial points. The grape counting results obtained in different occlusion conditions were compared with
a manual labeling procedure. On average, the percentage of extremely occluded grapes (occlusion higher
than 75%) in the clusters was 33%, whereas the average counting detection error obtained with the
automatic method proposed was 14% with only 7% of false positives, conﬁrming that this proposal
can count even highly occluded grapes.

1. Introduction
The viticulture and wine industries have a special interest in
research focused on improving management operations in vine-
yards by automatically estimating leaves (Arnó et al., 2013), fruit
ripeness (Rodríguez-Pulido et al., 2012), vineyard yield (Nuske
et al., 2011; Diago et al., 2012), and deﬁning new automatic har-
vesting procedures.
The task of predicting yield accurately is currently performed
manually by counting the number of individual fruit in selected
areas of an vineyard. This manual operation entails considerable
costs (Peacock et al., 2007) and the results obtained in large area
are prone to being affected by fatigue in the human operators.
For these reasons, manual counting is being replaced by automatic
methods based mainly on the application of image-processing
algorithms adapted to different scenarios and set-up possibilities
(Stajnkoa et al., 2004; Chinchuluun and Lee, 2006; Wijethunga
et al., 2008; Nuske et al., 2011; Linker et al., 2012; Payne et al.,
2013). These are seen to be more reliable, faster and more consis-
tent and with lower operating costs. However, the automatic
counting of fruit is highly dependent on such factors as the envi-
ronmental conditions, crop properties and typical occlusion, color
differences between the fruit and leaves, and lighting, so each case
may require a speciﬁc solution.
The new contribution of this paper is the deﬁnition of a new pro-
cedure to count the number of grapes in high-resolution vineyard
images obtained under controlled artiﬁcial lighting at night. This
new procedure is based on the hypotheses that the spherical sur-
face of the grapes generates specular reﬂection peaks when they
are illuminated from a single focus and that the number of grapes
can be calculated by counting the number of specular reﬂection
peaks in the image areas covered by grapes. The results obtained
have conﬁrmed these hypotheses and the utility of the proposed
procedure that is able to count even highly occluded grapes in
images from vineyards. This new procedure will contribute to
increasing the accuracy of yield production estimates as a way to
optimize vineyard management and to reduce production costs.
2. Related work
One of the topics addressed by the paradigm of precision agri-
culture is the counting of different agricultural elements in images,
such as trees (Recio et al., 2013), vegetables and fruit (Stajnkoa
et al., 2004; Chinchuluun and Lee, 2006; Wijethunga et al., 2008;
Linker et al., 2012; Grossetête et al., 2012; Song et al., 2014) in
orchards in order to estimate the production and, for example,
optimize the application of agrochemicals (Escolà et al., 2013).

Automatic algorithms have been developed to count fruit or veg-
etables in situ with the particularity of these being objects with cer-
tain dimensions and without occlusion problems that are, in most
cases, isolated from other fruit. For example, Stajnkoa et al.
(2004) automatically measured the number of apples using thermal
images with a good correlation coefﬁcient between manual and
automatic counting, from 0.83 to 0.88. Chinchuluun and Lee
(2006) developed a fruit counting algorithm by establishing illumi-
nation adjustments and removing the noise from the image. In this
case, the results of a segmentation stage applied in both the RGB
and the HSI color spaces were combined with a K-means clustering
in order to classify objects into fruit, split connected fruit by means
of a marker-controlled watershed algorithm, and count the individ-
ual fruit by performing a ﬁnal blob analysis. Wijethunga et al.
(2008) designed two automatic techniques to count kiwifruit in
the L * a * b color space by assuming that the pixels with higher
intensity levels were located at the center of the kiwi (a hypothesis
similar to the proposal in this paper). Both techniques counted the
number of fruit by identifying the regional peaks of the image. In
the ﬁrst technique, they were computed directly from a gray color
image whereas in the second technique, the image was transformed
into binary and then the distance from each fruit pixel (value zero)
to the nearest background pixel (non-zero value) was computed.
The results obtained showed accuracies up to 90% for gold and
70% for green varieties of kiwi. Linker et al. (2012) proposed an
alternative counting method based on detecting the fruit pixels in
the image, connecting fruit pixels into sets, segmenting the con-
tours of these sets and creating an apple model from the deﬁnition
of the contours. The results showed a success rate from 85% to 95%
depending on the lighting conditions. Payne et al. (2013) counted
mango fruit pieces from daytime orchard images both manually
and automatically. In that paper, the automatic results obtained
by analyzing images of the four sides of ﬁfteen trees were compared
with manual in-situ fruit counting. A strong correlation between
the counting results (R2 = 0.91) was obtained. The pixels in the
images were segmented into fruit and background pixels using
color segmentation in the RGB and YCbCr color ranges and texture
segmentation based on adjacent pixel variability. The resultant
blobs were counted to obtain the number of mangos in the image.
More recently, Payne et al. (2014) proposed a more general mango
counting procedure based on altered color matching and Hessian
ﬁltering applied to images acquired at night with artiﬁcial lighting.
Alternatively, Song et al. (2014) presented a method for recognizing
and counting fruit from images in cluttered greenhouses by using
the bag-of-words model and statistical approaches that obtained
a strong correlation (94.6%) with manual counting data.
In the case of grapes, Nuske et al. (2011) proposed the use of a
radial symmetry transform (Loy and Zelinsky, 2003) as a method
for counting grapes from images in large vineyards. The radial
symmetry transform is based on identifying pixels with a high
level of radial symmetry that are candidates for deﬁning the center
of the fruit, then the neighboring berries can be grouped into clus-
ters. The conclusion after analyzing 224 vines was that the yield
can be estimated with an error of 9.8%. Alternatively, Grossetête
et al. (2012) proposed a counting methodology based on the com-
putation of the correlation between the Gaussian proﬁle and the
neighborhood of each pixel. The correlation map obtained was seg-
mented by applying an intensity threshold and ﬁltered by applying
morphological dilation operators. More recently, Roscher et al.
(2014) proposed an automated image analysis framework for high
throughput determination of grapevine fruit sizes. The analysis
framework ﬁrstly detects circular structures and then classiﬁes
these into fruit by applying a conditional random ﬁeld based on
active learning approaches.
Lastly, Garrido-Novell et al. (2012) studied the reﬂectance of
different parts of an apple (background, apple, peduncle and the
center of the apple) in relation to their wavelength spectra by using
RGB and hyperspectral imaging. This related paper concluded that
the apples were better detected when using the hyperspectral dis-
crimination than the RGB image analysis. In this case, another
interesting conclusion was that the reﬂectance of the different
parts of an apple was clearly different and representative. In the
same direction, the new contribution of this paper is the proposal
to detect the specular reﬂection peaks from the spherical surface of
the grapes when using artiﬁcial lighting at night in order to count
the number of grapes in vineyard images.
3. Materials
This section describes the vineyard the images were obtained
from, the image acquisition system, and the main characteristics
of these high-resolution images.
3.1. Vineyard facility
The vineyard facility was located in California and the grape
grown was the early-season red table-grape Flame Seedless vari-
ety, which is destined for the fresh fruit market (see Fig. 1). Accord-
ing to the National Grape Registry (National Grape Registry, 2014),
the grapes of this variety are characterized as small, round, ﬁrm,
crisp and seedless while the clusters are medium in size and have
a conical shape. They usually ripen in early July (summer season).
3.2. Image acquisition system
The image acquisition system was designed to take RGB vine-
yard images at night under artiﬁcial lighting. The image acquisition
system was based on a high-resolution Nikon D300s camera with
Nikon AF-S DX Nikkor 18–55 mm 1:3.5-5.6G lenses set to a focal
length of 20 mm. The camera was set at F11 with ISO200 and an
exposure time of 1/250 s. The camera was connected to a ring ﬂash
used to illuminate the grapes directly. All the equipment was
mounted on a tubular structure and ﬁxed to a support vehicle.
The system provides images with a resolution of 4288  2848 pix-
els and 24-bit color depth stored in jpeg format with ﬁne compres-
sion. The data set of vineyard images was automatically obtained
by moving the support vehicle along the rows in the vineyard at
a constant speed of 0.25 m/s and a constant distance of 2 m from
the vines.
4. Counting red grapes
The aim of this paper is to count visible grapes in RGB vineyard
images collected at night by applying artiﬁcial lighting. This sec-
tion explains the automatic methodology proposed to count the
number of grapes and also a complementary manual counting
methodology used to validate the results obtained.
4.1. Manual counting for validation
In a ﬁrst stage, a manual grape labeling and counting procedure
was developed to validate the automatic counting results. The
manual counting procedure consisted of classifying individual
grapes in the vineyard images into three categories depending on
their individual degree of occlusion (Fig. 2): non-occluded grapes
(Fig. 2, green mark), grapes with occlusion of less than 75% of their
surface (Fig. 2, red mark), and grapes with high occlusion between
75% and 99% (Fig. 2, yellow mark). Fig. 2 shows the results of the
accurate manual labeling of the sample vineyard image shown in
Fig. 4. In this case, there were 20 grapes without any occlusion
(0%) (green mark), 47 with occlusion between 0% and 75% (red

4.2. Automatic counting
The proposed procedure for grape counting is based on detect-
ing the specular reﬂection intensity peak from the smooth skin of
spherical objects (such as red grapes) lit by a single light source.
The specular reﬂection of a spherical surface has predictable
behavior that generates higher reﬂection intensities (hot spots)
in the area of the sphere located on the plane of the artiﬁcial light-
ing source. Thus, each spherical object should have an identifying
brighter point (or area) with the highest color intensity level.
Fig. 3 is proposed to illustrate this effect in part of a vineyard image
containing several grapes. Fig. 3b) shows the transformed and nor-
malized gray intensity image representing low intensity levels in
blue1 and high intensity levels in red. The red identiﬁes the points
with higher reﬂection intensities. The relative position of the hot
spots (intensity peaks) coincides approximately with the center of
the grapes because of the use of a ring ﬂash to light the images.
One interesting point in Fig. 3b) is that the intensity peaks from
the specular reﬂection are also visible in most partially occluded
grapes.
Consequently, the procedure proposed in this paper for count-
ing the grapes in the background extracted from vineyard images
taken at night consists of detecting the specular reﬂection peaks
from the center of the almost spherical shape of the grapes. In
order to achieve this, the grape counting procedure has three steps:
red grape segmentation, image smoothing, and morphological
peak detection and counting. The parameters used in the different
steps of the procedure have been tuned with the 10 initial images
obtained from the vineyard.
4.2.1. Automatic red grape segmentation
The proposed procedure for segmenting red grapes is based on
creating a mask to extract the background in order to focus the
counting procedures only on the area covered by grapes. This seg-
mentation procedure has three steps: (1) applying a transforma-
tion to the original vineyard RGB color image to obtain the H
(hue) color layer, (2) applying a segmentation threshold value
obtained with the Otsu method (Otsu, 1979) (the optimal value
was 0.55) in order to have a rough segmentation between grapes
and background in the images, (3) applying a combined labeling
and morphological ﬁltering sequence to the segmented image to
remove small noisy objects with an area of less than 500 pixels
(grapes usually have an average of 52,000 pixels), then ﬁll all inter-
nal object holes, and then apply 5 erodes mainly to remove the
effect of unwanted plantation wires from the image. Fig. 4 shows
an example of this segmentation procedure: Fig. 4a) is a cropped
vineyard image with one grape. Fig. 4b) shows the segmentation
of the H color layer. Fig. 4c) is the resulting segmented image after
the morphological operation process. Then, Fig. 4d) contains the
original image combined with the segmented image to show the
background extraction effect.
4.2.2. Image smoothing
The aim of the image-smoothing step is to reduce color inten-
sity variability in the areas of the image covered by grapes. This
smoothing process basically reduces the possibility of detecting
more than one bright spot per grape. The high quality of the vine-
yard images, the controlled lighting conditions, and the large area
covered by one grape in the images simpliﬁes the requirements of
this ﬁltering stage. The optimal smoothing strategy applied in this
paper consists of a two-dimensional average ﬁlter covering a
radius of 10 pixels (Fig. 5).
4.2.3. Automatic red grape (specular reﬂection peak) counting
As deﬁned above, the red grape counting procedure is equiva-
lent to detecting and counting hot spots or peaks. There are many
methods suitable for intensity-peak detection in images (Yang
et al., 2009). However, the high resolution of the images and the
clear deﬁnition of the peaks (hot spots) in the vineyard images
enable the deﬁnition of a simpliﬁed and optimized morphological
detection procedure. The morphological detector deﬁned in this
paper is based on the deﬁnition of a central point and several radial
points around this center (see Fig. 6). The morphological condition
applied is that the intensity of the central point must be higher
than the intensity of the radial points. The reddish nature of the
skin of the grapes simpliﬁes the application of this morphological
detector to only the R color layer of the segmented and ﬁltered
vineyard images. This proposal for morphological detection has
two critical parameters that must be optimized: (1) the value of
the external radius and (2) the number of external points equally
distributed along the external radius.
4.2.4. Optimization of the parameters of the morphological detector
This section shows the empirical optimization of the two
parameters of the morphological detector proposed. A preliminary
analysis showed that the values for the radius of the grapes in the
vineyard images can vary from 30 to 90 pixels, with 75 pixels as
the most frequent radius. Therefore, the hot-spot peaks will have
always a lower radius than the grapes so the feasible values of
the radius of the morphological detector was then selected from
10 to 50 pixels, and was analyzed in steps of 10 pixels. The number
of external radial points considered was also selected from 5 to 35
points in steps of 5 points.
For example, Fig. 7 shows the results of applying the proposed
morphological peak detector to a smoothed vineyard image
(Fig. 7a). Here, the morphological detector was deﬁned with a cen-
tral point and the external circle deﬁned at a radius of 30 pixels
with 15 equally spaced external points around the circle. The mor-
phological detector was applied in the R color layer and the seg-
mentation mask that deﬁnes the area of the grapes in the
vineyard image. The result of the morphological detector was 1
in case of all external radial points having intensity values lower
than the intensity of the central point and 0 otherwise. This result
was placed in the image in the position of the central point of the
detector in order to generate a binary image showing the location
of the intensity peaks (Fig. 7b). The number of grapes was then

counting system detects highly occluded grapes without overesti-

6. Conclusions
This paper proposes an automatic method to count red grapes
in a vineyard from high-resolution images taken at night under
artiﬁcial lighting. The proposed method has three image processing
stages. In a ﬁrst stage, the cluster of grapes is segmented from the
background of the image by applying a threshold intensity level to
the transformed hue color layer of the image. In the second stage,
the cluster of grapes is ﬁltered to reduce the noise and to smooth
local image intensity variations by applying an average ﬁlter with
a radius of 10 pixels. In the third stage, the intensity peaks caused
by the specular reﬂection on the spherical surface of the grapes are
detected in the R color layer by means of an optimized morpholog-
ical detector deﬁned by 1 central point and 15 external points
equally distributed around a circumference with a radius of 30 pix-
els (from the central point). The morphological condition applied
to detecting the intensity peaks generated by the specular reﬂec-
tion on the spherical surface of the grapes was that the intensity
of the central point had to be higher than the intensities of the
external circumference points deﬁned in the morphological ﬁlter.
The results obtained when comparing automatic grape detec-
tion and manual grape labeling in 10 images showed that the aver-
age counting error was in a range from +5.4% to 26%, with an
average value of only 14%. This error value must be considered
very good because manual labeling showed that the number of
grapes highly occluded (more than the 75% of its surface occluded)
was very high, 33% on average. The results obtained also show that
the number of false positives was also very low, 7% on average.
In the future, efforts will be focused on estimating the correla-
tion between the automatic grape counting results and the degree
of ripeness of the clusters of grapes and on applying the proposed
counting method to other grape varieties and to other families of
fruit to estimate yield production.
