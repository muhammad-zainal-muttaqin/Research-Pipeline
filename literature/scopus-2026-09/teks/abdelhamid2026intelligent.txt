A B S T R A C T
Estimating mango maturity is critical for determining the optimal harvest time, controlling post-harvest oper­
ations, and meeting consumer demand in the market. However, non-destructive techniques often provide limited 
accuracy in maturity estimation. This accuracy can be improved by integrating these methods with artificial 
intelligence models. This review systematically evaluates these methods based on the PRISMA-ScR guidelines in 
terms of Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS), Hyperspectral Imaging (HSI), image processing coupled with Ma­
chine Learning (ML), and Deep Learning (DL). Distribution analysis showed that NIRS technology accounted for 
61.5 % of all studies, followed by image processing at 16.7 %, DL at 12.5 %, and HSI at 7.3 %. NIRS and HSI 
calibration are still prevalent, utilizing statistical and chemometric models. Over the last few years, the inte­
gration of DL with NIRS and HSI has enhanced preprocessing and calibration efficiency, and image-based DL 
models have been developed as strong predictors of mango maturity. Nevertheless, relatively little research has 
been conducted on attention-based DL models, indicating that these models are still in the exploratory stage. 
Comparative analysis reveals that imaging and DL techniques are highly accurate in estimating maturity for some 
varieties, while NIRS and HSI techniques are still superior in predicting internal quality (total soluble solids, dry 
matter, titratable acidity). Despite these advancements, limitations remain regarding cost, calibration transfer, 
and ease of field deployment. To make mango maturity estimation systems robust, scalable, and sustainable, 
future research should prioritize the integration of multi-sensory data, the application of lightweight artificial 
intelligence models, and the exploration of alternative approaches to overcome the limitations of current 
methods.
1. Introduction
Mango (Mangifera indica L.) is a commercially significant tropical 
fruit, which is cherished all over the world due to its unique taste, 
nutritional density, and economic aspect [1]. It is estimated that pro­
duction of mangoes around the world will be about 65 × 109 kg by 2026, 
which is an increase of 58.3 × 109 kg in 2021 at an average of 1.9 % per 
year. India is the largest producer of this output, producing over 25 
million tons per year, which is almost half of the global production [2]. 
The world market of mangoes is growing at a high pace, with an esti­
mated 2024 value of USD 67.97 billion, and is expected to grow to USD 
98.95 billion by 2029 [3]. Picking fruits at the appropriate stage of 
physiological maturity enables them to ripen consistently and attain 
optimal sweetness, taste, and texture, which are key characteristics that

inaccurate maturity grading often leads to premature loss in storage and 
transportation, resulting in significant economic losses and reduced 
export opportunities. Mango supply chains incur losses that are esti­

inaccurate ripeness assessment, and inadequate storage [4]. Thus, 
quality consistency, waste reduction, and sustainable supply chain 
management of mango require the reliable, non-destructive methods of 
maturity evaluation.
The conventional destructive methods for measuring mango matu­
rity, such as the total soluble solids (TSS), dry matter (DM), titratable 
acidity (TA), and firmness, are accurate but time-consuming, laborious, 
and not suitable for large-scale or real-time measurements [5]. Recently, 
there has been a growing reliance on integrated smart-technology sys­
tems combining sensing hardware, data acquisition platforms, and 
artificial intelligence algorithms. Non-destructive modalities such as 
Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS), Hyperspectral Imaging (HSI), and 
RGB imaging are no longer isolated laboratory tools; they function as 
components within digital grading lines, portable decision-support de­
vices, and automated sorting systems. NIRS predicts internal physico­
chemical characteristics, including TSS, DM, and firmness, through light 
absorption and scattering in the near-infrared range [6]. Meanwhile, 
HSI provides a multi-spatial-spectral methodology that allows visual­
izing both external and internal structure and gives a holistic picture of 
fruit homogenization and ripeness ranges [7,8]. Image processing, 
which is combined with classical machine learning (ML) algorithms like 
Support Vector Machines (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), and De­
cision Trees, employs visible color, texture, and shape features extracted 
from RGB images to categorize maturity levels effectively [9–11]. 
Recently, Deep Learning (DL) has revolutionized image-based maturity 
estimation using images by automatically learning hierarchical feature 
representations directly from raw images, reducing the need for manual 
feature engineering and enabling higher accuracy and reliability under 
different lighting, background, and variety conditions [12,13].
Despite significant progress in non-destructive techniques for 
assessing mango ripeness, there are still many gaps that need to be 
addressed. The majority of existing studies consider single-modality 
solutions, such as NIRS [14], HIS [15], or image processing [9], and 
few studies combined both HSI and NIRS methods to provide a 
comprehensive analysis of both internal and external fruit properties 
[6]. This lack of integration constrains the utilization of the comple­
mentary advantages of these modalities: The sensitivity of NIRS to in­
ternal biochemical composition and the mapping capability of 
spatial-spectral changes of the fruit surface by HSI. Therefore, the 
comparative strengths, shortcomings, and interoperability of these 
sensing techniques are not well comprehended, and this prevents the 
creation of a single scalable system for the evaluation of mango maturity 
with precision. Although NIRS and HSI have shown a good predictive 
ability when it comes to internal quality parameters like TSS, DM, and 
firmness, they have low application in the real world due to the cost of 
the instruments, complex calibration, and portability.
Conventional image processing and ML provide a low-cost method­
ology of external quality evaluation, but it is completely dependent on 
manual feature extraction, which can be ineffective in cross-cultivar, 
cross-lighting environment, or cross-viewing angle generalization. But 
most recently, DL methods have also proven to be formidable con­
tenders, exhibiting the ability to automatically learn discriminative vi­
sual features, and, as a result, demonstrate better classification 
performance; nevertheless, their applicability remains limited to small 
annotated datasets, cross-cultivar generalization problems, and lack of 
interpretability, and thus a more integrated and comparative method­
ology of detecting mango maturity is needed. Moreover, no study has yet 
compared or synthesized all these different significant methods to date, 
NIRS, HSI, ML-based image processing, and DL, within the particular 
mango maturity detection task. This gap highlights the importance of 
conducting an integrated analysis that evaluates sensing principles, 
preprocessing pipelines, modeling strategies, and practical applications 
within a unified framework. In this regard, this review describes four 
non-destructive approaches, NIRS, HSI, image processing with conven­
tional ML, and DL, that are applied to estimate mango maturity. In 
addition, study the methods of data collection, preprocessing, feature 
extraction, and predictive modeling. This review aims to establish a 
comprehensive reference framework for future innovations in non- 
destructive estimation of fruit maturity to benefit the mango industry.
2. Methodology
2.1. Review framework
This review systematically examines the current state-of-the-art non- 
destructive methods for measuring mango maturity from 2015 to 2025. 
It was a scoping systematic review study that followed the Preferred 
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension 
for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [16]. It was proposed to 
review and critically evaluate progress in four significant areas of 
technology: NIRS, HSI, image processing coupled with conventional ML, 
and DL, and their development, performance measures, and applica­
bility to the field. The methodological design is structured in six research 
questions (RQs), which all involve literature retrieval, screening, and 
synthesis.
2.2. Research questions
The review was driven by six principal questions that frame the 
analytical structure: 
• RQ1: What non-destructive methods (2015–2025) have been used 
for mango maturity, and how have they evolved? This question 
prompted a review of non-destructive methods, from image pro­
cessing to contemporary multimedia deep learning systems, high­
lighting the model used, data size, and prediction method.
• RQ2: How suitable are NIRS and HSI techniques for field application, 
and how efficient are they in predicting maturity indicators? This 
question guided a comparative study between NIRS and HSI in terms 
of their ability to predict TSS, DM, TA, and firmness, as well as their 
applicability to the real field.
• RQ3: What is the working methodology, challenges, and future 
progress of the image processing-ML approach for evaluating 
external mango maturity? This question addressed the traditional 
image processing pipeline, image acquisition, preprocessing, feature 
extraction, and ML classification, and highlighted its limitations 
under natural lighting and uncontrolled orchard conditions.
• RQ4: What is the working methodology of deep-learning models 
using NIR, HSI, and RGB-based feature extraction for mango matu­
rity prediction? This question focused on convolutional and hybrid 
architectures of DL used on spectral and visual data, assessing their 
preprocessing pipelines, network structures, and evaluation metrics 
(accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score).
• RQ5: What are the challenges that limit the widespread use of all 
mango maturity estimation methods in the fields? This question 
addressed obstacles such as equipment cost, environmental changes, 
calibration deviation, dataset scarcity, and model portability across 
species and regions.
• RQ6: What future directions can improve the accuracy, sustainabil­
ity, and scalability of these methods? This question has contributed 
to shaping future direction and identifying priorities such as the 
integration and transfer of multimedia data, learning using a few 
examples, interpretable artificial intelligence (XAI), lightweight 
structures, and the integration of smart agriculture based on the 
Internet of Things.

2.3. Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using Scopus, Web 
of Science, and Google Scholar databases. Scopus was chosen for its 
broad multidisciplinary coverage, Web of Science for its high-impact 
journal indexing, and Google Scholar to capture relevant grey litera­
ture. The literature search was conducted on 15 September 2025. The 
Boolean search string was adapted for each database and restricted to 
titles, abstracts, and author keywords to ensure relevance. The final 
query used was: TITLE-ABS [(near-infrared spectroscopy* OR NIRS* OR 
hyperspectral imaging* OR HIS* OR computer vision* OR machine 
vision* OR image processing* OR machine learning* OR deep learning* 
OR convolutional neural networks* OR CNN*) AND (mango) AND 
(quality* OR ripeness* OR maturity* OR detection* OR classification* 
OR grading*)] AND PUBYEAR < 2026.
The search encompassed publications from 2015 to 2025, spanning a 
decade of progress in non-destructive mango maturity assessment. The 
initial query retrieved a total of 19,594 records: 9132 from Scopus, 7812 
from Web of Science, and 2650 from Google Scholar.
2.4. Eligibility criteria
To ensure methodological rigor and thematic relevance, strict 
inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined a priori: 
• Inclusion criteria: 
a. Peer-reviewed journal articles published in English.
b. Studies focusing on non-destructive estimation of mango maturity 
or ripeness using NIRS, HSI, image processing, ML, or DL.
c. Experimental, empirical, or applied studies reporting measurable 
outcomes such as TSS, DM, TA, firmness, or maturity stage 
classification.
• Exclusion criteria: 
a. Studies unrelated to mango fruit maturity (e.g., canopy extrac­
tion, cultivar classification, leaf disease detection, yield estima­
tion, fruit localization, size measurement, pest or flowering 
detection).
b. Review papers, books, conference papers, editorials, or theoret­
ical works without experimental evidence.
c. Non-English publications.
d. Duplicates or redundant records across databases.

2.7. Data analysis and synthesis
A mixed quantitative–qualitative synthesis was applied (Fig. 2). 
Studies were organized into four methodological domains, NIRS, HSI, 
IP-ML, and DL, corresponding to RQ1–RQ4, while RQ5 addressed cross- 
cutting deployment limitations and RQ6 identified future directions.
Within each domain, findings were compared according to instru­
mentation, feature-extraction method, model type, and predictive per­
formance. Key performance indicators such as R², RMSE, and 
classification accuracy were extracted for cross-method comparison. 
The final synthesis integrates both quantitative performance data and 
qualitative insights into technological trends, field applicability, and 
limitations.
3. Mango maturity indices, importance, and limitations of 
destructive methods
The accurate determination of mango fruit maturity is a critical 
factor in optimizing harvesting, ensuring high-quality produce, and 
enhancing post-harvest storage, with profound implications for both 
economic and agricultural outcomes. Harvesting mangoes too early re­
sults in poor flavor, low sweetness, shriveling, and failure to ripen 
properly. Conversely, delayed harvesting increases the risk of over­
ripening, reduced shelf life, and susceptibility to microbial decay [17]. 
Accurate maturity assessment ensures consistency in quality, meeting 
consumer expectations and stringent export standards [18]. For storage, 
harvest maturity dictates the fruit's ability to withstand postharvest 
handling, transportation, and various storage conditions [19]. Maturity 
assessment plays a key role in postharvest management, influencing 
decisions on storage conditions, packaging, and the use of ripening 
treatments such as ethylene exposure [20]. Fig. 3 illustrates the link 
between maturity indicators and importance. Maturity indicators 
encompass biochemical (e.g., TSS, DM, TA) and physical (e.g., firmness, 
color) changes that occur during fruit development and ripening,

augmented production of yellow and red pigments, which are mainly 
carotenoids and, in a few cultivars, anthocyanins. Fig. 4 shows repre­

color of the green color usually takes place following the initiation of 
internal maturation events [24]. Nevertheless, peel color is not always 
reliable, especially of green-skinned cultivars that do not change color 
until the time of ripening. As a result, scientists have examined various 
other maturity indices to obtain more reliable and valid measurements.
Chemical properties provide more accurate insights into the devel­
opment of mango fruits. As starch reserves are broken down to release 
reducing sugars in the process of ripening, total soluble solids (TSS) rise 
considerably [25]. TSS is among the most popular maturity indices

produce sugars, a factor of desirable sugar/acid ratio [25]. DM during 
harvest was also found to be positively correlated with TSS and the 
entire eating quality following ripening. Low DM fruits tend to fall short 
in their safety and taste to the consumer, resulting in low satisfaction

Destructive methods for assessing mango maturity focus on internal 
fruit attributes that require sampling or cutting (Fig. 5). These include 
firmness, TSS, TA, DM, and color. Firmness gradually decreases as the 
fruit softens during ripening, while TSS increases as sugars accumulate. 
TA typically declines with maturity, reflecting the reduction in sourness 
and improved flavor balance. DM content provides a reliable indication 
of overall carbohydrate concentration and eating quality. Changes in 
flesh color, from pale greenish tones to deep yellow or orange, further 
signal internal maturity but require destructive sampling. While these 
methods are precise and widely used in research, their destructive 
nature, time requirements, and fruit loss make them impractical for 
large-scale or commercial maturity assessment, underscoring the need 
for non-destructive alternatives. These techniques support sustainable 
practices by reducing the need for destructive sampling, preserving 
more fruit for sale and consumption [27]. Ultimately, the integration of 
advanced maturity detection technologies into mango production sys­
tems is indispensable for achieving high-quality, market-ready fruit 
while ensuring economic and environmental sustainability. In addition, 
maturity detection is not merely a biological measure but also a deter­
minant of supply chain efficiency, profitability, and consumer 
satisfaction.

4. Non-destructive methods for mango maturity detection
Non-destructive methods for mango maturity detection have 
emerged as alternatives to conventional destructive indicators such as 
firmness, TA, DM, and TSS. These approaches allow repeated measure­
ments on the same fruit without causing damage, making them ideal for 
quality monitoring during harvesting, storage, and distribution. Tech­
niques such as NIRS, HSI, image processing-based ML, and DL have been 
increasingly investigated for their potential to provide rapid, accurate, 
and non-invasive assessment of physiological and chemical changes in 
mango fruit. This section critically reviews key methods, with a focus on 
their principles, workflow pipeline, and performance metrics (Fig. 6).
4.1. Mango maturity estimation based on VIS/NIR spectroscopy
The evaluation of mango maturity is essential for optimizing harvest 
timing, ensuring postharvest quality, and enhancing market value. 
Conventional destructive techniques, such as chemical analysis of TSS, 
TA, and DM, provide reliable indicators, but they are labor-intensive, 
time-consuming, and unsuitable for large-scale or in-field monitoring 
[28]. Recent advances in handheld and portable NIRS have enabled 
rapid, non-destructive, and field-deployable solutions for assessing 
mango maturity. VIS/NIR spectroscopy operates on the principle of light 
absorption, reflection, and transmission in the 400–1100 nm wave­
length range, where visible light (400–700 nm) detects pigments such as 
chlorophyll and carotenoids, and NIR (780–2500 nm) probes molecular 
vibrations related to C-H, O-H, and N-H bonds in sugars, water, and 
organic acids. When light interacts with mango tissue, diffuse reflec­
tance modes capture spectra influenced by maturity indicators, such as 
TSS, DM, and firmness. The process of assessing mango maturity with 
NIRS typically involves several systematic steps as follows (Fig. 7).
4.1.1. Data collection
The NIR probe is placed directly on the fruit surface, usually at 
multiple points around the equator, to account for variability in skin 
thickness and internal composition. The device emits NIR light, and the 
reflected or transmitted spectra are recorded across a defined wave­
length range. A wide range of portable NIR spectrometers, including 
SCiO, LinkSquare, DLP NIRscan Nano, and NeoSpectra, have been uti­
lized for the non-destructive prediction of key maturity attributes in 
mangoes, such as DM, TSS, TA, and firmness. These instruments enable 
rapid, contactless assessment of internal fruit composition by capturing 
reflectance spectra across the near-infrared region. In addition to 
commercially available devices, several studies have explored low-cost 
NIR spectrometer prototypes, such as one developed using the Hama­
matsu C14384MA-01 sensor, designed, constructed, and evaluated 
specifically for non-destructive mango maturity assessment [29]. Also, 
portable devices are developed by Shah et al. [30] and Khumaidi et al. 
[31].
4.2. Spectral preprocessing and dimensionality reduction
Spectral preprocessing represents a critical stage in NIRS data anal­
ysis, as it directly influences the accuracy, robustness, and interpret­
ability of the resulting predictive models. Raw spectral data are often 
affected by instrumental noise, baseline drift, and light scattering, which 
arise from sensor variability, environmental fluctuations, and differ­
ences in fruit surface morphology. Without proper correction, these 
artifacts can obscure meaningful spectral features and lead to model 
bias. To address these issues, a range of preprocessing techniques has 
been widely adopted. Common methods include smoothing (e.g., 
Savitzky–Golay (SG) filters) to suppress random noise, normalization to 
standardize intensity scales, and derivative transformations (first and 
second derivatives) to enhance subtle absorption features. Scatter 
correction methods, such as Multiplicative Scatter Correction (MSC), 
Extended Multiplicative Scatter Correction (EMSC), and Baseline 
Correction (BLC), further compensate for pathlength variability and 
heterogeneity among samples [32]. These approaches help isolate 
chemically relevant information, improving model reliability and 
generalization across instruments or batches.

Given the inherently high dimensionality and redundancy of NIRS 
and HIS datasets, dimensionality reduction is equally essential. Tech­
niques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), t-distributed Sto­
chastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), and feature selection algorithms 
(e.g., ReliefF, mutual information) are used to extract the most infor­

This process mitigates overfitting, enhances model interpretability, and 
accelerates learning in downstream regression or classification models. 
Additionally, outlier detection using Hotelling’s T² and Q-residual sta­
tistics is often performed to remove anomalous samples that could 
distort model training with DL. While traditional preprocessing aims to 
isolate absorption features, DL models can directly process raw NIR 
spectra containing both absorption and scattering information, allowing 
the network to learn physically meaningful representations automati­
cally [34].
Traditional filtering and correction techniques, such as SG, MSC, 
EMSC, and standard normal variate (SNV), have been widely applied to 
minimize these interferences and improve signal-to-noise ratios [35]. 
DL-based methods offer significant advantages by learning hierarchical 
feature representations directly from raw or minimally preprocessed 
spectra, enabling automatic correction of spectral interference and 
robust prediction performance. Thus, integrating spectral filtering with 
DL provides a powerful framework for enhancing non-destructive fruit 
quality assessment [34,36]. Ding et al. [37] validated the use of feed­
forward neural network (FNN) correction and Gaussian smoothing (GS) 
filtering to align NIR spectra of packaged mangoes with those of 
unpackaged mangoes, enabling accurate non-destructive quality 
assessment. For packaged mangoes, post-FNN and GS preprocessing, 
PLSR models maintained strong performance with R² values of 0.847 
(firmness), 0.932 (DM), 0.821 (TSS), and 0.907 (TA). These results un­
derscore the efficacy of FNN and GS preprocessing for reliable, 
non-destructive quality evaluation of packaged mangoes, with potential 
applications in supply chain management.
Mishra and Passos [34] enhanced DL model performance for NIR 
spectral data analysis, focusing on mango fruit DM prediction. The 
approach combines chemometric techniques with DL, employing outlier 
pre-filtering using Hotelling's T² and Q statistics from PLS analysis, 
alongside spectral data augmentation through stacking pre-processed 
data with SNV and derivative techniques. Applied to a dataset of 11, 
961 NIR spectra, the method achieved a RMSEP of 0.79 %,

4.2.1. Calibration and prediction
Calibration represents the core stage of spectral modeling, estab­
lishing the quantitative relationship between the measured spectral 
response and the reference physicochemical attributes of mangoes (e.g., 
TSS, DM, TA, and firmness). This process typically involves dividing the 
dataset into training and test subsets, allowing for model development 
and independent performance verification. Among the reviewed studies, 
statistical methods (Table 3 and Appendix 2A) such as PLS, PLSR, and 
MLR. PLSR remains the most widely employed calibration technique 
due to its robustness in handling multicollinearity and high-dimensional 
spectral data. However, ML models such as SVR, ANNs, and RF have 
increasingly been adopted to capture nonlinear relationships between 
spectra and fruit attributes (Table 4 and Appendix 3A).
Shah et al. [30] developed a handheld NIR device (400–1100 nm) 
that directly classified mango maturity (mature/immature) using 
DM-based labels. Compared with conventional threshold-based matu­
rity estimation, which achieved only 55.9 % accuracy, the direct clas­
sification approach with KNN reached 88.2 % accuracy. The authors 
concluded that direct classification is more effective than indirect esti­
mation and that their compact NIR tool holds strong potential for 
practical, non-destructive mango maturity detection in the field. 
Al-Sanabani et al. [56] developed a handheld micro-NIR spectroscopic 
device to assess mango quality. NIR spectra and Brix values (sweetness 
indicators) were collected from three mango varieties (Chokanan, 
Rainbow, and Kai Te), with 80 samples. The spectral data were 
pre-processed using GS and EMSC to reduce scattering effects. Predictive 
models were then built using SVM regression and PLS regression. The 
results showed that SVM achieved excellent prediction performance 
with R² of 0.96 for training and 0.95 for testing, while PLS yielded lower 
but still acceptable performance with R² values of 0.89 and 0.86 for 
training and testing, respectively. These findings confirm that handheld 
NIRS provides a reliable and non-destructive approach for rapid mango 
quality assessment, with SVM offering superior accuracy compared to 
PLS.

using a combination of statistical and predictive metrics to quantify 
accuracy, generalization, and reliability. For regression-based analyses, 
especially those predicting continuous parameters such as TSS, DM, or 
firmness, performance is typically evaluated using the coefficient of 
determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute 
error (MAE). A higher R² and lower RMSE or MAE indicate better pre­
dictive performance. These metrics [1–3] are essential for comparing the 
performance of different preprocessing and modeling approaches [57].

4.3. Mango maturity estimation based on hyperspectral imaging
Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) integrates spectroscopy and conven­
tional image-processing or computer vision techniques to capture both 
spatial and spectral data from samples. Spectroscopy provides detailed 
spectral information, while image-processing methods deliver spatial 
data visualization. This approach effectively addresses the subjectivity 
and limitations of traditional methods, offering a more objective and 
comprehensive analysis [58,59]. The spectral data from each pixel 
provides detailed information, with sample map attributes generated for 
each pixel and spatial region [60]. HSI can operate using reflectance, 
transmittance, fluorescence, or scattering modes to capture this data 
effectively [61,62]. HSI represents a rapid, non-destructive, and 
information-rich technique that enables comprehensive assessment of 
fruit internal quality attributes. By capturing the reflectance and ab­
sorption characteristics of electromagnetic radiation within the 
700–2500 nm wavelength range, HSI simultaneously provides spatial 
and spectral data that reflect both external appearance and internal 
biochemical composition. This broad spectral coverage encompasses 
key absorption bands associated with chlorophyll, carotenoids, water, 
sugars, and other metabolites, making it particularly effective for eval­
uating maturity-related parameters such as TSS, DM, TA, and firmness 
[7,8].
Unlike conventional destructive methods that require time- 
consuming laboratory analysis, HSI allows in situ and non-invasive 
monitoring of fruit quality, thereby offering significant potential for 
real-time applications in sorting, grading, and supply chain optimiza­
tion. The integration of HSI with advanced preprocessing and ML 
techniques further enhances its capability to provide accurate and 
robust maturity predictions across different mango varieties and 
growing environments. RGB imaging alone often struggles to capture 
subtle biochemical changes that occur beneath the skin. To overcome 
this limitation, the HSI technique has been employed, enabling the 
measurement of reflectance across a wide spectral range. These mo­
dalities reveal internal quality parameters such as TSS, TA, firmness, and 
DM, which are crucial for precise maturity assessment [63,64]. Fig. 8

Preprocessing is a crucial step in mango maturity assessment [11,64,
66–68], as raw image and spectral data are often contaminated by noise 
from lighting variations, surface irregularities, and background inter­
ference. To ensure reliability, both image- and spectrum-based ap­
proaches apply tailored preprocessing techniques. For HSI, noise is 
commonly reduced using GS, which suppresses high-frequency varia­
tions and enhances feature clarity. In parallel, region-of-interest (ROI) 
selection is employed to isolate relevant fruit areas, typically the peel 
surface, while excluding blemishes, stems, or background, thereby 
reducing computational load and focusing on maturity-related cues.

MSC, which correct scattering effects and normalize reflectance. SG

peaks, such as the chlorophyll absorption band around 680 nm, making

and DM. Outlier detection methods, such as Hotelling’s T² ellipse and Q 
statistics, are also applied to identify anomalous spectra caused by un­
even illumination or defective samples, thus improving model 
robustness.
Overall, the studies summarized in Table 5 highlight the strong po­
tential of HSI for estimating mango maturity, particularly in predicting 
internal quality parameters with high accuracy. Compared to conven­
tional RGB approaches, HSI consistently provides more robust and 
transferable models by capturing subtle biochemical changes not visible 
to the naked eye. However, its practical deployment is constrained by 
the high cost of sensors, the need for extensive preprocessing, and the 
computational complexity of handling high-dimensional data. These 
challenges have motivated recent research into sensor fusion and 
DL–based dimensionality reduction techniques, which aim to balance 
predictive accuracy with feasibility for real-world applications.

(iii) Feature extraction: Feature extraction plays a crucial role in 
the classification of mango ripeness, as it involves quantifying the 
visual characteristics that vary across different maturity stages. In 
most studies, three major color models, RGB, CIE L*a*b*, and HSV 
(Hue, Saturation, Value), are widely employed due to their ability to 
represent color information in distinct and complementary ways. 
The RGB model captures the basic color composition of an image 
through its red (R), green (G), and blue (B) channels, which are 
useful for evaluating overall color intensity and distribution. The CIE 
L*a*b* model, on the other hand, provides a perceptually uniform 
color space that separates luminance (L*) from chromaticity com­
ponents, specifically a* (red–green axis) and b* (yellow–blue axis) 
[75]. This model is particularly effective for distinguishing subtle 
color variations that correspond to different ripeness levels. The HSV 
model represents colors in terms of hue (H), saturation (S), and value 
(V), offering a more intuitive understanding of color attributes, 
especially for identifying changes in skin tone and intensity during 
the ripening process. Each color channel from these models, R, G, B, 
L*, a*, b*, H, S, and V, is analyzed independently to assess its 
contribution to ripeness determination.
(iv) Classification: In ML, classification refers to the process of 
automatically categorizing data into predefined groups or “classes” 
based on their distinguishing characteristics. A classification algo­
rithm learns from labelled training data and predicts the class of 
unseen instances by mapping features to corresponding output labels 
[76]. In supervised learning, image classification models are trained 
to recognize specific visual patterns from labelled datasets and are 
later used to classify new, unlabeled images. These algorithms 
significantly reduce manual effort and improve operational effi­
ciency, making them essential for automated fruit grading and 
ripeness detection systems [77,16,78–80]. In mango ripeness clas­
sification studies, various ML classifiers such as Support Vector 
Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Decision Tree (DT) 
have been widely employed due to their robustness and interpret­
ability. These algorithms utilize extracted color and texture features 
to distinguish between different maturity stages, typically unripe, 
partially ripe, and fully ripe fruits.
a) Support Vector Machine (SVM): The SVM is a discriminative classi­
fier that constructs an optimal hyperplane to separate data points 
belonging to different classes [81]. It performs effectively in both 
linear and nonlinear classification problems by employing kernel 
functions to project input data into higher-dimensional feature 
spaces. SVMs are particularly suitable for high-dimensional data, 
where they aim to maximize the margin between class boundaries, 
thereby enhancing generalization performance [82].
b) K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN): The KNN algorithm is a simple yet 
powerful non-parametric supervised learning method that classifies 
new data based on the majority vote of its nearest neighbors in the 
feature space. It relies on a distance metric, commonly the Euclidean 
distance, to determine the similarity between data points. The 
parameter K represents the number of neighbors considered for 
classification and must be carefully selected to balance bias and 
variance. Smaller values of K are sensitive to noise, while larger 
values may dilute the influence of local structure [83].
c) Decision Tree (DT): The DT algorithm is a supervised learning 
method used for both classification and regression tasks. It recur­
sively splits data into subsets based on feature thresholds, forming a 
tree-like structure with internal decision nodes and terminal leaf 
nodes that represent final class labels [84]. Each internal node cor­
responds to a test on a particular feature, and each branch represents 
an outcome of that test. The traversal from the root node to a leaf 
defines a specific decision rule or classification path. DTs are intui­
tive and easily interpretable, making them particularly valuable in 
agricultural applications where explainability is essential [85]. In 
mango classification, DT use extracted color and texture features, 
such as hue, saturation, and intensity, to divide the dataset into 
homogeneous subgroups. Their hierarchical structure allows for 
efficient handling of nonlinear relationships and mixed data types.

4.5. Mango maturity estimation based on deep learning
Deep Learning (DL) expands upon traditional ML by introducing 
greater model depth and representational complexity, enabling data to 
be transformed hierarchically through multiple layers of abstraction 
[101]. A key strength of DL lies in its capability for automatic feature 
learning, where features are not manually designed but instead learned 
directly from raw data. In such architectures, higher-level representa­
tions are progressively built from combinations of lower-level features 
[102]. This hierarchical learning structure allows DL models to capture 
complex, nonlinear relationships and perform exceptionally well in 
tasks involving large, high-dimensional datasets. Unlike classical ML 
models that rely on pre-engineered inputs, DL leverages deep neural 
architectures, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which 
incorporate components like convolutional filters, pooling layers, acti­
vation functions, memory cells, and encoder–decoder schemes. These 
elements enable the extraction of intricate spatial and temporal patterns, 
making DL highly effective in image recognition, spectral analysis, and 
time-series prediction [103–106].

tation, DL offers superior performance in terms of both accuracy and 
computational efficiency, provided that sufficiently large and repre­
sentative datasets are available [12,13,107]. In recent years, DL

5. Results and discussion
5.1. Overview of non-destructive techniques used (2015–2025)
A total of 94 peer-reviewed studies published between 2015 and 
2025 were systematically reviewed following the PRISMA-ScR protocol. 
The temporal distribution of research output (Fig. 12) shows a steady 
increase in publications after 2020, reflecting the growing global in­
terest in non-destructive mango fruit maturity evaluation and the 
availability of low-cost optical and imaging sensors. The peak in 2023 
coincides with the rapid expansion of AI-based computer vision and 
portable NIRS technologies, indicating a convergence between agricul­
tural engineering and data science for mango assessment.
5.2. Distribution of techniques across the literature
Fig. 13 represents the distribution of different techniques, NIRS, HSI, 
IP, DL, and combined NIRS + HSI, used across 94 research papers. As 
illustrated in Fig. 13, the majority of studies employed NIRS (61.5 %), 
followed by image-processing (IP) (16.7 %), DL-based imaging ap­
proaches (12.5 %), and HIS (7.3 %). This distribution indicates that NIRS 
remains the most dominant approach due to its high sensitivity to in­
ternal biochemical attributes such as TSS, DM, and TA. Despite its lab­
oratory origin, handheld NIR devices have enhanced accessibility and 
affordability, making them suitable for rapid, in-field maturity inspec­
tion. Conversely, IP and DL techniques are increasingly adopted for 
external maturity indicators, such as peel color, providing non-contact 
and high-throughput alternatives. HSI systems, although representing 
a smaller share, contribute significantly to integrated analysis by linking 
spectral and spatial information, yet their deployment is still constrained 
by cost, calibration complexity, and data size.
5.3. Algorithmic trends and analytical frameworks
The methodological analysis (Fig. 14) revealed three dominant 
algorithmic categories: statistical models (38 studies), ML algorithms 
(34 studies), and DL architectures (22 studies). Statistical approaches, 
primarily PLS, MLR, and PCR, remain the backbone of early non-

RQ1. What non-destructive methods (2015–2025) have been used 
for mango maturity/ripeness, and how have they evolved?
The systematic review of literature from 2015 to 2025 revealed a 
rapid evolution of non-destructive techniques used for mango maturity 
estimation. Early studies relied primarily on digital image processing 
and handcrafted feature extraction for visible color analysis, whereas 
recent research has progressively integrated NIRS, HSI, and DL to ach­
ieve more accurate and scalable maturity prediction. The development 
trajectory shows a clear transition from classical ML models, such as 
SVM and DT, to advanced CNNs and transfer learning. This evolution 
has been driven by the need for real-time, non-invasive assessment tools 
that can be deployed in both laboratory and field conditions.

Comparative analysis between NIRS and HSI methods demonstrates 
that both techniques provide strong predictive performance for internal 
quality parameters, including TSS, DM, TA, and firmness. While NIRS 
offers simplicity and speed, HSI delivers richer spectral–spatial

RQ3. What is the working methodology, challenges, and future 
progress of image processing-ML approaches for evaluating external 
mango maturity?
The standard workflow typically includes image acquisition, pre­
processing, feature extraction, model training, and validation. High- 
quality RGB images of mangoes are first captured under controlled or 
natural lighting conditions. Preprocessing steps, such as background 
removal, color normalization, and contrast enhancement, are applied to 
ensure image consistency. Distinct visual features are then extracted 
using color models (RGB, HSV, Lab*), which serve as input for ML 
classifiers such as SVM, RF, and DT. These models classify fruits into 
defined maturity stages under controlled environments. However, their 
performance decreases in natural field conditions due to variable illu­
mination and the reliance on handcrafted features. To enhance robust­
ness and automation, recent studies integrated DL-based feature 
extraction and illumination-invariant preprocessing, improving adapt­
ability and real-time performance in non-destructive mango maturity 
detection systems.
RQ4. What is the working methodology of DL models using NIRS, 
HSI, and RGB-based feature extraction for mango maturity prediction?
DL models have transformed non-destructive quality assessment by 
automatically learning complex relationships within spectral and visual 
data without relying on manual feature engineering. In NIRS and HSI 
applications, raw spectral or spectral–spatial data are preprocessed 
(noise reduction, normalization, dimensionality reduction) and then fed 
into CNNs, 1D-CNNs, or spectral–spatial hybrid networks, which extract 
latent features representing internal chemical attributes such as TSS, 
DM, TA, and firmness. These models outperform traditional chemo­
metric techniques (PLSR, SVM) by achieving higher prediction accuracy 
and robustness under varying conditions.
For RGB image-based systems, deep CNN architectures such as VGG, 
ResNet, Inception, and EfficientNet learn hierarchical visual represen­
tations of external features like color transitions. Transfer learning and 
fine-tuning enhance model generalization when datasets are limited. 
The typical DL workflow involves data acquisition, augmentation, 
model training, and validation using performance metrics such as ac­
curacy, recall, precision, and F1-score. DL models face challenges in 
cross-cultivar generalization, computational demand, and interpret­
ability. Recent trends therefore, must focus on multimodal fusion of 
RGB, NIRS, and HSI data and lightweight architectures for real-time, 
field-deployable mango maturity prediction systems.
RQ5. What are the challenges that limit the widespread use of all 
mango maturity estimation methods in the fields?
Although these methods have achieved high accuracy in laboratory 
conditions, several barriers prevent their translation to real-world 
agricultural environments. For NIRS and HSI, major limitations 
include high equipment cost, bulkiness of sensors, and sensitivity to 
ambient light, temperature, and fruit surface conditions, all of which 
affect calibration stability. Field variability, such as changes in humidity 
and illumination, leads to spectral noise and model drift, requiring 
frequent recalibration or complex correction algorithms. Image pro­
cessing systems face challenges due to inconsistent lighting, occlusions, 
and complex backgrounds, which reduce detection accuracy and hinder 
generalization across different orchards and seasons. These systems also 
rely heavily on handcrafted features, which may not capture subtle vi­
sual cues under uncontrolled conditions.
For DL-based models, the main constraints involve the requirement 
for large annotated datasets, computationally demanding training, and 
limited interpretability of the learned features. Additionally, trans­
ferring models across cultivars or geographical regions often leads to 
performance degradation due to variations in fruit morphology and 
environmental conditions.
RQ6. What future directions can improve the accuracy, sustain­
ability, and scalability of these methods?
The roadmap shown in Fig. 15 outlines future directions for non- 
destructive mango ripening assessment, focusing on how future in­
novations can contribute to simultaneously enhancing prediction accu­
racy, environmental sustainability, and system scalability. This path 
reflects a shift from discrete sensing and modeling methods to inte­
grated, intelligent, and interconnected agricultural systems. This can be 
achieved through the integration of multimodal data by combining 
complementary sensing methods such as RGB imaging, NIRS, HSI, and 
thermal sensors. While single-modal systems capture partial ripening 
information, hybrid frameworks enable the co-modeling of external vi­
sual indicators (color, texture, and shape) and internal biochemical

Furthermore, integrating interpretable artificial intelligence (XAI) 
frameworks will enhance transparency and trust in deep learning 
models by revealing how specific spectral or visual indicators influence 
maturity predictions, thus facilitating their adoption by farmers, ex­
porters, and regulators.
From a sustainability perspective, future trends will play a role in 
reducing post-harvest losses, optimizing resource utilization, and 
improving harvest timing and storage. Accurate maturity estimation 
enables selective harvesting at optimal physiological stages, preventing 
premature harvesting and over-ripening during storage and transport. 
Moreover, lightweight AI maturity prediction models, when applied to

6. Conclusion
This systematic review consolidated a decade of progress 
(2015–2025) in non-destructive mango maturity estimation, high­
lighting the evolution from traditional spectroscopic methods to 
advanced AI-driven imaging approaches. Among 94 analyzed studies, 
NIRS remains the most frequently adopted technique due to its strong 
sensitivity to internal biochemical attributes such as TSS, DM, and TA. 
HSI extends this advantage by providing spatial information, but is 
limited by cost and computational demands. In contrast, image pro­
cessing and DL methods have demonstrated remarkable performance in 
external maturity classification, achieving accuracies exceeding 90 % in 
controlled environments. The field has witnessed a significant paradigm 
shift toward hybrid and multimodal approaches, integrating spectral 
and imaging data with ML or DL frameworks. Recent trends reveal that 
DL models are being incorporated not only for image-based maturity 
prediction but also for spectral preprocessing and calibration, with 
attention-based architectures beginning to emerge as a promising 
research frontier. Despite this progress, several challenges persist, 
including limited cross-variety generalization, dataset scarcity, lighting 
variability, and field deployment constraints. Future studies should 
focus on developing lightweight, field-deployable sensor–AI systems, 
enhancing transfer learning and domain adaptation to support multi- 
cultivar applicability, and promoting standardized benchmarking 
datasets for transparent model comparison. Additionally, integrating 
environmentally sustainable technologies and IoT-enabled monitoring 
can facilitate real-time decision support for farmers and supply-chain 
operators. Overall, advancing toward scalable, interpretable, and low- 
cost multimodal systems will be crucial for achieving reliable, sustain­
able, and intelligent mango maturity evaluation under real-world agri­
cultural conditions.
