3D Reconstruction based on multi-view stereo in the deep learning
era: a survey and comparison of methods
Boyang Song1 · Bing Wu2 · Jin Xiao1 · Xiaoguang Hu1 · Jiaqi Shi1 · Baochang Zhang1

Abstract
This paper provides an in-depth review of deep learning methodologies to address Multi-view Stereo (MVS) challenges.
Through a detailed classiﬁcation of 3D reconstruction methods, we highlight the signiﬁcance of multi-view 3D reconstruction.
We ﬁrst examine the evolution of traditional approaches, including structure from motion, MVS, and surface reconstruction.
We then identify inherent limitations within the MVS stage. Subsequent sections focus on learning-based MVS methods
speciﬁcally designed to tackle these challenges. Our focus is primarily on mainstream methods that utilize depth maps for 3D
scenerepresentation,buildinguponthetraditionalplane-sweepingMVSalgorithm.Speciﬁcally,thisreviewﬁrstsystematically
categorizes current methods by their overall architecture, and then discusses their advancements across classical pipeline
stages: feature extraction, cost aggregation, cost volume regularization, depth inference, and depth fusion. Comprehensive
evaluations on public datasets show that recent learning-based methods achieve signiﬁcant performance gains. For instance,
leading approaches such as MVSFormer + + reach an overall error as low as 0.281 mm on the DTU dataset, outperforming
the baseline MVSNet by over 39%. On the Tanks and Temples benchmark, top methods achieve a mean F-score exceeding
67, demonstrating strong generalization in complex scenes. Key observations underscore the effectiveness of Transformers,
implicit neural representations, and geometry-aware depth prediction, along with the promising potential of progressive
reﬁnement architectures and unsupervised approaches. Finally, we discuss existing challenges and propose promising future
directions. These ﬁndings contribute to ongoing discussions on advancing monocular camera capabilities via deep learning,
particularly in the context of more mobile applications being deployed on smartphones and drones.

1 Introduction
Humans acquire at least 80% of external information through
vision [1]. Computer vision, aiming to simulate human
visual perception, has achieved remarkable progress. Human
scene understanding is inherently three-dimensional. Com-
pared to 1D data or 2D images, 3D models provide richer
structural, dimensional, textural, and motion information.
Consequently, 3D reconstruction, as a crucial branch of this
discipline, transforms real-world scenes into 3D models that
computers can process and display using algorithms.
Since the pioneering work on extracting 3D informa-
tion from 2D images [2], 3D reconstruction has evolved for
decades. It is now widely applied in diverse ﬁelds such as

Among various reconstruction methods, multi-view 3D
reconstruction builds object/scene models leveraging multi-
perspective images (ordered/unordered). Its advantages

realistic point clouds, and broad applicability—indicating

comprises three stages: Structure from Motion (SfM), Multi-
View Stereo (MVS), and surface reconstruction. MVS gener-
ates dense 3D point clouds; its performance directly dictates
reconstruction efﬁciency and quality.
Traditional MVS methods have achieved signiﬁcant
progress over decades, yet they still face limitations in
complex outdoor scenes. In terms of quality, they struggle
with illumination variations, challenging areas and invis-

demands substantial computation and memory, limiting
reconstruction scale and granularity. Based on deep learning
[32], AlexNet [33] achieved a breakthrough in the ImageNet
competition, leading computer vision research to diverge
into two major paradigms: traditional and learning-based
methods. However, learning-based MVS still faces similar
challenges. Achieving 3D reconstruction with high accu-
racy, completeness, efﬁciency, and generalization requires
further innovation. Given the rapid development of this ﬁeld,
a comprehensive survey of relevant algorithms is essential to
track advancements and support progress in this cutting-edge
domain.
As learning-based MVS is a rapidly evolving ﬁeld, exist-
ing survey studies exhibit certain limitations. Rong et al. [34]
classiﬁed 3D reconstruction and introduced the SfM stage,
but their analysis of deep learning-based MVS is conﬁned to
early research. Zhu et al. [35] attempted a classiﬁcation from
the MVS pipeline perspective but treated key components
like depth fusion and loss functions as background knowl-
edge, resulting in a fragmented analysis. Wang et al. [36]
provided a comprehensive survey but placed recurrent and
cascaded architectures within the cost volume section, mak-
ing it lengthy and complicated. Wu et al. [37] covered recent
advances in learning-based MVS but prioritized balanced
coverage of subcategories based on 3D scene representa-
tion, thereby limiting in-depth analysis of depth map-based
pipeline stages.
Compared to previous surveys, the main contributions of
this review can be summarized as follows:
(1) Historical continuity: It integrates foundational research
in traditional multi-view 3D reconstruction with recent
developments in learning-based MVS methods, provid-
ing a cohesive overview of the ﬁeld’s evolution.
(2) Novel taxonomy: It focuses on current mainstream
MVS approaches—learning-based methods via plane-
sweeping algorithm—and offers, to the best of our
knowledge, the ﬁrst systematic categorization from both
architectural and pipeline perspectives. This enhances
clarity and reduces redundancy.
(3) Benchmark analysis: It analyses the performance
of major learning-based MVS methods—grouped by
architectural categories—on widely adopted datasets.
Additionally, it provides the ﬁrst in-depth analysis of
progressive reﬁnement MVS methods.
This survey is structured as follows: Sect. 2 classiﬁes
3D reconstruction techniques and highlights the advantages

works in traditional multi-view 3D reconstruction. Section 4
discusses depth map-based MVS networks by architecture.
Section 5 introduces learning-based MVS methods using
the plane-sweeping algorithm, grouped by pipeline stage.
Section 6 lists common datasets and evaluation metrics.
Section 7 presents both qualitative and quantitative com-
parisons of most methods on two widely adopted datasets.
Section 8 summarizes current challenges and discusses
potential future directions. Section 9 concludes the survey.

2 Background
Based on 3D data acquisition methodology, 3D reconstruc-
tion divides into two primary categories: contact-type and
non-contacttype[38].Contact-typemethods[39]usedevices
like robotic arms to physically interact with objects and col-
lect data. Though precise, these methods are inefﬁcient and
have limited applicability. Non-contact methods are more
widely used and are divided into active and passive types,
based on how depth is obtained.
Active 3D reconstruction employs specialized equip-
ment actively emitting and receiving signals to acquire
depth. Laser scanners [40] and structured-light scanners
[41] can generate high-precision 3D point clouds. How-
ever, such devices are expensive, complex to operate, and
bulky. Structured-light scanners are also sensitive to ambi-
ent light, typically requiring controlled lighting conditions.
Depth cameras [42], commonly used for real-time dynamic
reconstruction in ﬁlm and gaming, are more affordable but
offer limited precision. Overall, active methods are con-
strained by a narrow depth range, making them unsuitable
for reconstructing large-scale outdoor scenes.
Passive 3D reconstruction uses visible light sensors cap-
turing single or multiple images, algorithmically solving

3 Traditional multi-view 3D reconstruction
At the dawn of the twenty-ﬁrst century, parametric geometry-
based 3D modeling matured, promoting the development
of professional software. However, these tools demanded
steep learning curves, signiﬁcant workloads, and offered low
efﬁciency. Against this background, multi-view 3D recon-
struction systems emerged [56], enabling direct 3D model
generation from multi-view images and greatly simplifying
the modeling process. The classic traditional multi-view 3D
reconstruction pipeline (Fig. 3) comprises three core stages:
(1) Structure from Motion: Estimates camera extrinsic
parameters (rotation and position) from multi-view
images, generating a sparse scene point cloud.
(2) Multi-View Stereo: Reconstructs a dense scene point
cloud using the images and camera parameters.
(3) Surface Reconstruction: Converts the dense point cloud
into a mesh model. Texture mapping can then add sur-
face textures as needed.
While vital for applications like Simultaneous Localiza-
tion and Mapping (SLAM), the sparse point cloud of SfM
only maps feature points and lacks clear object texture. Con-
versely, the subsequent MVS stage produces a dense point
cloud capturing nearly every surface point, preserving ﬁne
details. The quality of this dense point cloud directly dictates
model ﬁdelity, and this stage consumes the most computa-
tional time and memory resources. Consequently, MVS can
be regarded as the core step of multi-view 3D reconstruction.
This section reviews key traditional algorithms for each
stage, focusing particularly on traditional MVS methods to
establish groundwork for later chapters.
3.1 Traditional SfM
Camera parameters comprise intrinsic parameters (distor-
tion, focal length, etc.) and extrinsic parameters (pose infor-
mation). Intrinsic parameters are obtainable from camera
manuals or EXIF data; extrinsic parameters require solv-
ing from multi-view images using SfM, which concurrently
generates a sparse scene point cloud. This pipeline has three
modules: (1) feature point extraction and matching, (2) initial
estimation, and (3) Bundle Adjustment (BA).
3.1.1 Feature point extraction and matching
Feature extraction underpins many computer vision tasks like
object recognition and image segmentation. Feature points
represent image characteristics and require two properties:
distinctiveness across objects and repeatability for the same
object. As multi-view images are often captured outside well-
controlled laboratory settings, feature extraction algorithms
need robustness to reliably yield sufﬁcient accurate points.
Early feature extraction relied on corner detection.
Moravec et al. [57] proposed the Moravec operator. Har-
ris et al. [58] enhanced this with the rotation-invariant,
noise-resistant Harris operator. Mikolajczyk et al. [59]
incorporated scale, creating the scale-and-rotation-invariant
Harris-Laplace operator.
Lowe et al. [60] established a milestone with the Scale
Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT). Based on Laplacian
of Gaussian (LoG) ﬁltering [61], SIFT identiﬁes local
extrema in a Difference of Gaussians (DoG) pyramid scale
space as candidate points, exhibiting exceptional detec-
tion repeatability. It constructs a 128-dimensional descriptor
from the orientation gradient histogram, integrating loca-
tion/scale/orientation with strong distinctiveness and invari-
ance to rotation, scale, and illumination. Later work priori-
tized efﬁciency: Ke et al. [62] introduced PCA-SIFT, using

drastically reducing dimensionality. Though less robust and
distinctive than SIFT, their computational speed makes them
popular for real-time tasks like SLAM.
After extraction, feature matching establishes point-to-
point correspondences across images. This measures sim-
ilarity: ﬂoating-point descriptors (e.g., SIFT) use the Sum
of Squared Differences (L2 norm); binary descriptors (e.g.,
ORB) use Hamming distance. The matching strategy typi-
cally employs nearest neighbor search like k-Nearest Neigh-
bors (k-NN) algorithm [68].
Calculating correspondences by traversing all feature
points is computationally intensive. To avoid exhaustive
search, data indexing is essential. K-Dimensional Tree (KD-
Tree) indexing [69] is widely used, constructing a balanced
binary search tree that partitions feature space to minimize
maximum search depth and accelerate k-NN. Hash table
indexing is more efﬁcient for binary descriptors. Cheng et al.
[70] proposed Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) with cas-
caded clustering: short hash codes coarsely ﬁlter irrelevant
features, then long hash codes ﬁnely cluster candidates, sig-
niﬁcantly improving efﬁciency.
To eliminate mismatched points, most methods adopt the
cross-checking and ratio test of SIFT. Additionally, matched
feature points must satisfy epipolar geometry consistency.
The RANdom SAmple Consensus (RANSAC) algorithm
[71] commonly ﬁlters out points violating this constraint.
3.1.2 Initial estimation
Feature point extraction and matching establish inter-image
associations and epipolar geometry, enabling Scene Graph
construction. This graph treats images as nodes connected
by edges when sufﬁcient matched points exist. Using this
graph, camera extrinsic parameters (rotation matrix R and
translation vector t) can be solved, deriving camera rotation
(RT) and position (−RTt). Given camera parameters from
two or more views, the 3D coordinates of matched feature
points can be calculated based on epipolar geometry.
Extrinsic parameter estimation evolved from Hartley et al.
[72], who ﬁrst studied ﬁxed intrinsic parameters with pure
rotation. Luong et al. [73] extended this to combined rotation-
translation motion. Pollefeys et al. [74] further relaxed
constraints for varying intrinsic parameters. Based on pose
estimation strategy, SfM methods are categorized into three
types: incremental, global, and hybrid (Fig. 4).
3.1.2.1 Incremental SfM
Incremental SfM ﬁrst selects a
well-matched image pair, estimates camera parameters,
solves for corresponding 3D points via epipolar geometry
to form an initial sparse point cloud. Next, new images are
incrementally registered: based on correspondences between
new features and reconstructed 3D points, the method esti-
matesnewextrinsicparametersusingthePerspective-n-Point
(PnP) algorithm, removes outliers with RANSAC, and com-
putes new 3D points to expand coverage. To prevent scene
drift, bundle adjustment is performed after periodic registra-
tion.
Snavely et al. [75] pioneered large-scale unordered inter-
net image reconstruction using incremental SfM with Photo
Tourism. Subsequent studies improved key aspects. For out-
lier ﬁltering, Moulon et al. [76] proposed AC-RANSAC with
adaptive thresholds, enhancing pose estimation accuracy and
robustness at the expense of runtime. To improve efﬁciency,

3.1.2.2 Global SfM Global SfM ﬁrst estimates global cam-
era rotations by ﬁltering inconsistent scene graph edges
with thresholds. It subsequently estimates relative translation
matrices and solves global positions, again applying thresh-
old ﬁltering. Finally, it generates a sparse point cloud via
epipolar geometry using the ﬁltered scene graph, requiring
only a single bundle adjustment. Key improvements address
three aspects:
Regarding pre-ﬁltering of mismatched images, Zach et al.
[81] employed Bayesian networks to detect and remove visu-
ally conﬂicting matches, while Sweeney et al. [82] enhanced
accuracy through cycle consistency enforcement for funda-
mental matrix optimization.
For global rotation estimation (rotation averaging),
Govindu proposed solutions using quaternion [83] and Lie
algebra representations [84]. Based on L1-norm optimiza-
tion, Hartley et al. [85] applied the Weiszfeld algorithm,
while Chatterjee and Govindu [86] improved the accuracy
and robustness of the Lie algebra method through iterative
optimization. Eriksson et al. [87] treated the problem as min-
imizing multiple non-convex constraints and solved it using
the Lagrangian dual algorithm.
Concerning global position estimation (translation aver-
aging), Moulon et al. [88] achieved stable solutions using
an inverse trifocal tensor estimation method and a transla-
tion registration approach. Wilson and Snavely [89] (1DSfM)
method mapped relative translation matrices to 1D space,
removed outliers, and minimized angular error through
non-convex optimization. Ozyesil and Singer [90] (LUD)
minimized the L1 norm of translation matrix errors. Based
on convex optimization, Goldstein et al. [91] (Shapeﬁt/kick)
provided accurate and efﬁcient solutions even in the presence
of outliers. Zhuang et al. [92] combined LUD’s robustness
with Shapeﬁt/kick’s accuracy to eliminate outlier interfer-
ence.
Compared to incremental SfM, global SfM eliminates the
constraints of initial image pair selection and registration
order, avoids error accumulation, and improves efﬁciency.
Limitations include: (1) Inability to ﬁlter outliers during pose
estimation, leading to insufﬁcient robustness, especially for
translation averaging, which is sensitive to incorrect rela-
tive translation matrices; (2) Degraded reconstruction quality
under uneven viewpoint distribution; (3) Edge ﬁltering may
remove key views, affecting scene completeness.

view images, expands cluster overlaps, and estimates global
camera rotations for each set. It then reconstructs subsets
incrementally before fusing all results. The core innovation
lies in multi-view clustering and expansion:
Bhowmick et al. [93] adopted a divide-and-conquer strat-
egy and used normalized cuts for scene graph partitioning.

3.1.3 Bundle adjustment
Bundle adjustment optimizes initial camera parameter and
3D point estimations by minimizing the sum of reprojec-
tion errors. This critical step determines SfM accuracy and
signiﬁcantly impacts efﬁciency. This nonlinear least-squares
problem is typically solved using the Levenberg–Marquardt
(LM) algorithm initialized with preliminary estimations.
Exactsolvingalgorithmsinvolveprecisematrixdecompo-
sition and full parameter optimization but are computation-
ally expensive. Efﬁciency-focused research includes: Sparse
Bundle Adjustment (SBA) by Lourakis et al. [98] reduces
computational costs by leveraging the sparsity of the Jaco-
bian matrix corresponding to subgroups of parameters that
lack interaction. Choudhary et al. [99] proposed a GPU-CPU
hybrid acceleration scheme. Inexact iterative strategies can
further enhance efﬁciency. Agarwal et al. [100] introduced a
Newton-type algorithm for massive-scale bundle adjustment
(10 k + images), utilizing preconditioned conjugate gradi-
ents to achieve linear-complexity optimization. Wu et al.
[101] later provided a GPU-accelerated implementation of
this algorithm.
3.1.4 Summary of Sect. 3.1
Advances in hardware and algorithms enable mainstream
open-source SfM systems (VisualSfM [77], OpenMVG
[102], COLMAP-SfM [80], Photo Tourism System [75],
Bundler [103], MVE [104]) to prioritize incremental SfM for
its robustness and accuracy. These mature systems facilitate
efﬁcient sparse reconstruction. Agarwal et al. [105] deployed
Bundler on a parallel distributed system to process 2 mil-
lion “Rome”-tagged photos from Flickr. This achievement,
known as “Building Rome in a Day”, demonstrated the fea-
sibility of city-scale SfM reconstruction.
Nevertheless, in multi-view 3D reconstruction, its primary
role is to estimate camera parameters. Since feature points are
far fewer than image pixels, the sparse point cloud is merely a
by-productthatlackstextureandstructuraldetails.Exceptfor
million-image inputs like the Rome experiment, it generally
fails to meet practical requirements. Therefore, dense point
cloud reconstruction via MVS remains a necessary next step.
3.2 Traditional MVS
MVS methods reconstruct dense 3D scenes using multi-view
images and camera parameters. Given known camera param-
eters, this classical computer vision problem aims to ﬁnd
the corresponding 3D point for each 2D pixel to establish
stereo correspondences. The core objective of traditional
MVS methods fundamentally relies on identifying photo-
consistent points across multiple images. Photo-consistency
measures how well a point’s appearance matches across dif-
ferent viewpoints. For two pixels, f and g, from different
images, this is typically measured by:
(1) Sum of Squared Differences (SSD): ρSSD( f , g) =
∥f −g∥2. This measures the squared difference in pixel
values.
(2) Sum of Absolute Differences (SAD): ρSAD( f , g) =
∥f −g∥1. This measures the absolute difference in
pixel values.
(3) Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC): ρNCC( f , g) =
( f −f )•(g−g)
δ f δg
. This is a robust metric that accounts for
changes in illumination by normalizing for brightness
and contrast, where f and g are the mean pixel intensi-
ties within a local patch around the pixels, and δ f and δg
are the standard deviations of the local image patches.
As shown in Fig. 5, traditional MVS methods can gen-
erally be categorized into four types based on 3D scene
representation: voxel-based, point cloud-based, mesh-based,
and depth map-based.
3.2.1 Voxel-based methods
A voxel is a regular cubic unit formed by uniformly divid-
ing space. These methods discretize 3D space into voxel
grids and reconstruct surfaces by identifying object voxels.
Seitz et al. [106] proposed Voxel Coloring, which traverses
voxels in a ﬁxed visibility order, retaining color-consistent
ones. Conversely, Kutulakos et al. [107] introduced Space
Carving, eliminating inconsistent voxels through plane-
sweeping.
Alternatively, some approaches encode voxel-to-surface
distance as Markov Random Fields (MRFs), transforming
surface optimization into a global cost minimization problem

voxels introduce spatial discretization errors, while ﬁne vox-

these object-level methods struggle to scale to large scenes.
3.2.2 Point cloud-based methods
A point cloud consists of points locating the surface in
3D space, which can also carry color and normal infor-
mation. These methods densify initial sparse point clouds
through propagation strategies, covering target surfaces with
patches—hence termed point-propagation or region-growing
approaches.
Patch-based Multi-View Stereo (PMVS) [113] pioneered
this category, using small rectangular patches as fundamen-
tal 3D primitives. Its core workﬂow comprises: (1) Initial
point cloud generation: Matching features are extracted by
Harris and DoG operator, and 3D points are computed via
epipolar geometry; (2) Propagation: Points are projected onto
images and propagated to empty regions according to rules;
(3) Filtering: outliers with inconsistent depth or photometry
are removed. PMVS enhances accuracy by enforcing local
photometric consistency and global visibility constraints.
Requiring simultaneous image processing, PMVS incurs
substantial computational and memory burdens. For mas-
sive images, CMVS-PMVS [114] decomposes images into
clusters, runs PMVS per cluster, merges results, and applies
robust ﬁltering for error handling.
Given the representativeness of PMVS, subsequent
research often builds upon it: Li et al. [115] introduced Canny
edge features to enhance robustness, allowing the method to
handle sparsely matched image pairs; Locher et al. [116]
implemented a dynamic octree to manage patches, support-
ing priority-based propagation and interim output.
Point cloud-based methods generate accurate, uniformly
distributed point clouds but struggle with challenging areas
such as low-texture regions, reﬂective surfaces, and repetitive
patterns. This often leads to incomplete reconstructions, such
as holes in the point cloud. Moreover, these methods are gen-
erally inefﬁcient, and their sequential propagation strategy
limits potential speed improvements through parallelization.
3.2.3 Mesh-based methods
A polygon mesh provides topological coherence and is
often the ﬁnal 3D representation. These methods deform
an initial surface to ﬁt the target by minimizing an energy
function—hence termed surface-evolution approaches. The
initial surface is typically a visual hull formed by intersecting
spatial cones derived from reconstructed silhouettes of multi-
viewimages.Mesh-basedmethodscanbefurthercategorized
into level set-based and deformable mesh-based approaches.
Faugeras et al. [117] pioneered the level set pipeline: con-
struct a color-consistency-based energy functional (implic-
itly representing the target surface), transform minimization
into a Euler–Lagrange equation via variational principles,
then evolve the surface using the level set of partial differen-
tial equation. This automatically handles topological changes
during evolution. Later work focused on global energy func-
tions: Pons et al. [118] estimated multi-view stereo and
non-rigid 3D motion simultaneously from video sequences
via a new variational method, which computed global-image-
based matching costs and minimized prediction errors.
Gargallo et al. [119] integrated visibility constraints into
the derivative computation of an energy function based on
reprojection error. Kolev et al. [120] used continuous con-
vex relaxation with Successive Over-Relaxation (SOR) for
global optimization.

3.2.4 Depth map-based methods
A depth map encodes the distance from surface points to the
camera, representing 3D geometry in 2.5D per viewpoint.
Unlike voxel-, point-, or mesh-based methods, this approach
decomposes MVS into two more tractable subproblems:
(1) Depth Prediction: Treat each image as reference in turn.
Select source images (based on viewing angle, distance,
coverage or dispersion) to form pairs/groups. Estimate
the reference depth map using selected images and cam-
era parameters.
(2) Depth Fusion: Merge all depth maps into a uniﬁed point
cloud or mesh model.
This framework offers greater ﬂexibility than alterna-
tives. Processing only relevant image pairs/groups per refer-
ence image—rather than the entire set—enhances efﬁciency.
Since depth map quality greatly affects reconstruction qual-
ity, research focuses primarily on prediction. Fusion is
typically handled by mature open-source/commercial tools.
3.2.4.1 Plane-sweeping algorithm Early methods selected
only a single source image, relying on binocular stereo
matching. Goesele et al. [125] computed depth maps via
window-based voting, returning only well-matched points
for accuracy at the expense of completeness. Window
matching assumed fronto-parallel surfaces, causing errors
on oblique planes. Gallup et al. [126] introduced a real-
time, multi-directional method—Plane-Sweeping Stereo—-
for accurately handling oblique surfaces. Bradley et al. [127]
employed scalable matching windows to improve estima-
tion density and accuracy. Hirschmüller [128] proposed the
accurateandefﬁcientSemi-GlobalMatching(SGM)method,
which used pixel-wise mutual information-based matching
costs to handle radiometric differences and optimized global
costs under smoothness constraints.
Image pairs underutilize multi-view information. Select-
ing multiple source images better aligns with MVS essence.
To address mismatches caused by repetitive textures, occlu-
sions, and low-texture regions, Campbell et al. [129] (1)
selected multiple depth hypotheses and extracted depth esti-
mates based on spatial consistency, and (2) allowed for
unknown states in uncertain cases. This approach formulated
depth prediction as a discrete-label MRF optimization prob-
lem, solved through energy function minimization, resulting
in highly accurate depth maps with few outliers. Tola et al.
[130] utilized DAISY features for dense matching with
epipolar line sampling, offering low complexity and memory
efﬁciency for ultra-high-resolution reconstruction.
3.2.4.2 PatchMatch algorithm
Barnes et al. [131] pro-
posed PatchMatch for fast approximate nearest-neighbor
patch matching using random sampling and coherence-based
propagation. Bleyer et al. [132] extended it to binocular
stereo (PatchMatch Stereo): initialize each pixel with a ran-
dom 3D patch (depth + normal); the values are updated
during propagation from top-left to bottom-right by check-
ing neighboring/random perturbed/corresponding matching
points; then the process is repeated in reverse. The slanted
support window model enabled highly oblique surface recon-
struction and achieved sub-pixel matching accuracy.
PatchMatch effectively generalizes to MVS. Key meth-
ods include Gipuma [133], which applies red–black
checkerboard-based parallel propagation with GPU accel-
eration; COLMAP [134], which uses joint photometric and
geometric priors for pixel-wise view selection and jointly
estimates depth and normal maps, offering high accuracy,
efﬁciency, and robustness; ACMM [135], which combines
multi-hypothesis view selection with adaptive checkerboard
propagation and exploits multi-scale geometric consistency
to improve completeness; and TAPA-MVS [136], which gen-
erates additional superpixel-wise hypotheses through plane
ﬁtting, enhances the image consistency measure to improve
depth estimation in weakly-textured regions, and incorpo-
rates a reﬁnement step to eliminate errors and ﬁll missing
areas, thereby signiﬁcantly increasing completeness.

3.2.5 Summary of Sect. 3.2
Dense reconstruction forms the core of multi-view 3D recon-
struction pipelines. Traditional MVS methods—including
point cloud-based PMVS [113]/CMVS-PMVS [114] and

However, reliance on hand-crafted similarity metrics for

content, causing failures in challenging areas such as low-
texture regions, reﬂective surfaces (non-Lambertian sur-

Occluded or out-of-FOV (Fields-Of-View) pixels violate

While techniques like image segmentation, point cloud

overhead. For large-scale scenes, the prohibitive compu-
tational and memory requirements of traditional methods
constrain reconstruction scale and accuracy.
In summary, traditional MVS methods still possess sig-
niﬁcant room for improvement in terms of reconstruction
accuracy, completeness, efﬁciency, and robustness.
3.3 Traditional surface reconstruction
Traditional MVS methods typically output dense point
clouds. To construct renderable/measurable mesh models,
the complete pipeline requires additional surface reconstruc-
tion and texture mapping (Fig. 6). Surface reconstruction
establishes geometric connectivity among 3D points.
3.3.1 Surface reconstruction
Basic methods directly add geometric connectivity between
3D points but demand high-quality point clouds. Delau-
nay triangulation-based methods [137] establish connectivity
then remove redundant tetrahedrons from point cloud con-
vex hull. For instance: Hang et al. [138] (TetGen) robustly
generated high-quality tetrahedral meshes; Su et al. [139]
proposed an adaptive Hilbert curve insertion algorithm with
quasi-linear time complexity, enhancing triangulation efﬁ-
ciency. However, these methods risk incorrectly connecting
non-adjacent points, motivating seed-growing methods like
the Ball-Pivoting algorithm [140], which searched for new
connect vertices by rolling spheres of adaptive radius from
the seed triangle.
Alternatively,
surface-ﬁtting
methods
generate
new
meshes whose vertices do not strictly correspond to original
points. Kazhdan et al. [141] pioneered Poisson surface recon-
struction: based on the principle that the Laplacian of the
spatial indicator function equals the divergence of the surface
normals, this algorithm solved the Poisson equation to obtain
the indicator function and then converted it into a triangu-
lar mesh. Compared to previous radial basis function-based
methods [142], the Poisson algorithm uses hierarchically
structured local support basis functions, resulting in a sparse
linear efﬁcient solution. Subsequent enhancements include:
Zhou et al. [143] developed the ﬁrst fully GPU-parallelized
implementation; Kazhdan et al. [144] incorporated spar-
sity constraints to propose the Screened Poisson algorithm,
improving efﬁciency from the mathematical framework;
Estellers et al. [145] improved robustness by introducing
penalty terms for noise and outliers in the convex mini-
mization process. Implicit surface-based methods, like the
Poisson algorithm, are widely used for their robustness, accu-
racy, efﬁciency, and ability to reconstruct smooth surfaces.
3.3.2 Texture mapping
Dense point clouds can be directly reconstructed into meshes
retaining color information, or ﬁrst into surfaces (with-
out color) with ﬁner textures later added through texture
mapping. Since multi-view images commonly contain over-
lapping visible regions, key improvements focus on the
following aspects:
For optimal texture selection, Frueh et al. [146] con-
structed a cost function considering occlusion, resolution,
normal direction, and inter-triangle coherence. Lempitsky
et al. [147] constructed an MRF energy function through
view back-projection and optimized it for ﬁnal scheme.
For texture seam elimination, Lempitsky et al. [147]
applied gradient-domain compositing to smooth seams.
Allène et al. [148] ﬁrst optimized seam placement, then
applied pixel-wise color correction near seams via multi-
band blending algorithm. Gal et al. [149] used global
optimization to eliminate seams and attenuate color differ-
ences with gradient-domain methods.

methods in the deep learning era
Multi-view 3D reconstruction offers signiﬁcant potential due
to its wide applicability. However, traditional MVS methods
exhibitlimitedperformanceinnon-idealscenarios(including
challenging areas and invisible regions). As a pivotal step
in multi-view 3D reconstruction, MVS bottlenecks impede
its further practical applications. While recent studies have
attempted to exploit abstract image information, deﬁning its
explicit functional form remains challenging.
Deep learning, ﬁrst introduced in 2006 [32], encom-
passes Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) inspired by
biological vision mechanisms. Following the breakthrough
performance of AlexNet [33] in the ImageNet competition,
research in computer vision has bifurcated into traditional
and learning-based approaches. The latter typically surpasses
the former in reconstruction quality and efﬁciency.
Early efforts concentrated on voxel-based MVS networks.
These methods predict target occupancy within a 3D voxel
space, directly recovering globally consistent representa-
tions.
Ji et al. proposed SurfaceNet [150], predicting binary
labels indicating whether a voxel lies on the surface. The
method ﬁrst projects input images to form a Color Voxel
Cube (CVC) that stores pixel values and camera parameters.
CVC pairs are then fed into a network trained with a class-
balanced cross-entropy loss to predict the probability that a
voxel is a surface voxel. Finally, a weighted average fusion
using view relative weights handles viewpoint differences
and occlusions.
LSM [151] fully exploits 3D geometry. It projects image
features onto a discrete 3D grid by rasterizing viewing rays,
aligning features along epipolar lines. A Recurrent Neural
Network(RNN)sequentiallyprocessesthisfeaturegridintoa
cost grid, enabling implicit cross-view local matching. Since
initial cost grids are often noisy, a 3D U-Net [152] incor-
porates global context for smoothing. The smoothed cost
grid generates either a 3D voxel occupancy map or projected
depth maps representing the 3D geometry.
While SurfaceNet and LSM are limited to object-level
geometry, recent studies have explored the Truncated Signed
Distance Function (TSDF) for efﬁcient 3D scene represen-
tation [153, 154], as well as methods that fuse multi-view
projected features into a global 3D feature volume [155].
These networks directly infer 3D model representations, in
contrast to the binary labels of SurfaceNet or the voxel occu-
pancy map used in LSM.

4.1 Supervised approaches
Research on learning-based MVS methods has advanced
rapidly in recent years. MVSNet [156], proposed by Yao
et al., became a landmark work and established the stan-
dard pipeline to reconstruct dense point clouds from multiple
images for most subsequent supervised methods. How-
ever, since depth map-based MVS methods, as discussed in
Sect. 3.2.4, are decoupled into depth prediction and depth
fusion, the main contribution of MVSNet, especially in the
context of deep learning, lies in its end-to-end depth pre-
diction network. Nevertheless, MVSNet openly provides
practical engineering details for the entire pipeline, includ-
ing camera parameter estimation, source view selection, and
depth map fusion. It also supplies the essential training
data—ground truth depth maps—rendered from ground truth
point clouds. As a pioneer in learning-based MVS methods,
MVSNet’s work has been foundational to the ﬁeld.
Compared to state-of-the-art traditional methods, MVS-
Net signiﬁcantly improved the reconstruction quality and
efﬁciency. However, processing the 3D cost volume requires
time and memory that grow cubically with model resolu-
tion, resulting in high computational costs for depth accuracy
improvements, which require higher image resolution and
more depth hypotheses. This hinders practical application
on high-resolution or large-scale scene datasets.
The efﬁciency of learning-based MVS methods depends
primarily on the depth prediction process—speciﬁcally the
number of input views, their resolution, and the number of
depth hypotheses. Research in this ﬁeld remains largely in
the theoretical exploration phase. Many works focus on opti-
mizing MVSNet’s overall architecture to enhance efﬁciency.
Within MVSNet, the U-Net-like 3D CNN for cost vol-
ume regularization consumes the most runtime and memory.
Consequently, subsequent research emphasizes replacing
this 3D CNN with lightweight regularization networks or
constructing lightweight cost volumes. Two representative
approaches emerged: (1) Recurrent architectures employing
2D RNNs to regularize cost volume slices, offering sub-
stantial memory savings for high-resolution reconstruction
but increasing inference time; and (2) Cascaded architec-
tures using a cascaded cost volume formulation, effectively
reducing runtime and memory while maintaining quality,
consequently adopted by many recent methods for MVS
reconstruction in a coarse-to-ﬁne manner. Cascaded meth-
ods, however, inherently suffer from error propagation.
To improve efﬁciency, some early methods implemented
the inherent progressive reﬁnement concept of MVSNet.
The core idea involves ﬁrst predicting a low-resolution
depth map using a coarse MVS module (with fewer depth
hypotheses), then applying a reﬁnement module to generate
a high-resolution depth map. While progressive reﬁnement
architectures share the coarse-to-ﬁne paradigm with cas-
caded architectures, their depth reﬁnement does not strictly
center on predicted values from the previous stage, thereby
avoiding error propagation. However, designing efﬁcient and
powerful reﬁnement modules often necessitates incorporat-
ing prior knowledge from other domains, resulting in higher
design costs than adopting a cascaded architecture. There-
fore, most existing studies in this ﬁeld are in the early stages,
and recent works are relatively limited compared to the other
two architectures.
4.1.1 Recurrent architecture
RecurrentMVSmethodsreplaceMVSNet’scomputationally
expensive 3D CNN with 2D RNNs, sequentially regularizing
cost volume slices along the depth dimension. Compared to
winner-takes-all or spatial regularization (Fig. 7), recurrent
regularization aggregates information across both spatial and
depth directions. This reduces regularization dimensionality
from three to two dimensions, signiﬁcantly lowering memory
consumption at the expense of runtime. Yao et al. ﬁrst intro-
duced this concept [157] and implemented it in the scalable
framework R-MVSNet.
R-MVSNet employs
two-dimensional
Convolutional
Units (Conv) for regularization in the spatial direction and
Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) with sequential temporal con-
nections for regularization in the depth direction. Stacking
multiple Conv-GRU layers achieves a regularization effect
comparable to 3D CNNs with signiﬁcantly better memory
efﬁciency. Yan et al. proposed a hybrid recurrent regular-
ization network in D2HC-RMVSNet [158], utilizing a 2D
U-Net built with Long Short-Term Memory Convolutional
Cells (LSTMConvCells) to regularize slices. This lever-
ages rich contextual information, enhancing robustness and
accuracy while substantially reducing memory. Similarly,
AA-RMVSNet [159] employs LSTMConvCells in an RNN-
CNN hybrid network: an encoder-decoder CNN horizontally
regularizes slices, while ﬁve parallel RNNs vertically prop-
agate intermediate outputs. Targeting high-resolution urban
aerial reconstruction, RED-Net [160] places GRUs only at
the end of the encoder, achieving signiﬁcant memory reduc-
tion.
Although recurrent MVS methods alleviate the memory
bottleneck in high-resolution reconstruction, the predic-
tion time for a single depth map remains high. Moreover,
adjacent depth hypotheses only implicitly encode 3D cor-
relations—unlike the explicit spatial relationships between
pixels—resulting in the 3D cost volume inherently lacking a
sequential structure. Consequently, despite improvements in
memory efﬁciency, processing within recurrent architectures
may remain suboptimal.

4.1.2 Cascaded architecture
Cascaded MVS methods, grounded in residual processing,
achieve efﬁcient high-precision depth prediction in a coarse-
to-ﬁne manner. Using the representative CasMVSNet [161]
as an example (Fig. 8): (1) Low-resolution features from the
top level of a feature pyramid construct a coarse cost volume
with sparse depth hypotheses covering the full depth range,
predicting a coarse depth map. (2) A narrowed depth range is
centered on this coarse prediction, sampling new hypothesis
planes within it. (3) High-resolution features from the bottom
levelbuildapartialcostvolume,estimatingaﬁnerdepthmap.
The key challenge lies in determining the depth search
range and depth hypothesis interval for ﬁner stages. Cas-
MVSNet employs ﬁxed-size depth intervals and a ﬁxed
number of depth hypotheses, progressively narrowing the
range. Alternative strategies include: Cheng et al. [162] in
UCS-Net determined a pixel-wise adaptive range based on
depth prediction uncertainty (probability distribution vari-
ance), better characterizing actual depth residuals than ﬁxed
ranges and enhancing quality (also adopted by PVSNet
[163]). BP-MVSNet [164] directly uses the expected 3D
error along the depth dimension to set its adaptive range. IS-
MVSNet [165] sets non-uniform sampling intervals based
on the previous stage’s predicted error distribution, increas-
ing resolution near ground truth. Yi et al. [166] in DDR-Net
added a lightweight range estimation module via 2D CNN to
learnuncertaintyfromtheprobabilityvolume,outperforming
the UCS-Net’s strategy. ARAI-MVSNet [167] proposes two
modules to predict the depth range and adjust the depth inter-
val adaptively. Yang et al. [168] in CVP-MVSNet designed
a range criterion based on pixel offsets from source views.
Most recently, Mi et al. [169] in GBi-Net framed hypothesis
reﬁnement as a binary search problem, proposing a Gen-
eralized Binary Search Network that iteratively initializes
and updates hypotheses based on coarse depth. NR-MVSNet
[170] considers the normal consistency and collects the depth
hypotheses from neighboring pixels with the same normals,
improving depth accuracy in challenging areas.
A unique aspect of CVP-MVSNet is its cascaded structure
based on an image pyramid instead of the feature pyramid,
sharing network parameters across stages. This enables train-
ing on low-resolution data while handling high-resolution

Recent research innovates cascaded architectures by
introducing iterative processes within each stage (termed
cascaded-recurrent). The simplest form replaces the 3D CNN
in each cascaded stage with a 2D RNN. HR-MVSNet [175]
uses an LSTMConvCell network to process 2D cost volume
slices. Inspired by applying the Recurrent All-Pairs Field
Transforms (RAFT) optical ﬂow network [176] to binocu-
lar stereo matching in RAFT-Stereo [177], many methods
treat the 3D cost volume as a static lookup table, computing
a 2D cost volume analogous to a disparity ﬁeld: geometric
features are indexed from the 3D cost volume using the cur-
rent depth map and concatenated with it to form the 2D cost
volume; subsequent stages iteratively update this 2D cost vol-
ume using 2D GRUs without updating the 3D cost volume.
CER-MVS [178], closest to RAFT, introduces ﬁve improve-
ments for MVS including epipolar cost volumes and cost
volume cascading. DispMVS [179] combines MVS epipolar
geometry with RAFT computation, sampling features along
epipolar lines, computing similarity, solving for Epipolar-
ﬂow (E-ﬂow), and achieving iterative updates via multi-view
extension based on weighted sum and depth-ﬂow conversion.
CER-MVS initializes the 2D cost volume to zero; Disp-
MVS uses random initialization. Later methods provide
MVSNet-like initialization for the 2D GRU update, consis-
tent with the cascaded MVS coarsest stage. IterMVS [180]
constructs the 2D cost volume by concatenating the upsam-
pled depth map with geometric features from four-stage
similarity volumes, reducing its dimensionality by a 2D U-
Net, and then feeding it into 2D GRUs iterated four times
per stage. Efﬁ-MVS [181] and Efﬁ-MVS + [182] propose a
lightweight 2D dynamic cost volume fusing local geometric
features, depth features, and image context-aware features
(the ﬁrst two are updated per GRU iteration). IGEV-Stereo
[183] extracts geometric features from the multi-level sim-
ilarity volume pyramid and the full image-pair correlation
volume pyramid, then encodes and concatenates them with
the current disparity prediction and its encoding. The com-
bined geometry encoding volume is iteratively processed and
updated by 2D GRUs. Its extension IGEV-MVS supports
MVS.
These cascaded methods modify the MVSNet [156]
pipeline, grounded in plane-sweeping algorithm [126]. Wang
et al. [184] pioneered MVS networks based on Patch-
Match algorithm [127]. PatchMatchNet [184] also adopts
a cascaded architecture, with three modules per stage: (1)
Initialization: Generate depth hypotheses (random in the
ﬁrst stage; CasMVSNet-strategy uniform sampling there-
after); (2) Propagation: Propagate hypotheses to neighbors;
(3) Evaluation: Compute matching cost and predict depth.
Avoiding explicit cost volume construction and 3D CNN
regularization, PatchMatchNet further reduces computa-
tional costs. Its subsequent research, such as PatchMatch-RL
[185], incorporates normal estimation via dilated patch ker-
nels, adds a recurrent regularization process, and trains a
depth/normal joint estimation network with reinforcement
learning.
Cascaded MVS methods signiﬁcantly reduce compu-
tational burden through low-cost coarse prediction and
lightweight partial cost volumes. Compared to MVSNet,
its partial volumes in ﬁner stages offer higher spatial and
depth resolution, enabling high-quality depth maps and point
clouds. Combining efﬁciency with competitive quality, it
remains a major research focus. However, cascaded methods
inherently suffer from inadequately addressed error propa-
gation. PatchMatchNet [184] experimentally demonstrates
that errors originating in the coarsest stage persist despite
reﬁnement at the ﬁnest stage. AACVP-MVSNet [171] fur-
ther points out that large depth intervals at the coarsest
stage lead to erroneous estimates; subsequent stages perform
searches centered on these inaccurate depth values with-
out introducing structural modiﬁcations, thereby propagating
errors and degrading reconstruction quality. Although some
recent cascaded methods, such as NR-MVSNet [170] which
uses features from the reference image and cost volume to
reﬁne the coarse depth map, are trying to alleviate this prob-
lem, a perfect solution remains to be explored. Furthermore,
the widespread adoption of cascaded architectures fosters
the development of exclusive techniques, creating substan-
tial performance gaps compared to alternative approaches.
This trend risks the emergence of performance barriers that
couldundermineresearchdiversityandshiftfocusawayfrom
fundamental research directions toward excessive optimiza-
tionofcascadedframeworks,potentiallyhinderinglong-term
progress in the ﬁeld.

progressive reﬁnement methods differ from MVSNet and R-
MVSNet in two key aspects: (1) The coarse module uses
signiﬁcantly sparser hypotheses and lower resolution (e.g.,
96 hypotheses at H/8 × W/8 versus 256 at H/4 × W/4 in
MVSNet), reducing the cost volume size by approximately
10 times and substantially lowering resource consumption;
(2) The reﬁnement module is both more powerful and efﬁ-
cient—coarser inputs demand stronger reﬁnement, yet a
lightweight design ensures continued resource efﬁciency.
Chen et al. proposed point-based iterative reﬁnement in
Point-MVSNet [186] and VA-Point-MVSNet [187]. Coarse
depth is upsampled and converted into a local point cloud,
and residuals between the current prediction and the ground
trutharecomputeddirectlyin3Dspace.Byoperatingdirectly
on the 3D point cloud, these methods compute the residual
between the current depth prediction and the ground truth.
Residual estimation involves the following steps: (1) con-
catenating variance-based matching cost with projected 3D
positions to form point features; (2) generating hypotheses by
shifting along the reference camera direction; (3) enhancing
local features through k-NN edge convolution; (4) computing
probabilities and displacements; and (5) projecting displace-
ments to 2D depth residuals. The ﬁnal depth map is generated
by iteratively estimating and applying residuals to the input.
Fast-MVSNet[188]prioritizesefﬁciencywithasparse-to-
dense strategy: A dilated sparse cost volume predicts sparse
depth with low computational overhead and broad contex-
tual coverage; joint bilateral upsampling generates a dense
depth map guided by the reference image; and learnable
Gauss–Newton optimization reﬁnes depth by minimizing
reprojection error. This approach achieves signiﬁcant com-
putational reduction and quality improvement over MVSNet,
R-MVSNet, and Point-MVSNet.
Other architectures use simpler reﬁnement modules:
MVS2 [189] applies a Spatial Propagation Network [190];
PatchMatchNet [184] upsamples the depth map and predicts
residuals with MSG-Net [191], later used by CDS-MVSNet
[192]; GeoMVSNet [193] treats depth as a discrete signal and
uses the Discrete Fourier Transform for frequency-domain
ﬁltering; P-MVSNet [194] uses a reﬁnement network to
transform the concatenation of latent probability volume
and reference features into a high-resolution probability vol-
ume. Cascaded-recurrent methods such as IterMVS [180]
and IGEV-MVS [183] inherit the spatial upsampling mod-
ule from RAFT-Stereo [177], leveraging a 2D CNN to learn
weights from the reference feature map and computing high-
resolution depth for each pixel as a convex combination of
its nine coarse neighbors. DSC-MVSNet [195] proposes an
efﬁcient Feature Transfer Module to upsample the predicted
depth map for higher accuracy. However, these modules lack
the capacity required by progressive reﬁnement methods.
Although both progressive reﬁnement and cascaded archi-
tectures follow the coarse-to-ﬁne paradigm, they differ
fundamentally. The former optimizes depth values in later
stages using alternative methods, treating prior output only
as a reference. The latter continues to apply the MVSNet
paradigm for inference within prediction-centered ranges,
which inherently propagates errors. This makes the pro-
gressive reﬁnement architecture a highly promising research
direction, even though it currently has fewer methods and is
still in the early stages of development compared to the cas-
caded approach. However, designing powerful and efﬁcient
depth reﬁnement modules requires integrating knowledge
from other domains, increasing design and application com-
plexity.
Recently, Song et al. proposed ACINR-MVSNet [196].
An adaptive group-wise correlation similarity measure efﬁ-
ciently handles visibility ambiguity. After single-stage depth
estimation, the RGB-guided implicit neural reﬁnement mod-
ule recovers sharp boundaries and ﬁne details. A pyramid
feature extractor with repeated top-down/bottom-up paths
generates context-aware features, which also power an
enhanced Gauss–Newton module to drive ﬁnal upsampling
and optimization.
Their TTINS-MVSNet [197] integrates three compo-
nents: (1) Enhanced MVS Module: The Intra-view Epipolar
Transformer improves reconstruction in challenging areas
by aggregating non-local features along epipolar lines. The
Inter-view Epipolar Transformer builds a visibility-aware
cost volume through long-range 3D correlation model-
ing, reducing the impact of mismatched pixels in invisible
regions. (2) Implicit Neural Super-resolution (INS): An
RGB-guided lightweight MLP decoder jointly optimizes
interpolation weights and depth values to recover ﬁne details.
(3) Enhanced Gauss–Newton Reﬁnement (EGNR): A ﬁnal
optimization step.

Multi-view images are easier to acquire than ground
truth point clouds or depth. Theoretically, unsupervised
MVS holds greater potential for novel open-world scenes.
However, this ﬁeld remains nascent, and its performance
trails mature supervised methods. The lack of strong super-
vision complicates reconstruction in non-ideal scenarios.
Moreover, incorporating additional priors and architectures
incurs considerably higher resource consumption than super-
vised methods. Since quality and efﬁciency are critical for
users, well-generalized supervised methods trained on vary-
ing datasets remain the more practical choice for new scene
reconstruction today.
5 Learning-based multi-view stereo
methods using plane-sweeping algorithm
This survey focuses on current mainstream methods—partic-
ularly MVS networks based on the plane-sweeping algorithm
[126], which deﬁnes a series of parallel planes within the ref-
erence camera’s frustum. Each plane corresponds to a depth
hypothesis. The algorithm projects these planes onto other
images and compares pixel similarities to build a cost vol-
ume—a 3D structure that records matching costs for each
pixel under various depth (or disparity) hypotheses. The cost
volume constructed through the plane-sweeping algorithm is
also often simply referred to as the plane-sweeping cost vol-
ume. For more background on the traditional plane-sweeping
MVS algorithm, see Sect. 3.2.4.1. This section introduces the
baseline, MVSNet, and discusses its components and asso-
ciated research advancements.
5.1 Baseline process
Given the camera parameters for all views, a 3D model can
be uniquely projected onto multi-view images and depth
maps. Consequently, learning-based MVS methods recon-
struct the 3D model by predicting per-view depth maps.
While stereo pairs represent a special MVS case enabling
borrowing of cost computation and aggregation from binoc-
ular stereo matching, the inconsistent associations between
the parameters of each pair of multi-view cameras hinder
direct extension of learning-based stereo methods. MVSNet
[156] established a standard end-to-end MVS depth esti-
mation network using the plane-sweeping cost volume. Its
pipeline comprises ﬁve stages (Fig. 10): feature extraction,
cost aggregation, cost volume regularization, depth infer-
ence, and depth map fusion, ultimately generating the 3D
scene model.

the depth dimension yields the ﬁnal probability volume P.

5.1.5 Depth fusion in MVSNet
For depth ﬁltering, MVSNet applies two constraints: pho-

metric consistency constraint removes pixels with conﬁdence
scores below a threshold. Conﬁdence is calculated by sum-

to the estimated depth for each pixel. The geometric con-

across multiple views—speciﬁcally, those that do not meet n-
view consistency (where n is deﬁned based on the scenario).

5.2 Feature extraction
Research on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for
multi-view image feature extraction predates MVSNet [156].
Hartmann et al. [210] designed multiple “Siamese” CNN
branches to extract features from multi-view image patches
determined by plane-sweeping depth hypotheses. However,
their network lacked a direct connection to sub-pixel depth
estimation and its forward pass count was equal to the num-
ber of hypotheses, resulting in high computational cost.
Early works like MVSNet [156], R-MVSNet [157], Point-
MVSNet [186], and Fast-MVSNet [188] typically employed
stacked2Dconvolutionallayerstoextractlow-resolutionfea-
tures from high-resolution images. While these deep features
contain global semantics, they lack local spatial information
inherent in shallow features. Fusing global semantics with
local spatial information helps MVS networks better distin-
guish foreground objects from background scenes. Richer
contextual features enhance matching robustness, driving
widespread interest in context-aware extraction.
Multi-scale hierarchical extraction remains the predom-
inant strategy. DPSNet [211] utilizes a Spatial Pyramid
Pooling (SPP) module for gathering hierarchical contexts.
MVSCRF [212] applies Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN)
[213] to the MVS task. Cascaded methods widely adopt
FPN to extract multi-scale features of progressively increas-
ing resolution, naturally integrating hierarchical contexts
into coarse-to-ﬁne inference. UCS-Net [162] uses a com-
pact 2D U-Net [152] instead of FPN. MVSNet + + [214]
integrates instance normalization within FPN for robustness
to appearance variations. D2HC-RMVSNet [158] proposes
a lightweight Dense Reception Expanded (DRE) module,
applying serial dilated convolutions at different dilation rates
to capture multi-scale contextual information without res-
olution reduction. Recently, ICG-MVSNet [215] performs
feature extraction by a FPN with the help of Intra-View

To address Transformer resource consumption, ET-

epipolar lines: it decomposes reference/source features into
epipolar line pairs via a search algorithm; aggregates non-
local structural information within reference epipolar lines
via self-attention; propagates reference epipolar information

Line-based Transformer (ELT) module, which can encode
the epipolar line match relationship between the reference
and source images to enhance the feature representation and
correlation robustness.
Since the FMT module can only process low-resolution
features, TransMVSNet simply adds its upsampled out-
put to the high-resolution output of FPN in ﬁner stages.
CT-MVSNet [226] introduces a Cross-scale Transformer
(CT) module that enables multi-resolution processing with-
out increasing computation. The CT module’s Adaptive
Matching-aware Transformer (AMT) improves efﬁciency
through adaptive sampling and applies different attention
strategies across scales: cross-attention is prioritized in
coarser stages (where receptive ﬁelds are large and single-
view information is limited), while self-attention is empha-
sized in ﬁner stages (where receptive ﬁelds are small and
textures are rich). The stage conﬁgurations are 1 + 2, 1 + 1,
and 2 + 1 (self + cross attention modules).
Deformable Convolution Networks (DCN) [227] were
also prominent before Transformers. In textureless regions,
DCNs enlarge receptive ﬁelds to gather more semantic infor-
mation; in textured regions, they reduce receptive ﬁelds to
accurately localize spatial structures. AA-RMVSNet [159]
uses DCNs to perform intra-view adaptive aggregation
at original resolution, integrating textures from different
regions and multi-scale information to enhance feature
expressiveness. Masson et al. [228] veriﬁed DCN-enhanced
FPN improves reconstruction in cascaded networks. IB-
MVSNet [229] enhances source features along epipolar lines
via DCNs. TransMVSNet’s [221] Adaptive Receptive Field
(ARF) module, which connects FPN and FMT, uses DCNs
for smooth local-to-global feature transitions, signiﬁcantly
improving performance despite increased computation. To
better capture scene context: GBiNet [169] adds DCN lay-
ers at each FPN scale; DispMVS [179] integrates DCNs
into the decoder parts of both its matching feature extractor
and context-aware feature extractor; PatchMatchNet [184]
applies DCNs in its propagation and evaluation modules for
adaptive processing.
However, learning-based MVS efﬁciency is already lim-
ited by 3D cost volume computation and regularization.
DCNs and Transformers complexities further increase time
and memory costs. Therefore, efﬁciently extracting highly
discriminative and expressive features to improve recon-
struction quality in challenging areas remains crucial for
enhancing method effectiveness and practicality.

Cost metrics based on average, variance, or group-wise
correlation treat all views equally, though this doesn’t reﬂect
real-world conditions. While regularization can implicitly
suppress noise and reduce the impact of low-conﬁdence pix-
els, robustness remains limited in handling invisible regions
and illumination changes. Reconstructing invisible regions
is a key challenge—if not properly addressed during train-
ing, it can interfere with accurate pixel matching and degrade
depth and point cloud quality. Some studies aim to improve
cost volume accuracy and robustness by incorporating prior
knowledge. MVSNet + + [214] builds depth-based atten-
tion feature volumes and introduces a depth reliability mask
as prior. A curriculum learning framework gradually reduces
the mask’s inﬂuence during training and removes it dur-
ing inference. P-MVSNet [194] argues that treating all pixel
pairs equally in its pixel-wise Matching Conﬁdence Volume
(MCV) can be harmful. It instead aggregates the pixel-wise
MCV into a patch-wise MCV and learns separate func-
tions for aggregating intra-patch and inter-patch matching
information to prioritize reference pixels. CT-MVSNet [226]
integrates coarse global semantics into cost volumes in ﬁner
stages via a Dual-Feature Guided Aggregation (DFGA) mod-
ule, improving global and local feature perception.
Most studies employ an additional weight learning net-
work to assign view weights and fuse feature or similarity
volumes into a uniﬁed cost volume through weighted sum-
mation. Attention mechanisms also play a key role in cost
aggregation. AttMVS [233] improves upon P-MVSNet’s
patch-wise MCV by using average-pooled channel descrip-
tors to compute variance as channel-wise statistics of local
scene context, and a SE module [218] generates channel-wise
weighted attention to enhance the cost volume, combining
image consistency and contextual cues for better matching
robustness. PVA-MVSNet [234] introduces a module to learn
view weights and computes the cost volume as a weighted
sum of potential cost volumes. It provides two attention-
based adaptive aggregation methods: the pixel-wise one uses
2D attention maps that encode spatial saliency, shared across
depth planes; the voxel-wise one learns a 3D weight map that
assigns weights per voxel, increasing parameters but signif-
icantly improving performance.
AA-RMVSNet [159] builds an inter-view adaptive mod-
ule that generates pixel-wise self-attention maps for each
potential cost volume as view weights. PatchMatchNet [184]
predicts pixel-wise weights using a shallow network. Later
methods such as IterMVS [180] and GoMVS [235] adopt
a similar strategy: they ﬁrst transform the similarity vol-
ume into 2D space, aggregate local information and reduce
channels using a lightweight 2D CNN, then transform the
output back into a 3D weight volume, apply softmax along
thedepthdimension,andtakethemaximumvaluetocompute
pixel-wise weights. In contrast, GBi-Net [169] applies a 3D
CNN directly on potential cost volumes to predict pixel-wise
weights. TransMVSNet [221] ﬁrst computes group-wise cor-
relation similarity volumes with only one group, then also
uses a shallow 3D CNN to generate a pixel-wise weight
map for each volume, capturing the maximum correla-
tion along the depth dimension. Recently, ICG-MVSNet
[215] follows the common weighted-correlation-similarity-
based paradigm, but additionally proposes a lightweight
scheme—Cross-View Aggregation (CVA)—to leverage both
current and previous stage correlations during its ﬁner cas-
caded stages.
Transformers [220] can be applied to cost aggregation.
By treating the reference feature as the query vector and
source feature volumes as key vectors, the inner product com-
putes similarity volumes that act as value vectors. Based on
this, a cross-attention mechanism computes view weights to
aggregate similarity volumes, focusing on consistent pixels
across views. MVSTER [236] ﬁrst applied this idea to MVS
cost aggregation, using epipolar geometry to establish long-
range 3D associations only along epipolar lines for efﬁciency.
GeoMVSNet [193] directly adopts this epipolar transformer
for cost aggregation.

The aforementioned weights implicitly encode visibil-
ity, while some methods model it explicitly. PVSNet [163]
applies a 3D U-Net to each potential cost volume for initial

from visible views. Recently, ETV-MVS [225] proposes a
novel Supervised Visibility Estimation (SVE) module that
estimates high-precision visibility maps and implements
supervision by generating the ground truth visibility maps.
Experiments show that addressing invisibility during cost
aggregation improves the performance of MVS networks.
5.4 Cost volume regularization
Cost volume regularization powerfully ﬁlters the cost volume
throughCNNs, implicitlyeliminatingnoisegeneratedbyfac-
tors violating the consistency assumption. It is crucial for
accurate depth inference and high-quality 3D reconstruction.
Consequently, many works enhance the 3D CNN regulariza-
tion network in MVSNet [156].
MVSNet + + [214] leverages multi-scale information
from its 3D U-Net decoder for additional ﬁltering. FADE
[217] employs an autoencoder to compute Common and Pri-
vate Features, learning common features between reference
and source views to generate a denoised cost volume. Luo
et al. [194] argued that plane-swept volumes are inherently
anisotropic, unlike the approximately isotropic cost volumes
used in other methods. Therefore, in P-MVSNet’s hybrid
regularization network, they apply two types of anisotropic
convolutions along the spatial and depth dimensions. In
their later work, AttMVS [233], an attention mechanism
is integrated into hierarchical cost regularization: a Ray
Attention Module (RAM) iteratively combines the regular-
ized high-level cost volume with the unregularized low-level
one, guided by ray weighting maps that represent differ-
ences between adjacent-level cost volumes. DSC-MVSNet
[195] combines depth-wise separable convolutions and a
3D-Attention module to enable effective aggregation of
important information during regularization.
Convolutional units in regularization networks typically
perform local aggregation only. Conditional Random Fields
(CRF) constrain pixel-wise predictions using global proper-
ties. MVSCRF [212] adds a CRF network after its regulariza-
tion module, with a multi-scale extension to incorporate more
global information. BP-MVSNet [164] integrates a Belief
Propagation layer, adapted for multi-scale sub-pixel MVS
estimation, as a CRF inference module during regulariza-
tion.
Wu et al. [235] noted that 3 × 3 × 3 convolutional kernels
correspond to local regions where depth distribution is likely
non-uniform and geometric inconsistency may occur. Simply
increasing kernel size is inefﬁcient due to resource limits. To
address this, Go-MVSNet propagates neighborhood costs by
integratinglocalgeometricsmoothnesswithsurfacenormals.
First, it estimates surface normals via monocular normal
estimation.Then,itdesignsaGeometricallyConsistentProp-
agation (GCP) module that uses normals to compute depth
hypothesis correspondences within the pixel neighborhood
and propagates adjacent costs to that pixel. Finally, convo-
lution ensures local smoothness. The GCP and convolution
are combined into a geometrically consistent operator that
replaces the standard convolution block in a 3D CNN.
3D CNNs consume signiﬁcantly more runtime and mem-
ory resources than other pipeline stages. Therefore, many
studies focus on designing lightweight regularization net-
works while maintaining strong noise ﬁltering capabilities.
NP-CVP-MVSNet [174] applies a sparse convolutional net-
work to process sparse cost volumes at coarser stages,
preserving rigid geometric spatial relationships and avoiding
spatial ambiguity. GeoMVSNet [193] innovates by con-
structing a geometry-aware pyramid with varying sparsity
rates from a coarse probability volume using pooling layers.
This pyramid is embedded as a 3D position map into reg-
ularization networks at ﬁner stages for geometric guidance.
Like MVSTER [236], GeoMVSNet pre-integrates 3D asso-
ciation information into the cost volume through complex
operations, allowing the removal of depth dimension encod-
ing in the 3D CNN for efﬁciency: kernel size can be reduced
from 3 × 3 × 3 to 3 × 3 × 1.
Recurrent methods using 2D RNNs for cost volume reg-
ularization and cascaded methods that regularize partial cost
volumes were discussed in detail in Sect. 4.1 and are not
repeated here.
5.5 Depth inference
For deep learning networks, loss function design critically
affects convergence speed, learning effectiveness, and infer-
ence performance. In depth map-based MVS networks, it is
closely tied to the form of depth representation.
Most works follow MVSNet [156], using uniform depth
sampling with soft argmin [208] to regress depth from
the probability volume and L1 loss for training. PVA-
MVSNet [234] and CIDER [232] both sample hypotheses

Unlike the regression representation above, recurrent

to measure the distance between predicted and ground truth

Classiﬁcation representation can directly constrain the
probability volume during training, but argmax lacks sub-
pixel accuracy due to its discrete nature. Although soft
argmin in depth regression allows continuous depth values,
the network’s indirect learning of the cost volume often leads
to overﬁtting toward a strictly uni-modal distribution. In real-
world scenes under non-ideal conditions, depth distributions
are typically multi-modal, deviating from this assumption.
Peng et al. [238] proposed a uniﬁcation representation in
UniMVSNet. Unlike MVSNet’s use of softmax, UniMVS-
Net computes the probability volume from a latent one using
a sigmoid operator and selects the best depth hypothesis
based on the highest probability. Instead of using one-hot
encoding as in classiﬁcation, the corresponding label is a
continuous value indicating proximity to the ground truth,
deﬁnedasthecomplementofanoffset.Depthestimationwith
sub-pixel accuracy is then achieved by performing regres-
sion within an interval around the selected depth hypothesis,
using the sum of product of the interval length and the off-
set. Similarly, IterMVS [180] introduces a hybrid strategy:
ﬁrst identifying the index with the highest probability, then
computing depth as the expectation within a local inverse
depth range around that index, achieving both sub-pixel accu-
racy and robustness to multi-modal distributions. Although
the uniﬁcation representation is theoretically optimal, exper-
iments in GeoMVSNet [193] show that classiﬁcation better
suits its model, as regression and uniﬁcation representations
tend to get stuck in local optima early in training. Therefore,
the choice of depth representation should be based on the
characteristics of the method.
Notably, UniMVSNet and IterMVS only supervise the
depth at the ﬁnest stage, while earlier stages supervise
the probability distribution or conﬁdence maps. Identifying
appropriate ground truth and loss functions for these distri-
butions remains a key challenge. MSCVP-MVSNet [172]
addresses this by applying a stereo focal loss in Stage 2,
using a uni-modal reference distribution to constrain the
probability volume and reduce errors from multi-modal dis-
tributions.NP-CVP-MVSNet[174]approximatestheground
truth distribution using the normalized histogram of the
corresponding high-resolution depth patches, and trains the
network using binary cross-entropy.
Recent studies have focused on the problem of depth
multi-modal distributions. IterMVS, MSCVP-MVSNet, and
NP-CVP-MVSNet all observe that probability distributions
become dispersed and multi-modal at boundaries and in
challenging or invisible regions. Without a uni-modal con-
straint like that in MSCVP-MVSNet, and by only supervising
the depth computed under a uni-modal assumption, the true
multi-modality may cause prediction bias. GeoMVSNet fur-
ther proposes modeling the full scene distribution using a
Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), indicating most scenes
are well described by K = 1or2, with only a few requiring
K ≥3. Since GeoMVSNet accurately models the ground
truth distribution, it directly applies the Kullback–Leibler
(K–L) divergence as the loss function—a choice that per-
formed poorly in NP-CVP-MVSNet.
Some works introduce additional supervision constraints.
MVSNet++[214]addsconsistencyconstraintsbetweenthe
reference view and warped source views at both image and
feature levels, in addition to depth supervision, to mitigate
the effects of occlusion, illumination, and structural varia-
tions. AttMVS [233] introduces a spatial depth gradient loss.
RobustMVS [239] introduces a Depth-Clustering-guided
Whitening (DCW) loss to enhance multi-view feature con-
sistency by decorrelating viewpoint-speciﬁc styles using
depth-based geometric priors, with minor modiﬁcations to
the MVS network.
As for the issue of further reﬁning the depth after depth
inference, it has been discussed in detail earlier in Sect. 4.1.3
and will not be reiterated here.
5.6 Depth fusion
Most learning-based MVS methods follow MVSNet [156]
or CasMVSNet [161] for depth fusion. Both algorithms
rely on ﬁltering criteria to eliminate erroneous predictions.
Photometric consistency ﬁlters out depth values with low
peak conﬁdence. Geometric consistency removes depths that
violate the multi-view consistency assumption: inconsistent
pixels have reprojection points far from the original posi-
tions, and their depth values differ signiﬁcantly. Adjusting
threshold parameters allows a balance between reconstruc-
tion accuracy and completeness, leading to optimal results.
To improve large-scale scene reconstruction, D2HC-
RMVSNet [158] introduced a dynamic consistency checking
algorithm that evaluates depth reliability by combining
photometric consistency and geometric cycle consistency,
ﬁltering out incorrect estimations. This strategy has since
been adopted and further developed in later works.

In the MVS pipeline, depth fusion operates independently
from the depth prediction sub-pipeline. Most methods have
focused on improving the depth prediction accuracy of end-
to-end networks. However, these approaches carry a potential
pitfall: the ultimate evaluation metric for MVS methods is
the quality of the reconstructed point cloud, which does not
always correlate directly with depth map accuracy. Recently,
a few studies have begun integrating depth fusion geometry
into the depth prediction network to improve reconstruction
quality by enhancing reconstruction integrity.
Ye et al. [240] showed that even with the same depth
prediction error, depth maps with different geometric struc-
tures can produce fused point clouds of notably different
quality. Their D-MVSNet reduces interpolation errors in the
depth fusion stage by inferring depth maps with oscillat-
ing depth planes. These maps align with an ideal geometric
model based on saddle-shaped cells, where predicted depths
oscillate above and below the true surface. To achieve this,
D-MVSNet predicts dual depths for each pixel and uses a
checkerboard selection strategy along with a novel loss func-
tion to constrain the geometric shape of depth maps. Vats
et al. [241] noted that traditional MVS methods rely heavily
on photometric and geometric consistency constraints, while
learning-based methods typically apply them only for error
ﬁltering during depth fusion. Their GC-MVSNet explic-
itly introduces a geometric consistency loss that penalizes
inconsistent pixels, signiﬁcantly accelerating the learning
process by reducing training iterations to nearly half that of
other methods. Speciﬁcally, GC-MVSNet learns geometric
cues at three scales: at each scale, a Multi-view Geometric
Consistency (GC) module checks consistency across source
views using the reference view’s depth estimation and gener-
ates pixel-wise penalties. These penalties are combined with
depth errors estimated by cross-entropy loss to form the ﬁnal
training loss.
How to effectively combine depth prediction and fusion
is a promising direction for learning-based MVS meth-
ods. While existing works provide unique schemes—such
as oscillating planes and explicit geometric penalties—their
optimality requires further validation. This area is meaning-
fulandstillinearlydevelopment,withampleroomforfurther
exploration.
6 Open-source algorithms and datasets
High-quality datasets are essential for advancing learning-
based multi-view 3D reconstruction. The diversity of scenes
and the completeness of their ground truth data directly
affect algorithm performance. Besides, the rapid advance-
ment of learning-based MVS has been signiﬁcantly propelled
by the widespread availability of open-source implementa-
tions. These resources not only facilitate reproducibility and
fair comparison but also provide deep insights into practical
algorithm design, greatly lowering the barrier to entry for
new researchers.
6.1 Overview
Most current supervised deep learning methods rely on
ground truth depth maps for training. However, the standard
evaluation metric for MVS is the quality of a reconstructed
3D point cloud. Since plane-sweeping algorithms, the foun-
dation of many learning-based MVS methods, are sensitive
to inaccuracies in camera calibration, multi-view image col-
lections without precisely calibrated camera parameters are
generally unsuitable for training MVS networks.

ETH3D [245] is a comprehensive benchmark for SLAM
and stereo vision, often considered one of the most challeng-
ing datasets for MVS due to its prevalence of white walls,
textureless regions, and reﬂective surfaces. It comprises 25
high-resolution and 10 low-resolution scenes. Ground truth
point clouds were obtained via high-resolution laser scan-

consistency optimization. Although it offers an online evalu-
ation server, its focus on extreme scenarios limits its use for
training MVS models. It is mainly used in a few studies as a
supplementary test to evaluate robustness under challenging
conditions.
In contrast, DTU, T&T, and BlendedMVS have become
core benchmarks due to their representative scenes and
mature evaluation protocols. Most recent MVS networks are
ﬁrsttrainedandevaluatedontheDTUdataset,thenﬁne-tuned

DTU model on T&T. The following sections describe these

6.5 Open-source algorithms
The ﬁeld of learning-based MVS has ﬂourished on the solid
foundation laid by prior work, a cornerstone of which is the
prevalentcultureofopen-sourcingcode.Followingtheexam-
ple set by MVSNet [156], the vast majority of subsequent
research with key innovations released their code on plat-
forms like GitHub. These repositories typically include code
for training and evaluation pipelines, and often extend to
utilities for pre- and post-processing techniques in 3D recon-
struction, offering invaluable practical guidance beyond the
manuscript contents. This review sincerely appreciates these
contributions.
This survey has categorized learning-based MVS from
architectural perspectives—comparing supervised methods
(e.g., recurrent, cascaded, and progressive reﬁnement archi-
tectures) and unsupervised approaches—and has analyzed
advancements across the ﬁve key stages of the MVSNet
baseline. Notably, most of the discussed methods come with
open-source code, which we have listed in Table 2.
The community owes a particular debt to founda-
tional works like MVSNet, which not only introduced a
robust learning-based framework but also provided essential
resources such as depth and mask ground truths for the DTU
dataset, alongside scripts for extracting camera parameters
via COLMAP-SfM [80]. A key development was Xiaoyang
Guo’s unofﬁcial version, MVSNet-PyTorch, which has since
become the de facto codebase for a large number of subse-
quent studies. For instance, CasMVSNet [161] included two
fusion strategies from both MVSNet and MVSNet-PyTorch.
Since depth-based MVS methods decouple depth predic-
tion and depth fusion, a complete implementation necessarily
includes the fusion module. The implementation details and
parameter settings for fusion vary across different works and
are crucial for achieving optimal performance. Adjusting
theseparameters allows for atrade-off betweenAccuracyand
Completeness on the DTU dataset, enabling researchers to
either achieve the best Overall score or highlight a method’s
strength in one metric. Furthermore, parameter tuning for the
dynamic consistency checking algorithm [158] in the Tanks
and Temples benchmark is critical for achieving high-quality
reconstructions in real-world scenes.
Westronglyencourageresearcherstoconsultandcompare
these various open-source implementations. A productive
strategy is to trace the code backwards through the research
lineage—considering both the horizontal connections (com-
paring different approaches to the same pipeline stage)
and vertical evolution (improvements or extensions of prior
works) highlighted in this survey. Furthermore, gaining a
comprehensive understanding of all listed open-source algo-
rithms is highly beneﬁcial. These works form the cornerstone
of modern MVS networks, and their code often contains
undocumented yet critical engineering insights. For exam-
ple, Vis-MVSNet [237] provided a template for validating
depth map quality on the BlendedMVS dataset. Engag-
ing deeply with these resources will undoubtedly provide
valuable knowledge for developing new and effective MVS
systems.
6.6 Implementation details
Due to variations in experimental setups across differ-
ent methods, this review summarizes key considerations
commonly applicable to the training and evaluation of
learning-based MVS methods. Readers are encouraged to
consult these guidelines alongside speciﬁc literature to select
suitable parameters for their own projects. Standard experi-
mental conﬁgurations typically include: NVIDIA TITAN or
GeForce series GPUs for computation and PyTorch [246] as
the primary deep learning framework. When setting up the
environment, it is essential to ﬁrst determine the operating
system and GPU, then select compatible CUDA and cuDNN
versions accordingly, and ﬁnally install a matching PyTorch
version.
6.7 Training settings
For training on the DTU dataset, input images are typically
resized to 640 × 512 pixels, with the number of input views
set to N = 3or5. To ensure fair comparisons, most meth-
ods follow the view selection strategy from MVSNet [156],
which selects the source images according to the scores based
on angular diversity relative to the reference image. Camera
parameters should be scaled and cropped to match the input
image resolution. The depth range (dmin, dmax) is typically
set to (425mm, 921mm). For cascaded or progressive reﬁne-
ment architectures, the number of depth planes in coarse
estimation is typically D = 48, while recurrent methods
often directly use D = 256. Multi-stage or multi-prior meth-
ods usually assign proportionally scaled weights to different
loss components to emphasize critical objectives. Training
typically spans 16 epochs, with a batch size of 2 or 4 on
a single GPU, and multi-GPU parallelization is commonly
used to accelerate training. When using RMSProp [247] opti-
mizer, the learning rate is typically decayed by a factor of 0.9
every two epochs. However, most recent methods employ the
Adam [248] optimizer with [β1, β2] = [0.9, 0.999] and an
initial learning rate of 0.001, halved at the 8th, 10th, and
12th epochs. Notably, modern approaches often incorporate
a warm-up phase during the ﬁrst few hundred iterations, grad-
ually increasing the learning rate from a small value to 0.001
to improve convergence stability and prevent divergence. For
ﬁne-tuningontheBlendedMVSdataset,asimilarexperimen-
tal setup is adopted, with 768×576 image resolution and the
corresponding depth range from its camera parameter ﬁle.

6.8 Evaluation settings
Unless an ablation experiment is conducted, evaluations on
theDTUdatasettypicallyuse N = 5views.Forrecurrentand
cascaded methods, the number of depth hypotheses remains
unchanged during evaluation, while progressive reﬁnement
methods increase the number to D = 96 for improved
accuracy. Most recent learning-based MVS methods can pro-
cess full-resolution images directly: 1600 × 1152 for DTU,
1920 × 1056 or 2048 × 1056 for the T&T intermediate set,
and 768 × 576 for BlendedMVS. However, the depth maps
produced by different methods vary in size, having reso-
lutions of 1/4, 1/2, or full size of the input. While larger
depth maps generally improve point cloud quality in high-
resolution scenarios, many approaches achieve high-quality
3D reconstructions using depth maps at 1/4 or 1/2 resolu-
tion, provided they maintain sufﬁcient precision.
7 Performance evaluation and analysis
The previous section introduces several widely used datasets
for MVS research. This section summarizes the experimental
results of representative MVS networks on two mainstream
benchmarks: DTU and T&T.
7.1 Comparison on the DTU dataset
Table 3 compares the quantitative performance of various
MVS methods on the DTU dataset in terms of reconstruc-
tion accuracy (Acc.), completeness (Comp.), and overall
quality (Overall) (lower is better). Learning-based meth-
ods achieve substantially better performance than traditional
ones like COLMAP [134], thanks to improved feature extrac-
tion and visibility-aware cost aggregation, underscoring the
effectiveness of deep learning. Among them, cascaded meth-
ods excel due to their multi-stage optimization: MVSFormer
+ + [223] achieves the best Overall score of 0.281 mm,
a 39.18% improvement over MVSNet [156] (0.462 mm),
followed closely by ETV-MVS [225] (0.282 mm). Among
progressive reﬁnement methods, those using implicit neu-
ral representation—ACINN-MVSNet [196] (0.335 mm) and
TTINS-MVSNet [197] (0.308 mm)—perform well. TTINS-
MVSNet is currently the best in this category, showing a
33.33% improvement over MVSNet.
A breakdown of the metrics reveals further insights: In
accuracy, the traditional method Gipuma [133] (0.283 mm)
remains the best, though cascaded method CVPMVS-
Net [167] (0.296 mm) and progressive reﬁnement method
ACINR-MVSNet [196] (0.306 mm) are close. For com-
pleteness, GoMVS [235] leads with 0.227 mm. Despite
the risk of error propagation, cascaded methods dominate
the top ﬁve in Overall quality, proving their effectiveness.
TTINS-MVSNet [197] (0.308 mm) shows the potential
of progressive reﬁnement. A trade-off between accuracy
and completeness is common: for example, GoMVS [235]
sacriﬁces some accuracy for a breakthrough in complete-
ness, while TTINS-MVSNet [197] focuses on completeness,
accepting moderate accuracy loss to outperform ACINN-
MVSNet [196] in overall quality.
Several key trends emerge from a deeper analysis: First,
the Transformer architecture signiﬁcantly improves perfor-
mance. Transformer-based methods show marked improve-
ments in Comp. and Overall, consistently ranking near the
top [197, 221–226, 236]. Second, implicit neural represen-
tations in ACINR-MVSNet [196], TTINS-MVSNet [197],
and RC-MVSNet [204] has proven effective, pointing to
new directions for MVS. Third, D-MVSNet [240] and GC-
MVSNet [241] incorporate depth fusion geometry within
the prediction network, improving the coherence of depth
map-based methods and yielding excellent results. Finally,
unsupervised methods are narrowing the gap with supervised
learning. CL-MVSNet [206] and DIV-MVS [207] surpass

Table 4 shows the quantitative performance of various MVS
methods on the intermediate set of the T&T dataset (higher

leaderboard. Early methods evaluated DTU-trained models
directly, while more recent approaches usually ﬁne-tuned
DTU-pretrained models on BlendedMVS before testing on
T&T, unless the study aimed to demonstrate robustness
through dataset differences.
First, many observations from the DTU comparison
remain valid: effective learning-based MVS methods gen-
erally exhibit strong generalization and robustness, and
performance continues to improve with advances in research.
However, DTU benchmark scores and generalization are
not strongly correlated. For example, Efﬁ-MVS [181] and
MVSTER [236] achieved the same Overall score on DTU
(0.313 mm), but Efﬁ-MVS generalizes less effectively
(Mean: 56.88) than MVSTER (Mean: 60.92). Similarly, GC-
MVSNet [241], ranked 6th on DTU, does not appear in the
toptenontheT&Tintermediateset.Incontrast,Vis-MVSNet
[237], despite having the lowest Overall score among cas-
caded methods on DTU (0.365 mm), performs reasonably
well on occlusion-rich T&T scenes due to its explicit visibil-
ity modeling, achieving a mid-level Mean score.
Cascaded methods still lead the leaderboard: the top three
DTU performers [223, 225, 235] also rank in the top three
on the T&T intermediate set, conﬁrming the strength of this
architecture. Their advantage comes from a coarse-to-ﬁne
strategy that naturally produces full-resolution depth maps,
making them well-suited for large-scale T&T scenes. Recent
recurrent methods [158, 159], which trade computation time
for memory efﬁciency, are also effective for high-resolution
images. Early unsupervised methods [189, 199–201], with-
out strong supervision, had difﬁculty handling T&T’s real-
world variations in lighting and texture. However, since

results comparable to classical cascaded methods. However,

8 Summary and future directions
3D reconstruction is a fundamental task in computer
vision, converting the real world into computable 3D mod-
els with wide-ranging applications. Among existing tech-
niques, multi-view 3D reconstruction has become a leading
approach, valued for its easy data acquisition, low cost, high
automation, realistic results, and strong environmental adapt-
ability. The standard pipeline includes three key stages: SfM,
MVS, and surface reconstruction. Within this framework,
MVS generates dense 3D point clouds and directly impacts
the accuracy, completeness, and efﬁciency of the ﬁnal model,
making it the critical bottleneck in the process.
Despite decades of progress, traditional MVS meth-
ods still perform poorly in reconstructing complex outdoor
scenes. In terms of reconstruction quality, they struggle with
challenging areas such as low-texture regions, reﬂective sur-
faces (non-Lambertian surfaces), and repetitive patterns, and
are highly sensitive to illumination variations and occlu-
sions. Regarding efﬁciency, the process of generating a dense
pointclouddemandssubstantialcomputationalresourcesand
memory, which ultimately restricts the scale and granularity
of the reconstruction.

The rise of deep learning has brought transformative
innovations to MVS. Learning-based methods have outper-
formed traditional approaches in accuracy and efﬁciency
when reconstructing complex scenes, representing the cur-
rent state-of-the-art. However, this review shows that these
methods still face major challenges and offer room for
improvement in the following key aspects:
(1) Robust and Reliable Overall Architecture Current main-
stream architectures each exhibit limitations. Recurrent
models face time bottlenecks at high resolutions; cas-
caded architectures offer efﬁciency and high quality
but suffer from inherent error propagation; progressive
reﬁnement methods avoid these problems yet require
more efﬁcient and better-designed depth reﬁnement
modules—a direction still underexplored.
(2) Feature Extraction Enriched with Information Although
deep CNN features convey high-level semantics, their
representational capacity remains limited for complex
scenes. Developing more powerful and efﬁcient feature
extraction networks to capture discriminative and robust
visual information is essential for improving reconstruc-
tion quality.
(3) Visibility-Aware Cost Aggregation As the bridge
between 2D images and 3D structure, cost aggregation
remains sensitive to occlusions and viewpoint changes
when visibility is not considered. Instead of relying on
later cost volume regularization, there is a pressing need
for aggregation methods that explicitly integrate visibil-
ity information.
(4) Systematic and Efﬁcient Reconstruction Pipeline Most
methods decouple depth prediction from depth fusion,
focusing predominantly on improving per-view depth
maps. However, MVS evaluation ultimately depends on
point cloud quality, which does not directly correlate
with depth accuracy. Introducing geometric cues rel-
evant to fusion within the depth prediction stage—or
even building end-to-end trainable systems—could
substantially improve overall performance. Moreover,
efﬁciency remains a major impediment: performance-
enhancing modules often introduce heavy computa-
tional and memory costs. With growing demands for
high-resolution, large-scale, and real-time reconstruc-
tion (e.g., from drones, mobile devices, or city-scale
scenes), designing lightweight and efﬁcient architec-
tures without sacriﬁcing quality is essential for practical
deployment.
Despite their promise, learning-based MVS methods still
face critical obstacles in robustness, generalization, and real-
world applicability. The ﬁeld remains highly active, with the
transition from theoretical advances to large-scale practical
applications being particularly challenging. Notably, many
commercial systems still rely on traditional non-learning
algorithms, partly due to the limited robustness of deep mod-
els on unseen data and the scarcity of large-scale, diverse
training datasets. Most current learning-based methods are
trained and evaluated on lab-controlled or synthetic data,
and merely testing generalization on real-world benchmarks
does not sufﬁce for model improvement. More efforts like
RobustMVS [239] are needed, which focuses on domain gen-
eralization in MVS, introducing a challenging benchmark
with only single-source domain training.
Recent advances in machine learning and computer vision
show strong potential for further improving MVS meth-
ods. A key example is the integration of Transformers
[220] into MVS networks. More broadly, 3D reconstruc-
tion and view synthesis have increasingly overlapped in the
deep learning era. Inspired by implicit neural representa-
tions, researchers have developed various neural rendering
techniques, with Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [249] gain-
ing the most attention and inspiring numerous follow-up
studies. MVSNeRF [250] incorporates multi-view infor-
mation into NeRF to enhance generalization and suggests
its future potential for unsupervised depth estimation. RC-
MVSNet [204], proposed by Chang et al., combines implicit
neural representations with unsupervised stereo vision. How-
ever, applying 3D implicit representations to supervised
MVS methods remains challenging, and the lack of cross-
domain datasets limits progress. ACINR-MVSNet [196] and
TTINS-MVSNet [197] adapted ideas from 2D image super-
resolution to enable supervised MVS methods to beneﬁt from
implicit neural representations. Future research could further
explore this direction, advancing the development of uniﬁed
models in 3D reconstruction.
In summary, promising future research directions include:
(1) Architecture and Pipeline Optimization For architec-
ture, further investigation is needed to either develop
powerful and efﬁcient knowledge-driven depth reﬁne-
ment modules in progressive reﬁnement methods or ﬁnd
effective ways to suppress error propagation in cascaded
approaches. For pipeline, feature extraction enriched
with information and visibility-aware cost aggrega-
tion are crucial for improving reconstruction quality.
Advances in these areas will enhance the model’s abil-
ity to reconstruct challenging and invisible regions,
addressing longstanding issues in traditional MVS.
(2) Intrinsic Task Optimization and Extrinsic Technology
Integration Further exploration of the MVS task, par-
ticularly by incorporating depth fusion geometry into
depth prediction, can resolve the inconsistency between
depth map-based training and point cloud evaluation,
thereby improving process coherence. At the same time,

integrating advanced techniques from other ﬁelds—-
such as Transformers and implicit neural representa-
tions—can advance MVS methods and warrants deeper
investigation.
(3) Robustness Improvement and Dataset Development
Current algorithms struggle to reconstruct unseen open-
world scenes. In addition to the previously men-
tioned improvements, transfer learning can be applied
to enhance model robustness. Although unsupervised
methods reduce reliance on annotated data and improve
robustness, their accuracy still falls short compared to
supervised approaches. Therefore, there is an urgent
need to build real-world 3D scene datasets that cover
various scales, texture complexities, and environmen-
tal conditions, and provide both depth maps and point
cloud ground truth to support the training and evaluation
of MVS networks.
(4) Engineering Deployment and Efﬁciency Improvement
Promoting the deployment of theoretical advances
on intelligent mobile devices—such as smartphones
and drones—is essential for deepening the discussion
on enhancing monocular camera capabilities through
deep learning. MVS tasks inherently require signiﬁcant
resources,whichcanbefurtherstrainedbyenhancement
techniques like Transformers. This may hinder real-time
applications, such as virtual reality. Therefore, in addi-
tion to the previously mentioned research directions,
future work must also focus on model compression and
optimization algorithms to improve reconstruction efﬁ-
ciency and support practical implementation.
9 Conclusion
This survey provides a comprehensive overview of recent
advances in depth map-based Multi-View Stereo (MVS) net-
works, with a focus on learning-based methods that use
plane-sweeping algorithms. By organizing 3D reconstruc-
tion techniques systematically, the review highlights the
importance of multi-view 3D reconstruction and offers back-
ground through a summary of traditional approaches. A
key contribution is the two-dimensional analytical frame-
work—evaluating methods by both architecture type and
their role in the pipeline. The survey encompasses recurrent
and cascaded architectures within supervised MVS and unsu-
pervisedapproaches,andalsooffersasystematicsummaryof
progressive reﬁnement architectures. Furthermore, it reviews
recent developments aligned with the ﬁve stages of a typi-
cal MVS pipeline: feature extraction, cost aggregation, cost
volume regularization, depth inference, and depth fusion.
Finally, commonly used datasets and evaluation metrics are
introduced, and experimental results for most methods are
presented in a structured, comparative manner across two
primary benchmarks.
It is noteworthy that MVS networks integrating depth
fusion geometry into end-to-end depth prediction, as well as
those based on Transformers and implicit neural representa-
tions, have shown outstanding performance and promising
potential. Although current deep learning methods still face
challenges in architecture design, feature extraction, cost
aggregation, and efﬁciency improvement, the progress made
so far has laid a solid foundation. Future advances will likely
come from cross-domain integration with other areas of
computer vision and the development of diverse datasets.
Importantly, efﬁciently deploying high-performance MVS
models on intelligent mobile devices—such as smartphones
anddrones—isavaluableresearchdirectionthatwilladvance
the use of deep learning to enhance monocular camera capa-
bilities.
