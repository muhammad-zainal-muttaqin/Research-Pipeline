A B S T R A C T
Accurate estimation of vine canopy and berry cluster volumes is essential for precision viticulture, as it supports 
better vineyard management, yield prediction, and resource allocation. Traditional methods, such as manual 
measurements or expensive sensor-based systems, are often inaccessible to small and mid-scale growers. This 
study explores the use of smartphone-based 3D imaging and advanced machine learning techniques as an 
affordable and accessible alternative for estimating wine grape canopy and berry cluster volumes. In this study, 
point cloud data was collected using an iPhone 14 Pro Max to capture the spatial structure of grape canopies and 
berry clusters. Two separate datasets were used to evaluate canopy and cluster volumes independently, ensuring 
comprehensive analysis and validation. Canopy volume estimation involved segmentation using the Gradient 
Boosting Classifier, followed by computation of 3D point volumes, achieving an RMSE of 0.135 m³ and 98% 
classification accuracy for canopy point clouds. Berry clusters were segmented using YOLO11, and 3D point 
clouds were reconstructed using Structure from Motion (SfM) to create watertight meshes. Cluster volumes 
validated by water‑displacement ground truth yielded an RMSE of 46.47cm3. These findings demonstrate the 
potential of smartphone-based solutions to support precision viticulture through accurate estimation of vine 
canopies and berry clusters, which is expected to enhance vineyard productivity and berry quality.
1. Introduction
In wine grape production, accurately assessing vine canopy and 
berry cluster characteristics is fundamental to optimizing yields and 
maintaining quality. Among these traits, canopy and cluster volume are 
important because they serve as indicators of production potential and 
are key inputs for yield prediction models. Accurate yield estimation 
enables growers to allocate resources efficiently, plan harvests effec­
tively, and make informed management decisions in vineyards. Specif­
ically, vine canopy structure and volume are crucial indicators of vine 
growth and health, and provide information about the distribution of 
leaves, shoots, and clusters, influencing the vineyard’s microclimate. 
This information serves as a key indicator of water and nutrient status, 
as well as overall crop yield [1]. This correlation between canopy 
structure and productivity highlights its importance as an essential 
metric for assessing vine vigor and potential crop yield. Similarly, grape 
cluster volume provides a direct measure of crop-load in each vine that 
can be used to predict total yield. This information aids in crop pro­
duction management, harvesting and post-harvest planning (e.g., 
optimal distribution of labor, equipment, and storage facilities).
A variety of approaches have been developed for canopy and cluster 
volume estimation in wine grapes. Traditional techniques such as 
manual measurements, destructive sampling, and ground-based sensors 
have been used to estimate canopy and cluster volumes, but these 
methods can be labor-intensive, time-consuming, and limited in scal­
ability. Destructive approaches also compromise future productivity and 
prevent repeated measurement across growth stages. These challenges 
highlight the need for methods that are accurate, repeatable, and scal­
able, while remaining practical for vineyard operations.
To overcome these challenges, several sensor-based techniques have 
been explored for estimating canopy and berry cluster volume in wine 
grapes. These can be broadly grouped into LiDAR-based methods, stereo 
vision and photogrammetry, and RGB-D imaging approaches. Among 
these, LiDAR scanners mounted on ground vehicles or UAV platforms 
have been widely applied for canopy volume estimation [2,3]. These 
systems capture detailed 3D reconstructions of canopy geometry like 
height, width, and volume with high precision. However, they are 
expensive, computationally demanding, and require technical expertise

Alongside efforts to estimate canopy volume, several imaging-based 
methods have been developed for grape cluster detection and recon­
struction, particularly using stereovision, photogrammetry, and RGB-D 
systems. Stereo vision approach includes the 3-Demeter Capture meth­
odology proposed by Santos et al. [4], which combined visual odometry 
with stereo cameras to generate 3D models from RGB images. Similarly, 
the system developed by Mirbod et al. [5] used a vehicle-mounted stereo 
camera to capture vineyard images and estimate grape diameters

Photogrammetry-based methods have also been tested; for example, 
Herrero-Huerta et al. [6] reconstructed grape bunches under controlled 
conditions using a Canon camera, though the lack of automated seg­
mentation limited applicability in commercial vineyards. RGB-D and 
alternative reconstruction methods further expanded these efforts. 
Kurtser et al. [7] employed an RGB-D camera on a mobile robot to es­
timate grape cluster size in red varieties using color-based k-means 
clustering and bounding box fitting. However, the performance was 
hindered for green grapes due to foliage color similarity. Xin, Liu, and 
Whitty [8] introduced a stochastic l-system grammar for reconstructing 
Pinot Noir and Merlot clusters, which successfully represented natural 
variability but required extensive parameter tuning and struggled to 
capture fine details, reducing adaptability across varieties and field 
conditions. While these methods advanced research capability, they 
remain difficult to scale to routine vineyard use due to cost, complexity, 
or sensitivity to imaging conditions.
Recently, smartphones have emerged as an accessible and cost- 
effective alternative for small to mid-scale growers. The increasing 
availability of smartphone-based imaging systems integrated with 3D 
sensors offers a new opportunity for precision viticulture. Smartphone- 
based systems like the VitiCanopy App [9] use smartphone images and 
computer vision algorithms to estimate grapevine canopy vigor and 
porosity. Similarly, Ang et al. [10] and Liu, Zeng, and Whitty [11] both 
developed smartphone-based tools for measuring grape berry sizes. 
More advanced approaches include Mask R-CNN–based berry segmen­
tation for growth monitoring and lag-phase detection [12] and smart­
phone ToF-based depth imaging for berry size estimation [13]. 
However,

and cluster reconstruction. Many also relied on calibration objects or 
single-angle captures, which limit their practicality and accuracy in 
real-world vineyards. Overcoming these limitations would require 
multi-angle scanning and advanced 3D reconstruction techniques.
In contrast to prior smartphone-based studies, our method in­
troduces several technical and practical innovations aimed at enabling 
full 3D canopy and cluster volume estimation under real vineyard con­
ditions. The approach presented here combines YOLO11 instance seg­
mentation, Structure-from-Motion (SfM), and Gradient Boosting–based 
canopy classification into a single smartphone-driven workflow, 
enabling full 3D canopy and cluster volume estimation. This combina­
tion allows accurate estimation of both canopy and cluster volumes 
without reliance on calibration objects or controlled imaging setups. 
Beyond these technical aspects, the use of smartphone-based tools is 
designed for practical adoption. Canopy and cluster volume data can be 
collected during routine scouting and linked with vineyard management 
systems to support tasks such as irrigation scheduling, crop load 
balancing, and yield forecasting. Because the method relies on in-hand 
technology already available in smartphones, it can provide growers 
with timely feedback during critical stages of the season.
Addressing the limitations of existing methods, the objective of this 
study is to explore smartphone-based 3D imaging for estimating vine 
canopy and berry cluster volumes with improved accuracy and practi­
cality. Canopy and cluster datasets were reconstructed and compared 
against ground-truth measurements to evaluate the performance of the 
approach under vineyard conditions. This work also illustrates how 
widely available smartphone cameras and embedded 3D sensors can be 
adapted into practical vineyard tools, providing growers with an auto­
mated and scalable option for routine monitoring, yield estimation, and 
management decisions.

2. Methodology
This study utilizes recent 3D imaging systems available in smart­
phone devices and machine learning techniques for estimating grape 
canopy and berry cluster volumes in vineyards. Point cloud data were 
collected using an iPhone 14 Pro Max [14]. For canopy volume esti­
mation, a Gradient Boosting Classifier [15] was trained to segment 
canopies from the background. For berry cluster volume estimation, 
Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [16] technique was applied to reconstruct 
3D point clouds from segmented images. Post-processing steps, such as 
DBSCAN for outlier removal [17] and alpha-shape reconstruction [18], 
were applied for further refining the data, which was essential to 
improve the accuracy of the models used in segmenting and estimating 
volumes of both vine canopies and berry clusters. Fig. 1. illustrates the 
flow diagram for the overall process.
2.1. Data acquisition
Two separate datasets were collected using an iPhone 14 Pro Max 
[14] at the Washington State University experimental vineyard in 
Prosser, WA, during grape harvest season when Chardonnay wine grapes 
(green in color) reached E-L stage 38, a stage in the Eichhorn-Lorenz 
scale indicating fully ripe berries ready for harvest. This E-L scale is 
widely used in viticulture to standardize vine growth stages [19]. The 
iPhone 14 Pro Max was chosen for its advanced imaging capabilities, 
which include a 48MP main camera and LiDAR depth sensing, essential 
for capturing precise 2D images and 3D point cloud data. The LiDAR 
scanner, operating with a wavelength of 940 nm, provides 
high-resolution depth mapping by emitting infrared pulses and 
measuring their time-of-flight. It has a range of up to ~5 m, both indoors 
and outdoors, enabling accurate spatial reconstruction and effective 
integration with the camera system for detailed 3D modeling. Addi­
tionally, the photogrammetry process, which was integrated with the 
smartphone app, captured intricate cluster details by combining images 
from multiple angles, further enhancing the quality of the datasets. 
These features ensured reliable and precise data for analyzing canopy 
and cluster geometry. For canopy datasets, point cloud generation was 
carried out directly on the smartphone, while further processing and 
segmentation were performed on a workstation equipped with 64 GB 
RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU. For cluster datasets, the 
smartphone was used only for multi-angle image acquisition, with point 
cloud reconstruction and segmentation performed on the same 
workstation.
2.1.1. Canopy volume dataset

"Scaniverse” [20] iPhone application. This app utilizes the smartphone’s 
depth sensing and 3D imaging capabilities to reconstruct 3D point 
clouds representing the canopy structures. The app was used to scan 
each vine from multiple angles, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the 
canopy geometry by moving around the vine through the trellis wires. 
This approach captured data from all sides for complete representation 
of the canopy. However, for end users, a more practical approach would 
involve capturing data for half of the canopy from one side and the other 
half from the opposite side, then registering the two 3D point clouds as a

2.1.2. Cluster volume dataset
For cluster volume, the "3D Scanner" app [21] was utilized to capture 
detailed point cloud data of individual grape clusters. This app leverages 
the smartphone’s camera and depth sensors to generate high-resolution 
3D point clouds. To accurately represent the 3D structures of the clus­
ters, the camera was slowly moved around each cluster, capturing im­
ages from various angles and perspectives. This approach ensured wider 
coverage of shape and size of clusters. Images were captured from lateral 
angles and slightly elevated viewpoints, in addition to frontal and 
oblique perspectives, to maximize surface coverage and reduce occlu­
sion. Each cluster was photographed from multiple sides while main­
taining a consistent distance of 20–30 cm between the camera and the 
target. Furthermore, freely hanging clusters with maximum visibility 
were prioritized to ensure complete structural representation, while for 
clusters that were partially enclosed by foliage, additional side views 
were captured to enhance coverage. This acquisition process employed 
photogrammetric principle using Structure-from-Motion (SfM) rather 
than LiDAR, as it provided more detailed point clouds suitable for sub­
sequent segmentation. Point clouds were acquired for a total of 25 freely 
hanging grape clusters from different vines of the same experimental 
vineyard used for the canopy volume dataset, thus creating a dataset for 
cluster volume estimation. For every cluster, at least 30 images were 
taken to allow sufficient feature matching and reconstruction accuracy 
in the photogrammetry process of point cloud generation, using varying 
camera locations and orientations covering most areas of the clusters.
To validate the volume estimation technique, actual cluster volumes 
were measured by removing the clusters from the canopies and 
submerging them in a beaker filled with water to calculate the displaced 
water volume, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The process was repeated three 
times for each cluster, with the average value taken as the true volume. 
For cluster volume data, segmentation using point clouds was chal­
lenging due to the similarity in color between grape clusters and the 
surrounding canopy, as well as the limited availability of annotated 
training data to directly employ machine learning on point clouds. 
Therefore, an alternative methodology was employed for cluster seg­
mentation using YOLO11. A total of 250 RGB images of the clusters were 
annotated using an instance segmentation annotation tool (Roboflow; 
[22]) to train the YOLO11 model to delineate the cluster boundaries and 
separate them from the background. The dataset was split into 80 % 
training, 10 % validation, and 10 % testing to optimize model 
performance.
The dataset primarily consisted of close-up images, with one main 
cluster per image. During annotation, only the primary visible cluster in 
each image was labeled, to avoid including partially occluded or back­
ground clusters. In cases where background clusters were present, they 
were not annotated if they were occluded, as the primary objective was 
to segment the main cluster that would be reconstructed during the 
Structure-from-Motion (SfM) process. This ensured that the segmenta­
tion model was optimized for identifying the key cluster in each image, 
improving accuracy for subsequent 3D point cloud generation.

2.2. Canopy segmentation
To estimate canopy volume, the acquired point cloud data was 
segmented to isolate the grapevine canopy from the background such as 
sky, ground, trunk, cordon and canopies in the background. Then, the 
raw point cloud data was down sampled by factor of 10 to reduce the 
data size and simplify the analysis, making the subsequent processing 
steps more efficient. The down sampled point cloud data was used as 
inputs to the Gradient Boosting Classifier [15], which segmented vine 
canopies from the background in point cloud data. Gradient Boosting 
combines multiple weak models, such as decision trees, into a strong 
predictive system. The algorithm iteratively improves predictions by 
addressing errors from previous models, adding decision trees to mini­
mize these errors.
Gradient Boosting was selected for canopy segmentation because 
vineyard point clouds often exhibit irregular structures and overlapping 
elements, which create nonlinear boundaries between canopy and 
background points. Linear models are not well suited for such data, 
whereas tree-based boosting methods can adaptively partition the 
feature space to capture these complexities. Moreover, Gradient Boost­
ing performs well even when the training dataset is relatively small, 
making it a practical choice given the limited number of annotated 
canopy points available in this study. This balance of accuracy, data 
efficiency, and robustness made Gradient Boosting particularly suitable 
for the canopy segmentation task [15,23]. In each iteration, the model is 
updated as:

To train the Gradient Boosting Classifier, an annotated dataset of 
1000 sample points was prepared, with each point labeled as either 
canopy or background. The training process involved optimizing various 
hyperparameters, such as the number of estimators (decision trees), 
learning rate and maximum depth of the trees to achieve optimal per­
formance. The goal with this model was to maximize the accuracy in 
distinguishing grape canopy points from other background objects/ 
scenes in the point cloud data. The key model parameters were opti­
mized by evaluating a range of values and selecting the combination that 
achieved the highest accuracy. During this process, one parameter was 
varied while the others were held constant to evaluate its specific impact 
on model accuracy. Through this process, the following hyper­
parameters were identified: Number of Estimators set to 100, Learning 
Rate to 0.5, and Maximum Depth to 3. Once the training was completed, 
the model was applied to process down sampled point clouds of all the 
sample vine canopies to extract points representing the canopy surface.
2.3. Cluster segmentation
To achieve precise segmentation of grape clusters from point cloud 
data, a different approach was adopted due to the complex nature of 
grape clusters and the background structure. Unlike vine canopies, 
grape clusters are smaller in size and have a similar color to the sur­
rounding foliage, making direct segmentation challenging. Machine 
learning models, including deep learning techniques, struggled to 
differentiate clusters from the background without a sufficiently large, 
annotated dataset. Given these constraints, image-based instance seg­
mentation was employed prior to 3D reconstruction.
YOLO11, a state-of-the-art instance segmentation model [24], was 
employed to isolate grape clusters directly from vineyard images, 
addressing challenges posed by their small size, irregular structure, and 
frequent occlusion by foliage. As a one-stage detector, YOLO11 jointly 
predicts object locations and class labels in a single step, enabling effi­
cient end-to-end segmentation. Its architecture integrates multi-scale 
feature extraction and attention mechanisms, which enhance detection 
of small objects and improve robustness in cluttered vineyard scenes 
where grape clusters often overlap with leaves and branches. These 
capabilities are particularly valuable in vineyards, where clusters may 
visually resemble surrounding foliage or appear only partially in view. 
Compared to models such as Mask R-CNN, which are computationally 
more demanding, YOLO11 provides similar segmentation accuracy 
while maintaining real-time inference speed, making it a practical and 
effective choice for this study.
The YOLO11n-seg model was implemented in PyTorch and fine- 
tuned from pretrained weights available in the Ultralytics repository 
[24]. This lightweight version was selected to enable fast and efficient 
inference suitable for practical field applications, where scalability and 
processing speed are essential. Because the dataset primarily consisted 
of close-up images containing a single grape cluster with minimal 
background complexity, the model achieved high segmentation accu­
racy even without a large network architecture. To improve general­
ization on the relatively small dataset, data augmentation was applied 
during training, including random horizontal flips, rotations of ±15◦, 
scaling, and brightness/contrast adjustments. Training was then con­
ducted for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16, using the stochastic 
gradient descent (SGD) optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.01. 
Model performance was monitored using mean average precision (mAP) 
on the validation set. Minor hyperparameter variations were explored 
during preliminary experiments, and the final configuration was 
selected based on stability of the validation mAP. Under this configu­
ration, YOLO11 achieved an mAP@50 of 0.90 on the held-out test set, 
indicating that the training strategy was sufficient for the dataset size 
and complexity.
Once the images were segmented, they were utilized to generate the 
point cloud of grape clusters using the structure-from-motion (SfM) 
technique. Structure-from-motion is a method that reconstructs 3D 
structures of objects or scenes from a series of 2D images by analyzing 
the motion of the camera relative to the object. The process began with 
calibrating the camera’s intrinsic parameters to support precise 3D 
modeling. A checkerboard pattern was used for calibration, where 
multiple images of the pattern were captured, and corners were detected 
to calculate parameters such as focal length, principal point, and lens 
distortion coefficients. These parameters defined the camera’s internal 
characteristics, enabling the accurate projection of 3D points onto 2D 
images. This calibration was essential for minimizing distortion and 
ensuring reliable feature detection and reconstruction. Following cali­
bration, the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) algorithm was 
used to extract robust features from segmented images, identifying key 
points that would not vary under scaling, rotation, and illumination 
changes. Each key point was paired with a descriptor capturing local 
gradient characteristics, enabling reliable matching across images. 
These correspondences established spatial relationships between over­
lapping images, crucial for determining camera poses and constructing 
consistent 3D scene geometry.
After extracting and matching features, descriptors were aligned 
across overlapping images to identify common points, using a robust 
feature matching pipeline, which applied geometric filtering via 
RANSAC-based fundamental matrix estimation to remove false corre­
spondences [25]. RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus) is an iterative 
algorithm used to estimate parameters of a mathematical model while 
identifying and excluding outliers in the data, ensuring reliable

2.4. Post-processing and volume estimation
The segmented point clouds of the vine canopies and grape clusters 
underwent several post-processing steps to ensure accuracy in volume 
estimation.

clusters using the DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Appli­

noise, making it suitable for distinguishing well-defined structures like

2.4.3. Up sampling and down sampling of point clouds
To improve the density and accuracy of cluster volume estimation, 
3D point clouds were upsampled by adding new points in the neigh­
borhood of existing points, enhancing the precision of the mesh recon­
struction. This process was particularly important for cluster point 
clouds, as they were sparse due to the SfM process. The upsampling 
factor used for clusters was 10, which means that the approach created 
10 points for each original point, distributed within the local vicinity, 
thereby enhancing the detail and resolution of the point cloud for 
improved accuracy in segmentation and downstream analysis. In 
contrast, canopy point clouds were acquired using LiDAR, which 
incorporated distinct features of the canopies allowing for direct point 
cloud segmentation without photogrammetry. This process resulted in 
highly dense segmented point clouds due to the large size and detailed 
structure of vine canopies. To optimize computational efficiency and 
simplify mesh generation, down-sampling was applied to the canopy 3D 
point clouds, reducing the number of points while preserving its geo­
metric features.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Vine canopy volume estimation
Vine canopy volume estimation was performed using a Gradient 
Boosting Classifier to segment vine canopies in the point cloud data. As 
mentioned in the methods section, the classifier isolated canopy points 
from background elements such as the ground, sky, and overlapping 
canopies, with raw point cloud data being down sampled for efficiency. 
During the training process (See Section 2.2), the optimal hyper­
parameters were determined as follows: maximum depth was set to 3, 
which was an optimal value as deeper trees led to overfitting while 
shallower trees reduced segmentation accuracy; the number of estima­
tors was set to 100, balancing computational efficiency and perfor­
mance; and the learning rate was set to 0.5 to ensure stable convergence 
and avoid overfitting. Fig. 4 illustrates how variations in these param­
eters influenced the model’s performance, with accuracy plateauing at 
the selected values.
The Gradient Boosting Classifier achieved a segmentation accuracy, 
defined as the proportion of correctly classified canopy points in the test 
set, of 98 %. This value indicates that the classifier effectively separated 
canopy structures from background elements. The trained model was 
then applied to point clouds of 30 vine canopies, successfully isolating 
canopy points and removing background elements (and example shown 
in Fig. 5). The segmentation process preserved the structural details of 
the canopy, which was essential for accurately estimating the canopy 
volume.
Following segmentation, surface reconstruction was performed on 
the segmented canopy point clouds using the alpha shape reconstruction 
algorithm to create watertight meshes. The estimated canopy volumes 
were compared with ground truth volumes obtained through manual 
measurements, yielding a root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.135 m3. 
This RMSE corresponds to a relative error of 21.68 % when compared to 
the average volume of the sample vine canopies. As shown in Fig. 6, the 
estimated and ground truth volumes were moderately (positive) corre­
lated (0.7), based on a linear regression model.
It was also found that there was a bias of −0.027 m3, which indicated 
a slight underestimation of calculated canopy volumes compared to 
ground truth measurements. This error likely arises from the ground 
truth data collection method, which approximated canopy volumes 
using average height and breadth measurements and treated the canopy 
as a rectangular prism. Even though this is a practical approach for 
estimating actual canopy volumes, the method introduced simplifica­
tions that did not fully capture the irregular and non-uniform canopy 
structures. Additionally, empty spaces within the canopy surface and 
unpruned shoots made surface reconstruction challenging. The alpha 
shape reconstruction algorithm required an increased alpha value to 
create watertight meshes, which may have introduced inaccuracies, 
particularly in highly variable and structured parts of the canopies.
In addition to accuracy, the computational efficiency of the pipeline 
was evaluated. For down-sampled canopy point clouds, preprocessing, 
Gradient Boosting segmentation, and alpha-shape volume estimation 
typically required 20–40 s per vine on the workstation. This demon­
strates that the approach is not only accurate but also efficient enough 
for practical vineyard-scale adoption.
3.2. Grape cluster volume estimation
The novel approach used for cluster volume estimation included 
segmentation of grape cluster region in RGB images using YOLO11 
instance segmentation model, followed by cluster point cloud genera­
tion through a structure-from-motion (SfM) process. The YOLO11 seg­
mentation model was trained specifically to segment grape clusters, 
achieving a mean average precision (mAP@50) of 0.9, indicating high 
accuracy in detecting and isolating the grape clusters in color images.

For the example shown in Fig. 8, the histogram of reprojection re­

struction. Reprojection residuals are defined as the differences between 
the observed 2D image points and their corresponding 2D points 
reprojected from the reconstructed 3D model. These residuals quantify 
the alignment between the reconstructed geometry and the input im­
ages. In Fig. 9, x-axis shows reprojection residual values, while y-axis 
displays the frequency of these residuals. The distribution has a high 
frequency of points having minimal errors and a sharp drop-off for 
higher residual values, showing the overall success of the reconstruction 
process, with most points exhibiting low errors. It is noted, however, 
that the presence of even a small number of points with higher residuals 
may lead to high overall error, necessitating further refinement of the 
volume estimation method used as discussed in Section 2.4. These 
refinement techniques included improved outlier removal, feature 
matching, and camera calibration, which were applied to enhance 
reconstruction accuracy. Once the reconstruction refinement was 
completed, volumes were calculated again using watertight meshes 
derived from the point clouds. Processing 25–30 images required 
approximately 2 min per cluster on the workstation, demonstrating 
practical feasibility for routine vineyard data analysis. Fig. 10 shows and 
example segmented cluster and its watertight surface mesh, which was 
used for volume estimation.
The accuracy of these measurements was significantly influenced by 
the completeness of the point cloud data. When using the entire dataset, 
which included both complete and partially occluded clusters, the RMSE 
was higher (46.47 cm³) corresponding to 44.72 % of average actual 
volume with a weaker correlation (r = 0.272), highlighting the critical 
impact of missing areas in the clusters imaged. Fig. 11 shows the plot of 
estimated volumes against ground truth values for all clusters, demon­
strating the overall performance of the method. The results shows that, 
for clusters with complete point clouds, the correlation improved sub­
stantially (r = 0.653), and the RMSE was lower (24.50 cm³; equivalent to 
22.28 %). Additionally, the bias observed in volume estimation for the 
entire dataset, which included both complete and partially occluded 
clusters, was −11.7 cm³, indicating consistent underestimation. For 
clusters with complete point clouds, the bias improved to −6.16 cm³, 
reflecting better alignment between estimated and actual volumes.
Occlusions during the scanning process, such as overlapping leaves 
or branches, and limitations inherent to the Structure-from-Motion 
(SfM) technique, led to missing data and affected the completeness of 
some clusters. SfM, which relies on feature matching between 2D im­
ages, can result in sparse or incomplete point clouds when features are 
occluded or poorly matched. These factors contributed to consistent 
underestimation of cluster volumes, as evidenced by the negative bias. 
Since accurate cluster volume estimation is critical for yield prediction, 
errors in volume measurements could propagate into inaccuracies in 
estimated yield. To further address the challenges posed by occlusions 
and incomplete data, future work should focus on practical solutions

4. Conclusion
This study investigated the use of smartphone-based 3D imaging 
system for acquiring and processing detailed geometrical/structural 
information of grapevine canopies and grape clusters in those canopies 
and estimate their volumes. The results demonstrated that accessible, 
ubiquitous, and low-cost technology that smartphone devices provide, 
combined with advanced machine learning algorithms, could be appli­
cable for characterizing grapevine canopies (e.g., canopy volume esti­
mation) and crops (e.g., grape cluster volume estimation), supporting 
better decision-making in vineyard management and precision viticul­
tural practices. The Gradient Boosting Classifier-based approach for 
volume estimation used in this study achieved a root mean square error 
(RMSE) of 0.135 m³, corresponding to a relative error of 21.68 % for 
canopy volume estimation. For grape cluster volume estimation, the 
entire dataset, which included both complete and partially occluded 
clusters, yielded an RMSE of 46.47 cm³ (44.72 % relative error) and a 
weaker correlation (r = 0.272). In contrast, for clusters with complete 
point clouds, the RMSE improved to 24.50 cm³ (22.28 % relative error) 
with a stronger correlation (r = 0.653).

Several limitations were encountered in this study. One major limi­
tation was the occlusion in some point cloud captures, where parts of the 
grape clusters were obscured, resulting in incomplete datasets. Addi­
tionally, a significant amount of 3D spatial data was lost during the 
feature matching and Structure-from-Motion (SfM) process, which 
affected the accuracy of grape cluster reconstruction method. To address 
these issues, data acquisition could be conducted more rigorously to 
ensure the full structure of the cluster is acquired. Furthermore, 
employing better feature matching techniques for SfM could improve 
the retention of point clouds after segmentation. Additionally, more 
advanced surface reconstruction methods for mesh generation could be 
explored to improve the accuracy in representing the shape of grape 
clusters.
Another limitation is that both segmentation models were trained 
only once on the available datasets. The Gradient Boosting classifier 
performed reliably for canopy segmentation, and YOLO11 achieved 
strong accuracy on an independent test set, but the stability of these 
results across repeated training runs was not assessed. Future work with 
larger datasets should include multiple trials to better evaluate robust­
ness across different data splits and vineyard settings. The performance 
of cluster volume estimation can also vary with morphology, as loose 
clusters with larger gaps are more difficult to reconstruct accurately 
than compact clusters. Occlusion from leaves or neighboring clusters can 
further reduce point cloud completeness, leading to underestimation of 
cluster volume. In addition, variations in lighting, shading, and back­
ground complexity may influence both SfM feature matching and seg­
mentation accuracy, particularly under diverse vineyard environments. 
Therefore, expanding datasets and incorporating repeated training trials 
across diverse vineyard conditions will be essential to ensure the sta­
bility and reliability of the proposed approach. In the broader context, 
future enhancements in training data, sensor technology, and predictive 
capabilities could further refine the performance, and improve the 
practical adoptability of the proposed smartphone-based approach for 
vine canopy and grape cluster volume estimation in commercial 
viticulture.
