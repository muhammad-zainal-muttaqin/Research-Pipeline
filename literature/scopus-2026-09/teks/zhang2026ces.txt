A B S T R A C T
Accurately detecting and quantifying the number and distribution of fallen fruits after a disaster is of great 
significance for the rapid loss assessment of agricultural fruit insurance and orchard yield estimation. In view of 
the problems in the unstructured orchard environment, such as the small target size of fragrant pear fruits in the 
aerial images obtained by drones, the similar color between fruits and leaves, and susceptibility to occlusion by 
ground debris. This study proposed the CES-YOLO model for detecting and counting fallen fragrant pears after 
hail disasters, which is based on the YOLOv8s model. First, CAS-ViT was used as the backbone feature extraction 
network to improve the ability of CES-YOLO to perceive global information. Second, an efficient multiscale 
attention mechanism (EMA) is embedded before the output detection head to capture more detailed information 
and enhance the correlation between local features. Finally, to mitigate the problems of false detection and 
localization inaccuracies caused by overlapping occlusions in fragrant pear fruit clusters, we introduced SIoU for 
bounding box regression, focusing on the difference between the directions of the predicted box and the ground 
truth box, improving both the localization accuracy and convergence speed. The experimental results showed 
that the mAP@0.5, accuracy, and recall rate of the CES-YOLO model in detecting and counting fallen fragrant 
pears in complex environments were 93.9 %, 89.9 %, and 86.4 % respectively, with improvements of 0.5 %, 2.5 
% and 0.9 %, respectively, compared to the YOLOv8s model. When the CES-YOLO model was transferred to the 
fragrant pear gale disaster dataset for cross-disaster generalization experiments, mAP@0.5 reached 95.5 %. The 
CES-YOLO model proposed in this study has a high counting accuracy for fallen fragrant pears in complex post- 
disaster environments, and could provide a reliable technical support for improving the accuracy and efficiency 
of agricultural fruit insurance disaster loss assessment.
1. Introduction
In 2022, China's fragrant pear production exceeded 1.8 × 107 t [1]. 
As the nation's largest cultivation base for fragrant pears, fragrant pears 
have become a pivotal pillar industry in Xinjiang and the main primary 
source of economic income for local fruit farmers. In recent years, with 
the continuous expansion of fragrant pear-planting areas and the 
frequent occurrence of natural disasters, the latter has posed a serious 
threat to the yield and quality of local fragrant pears, as well as farmers' 
income. Against this backdrop, fragrant pear-loss insurance coverage is 
a critical component of Xinjiang's agricultural insurance system. 
Following a disaster, obtaining accurate data on disaster losses and 
disaster-induced fallen fruits quickly and efficiently is a shared need for 
both agricultural insurers and farmers [2]. Traditional orchard disaster 
assessments primarily rely on manual visual estimation based on 
empirical judgment. This manual approach is not only time-consuming 
but also susceptible to subjective bias, making it difficult to accurately 
evaluate the disaster situation across the entire orchard field. Therefore, 
developing an automated large-scale detection and counting system for 
post-disaster fallen fragrant pears could effectively assist insurance 
companies in disaster loss assessment, which is of substantial practical 
value for enhancing operational efficiency, insurance loss assessment,

With the advancement of UAV remote sensing technology and the 
reduction in equipment costs, UAV-based remote sensing has been 
increasingly adopted in agriculture [3–5]. Although satellite remote

remote sensing has the advantages of high spatial resolution, simple 
operation, and low cost [8–10], providing a novel technical means for 
large-scale crop yield estimation based on fruit counts [11].
With the advancement of deep learning, it has progressively pene­
trated the realm of fruit detection [12–14]. Tan et al [15] focused on 
detecting fragrant pears in unstructured environments and developed an 
enhanced YOLOv8n-based approach for fragrant pear detection. They 
optimized the C2F (CSP bottleneck with two convolutions) for feature 
fusion using a simplified residual and convolution module, and replaced 
SPPF (spatial pyramid pooling fast) with simSPPF (simple spatial pyra­
mid pooling fast). The mAP@0.5 of the improved model achieved 88.3 
%, surpassing the standard YOLOv8n by 0.5 %. Yu et al [16] developed a 
lightweight winter jujube counting model based on APP, which intro­
duced GSConv at the Neck end to reduce the model computation and 
enhance its detection speed. The average detection time of 
SOD-YOLOv5n for winter jujube detection ranged from 30 ms to 90 ms, 
providing support for the detection and counting of the winter jujube 
fruits. Yin et al [17] proposed an automatic watermelon counting 
method for UAV videos by integrating the YOLOv7-GCSF model with the 
DeepSORT algorithm to solve the problem of uneven distribution and 
severe occlusion in the natural environment, which led to the difficulty 
of manual counting. The average counting accuracy achieved 96.3 %. 
This method effectively counted the number of watermelons and pro­
vided an effective technical approach for watermelon yield prediction. 
Zhou et al [18] combined the IoT and optimized image detection tech­
nology to identify images of a pear tree in an orchard obtained by UAV. 
The results indicated that the optimized pear detection and extraction 
method had a good prediction, and improved the picking efficiency and 
sorting quality of the pear-picking robot.
Jing et al [19] used the Faster R-CNN model to detect fallen apples on 
the ground, achieving a recognition accuracy of 95.53 %. The recogni­
tion accuracy exceeded 90 % under unfavorable conditions, such as low 
light and long shooting distances. Li et al [20] proposed an improved 
YOLOv3 model for detecting fallen fruits, achieving an overall 
recognition accuracy exceeding 89 %. However, the method still pro­
duced false detection for fruits in partially occluded and overlapping 
scenes.
In summary, vision-based fruit detection technology has made 
progress in detecting multiple fruit varieties, but there are still signifi­
cant limitations in research specifically focused on post-disaster fallen 
fruit detection. Most existing achievements are focused on the detection 
of normal-growing canopy fruits, and there are few studies on target 
detection and counting of fallen fruits after disasters. Especially in 
complex natural environments, the recognition accuracy of existing 
methods for fruits affected by weed interference, trunk occlusion, or 
green fruit appearance still needs to be improved, and their localization 
errors become particularly pronounced in overlapping-target scenarios.
To address the challenges of large-scale fragrant pear orchards, small 
and densely distributed fruit targets in low-altitude UAV imagery, and 
severe leaf occlusion caused by hail damage, we propose an improved 
model, CES-YOLO, for detecting and counting fallen fragrant pears. 
Based on the YOLOv8s network’s structure, we made improvements and 
optimizations in three aspects of the model. To strengthen the model's 
capacity for global contextual modeling, we used CAS-ViT (Convolu­
tional Additive Self-attention Vision Transformer) to optimize the 
network backbone. Secondly, an efficient multi-scale attention mecha­
nism (EMA) was inserted into the front of the detection head to enhance 
the attention to the occluded fragrant pear through channel-space 
double weighting and capture more detailed information in over­
lapping regions. Finally, the SIoU loss function was preferred as the loss 
function to optimize the bounding box regression accuracy of dense 
fragrant pear fruit targets.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Research area and data acquisition
2.1.1. Research area overview
The study area is located in Puhui orchard (longitude: 85◦53′23′′E, 
latitude: 41◦24′6′′N, altitude: 890–952 m), Korla City, Bayingolin 
Autonomous Prefecture, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China 
(Fig. 2). The region has a typical temperate continental climate. The 
average annual precipitation is 58.6 mm. The total area of the farm 
reaches 13586 hm2, which is one of the main production areas of 
fragrant pears.
2.1.2. Data acquisition and preprocessing
The UAV equipment used in this study was DJI Mavic 3M (DJI 
Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), equipped with Hasselblad 4/3 
CMOS RGB aerial camera, a focal length of 24 mm, a focal ratio of f / 
2.8~f / 11, and a focus ranging from 1 m to infinity. The image reso­
lution of the camera was 5268 × 3956 pixels (JPEG format). The fallen 
fragrant pear image data caused by the hail disaster were collected from 
July 20th to July 22nd, 2024. The UAV flight altitude was 15 meters, 
with a flight speed of 1.5m⋅s-1, with a forward overlap of 85 % and a side 
overlap of 75 %; a total of 650 original image samples were acquired. 
Orthophoto stitching was performed using Pix4D Mapper software.
2.2. Dateset building
The construction of the fallen fragrant pear fruit sample dataset 
included four parts: data annotation, data cropping, data division, and 
data augmentation. The annotation was completed by Labelme (https: 
//github.com/wkentaro/labelme), whereby all fragrant pear targets 
were manually annotated with a label “pear” using a bounding box. The 
annotation file was saved as JSON format. Considering the large size of 
the stitched UAV images and the excessively small size of fragrant pear 
targets in the original images, which made detection difficult, the im­
ages were uniformly cropped into 640 × 640 pixels sub-images. To 
prevent fragrant pear targets from being truncated, which could lead to 
reduced model accuracy, an overlap rate of 10 % was set between 
adjacent sub-images during the cropping process. Sub-images contain­
ing no targets were subsequently removed, resulting in a final total of 
5852 valid images. The dataset was divided into a training dataset, 
validation dataset and test dataset at a ratio of 7: 1.5: 1.5.
In practical applications, high-altitude UAV imaging is often affected 
by variations in illumination, noise, and blur. To enhance the diversity 
and complexity of the training samples, data augmentation is employed, 
enabling the network to fully learn the characteristics of fragrant pears 
in complex natural scenes and thereby improving its generalization 
capability under challenging conditions. Various augmentations were 
utilized for the training set, including 45◦rotation, horizontal flip, 
Gaussian blur, salt-and-pepper noise, and HSV transformation, as shown 
in Fig. 3. The distribution of training set, validation set and test set is 
presented in Table 1.

2.3. Establishment of detection and counting model for fallen fragrant

2.3.1. YOLOv8
YOLOv8 is a new state-of-the-art (SOTA) model open-sourced in 
2023. It introduces new modules based on YOLOv5 and integrates tasks, 
such as object detection, instance segmentation, and real-time tracking 
[21]. There are five different versions of the YOLOv8 model: YOLOv8s, 
n, m, l, and x. To avoid computational redundancy, which may lead to 
slow target detection speeds. This study selected YOLOv8s as the base 
model after comprehensive consideration of the detection accuracy and 
model size.
YOLOv8 consists of three parts: Backbone, Neck and Head. The 
structure of the YOLOv8 network is shown in Fig. 4. The backbone 
network and Neck adopted the idea of ELAN [22] in YOLOv7, replacing 
the C3 module in YOLOv5 with the C2F module with a richer gradient 
flow, which significantly reduces the number of parameters and makes 
the model more lightweight. The Neck stage adopts the design idea of

PAN-FPN, which includes a bottom-up Path Aggregation Network

2.3.2. CES-YOLO model
In view of the challenges in which the fragrant pear targets in UAV 
aerial images are relatively small and there is more occlusion, this study 
improved the Backbone and Head sections of the YOLOv8s model to 
address the above problems. The CES-YOLO model achieved its im­
provements by incorporating three key modules into the YOLOv8s 
network: the CAS-ViT network structure, EMA module, and SIoU loss 
function. First, we introduced the CAS-ViT structure to replace the 
backbone network CSPDarkNet, overcoming the limitation of traditional 
convolution that only focuses on local receptive fields, and enhanced the 
global feature characterization capability through the window self- 
attention mechanism. Second, to improve the performance of the 
output detection head and alleviate the issues of crowded and over­
lapping targets in the image, an efficient multiscale attention mecha­
nism (EMA) was embedded in front of the detection head to improve the 
feature separability of overlapping targets. The SIoU loss function 
enabled the network to focus on the differences between the predicted 
box and ground truth box during model training, incorporating direc­
tional information to enhance the convergence speed and efficiency of 
the model. The application of the SIoU loss function further alleviated 
the problems of false detection and localization bias caused by over­
lapping occlusions of fragrant pears. The network structure of CES- 
YOLO is illustrated in Fig. 5.
2.3.3. Convolutional Additive Self-attention Vision Transformer (CAS-ViT)
The ViT (Vision Transformer) model [23] was developed by the 
Google team in 2020 for the application of the Transformer [24] for 
image classification. Vision Transformer (ViT) utilizes the self-attention 
mechanism to expand the dependence of the model on images and 
automatically learns the correlations between different regions within 
the image. The ViT model divides the image into a series of equally sized 
patches, and the embedding layer encodes each patch (Patch Embed­
ding), 
transforming 
the 
image 
classification 
task 
into 
a 
sequence-processing task. Although the ViT model performs excellently 
for image classification, it has certain limitations. Specifically, its 
computational complexity is relatively high, and balancing the accu­
racy, efficiency, and ease of deployment in mobile and real-time appli­
cations remains a challenge.
To solve the above challenges, Zhang et al [25] developed the 
lightweight network called Convolutional Additive Self-attention Vision 
Transformer (CAS-ViT). The CAS-ViT backbone network consists of four 
stages of encoding layers, and the structure of CAS-ViT is illustrated in 
Fig. 6. Each encoding layer uses Patch Embedding to downsample twice, 
obtaining feature maps with sizes of H

sisting of an integration subnet, CATM, and MLP. The integration subnet 
consists of three deep convolutional layers activated by ReLU [26], 
which merge the inputs of the convolutional layers and self-attention 
layers to alleviate the problem of gradient vanishing. CAS-ViT com­
bines the advantages of convolutional neural networks and Vision 
Transformers, and integrates global information by introducing a con­
volutional additive self-attention mechanism to accurately grasp the 
overall contour of the fragrant pear. Different from traditional convo­
lutions that only focus on local contours, CAS-ViT associates the local 
features of the fragrant pear, so that when the pear is obscured by leaves 
or weeds, its complete shape can be accurately inferred based on the 
global feature information obtained, thereby improving the recognition 
accuracy of fallen pear in natural environments.
2.3.4. EMA module
To enhance the performance of the output detection head and 
address the challenges posed by small, crowded, and overlapping 
fragrant pear targets in UAV-captured images, we integrated an efficient 
multiscale attention mechanism [27] before the detection head to 
improve the ability of CES-YOLO to capture and extract features of 
fragrant pear targets. When detecting and localizing targets, the detec­
tion head may lose some detailed information, leading to missed de­
tections and false positives. The EMA module employs parallel

sub-networks to evenly distribute spatial semantic features between 
every feature group. To achieve rich feature aggregation, the cross-space 
learning method is utilized to aggregate multiscale spatial structural 
information, thereby avoiding performance degradation caused by more 
complex sequential processing and deep network convolutions. This 
effectively captures the pixel-level attention feature values of fragrant 
pears and establishes dependency relationships between the dimensions. 
The learned attention weights are used to enhance the key regions in the 
sub-features, thereby generating accurate localization information for 
fragrant pear targets.
The EMA structure is shown in Fig. 7. EMA divides the input feature 
map into G sub-features along the channel dimension to learn different 
semantic features. Each sub-feature has a shape of ⌊C/G⌋ × H × W, 
where C is the number of input channels, H and W are the spatial height 
and width of the input features, respectively, and ⌊⌋ denotes the floor 
(round-down) operator. In order to aggregate multi-scale spatial struc­
ture information, 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 branches are placed in parallel, and 
the attention weights of the grouped feature maps are extracted. Spatial 
directional information is encoded in two 1 × 1 branches using one- 
dimensional global average pooling, where X Avg Pool is the average 
pooling along the horizontal direction and Y Avg Pool is the average 
pooling along the vertical direction. After factorizing the output into two 
vectors, two nonlinear Sigmoid functions are employed to approximate 
the 2D bidirectional distribution of linear convolution. In the 3 × 3 
branch, a single 3 × 3 convolutional kernel is stacked to capture local 
channel interactions while expanding the feature space. Global spatial 
information is then encoded in the outputs of the 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 
branches using a cross-space learning strategy. Finally, a Sigmoid 
function is applied to the fused features to perform a nonlinear mapping 
and generate a spatial attention map for each group. In the Re-weight 
module, the spatial attention map is expanded along the channel 
dimension and multiplied element-wise with the grouped feature map 
(with a residual connection), thereby enhancing high-response regions 
and suppressing less informative background areas. The reweighted 
feature maps of each group more clearly emphasize the spatial re­
lationships between target regions and their neighboring pixels, which 
further strengthen the semantic and structural representation of the 
entire image.
2.3.5. SIoU loss function
The YOLOv8 network used the CIoU loss function, which does not 
consider the orientation localization between the predicted box and the 
ground truth box [28]. This shortcoming leads to a slower efficient 
model convergence, and the prediction bounding boxes may “wander 
around” during the model training process, finally resulting in a worse 
result. To solve the above problem, this study replaced the CIoU with the 
Shape Intersection over Union (SIoU) loss function [29]. The SIoU loss 
function considers the vector angle between the desired regressions to 
stabilize the prediction bounding box, which significantly accelerates 
the convergence process of training.

and IoU loss (U). Fig. 8 shows the schematic diagram of the computa­

All experiments in this study were conducted on the Ubuntu 20.04 
Focal operating system with the CUDA 12.2 computing platform. The 
run environment was Anaconda 3 for Python 3.8.0, and the deep

The YOLOv8 (n, s, m, l, x) models were trained using the dataset of 
fallen fragrant pear fruits, and the precision, recall, mAP@0.5,

3.4. Ablation experiments
To evaluate the impact of each improvement module on algorithm 
performance, ablation experiments were conducted based on the 
YOLOv8s model. These experiments were performed under identical 
training protocols, hyperparameter settings, and environmental condi­
tions to analyze the effects on detection accuracy, computational 
complexity, and inference speed. The results are presented in Table 3.
As shown in Table 3, the YOLOv8s model achieved an mAP@0.5 of 
93.4 % for the fallen fragrant pear detection and counting task. By 
replacing the backbone network with the CAS-ViT architecture, the 
optimized architecture leveraged global receptive fields to overcome the 
limitations of local convolutions. This established long-range feature 
dependencies within images, effectively reducing false positives and 
missed detections of fragrant pear. Although this modification increased 
the computational complexity from 24.2 to 25.6 GFLOPs and caused a 
minor decline in FPS, the precision increased by 1.0 %, and the 
mAP@0.5 improved by 0.3 %.
Furthermore, by embedding the EMA attention mechanism module 
before the detection head, the model’s precision, Recall, and mAP@0.5 
were further improved to 89.9 %, 86.4 %, and 93.9 %, respectively. 
These represent increases of 2.5 %, 1.1 %, and 0.5 % compared to the 
baseline YOLOv8s. This performance gain is primarily attributed to the 
EMA module, which achieves pixel-level attention allocation, thereby 
enhancing feature extraction capabilities and enabling the model to 
effectively differentiate between target regions and the background. 
Simultaneously, the computational complexity of the final model was 
reduced by 22.7 %, and the inference speed increased to 85.4 f⋅s-1. These 
results indicate that the integration of these improvement modules not 
only effectively enhances feature representation capabilities but also 
reduces computational complexity and boosts inference speed while 
maintaining high detection and counting accuracy. Consequently, the 
improved model is highly suitable for real-time detection tasks in 
resource-constrained environments, demonstrating the effectiveness of 
the proposed methods.
To further validate the advantages of the CES-YOLO model on the 
fallen fragrant pear fruit dataset, we used the Grad-CAM heat map 
visualization method [30] to visualize the key regions in the image, and 
analyzed which regions of the image contributed significantly to the

As illustrated in Fig. 10, the YOLOv8s model is susceptible to inter­

which diminish its attention to fragrant pear fruits. This is the primary 
factor leading to missed detections and incorrect detections of fragrant 
pear fruits by the YOLOv8s model during the detection and counting 
process. However, through the implementation of optimization opera­
tions on the YOLOv8s model, as shown in the heatmaps of CES-YOLO, a 
significant improvement in the model's focus on the regions of fragrant 
pear fruit was achieved. Meanwhile, the interference from the back­
ground was mitigated. The evaluation results of the heat map visuali­
zation demonstrate that the CES-YOLO model can better handle complex 
tasks and is more practical and reliable in practical applications within 
natural orchard environments.

3.6. Generalization capability experiments
To further evaluate the generalization capability of the proposed 
method under different disaster scenarios, validation experiments were 
conducted using a dataset of wind-induced fallen fragrant pear images 
acquired with mobile phones, and the CES-YOLO and YOLOv8s models 
were comparatively analyzed. This dataset was characterized by densely 
distributed fruits and severe occlusions, with visual characteristics 
similar to the hail-induced fallen-fruit scenes described above, and was 
used to examine whether the models could maintain stable fallen-fruit 
detection and counting performance under a new wind-damage sce­
nario. In this experiment, 800 images of fallen fragrant pear fruits 
caused by a gale disaster were selected and divided into the training 
dataset, the validation dataset, and the test dataset according to the ratio 
of 8:1:1. The experimental environment and parameter configurations 
were kept consistent with those described in Section 3.1. The experi­
mental results are shown in Table 5.
As shown in Table 5, the CES-YOLO model achieved higher preci­
sion, recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95 than the YOLOv8s model, 
indicating its superior capability for detecting and counting fallen fruits 
in complex orchard environments. The detection results of CES-YOLO 
and YOLOv8s on the gale disaster-induced fallen fragrant pear fruit 
dataset are shown in Fig. 13. It could be observed that CES-YOLO 
demonstrated superior detection performance under challenging envi­
ronmental conditions. As illustrated in Figs. 13 (b) and 13(c), although 
CES-YOLO still exhibited some false positives and missed detections due 
to lighting conditions and leaf occlusion, it achieved a more accurate 
identification of fallen fragrant pear targets and quantity estimation in 
complex environments than the original YOLOv8s model. This outcome 
further demonstrated the generalization capability of the proposed 
method.
4. Conclusion
To address the challenges of accurately detecting and counting small 
hail-damaged fallen fragrant pear fruits in complex natural environ­
ments, where targets are often obscured by branches and foliage, this 
study proposed a new method based on UAV remote sensing and the 
CES-YOLO model to detect and count fallen fragrant pear fruits in an 
unstructured environment, which achieved the accurate detection and 
counting of fallen fragrant pear fruit after a hail disaster in the natural 
environment. We constructed a remote sensing dataset of post-disaster 
fallen fragrant pears with UAV, and then integrated the CAS-ViT, EMA 
attention mechanism, and SIoU loss function into the YOLOv8s network, 
and developed an improved post-disaster fallen fragrant pear fruit 
detection model, CES-YOLO. Experimental results show that the inte­
gration of CAS-ViT, EMA attention mechanism and other modules into 
YOLOv8s to construct CES-YOLO can improve the detection and 
counting accuracy of fallen fragrant pear fruits from 87.4 % to 89.9 %. 
Compared with the current mainstream detection algorithms, CES- 
YOLO demonstrates high competitiveness in detection and counting 
accuracy under complex conditions such as dense fruit, weed occlusion, 
and overlapping fruit.
Furthermore, CES-YOLO was applied to a dataset of fallen fragrant 
pears affected by gale disasters for generalization experiments. CES- 
YOLO achieved a detection accuracy of 95.7 % and a mAP@0.5 of 
95.5 % for detecting and counting fallen fragrant pears affected by gale 
disasters, showing improvements of 1.1 % and 0.7 % compared to 
YOLOv8s. This proves the generalization capability of the model pro­
posed in this study. This research offers new ideas and technical refer­
ences for the damage assessment of disasters in agricultural fruit 
insurance based on UAV and vision technology.
In future work, we plan to integrate the proposed fallen fragrant pear 
detection and counting method into practical automated applications, 
exploring its lightweight deployment on onboard UAV platforms and 
conducting field validation in scenarios such as orchard surveys and 
agricultural insurance claims. Meanwhile, we will expand the spatial 
and temporal coverage of the dataset by incorporating different regions, 
various disaster types and multi-source data, in order to examine the 
robustness and generalization capability of the model, thereby laying 
the foundation for building a scalable technical system for orchard 
disaster monitoring and loss assessment.
