A B S T R A C T  
Accurate fruit counting helps grape wine industry make better logistics and decisions before harvest, and 
therefore produce higher quality wine. In view of poor real-time performance of the existing fruit tracking and 
counting methods, and a lack of effective counting methods for cluster-like fruits due to their huge shape var­
iabilities. In this study, an end-to-end lightweight counting pipeline is developed to automate the processing of 
video data for real-time tracking and counting of grape clusters in field conditions. First, based on channel 
pruning algorithm, a more lightweight YOLOv5s cluster detection model is obtained, where number of model 
parameters, model size and floating-point operations (FLOPs) are reduced by 79 %, 76 %, and 58 %, respectively, 
and the pruned model size is only 3.4 MB. Secondly, the soft non-maximum suppression is introduced in pre­
diction stage to improve detection performance for clusters with overlapping grapes. Test results show that mAP 
reaches 82.3 % and average inference time is 6.1 ms per image, which effectively reduces model parameters and 
complexity while ensuring detection accuracy. Finally, online multiple object tracking of clusters is implemented 
by integrating the detection results and SORT algorithm, where two counting modes are set by introducing 
counting lines. Test results on 8 videos indicated that the average counting accuracy of the proposed method 
reached 84.9 %, correlation coefficient with manual counting reached 0.9905, and speed of video processing 
reached up to 50.4 frames per second (FPS), meeting field real-time requirements. This study provides a timely 
technical reference for the development of orchard robots to achieve real-time automated yield estimation and 
accurate crop management decisions.   
1. Introduction 
A major challenge of wine industry historically has been the accurate 
estimation of vineyard yields and winemaking potential, i.e., yield 
forecasting before harvest. Currently, this remains an unresolved chal­
lenge with significant technical and economic implications (Barriguinha 
et al., 2021; Dunn and Martin, 2004; Laurent et al., 2021). As grape 
acreage and yields expand, accurate estimation of wine grape yields will 
help winemaking industry make better logistics and decisions and pro­
duce higher quality wines (Fern´andez-Gonz´alez et al., 2011). Addi­
tionally, growers are also able to more precisely plan storage and sale of 
fruit, which is critical to improving winery efficiency and wine 
marketing strategies (Dorj et al., 2017). 
Current methods to estimate vineyard productivity at an industrial 
scale are destructive and labor-intensive, and lack of economic feasi­
bility (Herrero-Huerta et al., 2015). In addition, some methods are 
mainly based on manual visual inspection of vineyards, where experts 
only sample small areas of large vineyards and extrapolate the results to 
the whole area, with no guarantee of reliability and accuracy (Zabawa 
et al., 2020). These methods are cumbersome and insufficient to obtain 
representative yield data (Aquino et al., 2018). Therefore, there is an 
urgent need to develop an accurate and efficient method for counting 
grape clusters in field conditions to automate orchard yields estimation.

automated counting. With the advent of inexpensive sensors and elec­
tronic systems, off-the-shelf RGB cameras and computer vision systems 
based on advanced deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can 
provide affordable and versatile solutions for fruit detection (Kamilaris 
and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018; LeCun et al., 2015). For example, Bargoti 
and Underwood (2017) used a representative two-stage regional CNN, 
Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2015; Yi et al., 2021), to detect mangoes, al­
monds, and apples. Similarly, it has also been used to detect coconuts in 
complex backgrounds to determine their maturity stages (Parvathi and 
Selvi, 2021). Further, Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017) instance segmen­
tation network developed on the basis of Faster R-CNN has also been 
successfully used to detect apples and grapes in the field (Santos et al., 
2020; Shen et al., 2022; Wang and He, 2022). Compared with two-stage 
detection networks, one-stage object detection network YOLO (You 
Only Look Once) has the advantages of small size and fast detection, and 
is widely used for detection of tomatoes, kiwis, table grapes, and 
mangoes in complex field environments (Fu et al., 2021; Koirala et al., 
2019; Liu et al., 2020). These studies have well demonstrated the po­
tentials of CNNs in fruit recognition. However, most of these deep 
learning-based methods are mainly used to detect fruits in single images, 
which are only applicable to high-performance detection platforms due 
to their model and parametric sizes (Wu et al., 2020). This severely af­
fects the deployment and application of fruit detection models on small 
mobile terminals and limits the development of orchard robots. 
Several studies have shown that deep CNN-based detection results 
can be integrated by data association to count fruits in orchards through 
object tracking or mapping (Liu et al., 2019). To address the problem of 
video-based fruit counting, Vasconez et al. (2020) achieved the counting 
of three fruits using Faster R-CNN with InceptionV2 combined with 
Gaussian estimation-based multiple object tracking, where the counting 
performance ranged from 44 % to 93 %. Gao et al. (2022), based on 
YOLOv4-tiny and trunk tracking, successfully detected and counted 
apples in videos. However, in the above studies, fruit tracking is lost due 
to fruit occlusion or sudden movement of camera platform, which is 
prone to double counting. And this problem can be effectively solved by 
multi-view method or 3D reconstruction. In this regard, Stein et al 
(2016) developed a multi-view approach based on Faster R-CNN to 
detect fruits on mango trees and applied trajectory data provided by the 
navigation system to establish the correspondence between images for 
fruit counting, and the obtained counting accuracy was compared with 
ground truth with an error of 1.36 %. Exceptionally, Liu et al. (2018) 
proposed a novel fruit counting pipeline using a fully convolutional 
network (FCN) to segment video frame image into fruit and non-fruit 
pixels. The fruit is then tracked across frames using a Hungarian algo­
rithm that combines the tracking results with a structure of motion (SfM) 
algorithm to compute relative 3D position and size estimates to reject 
outliers and double-counted fruit trajectories. Although these methods 
can achieve high counting accuracy, they are usually time-consuming 
and therefore are difficult to meet the demand for real-time counting. 
Compared to fruits such as apples and mangoes with more regular 
shapes, for cluster-like fruits, grape inter-cluster shapes exhibit great 
variabilities, and it is still a challenge to achieve tracking counts for 
clusters via video. 
To address the above-mentioned challenges, this study develops a 
lightweight counting pipeline based on channel pruning of YOLOv5 and 
SORT algorithm, which enables real-time tracking and counting of grape 
clusters in the field based on video data. The proposed pipeline visual­
ization is given in Fig. 1. The main contributions are summarized as 
follows.  
(1) YOLOv5s model was pruned based on channel pruning algorithm, 
which effectively reduces model parameters and complexity 
while ensuring detection accuracy.  
(2) Soft non-maximum suppression (Soft-NMS) was introduced in the 
prediction stage to enhance model detection performance for 
overlapping grape clusters.  
(3) SORT algorithm was drawn to achieve online multiple object 
tracking of grape clusters.  
(4) Counting line was proposed in the video, and two counting modes 
were set, which effectively alleviated counting error caused by ID 
switching during tracking process. Tested on 8 videos, the 
average accuracy rate reached 84.9 %, and the processing speed 
reached up to 50.4 frames per second (FPS).

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Data preparation 
2.1.1. Data acquisition and annotation 
Grape cluster (Chardonnay wine grape variety) images and video 
datasets used for the experiment were collected at a wine grape culti­
vation site in Yangling District, Shaanxi Province, China. The grapevines 
were planted in rows from north to south with a spacing of about 3 m 
between rows. The image dataset was collected by using a digital camera 
(SONY ILCE-5100L) with an image size of 3008 × 1668 pixels. 218 
images were collected in 2020 (with acquisition distance between 0.5 
and 1 m) and 464 images in 2021 (with acquisition distance between 1 
and 1.5 m), both from July to September before grape harvest, in 
different weather and lighting conditions. The video dataset was 
collected before harvest in 2021 by using a remote-controlled field 
vehicle mounted with a digital camera. The vehicle dataset was 
collected randomly by row at a time, containing different directions of 
travel (south to north or north to south), and the recording was done 
with the vehicle at a horizontal distance of about 1.5 m from the 
grapevines, the camera at a vertical height of 1.2 m above the ground, 
and the movement speed of about 0.5–1 m/s. A total of 8 videos of 
different lengths were collected, named from V1 to V8 respectively, 
where video capture was at a frame rate of 30 FPS. 
Considering the huge variations of grape cluster shapes in the field 
and the problems of occlusion, it is difficult to annotate accurately using 
rectangular boxes directly. Therefore, the acquired images were anno­
tated with Labelme (Russell et al., 2008) polygon annotation tool, which 
can achieve accurate annotation of cluster outlines, with a total of 6227 
grape cluster instances. After annotation, the external rectangular boxes 
were automatically calculated based on grape cluster polygon outlines, 
which were uniformly converted to YOLO standard dataset format for

2.2. Rapid grape clusters detection by channel pruning YOLOv5s 
In this section, firstly, YOLOv5s grape cluster detection model is 
obtained by transfer learning training on the augmented image dataset. 
Second, to further simplify the model and improve its detection effi­
ciency, YOLOv5s model is pruned based on channel pruning algorithm 
to reduce model parameters and complexity as well as to decrease 
running memory occupation. This allows faster and more accurate 
detection of grape clusters, and facilitates model deployment. The de­
tails will be explained in the following subsections. 
2.2.1. Grape clusters detection by YOLOv5s. 
YOLO is a typical one-stage object detection algorithm, where its 
main idea is to input model with whole image and directly regress object 
coordinates and classification probability of the target using CNN 
(Redmon et al., 2016). This end-to-end detection network greatly im­
proves model detection speed (Tian et al., 2019). YOLOv5s, as a network 
with the smallest depth and the smallest width of feature map in 
YOLOv5 series, has a small model size, which is convenient for subse­
quent expansion applications (Jocher et al., 2020). Therefore, the 
advanced YOLOv5s network is selected as the baseline network for grape 
clusters detection in this study, and the main model structure is shown in 
Fig. 3, which includes input, backbone, neck, head and loss function, 
detailed as below.  
(1) Input: mosaic data enhancement is used, and four images are 
stitched by random scaling, random cropping, and random 
scheduling to improve network robustness. In addition, for 
different object sizes in datasets with different acquisition dis­
tances, adaptive anchor box technique is used to automatically 
find the most suitable anchor box length and width through the 
network.  
(2) Backbone: using CSPNet (Wang et al., 2020) structure to extract 
information-rich features from input image.  
(3) Neck: using FPN + PAN structure for the aggregation of three 
scale semantic features to recognize target grape clusters of 
different sizes.  
(4) Head: 1 × 1 convolution structure is used to output three sets of 
feature maps with different scales. Apply anchor boxes to output 
feature map to generate a final output vector with category 
probabilities, confidence scores and bounding boxes. 
(5) Loss function: representation of the difference between predic­
tion and ground truth during training phase. A Complete IoU 
(CIoU) loss (Zheng et al., 2020) is used as the loss function of the 
bounding box. 
Detailed module description is given as below: 
(a) CBS (Fig. 3a): A module consisting of a convolutional layer, a 
batch normalization layer and a SiLu activation function, which is the 
most basic structure in YOLOv5s network. 
(b) CSP: Evolved from CSPNet network, two CSP network structures 
are designed in YOLOV5s. CSP1_x (Fig. 3c) is applied to backbone and 
consists of CBS module, x residual units (Fig. 3b) and a concatenate 
function. The other one, CSP2_1 (Fig. 3d), is applied to Neck and consists 
of a CBS module and a concatenation function to enhance network 
feature fusion.

2.2.2. Channel pruning for YOLOv5s grape clusters detection model 
Although the fine-tuned YOLOV5s can accurately detect grape 
clusters under field conditions, model pruning is necessary to reduce 
model complexity, save computational cost and memory usage, and 
facilitate application to subsequent real-time tracking and counting. The 
fine-tuned YOLOV5s grape clusters detection model is pruned by using 
channel pruning algorithm (Liu et al., 2017). The essence of channel 
pruning is to eliminate channels that contribute less to model perfor­
mance so that network complexity can be reduced (Wang and He, 2021; 
Wu et al., 2020). In the channel pruning algorithm, the scaling factor γ is 
introduced into batch normalization (BN) layer. After training both 
weight parameters and scaling factor γ, all γ are counted and ranked to 
determine the maximum γ that satisfies the pruning rate requirement, 
which is used as the threshold for pruning. Channels smaller than the 
threshold are removed and do not participate in the connection of 
neurons. The channel pruning algorithm crops model parameters in a 
simple and effective way to improve model computation efficiency. 
Finally, the accuracy loss can be minimized by model fine-tuning to 
obtain a more compact model (Molchanov et al., 2019). The main steps 
are detailed as below: 
(1) Sparse training. 
Since the relationship between weights and inputs in deep learning 
networks is multiplicative and additive, if most of the network weights 
are zero or close to zero, removing this part has little impact on the final 
network output accuracy. The L1 regular constraint is applied to the 
scaling factor of BN layer of YOLOV5s grape clusters detection network 
to increase the constraint, thus the parameters are sparse, so that most of 
the learned network weights are close to zero. The loss function used in 
sparse training process is shown in Eq. (1).

YOLOv5s solves the problem of multiple prediction boxes around a 
predicted object by using non-maximal suppression (NMS) during pre­
diction. This method can effectively eliminate redundant bounding 
boxes by forcibly removing boxes with confidence levels less than a 
threshold and calculating the Intersection over Union (IoU) between the 
maximum confidence detection box and the remaining detection boxes 
in a region of the same class of object, while removing all detection 
boxes that are larger than a preset IoU threshold. However, in field 
conditions, there is an overlap between grape clusters due to grape 
cluster shape variations. Using this method will force to make the score 
of partially overlapping detection boxes zero, which will result in missed 
detection. Therefore, this study introduces Soft-NMS (Bodla et al., 2017) 
in YOLOv5s model prediction to improve the model’s ability to detect 
overlapping clusters. When there are multiple predicted boxes around 
the cluster, the scores of the predicted boxes are multiplied by a 
weighting function that attenuates the scores of the neighboring boxes 
that overlap with the highest scoring box (Li et al., 2021). The more 
boxes overlap with the highest scoring box, the more severe the atten­
uation. The Gaussian function is chosen as the weighting function and is 
calculated as in Eq. (2).

2.3. Online multiple object tracking of grape clusters by SORT algorithm 
To achieve fast multiple object tracking of grape clusters under 
natural conditions, this study performs online multiple object tracking of 
clusters based on the SORT association algorithm (Bewley et al., 2016). 
The detailed steps are as follows. 
(1) Each video frame is first obtained by video stream data pro­
cessing algorithm, and then each cluster coordinate position is gener­
ated by the pruned YOLOv5s grape cluster detection model. 
(2) State estimation of the object’s position in the next video frame is 
done by Kalman filter (Bewley et al., 2016), which is mainly comprised 
of two phases including prediction and update. The prediction phase 
predicts position of the grape cluster in current frame based on position 
of the tracked cluster in previous frame; the update phase updates 
cluster position in prediction phase based on the detected position of the 
cluster in current frame (Wang et al., 2019).

represents velocity information corresponding to the cluster in image 
coordinates. The state of the clusters was predicted by Eq. (3).

2.4. Counting grape clusters by cross-line counting 
Due to irregular shape of grape clusters and complex background in 
the field (e.g., occlusion), the identity code (ID) assigned during tracking 
may switch, leading to over-counting. Therefore, this study counts the 
clusters by setting a counting line in the video, which is set at the po­
sition where the ratio of left to right area is about 2:3. Counting is 
performed by determining the relationship between the position of the 
count line and the coordinates of the detection box. If the intersection of 
the line connecting center points of the detection boxes of the same 
tracking object and the counting line exists between adjacent frames, the 
count is activated. Two counting modes are set according to different 
vehicle moving directions, and the counting modes can be automatically 
judged by the relative coordinate positions of detection boxes between 
adjacent frames. Fig. 7 shows the first counting mode as an example of 
the process of counting across lines. The two counting modes are 
described in detail as follows:  
(1) Cluster relative count line to the left moving pattern count step: 
(a) first initialize the number of detections on the left side of the 
counting line in 1st frame as N0. (b) from 2nd frame, count the 
number of clusters crossing the counting line from right to left in 
real time to accumulate on the basis of N0 until (T-1)th frame, and 
the result is recorded as Nm. (c) count the number of detections on 
the right side of the counting line in the last frame as Nf1. The 
final count result is Nm + Nf1.  
(2) Similarly, the clusters are counted relative to the counting line in 
the rightward movement mode: (a) firstly, the number of de­
tections on right side of the counting line is initialized in the first 
frame. (b) from the second frame, the number of clusters crossing 
the counting line from left to right is counted in real time and 
added on top of it until (T-1)th frame, and the result is recorded as 
Np; (c) the number of detections on the left side of the counting 
line is counted as Nf2 in the last frame. The final count result is Np 
+ Nf2.

3. Results 
3.1. Model performance before and after pruning 
In order to verify the impact of the channel pruning algorithm in this 
study on performance of grape cluster detection model, performance of 
YOLOv5s baseline model and the pruned model were compared on the 
image test set. The results are shown in Table 1. In particular, model 
efficiency is compared in terms of the number of model parameters, 
floating-point operations (FLOPs) and model size. FLOPs are used to 
measure model computational complexity. Model size includes not only 
number of parameters, but also network architecture information and 
optimizer information. From Table 1, it can be seen that after pruning, 
the amount of model parameters is reduced by 79 %, FLOPs are reduced

3.2. Performance comparison of different object detection algorithms 
To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed grape cluster 
detection method, four current widely used object detection algorithms, 
namely YOLOv4 (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020), YOLOv4-tiny, SSD (Liu 
et al., 2016), and EfficientDet-D1(Tan et al., 2020), were trained by 
using the same training set. The performance of these five algorithms 
was evaluated under the same test set, and the obtained results were 
shown in Table 2. As can be seen from the table, recall, F1-score, and 
mAP of the proposed method are always higher than the other four al­
gorithms, and the model size is the smallest. The recall of the proposed 
method is higher than YOLOv4, YOLOv4-tiny, SSD 300, and 
EfficientDet-D1 by 11.08 %, 15.87 %, 26.54 %, and 6.29 %, respectively. 
The F1-score is higher by 5.5 %, 10.5 %, 16.5 %, and 2.5 %, respectively. 
The mAP is higher by 2.19 %, 9.43, 18.52 %, and 1.11 %, respectively. 
The detection speed is 124.1f/s, 38.8f/s, and 138.3f/s higher than 
YOLOv4, SSD 300, and EfficientDet-D1, respectively. Although the 
detection speed of YOLOv4-tiny is faster than the proposed method, its 
detection accuracy is lower and model size is larger. In addition, despite 
the fact that YOLOv4 has the highest precision among the five algo­
rithms, its model size is also the largest among these five algorithms, and 
its detection speed is slower. It is noted that detection accuracy of 
EfficientDet-D1 is second only to the proposed method, but its model 
detection speed is the slowest. As a whole, the proposed method has 
better performance than the other four algorithms and can achieve ac­
curate and fast identification of grape clusters in field conditions, which 
lays the foundation for the follow-up tracking and counting.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Effect of different field conditions on grape clusters detection 
The complex and changing natural conditions in the field may have 
an adverse impact on accurate detection of grape clusters. The detection 
performance of the pruned YOLOv5s model was compared on test set 
under four different conditions, and the results are shown in Fig. 12. 
Although the pruned model is smaller in size and less complex in 
network architecture, it does not significantly reduce the ability to 
accurately identify grape clusters under complex background condi­
tions. The grape clusters were accurately detected under both natural 
light (Fig. 12a), smooth light (Fig. 12b), backlight (Fig. 12c) and 
different acquisition distance conditions (Fig. 12d). This may be due to 
data enhancement of the training set, where the light and dark changes 
in brightness and contrast may truly reflect the changes in field lighting. 
Although the proposed model was able to achieve 82.3 % in mAP for 
cluster recognition on test set, there is still some uncertainty in the re­
sults obtained, as the dataset is manually annotated. Due to extreme 
occlusions, overlaps between clusters and the fact that some clusters are 
at image edge, the model may detect clusters that are not fully anno­
tated, causing the presence of false positives. In a previous study by Li 
et al. (2021) on table grapes recognition, an attention mechanism was 
introduced in YOLOv4-tiny to improve model recognition accuracy 
under complex growth conditions. However, potentially, it also in­
troduces additional time overhead to the model. Moreover, there are 
some differences in cultivation methods of table grapes and wine grapes, 
and the location and status of grape clusters in the grapevine distribu­
tion are also important factors affecting detection results. For the dataset 
in this study, owing to the variations in camera capture distance, there 
are significantly fewer examples of clusters in the images collected in 
2020 than 2021. However, for the same-size images, the individual 
cluster in the images collected in 2020 occupy a larger pixel area and can 
be more easily detected by the model. This model trained at different 
collection distances is highly robust and does not vary significantly in 
the field due to changes in distance from grape rows by tractor platform 
or robot with camera.

the number of IDs assigned to the tracking is higher than the actual 
count value. The cluster matching error between frames, where the same 
object is assigned a new ID multiple times, causes the ID to change 
(Fig. 13a). In a previous similar study by Vasconez et al (2020), which 
counted three fruits, Hass avocado, lemon, and apples, by the number of 
IDs tracked, also reported higher algorithmic count estimates. Compared 
to the three relatively regular shaped fruits, tracking is very challenging 
with a large variability in shape among grape clusters and cluster 
overlapping occlusion. It is highly likely that trajectory error matches or 
even mismatches occur in cluster data association, and the number of 
grape cluster instances tracked is often higher than the actual number. 
Surprisingly, this study was able to greatly alleviate this situation by 
setting up counting line. The ID switching does not affect the result of 
the count until the tracked target cluster reaches the count line and after 
it crosses the count line. This is due to the fact that we set the count to be 
valid only when center point of the tracked target crosses count line. 
However, there are special cases where persistent miss detection 
generated before and after the cluster crosses the count line in the vi­
cinity can cause the cluster to cross the count line directly and not be 
detected. Although counting errors exist, the counting line greatly re­
duces the observation range of the tracking error, improves the proba­
bility of the cluster being counted effectively, and better alleviates the 
wrong counting result caused by frequent ID switching. 
Further, linear regression analysis results of real counts and algo­
rithmic counts are shown in Fig. 14. R2 of 0.9905 indicates that the 
proposed method has a significant linear correlation real count. This 
implies that the number of clusters estimated by the algorithm is similar 
to the ground truth number, which is significant for yield prediction and 
yield mapping related applications. 
4.3. Effect of different counting line positions on counting results 
In order to investigate the effect of counting line position on counting 
results, we set two other counting line positions, 3:2 and 1:1 for the left 
and right areas of the counting window, and compared them with the 
previously 2:3 position, where the results are shown in Fig. 15. Sur­
prisingly, the effect of counting line position is very slight, and the 
average standard deviation of the three different counting line positions 
is about 1.70 on the 8 test videos, which is almost negligible. This also 
indicates that the setting of count line position in this study does not 
make a significant difference to the cluster counts within a certain range. 
However, for very extreme cases, center point of the target detection 
frame falls exactly at the position of count line during the initialization 
of the first frame or during the counting on the count line side of the last 
frame, which may cause a counting error. In addition, due to the un­
certainty of intermittent tracking, it can potentially occur at every po­
sition of the counting window. For the same tracking target, tracking 
loss may not have occurred at one point in time, but over time, it is 
undoubtedly valid to be counted during the time difference between the 
two when it is not tracked due to occlusion or a change in camera pose at 
another point in time. The position of count line setting essentially re­
flects the temporal order in which the tracked targets are spatially and 
sequentially counted. The difference in count line position causes the 
same target to cross the count line with different time consumption, 
which also means that the location of the valid count event detected by 
the count line occurs differently. It is worth mentioning that the 
counting results obtained by V2 video remain exactly the same for the 
three different count line positions, which may be related to a potential 
equilibrium relationship between the variation of count line positions 
and intermittent tracking.

4.4. Limitations and future work 
The proposed tracking and counting method based on channel 
pruning YOLOv5 in this study enables accurate and rapid counting of 
grape clusters in field conditions. However, there is room for further 
improvement, which is summarized as below. 
In commercial vineyards, partial clusters appear on both sides of the 
vine, whereas the proposed method in this study only considers clusters 
visible from one side of grape row. These clusters may be double counted 
due to the change in camera view during vehicle movement on both

sides of the row. Therefore, future work will improve orchard yield 
estimation performance by developing a method that is able to identify 
clusters on both sides by using a depth camera, combined with the 
proposed tracking and counting algorithm to reject the same cluster with 
repeated counts on both sides. 
In addition, due to the complex field conditions, the offset of video 
images caused by sudden changes in speed of moving platforms can lead 
to the loss of cluster tracking. Even though it can be effectively mitigated 
by setting count line, we plan to consider platform moving speed in data 
association matching of tracking in future studies to reduce the negative 
effects of video jitter. 
From the results in Section 3.1, the model inference time does not 
change significantly before and after pruning, which is related to the 
high performance of the computing device in the study. However, the 
proposed method is also suitable for more ordinary hardware and is able 
to deliver good performance even for devices under 10G FLOPS 
(floating-point operations per second). Further, the pruned model is 
more lightweight, only 3.4 MB, and can process up to 50.4 FPS on 
average on the video in combination with cluster tracking counting 
method, which means that the proposed method can be deployed in 
small mobile terminals for orchard estimation robots in the future. 
5. Conclusions 
To achieve fast and accurate counting of grape clusters in field 
conditions for orchard yield estimation. In this study, channel pruning 
YOLOv5s was first conducted, where the number of model parameters, 
model size and FLOPs were reduced by 79 %, 76 % and 58 %, respec­
tively. This approach effectively reduced model complexity with a 
model size of only 3.4 MB. In addition, Soft-NMS was also introduced in 
prediction stage to improve detection performance for overlapping 
grape clusters. The mAP and F1-scores on image test set reach 82.3 % 
and 79.5 %, respectively, and the average inference time per image is 
only 6.1 ms. Comparative results against four state-of the-art object 
detection algorithms showed that the proposed model could not only 
improve model efficiency but also guaranteed model detection accuracy. 
Secondly, the object detection results were combined with SORT algo­
rithm for online cluster tracking via video data, and the concept of 
counting line was introduced to set two counting modes according to 
vehicle movement direction to achieve real-time grape cluster counting. 
Finally, test results on eight videos showed that the average counting 
accuracy of the proposed algorithm could reach 84.9 %, and the corre­
lation coefficient with manual counting reached 0.9905. The average 
speed of the proposed method can reach up to 50.4 FPS, which is able to 
meet the real-time requirements and provides timely technical support 
for online yield estimation and accurate crop management in orchards.
