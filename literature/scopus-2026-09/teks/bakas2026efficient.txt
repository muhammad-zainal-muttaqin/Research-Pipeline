Abstract. Plant health monitoring and fruit counting are essential
for optimizing crop yields, minimizing economic losses, and produc-
ing high-quality agricultural products. This study focused on identify-
ing an optimal deep learning model for real-time and accurate auto-
mated visual assessment of orange health and yield, speciﬁcally tar-
geting deployment on mobile devices with limited resources. The per-
formance of four lightweight YOLO (You Only Look Once) models
is compared, for the real-time detection and classiﬁcation of oranges
(Yolov5nu, Yolov9t, Yolov10n, and Yolo11n). The evaluation of YOLO
models considered precision, recall, mAP50, mAP50-90, and inference
time. YOLOv10n achieved the highest precision (88.8%), YOLOv9t
combined strong recall (80.5%) with the best mAP50 (87.6%), while
YOLOv5nu was the fastest (1.6ms). Overall, YOLO11n provided the
best balance of precision (87.2%), recall (80.5%), and low inference time
(1.7ms), making it ideal for real-time use. As a result, YOLO11n was
deployed in an Android app for on-device orange detection, classiﬁca-
tion, and counting, with real-time CSV logging. This demonstrates that
optimized YOLO models can deliver eﬃcient, accurate, and practical
solutions for mobile-based smart farming.

Although AI models, particularly deep learning-based object detectors, have
advanced signiﬁcantly, their real-world deployment in agricultural applications
is not straightforward. There are many regions where the majority of farms are
located in remote or infrastructure-constrained areas, where internet connectiv-

of models is necessary, particularly for mobile phones, drones, and embedded
systems that must operate independently in the ﬁeld. However, these devices
have limited processing capacity, memory, and battery life, raising the need for
computationally-light models [4].
Several studies have concentrated on object detection in precision farm-
ing, Tapia-Mendez et al. [5], implemented and compared twelve state-of-the-art
object detection models for crop quality classiﬁcation, on a custom dataset con-
sisting of 1,535 annotated images, establishing the potential of DL techniques
for post-harvest quality monitoring. Similarly, Saddik et al. [6] have developed
a model using multispectral data for yield estimation and disease detection of
Argan plants, where non-irrigated Argan trees show high disease resistance than
irrigated ones. Focusing on model deployment under real-world conditions, Bell-
out et al. [7] investigated the eﬀectiveness of various YOLO-based object detec-
tors for tomato leaf disease detection. They merged PlantVillage and PlantDoc
datasets, compared ﬁve YOLO variants and concluded that YOLOv5 achieved
the best performance, the outperformed model was packaged into a smartphone
application. Beyond crop and pest monitoring, In eﬀort to balance accuarcy and
computational constraints, Zarboubi et al. [8] proposed a lightweight tomato
disease classiﬁcation model using a CustomBottleneck-VGGNet architecture
enhanced through transfer learning. Extending the scope of YOLO-based detec-
tion beyond agriculture, a YOLOv9-based system was employed to detect motor-
cycle violations, helmet usage, and traﬃc light infractions in Marrakech, provid-
ing insight into gender-based diﬀerences and temporal patterns of violations
while maintaining high consistency with manual observations [9]. Furthermore,
a deep learningbased mobile application for cataract detection achieved high
diagnostic accuracy (above 91%) across multiple CNN architectures and was
successfully optimized for real-time deployment on Android devices, demonstrat-
ing the feasibility of mobile AI solutions for healthcare diagnostics [10]. These
studies collectively underscore the growing eﬀectiveness of deep learning models
in addressing diverse real-world challenges.
This paper aims to evaluate and compare lightweight YOLO variants for on-
device fruit detection, with the goal of identifying an optimal balance between
detection accuracy and inference eﬃciency. The models were assessed under stan-
dardized metrics and consistent experimental conditions. Based on these results,
the best-performing model was deployed in an Android application, demonstrat-
ing a practical and cost-eﬀective solution for real-time crop monitoring in agri-
cultural ﬁelds.

Methodology
The proposed methodology, illustrated in Fig. 1, presents the end-to-end pipeline
for detecting and classifying healthy and diseased oranges using YOLO-based
models. The workﬂow begins with image acquisition and annotation, followed
by dataset partitioning into training and test subsets. Several YOLO variants
were trained and systematically evaluated, with YOLO11n emerging as the best-
performing model. This model was subsequently converted into TensorFlow Lite
format for deployment within a mobile application. The application, developed
using Kotlin in combination with the Flutter framework, enables real-time detec-
tion and counting of oranges directly on Android devices, thereby demonstrat-
ing the feasibility of integrating lightweight deep learning models into ﬁeld-level
mobile solutions. Beyond showcasing technical feasibility, this pipeline highlights
the potential of AI-powered mobile tools to support precision agriculture and
improve yield monitoring practices.

2.1
Dataset Description and Preprocessing
The study was conducted within the Souss Massa basin in central-western
Morocco, a region widely recognized for its agricultural productivity and crop

258

diversity [11]. The area is characterized by a semi-arid climate, which strongly
inﬂuences agricultural practices and crop performance [12]. The experimental
ﬁeldwork was carried out in a citrus orchard belonging to an agri-business located
in the Sebt El Guerdane region, where a custom dataset was gathered to support
this research. The dataset was speciﬁcally prepared for the task of citrus health
classiﬁcation, targeting two classes, ”orange saine” (healthy orange) and ”orange
malade” (diseased orange). A total of 4,212 images were resized to 640×640 pix-
els, were annotated using the Roboﬂow platform [13].
The dataset was randomly divided into 80% training and 20% test sets. The
main challenge observed during the labeling process was class imbalance, with
a higher number of instances in one category over the other, potentially leading
to bias in the learning process and inﬂuences model generation. To address this
issue a variety of data augmentation techniques were applied to enhance feature
diversity contributing to model robustness during training.
2.2
Evaluated YOLO Variants for Object Detection
In this study, a comparative evaluation of four lightweight variants of the YOLO
architectures, including YOLOv5nu, YOLOv9t, YOLOv10n, and YOLO11n.
These variants are known for their compact designs and fast inference, mak-
ing them suitable for deployment in embedded and mobile environments. Each
model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16 and a learning rate
of 0.01, using an input image resolution of 640 × 640 pixels. Model performance
was assessed using Precision (Eq. 2), Recall (Eq. 2), F1-score (Eq. 3), mAP50,
mAP5095, and inference time. These metrics, along with runtime analysis, give a
comprehensive overview of model eﬃciency and deployability. For more compre-
hensive evaluation, mAP50 measures detection accuracy with an IoU threshold
of 0.50, while mAP5095 provides a more stringent measure by averaging over
the threshold range (0.50 to 0.95).

Experimental Environment and Conﬁguration
All the training and testing experiments were conducted on a high-performance
laptop NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Laptop GPU with a 16 GB memory, sup-
porting CUDA version 12.5 and driver version 556.12. The PyTorch deep learn-
ing framework was utilized for training models, with CUDA acceleration being
enabled to eﬀectively reduce training process time. Additionally, the deployment
was performed on a Samsung A30 smartphone to validate model suitability for

3
Experimental Results and Discussion
This section presents the experimental results obtained during the evaluation
process. Table 1 summarizes the comparative analysis of the YOLO variants,
highlighting their detection performance and suitability for real-time applica-
tions.
The evaluation highlights the compromise between detection precision and
computational cost. YOLOv10n recorded the best precision of 88.8%, showing
great performance in minimizing false positives. Its relatively lower recall of
78.9% shows that it has some vulnerability to detecting all the positive instances.
YOLOv9t, on the other hand, recorded the best F1-score of 84.3%, showing an
excellent balance even though it had the highest inference time. YOLO11n was
the most balanced model with competitive precision of 87.2% and recall of 80.5%
and the fast inference time of 1.7 ms. Figure 2 represents the Precision-Recall
(PR) curve and the F1-Conﬁdence curve, indicating the quality of detection per
class and exhibiting good balance between precision and recall for all classes.

4
Conclusion and Future Work
This study evaluated and compared four lightweight YOLO models for orange
detection and classiﬁcation in agricultural ﬁeld conditions. The results demon-
strated that YOLOv11n oﬀered the most balanced trade-oﬀbetween accuracy
and computational eﬃciency, making it particularly well suited for real-time, on-
device deployment. Its integration into a mobile application further conﬁrmed
the practical feasibility of such compact deep learning models, enabling real-
time detection, counting, and automated logging through timestamped CSV

reports. These ﬁndings highlight the potential of lightweight architectures to
deliver actionable insights directly at the farm level, supporting decision-making
in environments where connectivity, power, and computational resources are
limited.
Nevertheless, several challenges remain that restrict the robustness and scal-
ability of the proposed system. Environmental variability, such as illumination
changes, occlusions, and background complexity, continues to aﬀect detection
performance, while biases in the training dataset limit generalization across
orchards, fruit varieties, and growth stages. Furthermore, the current pipeline
does not incorporate fruit tracking across frames, which is essential for yield
estimation and to prevent double counting. Future work will therefore focus on
extending the system to include additional fruit categories and disease classes,
integrating temporal tracking to support long-term monitoring, and exploring
multimodal inputs (e.g., depth or multispectral data) to improve resilience under
ﬁeld conditions. Additionally, linking the application to larger farm management
platforms will enhance its role within precision agriculture, contributing to more
intelligent and scalable solutions for smart farming.
