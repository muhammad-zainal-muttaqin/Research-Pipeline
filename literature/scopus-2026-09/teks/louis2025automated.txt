Abstract— Fruit detection and counting in real farm 
environments remain challenging due to occlusion, variable 
lighting, and cluttered backgrounds. This study presents a 
robust computer vision pipeline for accurately detecting, 
tracking, and counting melons using the You Only Look Once 
version 11 nano object detection model. To evaluate and enhance 
performance, multiple dataset versions with different types of 
annotation (large annotation, front-row strict annotation, and 
front-row/rear-row annotation 
distinction) 
were 
utilized 
alongside various image processing pipelines (Roboflow 
workflows, detection, tracking, and line-counting tracking). 
Among these, the 4th method, with a line-counting strategy, 
significantly improved counting accuracy. Our best result was 
achieved with the front row strict annotation and line counting 
tracking combination which yielded a counting error rate of just 
2.74%. The system was tested on footage captured by an 
automated guide vehicle in a greenhouse, demonstrating its 
practical 
applicability 
in 
real-world, 
non-laboratory 
agricultural environments. Overall, this work contributes an 
efficient and accurate melon counting pipeline, laying the 
foundation for future work such as fruit maturity prediction and 
disease detection. 
Keywords— Computer Vision, YOLO, Crops recognition, 
Object Tracking 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Computer recognition in the agricultural field could 
become an important system to enhance productivity and 
reduce waste of farms. With computer recognition, farmers 
could monitor their plants and take decisions based on 
accurate footage and analytics of the past days or month. 
Monitoring data can help farmers in making decisions such as 
making decisions on the optimal moment to have the best 
harvest, detect and contain diseases, estimate the yield of the 
harvest and make optimal operational decisions such as 
advance logistics planning. 
However, before taking any decisions, there must be a way 
to detect and at least to count the fruits in the fields. 
Unfortunately, farms are often not the best environment to 
have computer recognition. Computer vision struggles when 
the background is not uniform and in contrast of the subjects. 
Background of farms are often full of the same crops at a near 
distance on a different row and often be detected as well as 
those at the row in front of the camera. The recognition of 
crops can also be obstructed by themselves, when there are 
leaves are in front of the flower/fruits.  
This work proposes a model and a counting solution based 
on video footage of a melon greenhouse from automated guide 
vehicle (AGV). The proposed system is perfect to cases when 
it comes to recognition and detection of other fruits and 
vegetables in the farm that are suspended similar to the 
muskmelon. Meaning that melons will have a different height, 
like grape-based plants (e.g.: Tomato), improving the chance 
to be switchable to other types of crops. The background is 
also very saturated and confusing due to plants going from the 
floor to the ceiling and with multiples rows in sight. 
In this study, the proposed solution was evaluated against 
standard YOLOv11 methods, yielding reductions in double 
counting, enhanced isolation of back-row fruits, and an 
increase in counting accuracy to 97.26%. The processing time 
measured 153.09 s for an 85 s video on a local machine, 
indicating the partial applicability of the method. 
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
Computer vision has increasingly found practical 
applications in agriculture, ranging from plant identification to 
maturity estimation or disease recognition. Among the many 
architectures used in this field, YOLO (You Only Look Once). 
Zan et al. [1] proposed an improved YOLOv8 model 
(GSSS-YOLO) to classify tobacco leaves into maturity stages 
(under-ripe, ripe, and over-ripe). With a dataset of around 
3,700 annotated images, their model achieved a precision of 
93.3% and a recall of 92.4%, demonstrating YOLO's 
relevance in the precise detection of crops in real-world 
conditions. 
Plant identification is another common use of computer 
vision in agriculture. Zhou et al. [2] developed a system based 
on ResNet-50 and NTS-Net to recognize Tibetan medicinal 
plants across 70 species, achieving a 93.3% success rate on 
over 13,000 images, emphasizing the effectiveness of 
attention-based feature localization in field conditions. 
In addition, Faisal et al. [3] applied CNN models such as 
ResNet, VGG-19, and Inception-V3 to estimate the type, 
maturity, and weight of date fruits. Their system combined 
deep learning with Support Vector Machines (SVMs), 
achieving over 99% accuracy in classification tasks and 
84.27% in weight estimation, highlighting the potential of 
hybrid models in multi-attribute recognition. 
Disease recognition is another area where computer vision 
has shown promise. Liu et al. [4] presented a three-stage 
framework for plant disease identification involving region 
reweighting and feature integration. Trained on over 150,000 
images spanning 271 plant diseases, the model reached a 
90.01% success rate, although its complexity limited real-time 
deployment. 
Barburiceanu et al. [5] explored an alternative approach by 
using pretrained CNNs such as AlexNet, VGG16, and ResNet 
variants for leaf texture feature extraction. Classification was 
performed using SVMs, enabling high accuracy with smaller 
datasets and reduced training requirements. 
Computer vision has also been integrated into robotic 
systems. Hsia et al. [6] implemented a deep learning-based 
leaf detection system using ResNet-50 to automate a leaf-

Beyond object classification, some studies addressed the

While many prior works focused on recognition tasks such

explored object tracking and counting in real-world 
environments.

by

clutter, common challenges noted in the literature but seldom 
directly addressed in counting applications. 
III. METHODOLOGY 
The proposed work is based on the make of multiples 
versions and the usage of two different tools for the 
processing, Roboflow[8] and Ultralytics[9]. Roboflow will be 
to test if a cloud solution to compute the image could be viable, 
and Ultralytics will be to run the models on a local machine.  
In addition to that, there are several annotation strategies 
tested to investigate their impact on detection accuracy. Each 
test is conducted using the same video, which has a duration 
of 1 minute and 25 seconds and contains 73 melons in the front 
row to be detected. The test data is video footage that is not 
used to training. The video  consists of 73 muskmelons and the 
duration of the video is 85 seconds. 
Due to the scarcity of muskmelons datasets online and due 
to limits in number of greenhouses in the area, only 2 videos 
were relevant enough to be used. On the 2 videos, one is used 
to make the dataset while one is used for testing and evaluation 
purposes. 
All local experiments are performed on a laptop equipped 
with an NVIDIA RTX 4060m 8GB GPU and an Intel i5-
12450HX processor. 
A. Datasets Creation 
To train a reliable melon detection model, custom datasets 
were created using Roboflow. The source material consisted 
of a single video from which only 187 relevant images were 
extracted.  
During the project, multiples versions were created: 
• 
V1D: Every visible melon in each image was 
annotated, with at least 75 percent of the melon visible and 
regardless of its position. 
• 
V2D: Only melons in the front row that were clearly 
visible and mostly unobstructed by leaves were annotated. 
This version aimed to reduce false positives by focusing only 
on easily detectable fruits. 
• 
V3D: Melons were annotated in two separate 
categories: front-melon and rear-melon. This version also 
included melons that were partially obscured, at least 50% of 
the fruit needed to be visible. This approach was designed to 
test whether classifying by position would improve detection 
accuracy. 
For each version, the images were split into 3 categories, 
70% for training, 20% for validation and 10% for testing. Of 
the 187 images, there are 131 images for training, 37 for 
validation and 19 for testing. To increase the detection 
accuracy and detection capability, different effects were 
applied to training images: 
• 
Brightness: Between -10% and +10% 
• 
Exposure: Between -10% and +10% 
• 
Outputs per training example: 3 
The brightness and exposure effects serve to increase the 
chance of detection when the lights level varies. 
This means that there are now 392 images for training, for 
a total of 448 images in the dataset. With this custom dataset, 
it is possible to use You Only Look Once version 11 nano 
(YOLOv11n) to create a custom model.  
B. Running the model 
The first running version (V1R) of the trained model 
remained hosted on Roboflow. For images, the model can be 
used directly within Roboflow web interface via its workflow. 
However, when applying the model to video data, a Python 
script is required to process the video frame by frame and then 
send each frame to the workflow. 
To process the video, each frame is extracted from the 
video and sent via the Roboflow API to the workflow, 
returning the frame with annotations. The annotated frame is 
saved on the local computer to reassemble the video once 
every frame is treated 
This version presents some inconveniences, need to be 
online, slow to process and take more space on disk than the 
other solutions. Plus, when moving from Roboflow workflow 
to Ultralytics library, the model couldn’t be exported from 
Roboflow due to the free plan. Only the dataset can be 
downloaded, and the training must be redone on the local 
computer. 
The V2R version is made with the ‘predict’ method from 
Ultralytics library. The video treated is split thanks to the 
OpenCV library. The ‘predict’ method is run on each frame to 
detect all the melons on it and add them to a counter. This 
version does not do persistence between the frames. It is very 
common for a single melon to be counted multiple times.  
The V3R version is made with the ‘track‘ method. The 
video is not split with OpenCV because the track version 
natively supports videos as an input. The ‘track’ method will 
track melons between frames, limiting the amount of 
duplication while counting the melons. This version of 
running the model will keep track of melons until they leave 
the view of the camera. But, if a melon is suddenly obstructed 
by a foreign object for too long the AI will lose tracking, and 
if it reappears later in the video, the ID of the melon will be 
different due to being considered as a new melon while it isn’t. 
This issue can cause multiples case of duplication when 
counting.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 
To fully see what the most impactful parameter between 
the dataset and the type of process is, multiples cross runs will 
be done to test all the datasets with all the types of process. 
Then, a comprehensive analysis was conducted for each 
dataset and type of process. 
A. Datasets Comparisons 
The V1D dataset includes annotations for every visible 
melon in each image, with at least 75 percent of the melon 
visible, regardless of its position. One strength of the dataset 
during the tests is its wide coverage of melons in various 
positions and visibility levels with ease of use thanks to the 
Roboflow platform. However, it has multiple weaknesses. 
One is the high number of false positives due to the inclusion 
of partially obscured melons, another is the inclusion of 
melons from other parts of the farm that are not currently being 
treated. The results in Fig 3 shows that this is the least accurate 
dataset  with 5927 melons detected at most, an 8019,17% error 
rate. And it only managed to get a 56.16% error rate in the best 
case with the V4R method. This suggests that while V1D is 
comprehensive, 
its 
inclusivity 
leads 
to 
significant 
inaccuracies, likely due to the complexity and noise 
introduced by including partially visible and distant melons.  
The V2D dataset focuses exclusively on melons in the 
front row that are clearly visible and mostly unobstructed by 
leaves. A notable strength of the V2D dataset is its higher 
accuracy in detecting clearly visible melons, which reduces 
false positives. However, it has limited capacity when partially 
obscured. The V2D dataset showed significant improvements 
in error rates, particularly when combined with the V4R 
method, achieving the lowest error rate of 2.74%. This 
combination also demonstrated a reasonable processing time 
of 153.09 seconds, making it the most efficient and accurate 
configuration. At worst, it managed to have a 1953.42% error 
rate, much lower than the V1D and can be explained by the 
nature of the process not tracking melons between frames. The 
focused nature of V2D likely contributes to its superior 
performance by minimizing the complexity and potential for 
error in detection and counting. 
The V3D dataset classifies melons into two categories: 
front-melon and rear-melon. A strength of the V3D dataset is 
its detailed classification by position, which potentially 
improves detection accuracy. However, in application, it 
introduces increased complexity in annotation and potential 
for misclassification. The V3D dataset showed varied results, 
with the V3R method achieving a moderate error rate of 
6.85%, but the V2R method still struggled with a high error 
rate of 1763.01%. The mixed results suggest that while 
position-based classification can improve accuracy, it also 
introduces complexities that may not always lead to better 
performance. 
B. Running Method Comparisons 
The V1R running version utilizes the Roboflow hosted 
model. A strength of the V1R method is its utilization of cloud 
computing resources, reducing local computational load. 
However, it has a slow processing time of 52.6 minutes 
(3157.44 seconds) and requires a stable internet connection. 
Thus, it is not suitable for real-time usage due to its long 
processing time. The V1R method, when combined with the 
V1D dataset, resulted in an extremely high error rate of 
8019.17%, making it the least effective combination. Fig 4 
show the huge in computation when compared to other 
solutions. The slow processing time and high error rate 
indicate that cloud-based processing, while resource-efficient, 
introduces significant, likely due to communication overhead. 
It was abandoned as a suitable solution immediately for these 
reasons. 
The V2R version employs the 'predict' method from the 
Ultralytics library. A strength of the V2R method is its faster 
processing time compared to V1R, along with local 
computation. However, as it lacks persistence between frames, 
the number of duplications when counting the melons make 
this solution extremely inaccurate as it manages to get 1360 
melons in its best run, an 1763,01% error rate. But it is at least 
faster than the previous method with 202.35s in computation 
time at the best. The lack of frame persistence likely 
contributes to the high error rates, as melons can be counted 
multiple times, leading to significant inaccuracies.

The V3R version uses the 'track' method from the 
Ultralytics library. A strength of the V3R method is its 
improved tracking between frames and largely reduced 
duplication. However, there is a chance for losing track of 
melons, leading to miscounting. The V3R method 
demonstrated moderate error rates, with the V2D dataset 
achieving an error rate of 21.92% and the V3D dataset 
achieving 6.85%. The processing times were also reasonable 
and very close to each run, ranging from 184.90 to 188.18 
seconds. Showing great stability with any dataset. The 
improved tracking capabilities of V3R likely contribute to its 
moderate error rates, but the potential for losing track of 
melons still shows some inaccuracies and potential 
improvement. 
The V4R version counts melons as they cross a line, 
ensuring that each melon is counted only once. A strength of 
the V4R method is its accurate counting with minimal 
duplication, making it suitable for real-world applications. 
However, it has a slightly slower processing time compared to 
V3R, and there is a potential for missing a melon if it is 
obstructed when passing the line. The V4R method showed 
the most promising results, particularly when combined with 
the V2D dataset, achieving the lowest error rate of 2.74% and 
a processing time of 153.09 seconds. Even with other datasets 
and when compared to same results with other methods, the 
V4R method demonstrated reasonable error rates, such as 
56.16% with the V1D dataset and 19.18% with the V3D 
dataset. The line-counting strategy of V4R ensures accurate 
and consistent counting, making it the most effective method 
for real-world applications despite its slightly slower 
processing time.

C. Point of reference 
As a point of reference, a benchmark with the basic 
YOLOv11n model has been made with two of the best running 
methods, the V3R and the V4R.  
When running the model, a few issues were observed. 
First, YOLOv11 detects the crops as oranges, not melons, and 
second, the model struggles to keep a melon detected, even 
when not obstructed. 
The results in TABLE 2 show that when used with the V3R 
running method, it achieved a processing time of 196.26 
seconds and detected 112 melons with an error rate of 
53.425%. This indicates that while the V3R method is 
relatively fast, it still struggles with accuracy, likely due to the 
lack of a specialized dataset tailored to the specific conditions 
of the melon greenhouse. The error rate suggests that the 
model overcounts melons, possibly due to tracking issues and 
misclassifications. 
On the other hand, the YOLOv11n model combined with 
the V4R running method showed a slightly faster processing 
time of 186.65 seconds but detected significantly fewer 
melons, with a count of 37 for  an error rate of 49.315%. This 
undercounting could be attributed to the line-counting strategy 
of V4R and to the model struggling to detect “oranges” even 
without obstruction. 
Overall, the benchmark results with the basic YOLOv11n 
model highlight the importance of using specialized datasets 
and advanced running methods to achieve accurate, constant, 
and more efficient melon detection and counting. The custom 
datasets developed above significantly outperform the basic 
YOLOv11n model and serve to add correct labelling to the 
subjects.

V. CONCLUSION 
In conclusion, this study shows it is possible to have an 
automated crop detection and counting system in a non-lab 
environment while having a minimal error rate, especially 
when combining a strict detection dataset with a line counting 
system to ensure no duplication and accurate counting. It also 
shows the importance to have custom datasets to ensure 
smooth operation and correct labelling.  
The results obtained point to several areas for 
improvement. First, the tracking could be upgraded to better 
handle temporary occlusions. Second, the time taken to 
compute is almost twice as long as the original video, such a 
long time makes the actual system unable to be used in real 
time. In third, with only 392 images for training, the datasets 
are considered small. A small dataset can limit the results and 
tend gave worse results, higher computation time due to 
having more difficulties when rare cases or partially obscured 
cases occurred. A larger dataset could help for computation 
speed and accuracy with a bonus point to demonstrate the 
practical usage if the larger dataset make use of different 
environments. 
Furthermore, the practical deployment of such a system 
raises the question of infrastructure design: should the 
processing be performed locally on the device capturing the 
video, on a dedicated server within the farm, or through a 
cloud-based solution? Each of these options presents trade-
offs in terms of latency, operational costs, connectivity 
requirements, and scalability.
