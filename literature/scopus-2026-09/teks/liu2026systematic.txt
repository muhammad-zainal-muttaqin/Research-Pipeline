A B S T R A C T
With the development of smart agriculture, automatic on-plant maturity grading of tomatoes has become a key 
factor in optimizing harvesting decisions. Based on the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, this study systematically re­
views 42 high-quality papers published before September 10, 2025, aiming to address the fragmentation of 
existing research in terms of methodology, datasets, and evaluation criteria. The study found that: (1) the 
technical path has achieved a leap from manual features to end-to-end deep learning (such as R-CNN, YOLO, and 
Transformer), significantly improving detection performance in complex environments; (2) datasets are still 
mainly self-built, generally facing challenges such as inconsistent annotation standards and a lack of scene di­
versity; (3) evaluation criteria have shifted from a single accuracy indicator to a multi-dimensional balance of 
"accuracy-power consumption-latency". The conclusion indicates that lightweight model design and edge 
deployment are the core of realizing the transformation of laboratory technology to the field. Future research 
should focus on the construction of large-scale multimodal datasets, robustness enhancement in complex 
backgrounds, and multi-task collaboration in harvesting decisions. This study provides a systematic technical 
reference for the intelligent upgrading of the tomato industry.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background
The tomato (Solanum lycopersicon L.) is a nutrient-dense food rich in 
lycopene, flavonoids, and vitamins [1], the consumption of which is 
associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular diseases and certain 
cancers [2]. As shown in Fig. 1, the planting area and yield of tomatoes 
are both enormous. However, approximately 20% to 30% of fruits and 
vegetables suffer postharvest losses annually due to inaccurate maturity 
classification and improper harvesting timing, with tomatoes being 
among the most severely affected commodities [3]. Achieving accurate 
identification and maturity grading is therefore crucial for agronomic 
management, as it determines optimal harvest timing, enhances fruit 
quality, and optimizes labor allocation [4]. Furthermore, analyzing 
maturity distribution patterns provides vital insights for phenotypic 
detection and variety improvement [5]. Understanding tomato maturity 
is particularly vital for various downstream applications in the tomato 
industry, as fruit maturity directly impacts processing quality and pro­
duction efficiency [6].
While existing literature extensively covers postharvest sorting in 
controlled laboratory environments, the practical implementation of 
robotic harvesting necessitates accurate "on-plant" maturity classifica­
tion. In this review, "on-plant" strictly refers to the state where fruits 
remain physically connected to their parent plants in natural growing 
environments, such as greenhouses or open fields. Unlike postharvest 
scenarios characterized by uniform lighting and homogeneous back­
grounds, on-plant detection faces significant real-world challenges, 
including dynamic natural light, complex backgrounds, and severe oc­
clusion [7,8]. As Espinoza et al. [9] demonstrated, these uncontrolled 
environmental factors significantly impact the generalization ability of 
computer vision models. Consequently, this review focuses exclusively 
on how modern deep learning architectures overcome the noise and 
occlusion challenges unique to pre-harvest operations, excluding studies 
dedicated solely to postharvest quality control.

2. Related Work
In recent years, the application of computer vision (CV) technology 
in agriculture has garnered significant global attention. CV technology 
finds extensive use in agriculture, encompassing object recognition and 
localization, counting, harvesting, and monitoring [21]. Furthermore, 
the operational scope of CV has expanded from stationary ground sen­
sors to Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), which provide a multi-angle, 
high-throughput perspective for large-scale orchard monitoring and 
crop health assessment [22,23]. Leveraging CV to discern tomato ripe­
ness constitutes a cornerstone of modern crop management. Contem­
porary literature reveals a broad spectrum of diagnostic strategies, 
ranging from heuristic image processing techniques to state-of-the-art 
Deep Learning (DL) models, particularly Convolutional Neural Net­
works (CNNs). These technological innovations offer the requisite 
computational fidelity to underpin smart farming initiatives, substan­
tially enhancing the efficacy and throughput of automated sorting eco­
systems [24].
Fundamentally, CV functions as a synthetic surrogate for biological 
perception, delegating the ocular mechanism to optical instrumentation 
and the cognitive interpretation to computational algorithms. The ar­
chitecture of CV is bipartite, predicated upon high-fidelity image 
acquisition hardware and sophisticated software-based processing 
pipelines [25]. Beyond local processing, contemporary agricultural CV 
architectures are increasingly integrating Internet of Things (IoT) sen­
sors and cloud-based computing platforms. These cloud-integrated sys­
tems facilitate the storage and processing of massive datasets, enabling 
remote real-time decision-making and collaborative farming manage­
ment. To manage the massive data streams generated by these plat­
forms, cloud-based applications- such as Agroview and dedicated 
harvest management information systems- have been developed to 
provide integrated environments for real-time data analysis, visualiza­
tion, and precision management of specialty crops [26,27]. As the 
quality of raw data establishes the upper bound for analytical accuracy, 
the acquisition phase—mediated by cameras and sensors—is critical. 
Subsequent processing entails a cascade of operations, including noise 
filtration, adaptive segmentation, and feature extraction, designed to 
distill semantic information from visual data [28]. Within the agrarian 
domain, this technology has evolved into a linchpin for automation, 
enabling precise, non-invasive crop management. Fueled by the synergy 
of high-throughput computing and extensive annotated datasets, Deep 
Learning models have precipitated a paradigm shift in fruit recognition 
[29–35]. These data-driven approaches now demonstrate exceptional 
efficacy in tasks ranging from varietal identification to the granular 
assessment of tomato phenotypic traits [36].

Algorithms such as Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), clustering

However, these deterministic models exhibit significant fragility when 
deployed in unstructured agricultural environments. Their predictive

most notably variable ambient illumination, background clutter, and 
severe occlusion by foliage [20]. These environmental constraints are

variable lighting and foliage interference, have been extensively 
addressed in other fruit crops. For instance, recent integrated systems for

potential of DL to overcome in-field noise and achieve robust sorting 
performance [37,38]. In contrast, DL-based digital image processing 
techniques offer numerous advantages over traditional approaches. DL 
models can autonomously learn complex features within images, 
achieving high classification accuracy and robust performance. They 
effectively adapt to varying lighting conditions, complex backgrounds, 
and changes in viewing angles [39].
The formidable learning capacity of DL models stems from their 
high-dimensional parameter spaces, which allow for the autonomous 
extraction of latent features from complex datasets. This architectural 
plasticity enables DL to address multifaceted agricultural objectives, 
ranging from biotic stress quantification to yield forecasting, without 
relying on manual feature engineering [40]. The efficacy of transfer 
learning has been particularly evident in cross-platform applications, 
where models pre-trained on generic datasets were successfully 
fine-tuned for specialized tasks, such as aerial crop classification and 
cloud-based disease diagnostics [41]. However, the efficacy of these 
models is contingent upon the precise calibration of hyperparameters, 
such as network depth, learning rates, and activation functions. Given 
that designing bespoke architectures requires prohibitive computational 
resources and specialized expertise, a pragmatic consensus has emerged: 
leveraging pre-trained architectures validated on large-scale datasets 
and adapting them via transfer learning [40]. Among these, CNNs serve 
as the paradigmatic framework for visual analysis. By hierarchically 
stacking convolutional and pooling operations, CNNs effectively encode 
spatial dependencies, making them the de facto standard for processing 
the massive image streams generated in modern agriculture [42].
Rather than conducting a granular recitation of isolated case studies, 
this manuscript presents a holistic synthesis and critical appraisal of 
contemporary technologies deployed for tomato maturity assessment. 
The analysis prioritizes methodological architecture and operational 
challenges over mere experimental enumeration, aiming to establish a 
robust framework for designing effective recognition systems. To ensure 
methodological rigor and attenuate the subjectivity inherent in narra­
tive reviews, we adhered to the SLR protocol. Consequently, this study 
dissects the application of DL through five strategic dimensions: algo­
rithmic modalities, dataset composition, evaluation metrics, practical 
implementation, and the trajectory of future research frontiers.
3. Research Methodology
To ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility, this 
systematic review was orchestrated in strict alignment with the PRISMA 
2020 guidelines. The adoption of this standardized protocol provides a 
robust scaffolding for the study, serving three critical functions: (i) the 
precise formulation of systematic Research Questions (RQs); (ii) the 
rigorous demarcation of inclusion and exclusion criteria to mitigate 
selection bias; (iii) the systematic interrogation of extensive biblio­
graphic databases within a defined temporal scope; and (iv) the stan­
dardized extraction and quality assessment of the synthesized data [43,
44].
3.1. Research Questions
In accordance with the PRISMA protocol, we defined a series of RQs 
to systematically evaluate the current state of deep learning applications 
in tomato ripeness classification. Based on this, this study developed 
four key RQs, as shown in Table 1. These questions formed the foun­
dational framework for the literature review, data extraction, and sub­
sequent results synthesis. They also guide the investigation of 
methodologies, datasets, performance metrics, and future challenges in 
the field of tomato ripeness classification.
3.2. Search Strategy
The systematic literature search interrogated seven premier elec­
tronic databases: Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Scopus, 
Springer, Wiley, and the ACM Digital Library. These platforms were 
selected for their comprehensive coverage of high-impact journal arti­
cles and conference proceedings. The retrieval window concluded on 
September 10, 2025, capturing all relevant English-language studies 
published before this date.
To ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant literature, we con­
structed a multi-layered search algorithm based on Boolean logic. As 
shown in Table 2, keywords were divided into three main topic groups 
(deep learning, tomato, and maturity stage) and combined using the 
"OR" and "AND" operators. This structured approach enabled us to sys­
tematically search seven electronic databases while maintaining high 
retrieval rates and precision. To accommodate ScienceDirect's syntax 
limitations (which restrict the number of Boolean connectors), we 
adopted a segmented search strategy, executing eight different iterative 
subqueries to ensure comprehensive coverage. This rigorous process 
ultimately yielded an initial corpus containing 683 articles.
3.3. Selection Criteria and Process
The preliminary search across seven electronic databases yielded a 
total of 683 documents. The numerical distribution of these initially 
retrieved records is illustrated in Fig. 2, which highlights the predomi­
nant contribution of databases such as Scopus (n = 438) and Science­
Direct (n = 107) to the initial corpus.
These 683 documents were imported into EndNote for deduplication 
and subsequent screening in strict accordance with the PRISMA 2020 
guidelines. To ensure a focused and high-quality analysis, we imple­
mented a multi-stage screening process based on the stringent Inclusion 
and Exclusion Criteria (IC/ EC) delineated in Table 3, which defines the 
specific constraints for filtering out postharvest studies and laboratory- 
based experiments.

portion of the excluded records consisted of studies conducted in post­
harvest sorting facilities or fully controlled laboratory environments (EC 
9). These studies were removed because they fail to encounter the 
complex environmental challenges—such as dynamic lighting, variable 
backgrounds, and foliage occlusion—that are inherent in real-time, in- 
field orchard monitoring. Following this rigorous filtration, the final

3.4. Quality Verification
To ensure the reliability of the review, the screening and selection 
process was conducted using a cross-verification protocol. While the 
primary screening was performed by the lead author using EndNote and 
Excel, a secondary audit of a random sample of the excluded papers 
(20%) was conducted by another researcher to verify consistency with 
the established IC/ EC criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through 
consensus meetings.
Following the final selection, a standardized data extraction form 
was utilized to synthesize information from the 42 papers. The extracted 
parameters included: (1) Contextual data (target tomato variety, culti­
vation environment); (2) Technical data (DL architecture, backbone 
network, optimization algorithms); (3) Dataset details (size, annotation 
method, public vs. private status); and (4) Evaluation data (Accuracy, 
Precision, Recall, F1-score, and mAP). This structured approach ensured 
that the subsequent analysis and discussion were based on a robust and 
uniform data foundation.

4.1. RQ 1: What are the existing DL-based approaches currently available 
for classifying the maturity of on-plant tomato fruits?
As a globally significant crop, the tomato's maturity directly de­
termines its food safety, market value, and optimal harvest timing [45]. 
Accurately and rapidly classifying tomato maturity on-plant is therefore 
crucial for enhancing yield quality and ensuring profitability in modern 
agriculture [46].
4.1.1. Original Method
Initial tomato maturity classification primarily relied on the sensory 
perception and manual assessment of workers [14,47]. While these 
traditional methods offer low costs and simple operation, they suffer 
from strong subjectivity, low efficiency, and poor consistency [48]. Such 
limitations make it difficult to meet the rigorous demands for precision 
and continuity in modern large-scale farming [49].
Furthermore, tomatoes within the same cluster do not mature 
simultaneously. If mature fruits are not harvested promptly, they rot and 
adversely affect the surrounding immature ones. Conversely, simple 
mechanical harvesting lacks the necessary selectivity and intelligence, 
often leading to the inadvertent inclusion of approximately 20% 
immature tomatoes during the picking process [46]. This creates sub­
stantial challenges for post-harvest management and potential hazards 
for food consumption.
As labor costs rise and the demand for high-efficiency production 
grows, the advantages of automation have become increasingly evident. 
Consequently, developing precise and automated classification tech­
nologies has become an inevitable trend to ensure selective harvesting 
and optimize agricultural output [36,50].
4.1.2. Traditional Methods
Conventional CV paradigms have historically served as the founda­
tional framework for tomato maturity assessment [2]. Unlike end-to-end 
deep learning, these systems utilize a bifurcated workflow predicated on 
explicit "hand-crafted" descriptors. This methodology requires sequen­
tial execution: a feature abstraction phase—utilizing color-based

segmentation [51], dimensionality reduction via Principal Component 
Analysis (PCA) -based dimensionality reduction [52], or spatial variable 
calculation [53]—followed by a classification phase using deterministic 
algorithms like Decision Trees (DT) [54], Support Vector Machines 
(SVM) [55], and shallow Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) [56].
Early research prioritized chromatic indicators to quantify pheno­
logical stages. Choi et al. [57] pioneered the Tomato Maturity Index 
(TMI) to correlate hue with maturation, while Darrigues et al. [58] 
utilized the Tomato Analyzer-Color Test (TACT) for uniformity. How­
ever, these reductionist approaches often failed to account for volu­
metric morphology, gloss, or ambient light fluctuations. To improve 
robustness, researchers moved toward complex color-space trans­
formations and logic. Yin et al. [59] and Huang et al. [60] leveraged 
L*a*b* domains combined with K-means or fuzzy logic to decouple fruit 
from greenhouse backgrounds. Similarly, Goel and Sehgal [54] estab­
lished a six-level RGB (Red, Green, Blue) grading system, and Qing et al. 
[61] optimized robotic localization via HSI conversion.
As the field evolved, spatial and morphological strategies were in­
tegrated to address structural complexity. Zhang [62] applied Canny 
[63] operators for edge detection, and Lin et al. [64] used a probabilistic 
Hough Transform to generalize fruit detection in natural settings. 
Despite high laboratory accuracy—such as Arefi et al. (2011) reaching 
96.36% and Khoshroo and Khodaei [65] achieving 82.38%—these sys­
tems struggled with occlusions and dynamic lighting.
Recent hybrid methodologies have attempted to fuse multiple fea­
tures to bolster performance. Zhao et al. [66] utilized AdaBoost and 
multi-feature fusion (93% accuracy), while Indriani et al. (2017) and 
Sari et al. [67] integrated HSV/YCbCr color data with GLCM textural 
descriptors and Otsu’s thresholding. Furthermore, Liu et al. [68] and Wu 
et al. [69] introduced illumination enhancement and automated classi­
fication algorithms, achieving precisions near 95%.
Despite these advancements, traditional methods remain con­
strained by their reliance on manual feature engineering. As noted by 
Yamamoto et al. [70], El-Bendary et al. [48], and Malik et al. [71], the 
lack of self-learning capabilities renders these models susceptible to 
environmental noise and restricts their adaptability to "in-situ" agricul­
tural settings. High computational overhead [69], lack of depth 
perception [72], and poor generalization in unstructured environments 
[73] underscore the limitations of non-deep learning approaches. Ulti­
mately, while automated maturity assessment is essential for maxi­
mizing yield and reducing labor costs in robotic harvesting [74], 
traditional paradigms are increasingly inadequate for the demands of 
complex, real-world dynamics.
4.1.3. Deep Learning Methods
In recent years, with the rise of CV technology, researchers have 
attempted various methods to detect fruits and classify their maturity. 
Traditional approaches typically require manual feature extraction, and 
conventional image processing is sensitive to environmental variations. 
Moreover, under complex background conditions, traditional methods 
relying on edge detection and contour extraction exhibit poor robust­
ness, making it difficult to accurately extract objects. Furthermore, ap­
proaches relying on manually designed filters for texture feature 
extraction not only result in inefficient computation but also struggle 
when processing large-scale texture samples [16].
Driven by the architectural advantages of local receptive fields and 
parameter sharing, CNNs have become the de facto standard in 
computational linguistics and CV [75]. The genesis of this paradigm 
traces back to the Neocognitron proposed by Fukushima [76], which 
laid the groundwork for a lineage of progressive refinements. This 
evolutionary path spanned from the foundational LeNet [77] and 
AlexNet [78] to deeper, more complex topologies such as GoogleNet 
[79] and ResNet [80]. The subsequent introduction of Region-based 
CNNs (R-CNN) [81] solidified the dominance of these architectures in 
digital image analysis [82].
Despite this deep learning surge, heuristic and hybrid methodologies 
have retained significant utility in agricultural applications. For 
example, Goel et al. [54] engineered a Fuzzy Rule-Based Classification 
System (FRBCS), synergizing Decision Trees with Mamdani logic to 
achieve a 94.29% classification precision across six maturity tiers. In 
parallel, Pacheco et al. [83] exploited statistical color descriptors (in 
RGB, HSI, and Lab* spaces) to train classical algorithms like K-NN and 
MLP for varietal differentiation. A comprehensive benchmarking of 
these shallow learning approaches was conducted by Kaur et al. [84], 
who identified Random Forest (92.49% accuracy) as the superior clas­
sifier. Specific use-cases were further refined by Sherafati et al. [56] for 
individual fruit assessment and Goyal et al. [85], who integrated color 
intensity modeling into robotic harvesters for real-time shelf-life 
prediction.
Reverting to the deep learning frontier, Alam et al. [86] demon­
strated the capacity of advanced DL models to navigate complex 
greenhouse environments. Building on this, Zhang et al. [10] introduced 
a sophisticated framework incorporating geometric data augmentation 
and t-SNE-based outlier rejection. This system is notable for balancing 
high accuracy (91.9%) with sub-0.01 second inference latency, under­
scoring its potential for scalable precision agriculture.
Traditional image processing technologies face limitations in scal­
ability, struggling to adapt to diverse fruits and varying agricultural 
environments. Each new application or environmental change necessi­
tates redesigning and modifying algorithms [19]. These shortcomings 
have driven researchers to increasingly adopt deep learning techniques 
[87].
DL evolved from traditional machine learning and can automatically 
extract features from data. It often outperforms conventional machine 
learning in numerous tasks. By integrating multi-layer abstraction, it 
achieves hierarchical data representation [88,89], enhancing analytical 
capabilities for numerous large-scale data processing tasks [90,91]. 
Deep learning models refine data representations through multi-layer 
neural networks composed of neurons representing various functions. 
Neurons across layers are connected by weights that automatically 
adjust during training. By integrating multiple layers, deep models 
effectively tackle complex real-world problems [89]. Gradually, DL has 
become one of the most widely adopted methods today, emerging as a 
novel solution in the agricultural sector. Its success stems from its high 
level of abstraction and its ability to automatically learn complex fea­
tures within images [92].
4.1.3.1. R-CNN Series Methods. The primary architecture for models 
detecting tomato fruit maturity is the CNN [77], which utilizes con­
volutional operations in at least one layer. Due to weight sharing and the 
use of more complex models that enable large-scale parallelization, 
CNNs learn and interpret complex, large-scale problems more rapidly 
[93]. Researchers use CNN-based models to perceive each pixel, 
enabling tasks such as object classification and localization. These 
models are commonly used for object detection, recognition, and clas­
sification tasks. Similarly, deep learning approaches based on CNNs 
demonstrate outstanding performance in fruit maturity identification, 
effectively mitigating or even resolving adverse factors encountered in 
practical application scenarios [94].
R-CNN is a significant branch and research direction within CNNs. 
Proposed by Girshick et al. [81], it achieved a mean average precision 
(mAP) of 58.5% on the PASCAL VOC 2007 dataset, marking the first 
successful application of DL in this domain. As the pioneering algorithm 
applying deep learning to object detection, it integrates techniques such 
as CNN, SVM, and bounding box regression to identify object categories 
and locations within images. The algorithmic workflow comprises four 
stages: candidate region extraction, feature extraction, classification, 
and bounding box refinement. Approximately 2000 candidate regions 
are generated via Selective Search. Each region is resized before being 
fed into the CNN for feature vector extraction. A multi-class SVM clas­
sifier determines object categories, while a bounding box regression

model refines candidate box positions. The core innovation lies in

significantly higher detection accuracy compared to conventional

Hsieh et al. [95] innovatively combined the R-CNN deep detection 
model with binocular imaging technology to identify the location and 
estimate the size of mature tomatoes. However, the model's substantial 
computational demands, coupled with limitations in system real-time 
performance and hardware adaptability, hinder satisfactory accuracy 
under complex conditions. Furthermore, it lacks generalized validation 
across different tomato varieties and growth stages. Although R-CNN 
represents a significant advancement, it suffers from inherent limita­
tions: slow detection speed, complex training, and high storage 
demands.
Therefore, Fast R-CNN, proposed by Girshick et al. [96], incorporates 
the concept of “shared convolutional features,” achieving substantial 
improvements in detection efficiency. It leverages the powerful feature 
extraction capabilities of CNNs to transform input images into 
high-dimensional feature representations, then generates a series of 
candidate regions. These regions undergo size normalization via RoI 
Pooling layers before being fed into fully connected layers. For instance, 
Rajagopal et al. [97] developed a system based on Fast R-CNN to eval­
uate tomato quality. This approach facilitated a shift from traditional 
manual feature extraction to DL-based methods, significantly enhancing 
classification accuracy. However, limitations still exist: training data is 
limited and insufficiently accounts for factors like lighting variations. 
Additionally, the heavy architecture poses challenges for real-time 
performance, while its generalization capabilities require further 
validation.
Although Fast R-CNN represented an improvement over R-CNN, its 
candidate regions were still generated by Selective Search, meaning the 
speed bottleneck remained in the external generation process. Conse­
quently, Faster R-CNN [98] was proposed. To address the issue of 
interference factors in tomato maturity classification, researchers widely 
applied it to this task [12]. Faster R-CNN introduced the Region Proposal 
Network (RPN), eliminating the external Selective Search used in R-CNN 
and Fast R-CNN. This makes the candidate region generation process 
learnable and enables end-to-end optimization.
In the pursuit of enhanced feature representation, Sun et al. [12] 
engineered a refined CNN-based architecture for organ detection. While 
this model demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in Mean 
Average Precision (mAP), its practical utility remains constrained by 
limited robustness in unstructured environments. Conversely, seeking to 
integrate uncertainty handling, Hu et al. [99] formulated a hybrid 
framework merging Faster R-CNN with intuitionistic fuzzy sets. 
Although designed for the automatic localization of ripe tomatoes, this 
methodology is intrinsically limited by its focus on solitary fruit detec­
tion. Consequently, the approach suffers from high computational 
complexity, rendering it suboptimal for real-time applications. Mu et al. 
[100] developed a Faster R-CNN-based tomato detection model for 
detecting, counting, and estimating the size of green tomatoes. The 
model's ability to recover features in occluded areas still relies heavily 
on large-scale data, resulting in limited generalization. Widiyanto et al. 
[101] applied the Faster R-CNN framework to tomato detection, main­
taining high recognition accuracy even in complex backgrounds. How­
ever, the model has high parameter complexity and lacks 
comprehensive validation for small targets. Wang et al. [102] designed 
the MatDet model based on Faster R-CNN for multi-objective classifi­
cation. The high computational requirements of this model remain a 
major challenge.
Although Faster R-CNN-based models are generally capable of 
achieving efficient object detection, in many practical tasks, simply 
knowing "where the bounding box is" is not enough. Obtaining the 
precise contour of an object remains challenging. Thus, Mask R-CNN 
emerged to relieve or even resolve this need. Mask R-CNN represents a 
pivotal advancement; building upon the Faster R-CNN framework, it 
incorporates the ROIAlign module and a pixel-level mask branch. This 
enables the network to simultaneously detect objects and output seg­
mentation masks, unifying object detection with instance segmentation. 
The model significantly enhances the ability to recognize precise object 
boundaries, marking a crucial milestone in scene understanding.
Huang et al. [103] proposed a Mask R-CNN model combining fuzzy 
C-means clustering and Hough transform. Due to its complex structure, 
this model requires significant investment in training and deployment. 
Rahim et al. [104] used a Mask R-CNN model that combined data syn­
thesis and color analysis to segment and count tomatoes. However, the 
realism of the synthesized data remained limited, resulting in short­
comings in generalization. Minagawa et al. [105] used Mask R-CNN to 
detect tomato clusters; since the test images were taken during the day, 
changes in ambient lighting can affect the model's performance. Nour­
bakhsh et al. [106] developed a model based on Mask R-CNN. The high 
computational complexity limited its lightweight deployment, and the 
small dataset reduced the model's robustness. Tomas et al. [107] built a 
model using Mask R-CNN and VGG16. Due to its relatively large size, 
this model is not suitable for real-time deployment. Furthermore, it 
cannot handle issues such as severe occlusion. Liu et al. [108] proposed 
the "Attribute-Labeled Object Detection" (OD-AT) model; however, the 
robustness of this model in complex scenarios still needs to be improved.
The detection processes of R-CNN-based algorithms can be summa­
rized as a two-step procedure. First, candidate regions are generated 
(Region Proposal Stage). In the R-CNN series, traditional algorithms like 
Selective Search generate approximately 2000 candidate regions, while 
Faster R-CNN upgrades this to an automatically generated Region Pro­
posal Network (RPN), making it an end-to-end trainable component. 
This is followed by the Detection/Refinement Stage, where the candi­
date regions undergo classification and location regression. This entire 
process requires extracting features from each candidate region indi­
vidually, resulting in massive computational demands that pose signif­
icant challenges for edge device applications. Additionally, their 
complex training workflow and heavy reliance on external algorithms 
lead to slow detection speeds and insufficient robustness. Furthermore, 
their poor performance in detecting small and densely packed objects 
makes them prone to missed detections or false positives in complex 
backgrounds.
4.1.3.2. YOLO Series Methods. The advent of the You Only Look Once 
(YOLO) framework [109] marked a paradigm shift in object detection, 
establishing a benchmark for real-time inference efficiency. Diverging 
fundamentally from the 'two-stage' methodology characteristic of 
R-CNN architectures, YOLO implements a unified 'one-stage' protocol. 
By circumventing the computationally expensive region proposal gen­
eration step, the algorithm performs spatial discretization via grid par­
titioning. It simultaneously regresses bounding box coordinates and 
predicts class probabilities across the entire image in a single forward 
pass.
This capability for high-speed, end-to-end processing has driven 
significant advancements in agricultural automation, specifically push­
ing the boundaries of precision and efficiency in fruit detection 
[109–114]. However, a structural dichotomy exists. As YOLO is inher­
ently designed as a generic, multi-class detector, it suffers from archi­
tectural over-provisioning when applied to the specific, limited-class 
domain of fruit maturity. This results in a superfluous parametric load, 
which not only inflates model size but also introduces risks of over­
fitting. Consequently, model compression and optimization are pre­
requisites to balancing computational efficiency with detection accuracy 
in practical deployment [115].
Liu et al. [116] proposed a dense architecture based on YOLOv3 to 
establish a YOLO-Tomato model. The improved model performs 
particularly well under complex environmental conditions, but its 
detection of small targets still needs further improvement. Chen et al.

4.1.3.3. Transformer Series and Other Methods. Vaswani et al. [145] first 
introduced the Transformer architecture, which began to be widely 
adopted in both NLP and CV fields. The Transformer is a model archi­
tecture based on the “self-attention mechanism.” It does not directly 
correspond to ‘stages’ or “anchor box strategies,” but rather serves as a 
core component for feature extraction or inference. Since its introduc­
tion to the CV field in 2020, it has rapidly become a crucial technical 
approach in this domain.
Carion et al. [146] introduced DETR (Detection with Transformers), 
pioneering the use of Transformers for object detection tasks. Doso­
vitskiy et al. [147] also introduced ViT (Vision Transformer), drawing 
heavily on the Transformer model architecture from NLP to tackle 
challenges in the image domain. Building upon DETR and ViT, a series of 
rapid improvements emerged, focusing on efficiency and effectiveness. 
For instance, Han et al. [148] introduced the TNT model while Wang 
et al. [149] built a Transformer model based on the idea of predicting 
each pixel and simultaneously assigning a label. Since then, new models 
and ideas based on Transformers have begun to emerge explosively.
Pardede et al. [150] combined Transfer Learning with VGG16 for 
tomato fruit maturity classification. However, the model suffers from 
large parameter counts and limited adaptability. Begum et al. [6] used 
pre-trained deep learning models to classify tomato maturity levels. 
While the model exhibited robust performance, the study lacked robust 
analysis for complex scenarios and was relatively deficient in light­
weight implementation. Rong et al. [151] developed an algorithm based 
on an improved Swin Transformer V2. This method relied on manual 
annotation, limiting its generalization ability in complex backgrounds. 
Wang et al. [152] developed a Transformer-based R-CNN model; how­
ever, the lack of validation for occluded areas and its high computational 
cost limited its application potential. Khan et al. [153] combined the 
advantages of CNNs and Transformers; however, this model still suffers 
from high complexity and slow inference speed. Shinoda et al. [154]

dependencies, it is insensitive to fine-grained features such as local 
texture, and its performance is unstable in complex scenes. Due to the 
large number of stacked layers, the model's decision-making process is 
difficult to interpret, and the balance between accuracy, speed, and 
parameter count remains immature.
4.1.4. Comparative Synthesis of Model Robustness and Efficiency
The preceding sections provided a structural overview of various 
deep learning architectures, but a comprehensive cross-sectional anal­
ysis is crucial for evaluating their practical effectiveness in unstructured 
"field" environments. The main challenge in tomato maturity classifi­
cation lies not only in theoretical accuracy on static datasets but also in 
the inherent robustness of models to environmental noise. Based on our 
comprehensive comparative analysis (see Table 4), this section evalu­
ates the reviewed research across three key dimensions: occlusion 
handling, robustness to illumination variations, and the fundamental 
trade-offs between global context and real-time deployment.
As shown in Table 4, we synthesized the core architectural in­
novations, performance metrics, and key trade-offs of representative 
deep learning models. This comparative table does not merely list ac­
curacy scores but focuses on how different base models address specific 
unstructured complexities, providing data-driven support for subse­
quent deep analysis.
4.1.4.1. Treatment of shading by branches and leaves and overlapping 
fruit. Severe occlusion by foliage and overlapping fruits remains a pri­
mary cause of feature fragmentation in traditional CNNs. As indicated in 
our analysis, highly compressed lightweight models, such as GPC-YOLO 
[87], inevitably experienced increased miss rates when facing extreme 
occlusion due to reduced parameter capacity. To address this, current 
research has shifted towards capturing global contextual information. 
For instance, the Transformer-based RT-DETR [156] and SegFormer 
architectures [169] broke the inherent limitations of the CNN receptive 
field, demonstrating SOTA robustness against occlusion. Similarly, 
integrating self-attention mechanisms into CNNs, as seen in PCIA-YOLO 
[135], successfully locked onto features within occlusion gaps. This in­
dicates that relying solely on local texture extraction is insufficient for 
plant environments.
4.1.4.2. Robustness to complex illumination. Greenhouse and field envi­
ronments introduce high-frequency dynamic lighting and harsh 
shadows. Traditional color-space models are highly sensitive to these 
variations and frequently fail. While deep learning models are generally 
more robust, model compression can compromise this stability. For 
example, overly pruned convolutional networks lacked the parameter 
volume to resist lighting noise in certain tests [140]. Conversely, 
advanced architectures employing bidirectional feature pyramids, such 
as YOLOv11-SLBA [141], showed extreme stability under alternating 
strong light and heavy shadows. Furthermore, models leveraging Vision 
Transformers (ViTs) or global attention maintain high accuracy in un­
even illumination because they evaluate the entire plant structure rather 
than isolated pixel intensity fluctuations.
4.1.4.3. The trade-off between global context and real-time edge 
deployment. The comparative synthesis reveals a critical trade-off be­
tween algorithmic robustness and hardware constraints. While 
Transformer-based models [154,155] e excel in handling occlusion and 
lighting through massive global receptive fields, their high computa­
tional density and memory bandwidth requirements make them un­
suitable for low-power edge devices. On the extreme opposite,

4.3. RQ 3: What are the indicator matrics for evaluating model 
performance?
To comprehensively evaluate the performance and effectiveness of 
algorithms, most studies apply a combination of qualitative analysis and 
quantitative evaluation. Qualitative analysis primarily studies the at­
tributes and characteristics of objects from a conceptual perspective, 
focusing on description and explanation to understand the essence of the 
data. Quantitative evaluation investigates the numerical relationships of 
objects, focusing on measuring system performance through statistical 
indicators. For the task of classifying tomato maturity, the process is 
typically decomposed into two sub-tasks: first, the model locates the 
tomatoes in the image (object detection), and second, it classifies the 
maturity of the located targets (image classification). Thus, the evalua­
tion of tomato maturity classification requires metrics spanning both 
detection and classification domains.
In object detection and classification tasks, metrics such as the 
Confusion Matrix, IoU (Intersection over Union), P (Precision), R 
(Recall), AP (Average Precision), mAP (mean Average Precision), Acc 
(Accuracy), and F1-score are commonly used to evaluate model results. 
Before analyzing these metrics, it is essential to clarify several funda­
mental concepts, which are summarized in Table 6.
A confusion matrix [175] is a visualization tool used in classification 
tasks to evaluate model performance. It shows the comparison between 
the model's predictions and the ground truth labels. It helps researchers 
intuitively understand classification performance across different cate­
gories. Common metrics derived from the confusion matrix include Acc, 
P, R, and F1-score.

serves as a threshold to distinguish between positive and negative

FLOPs represent the total number of operations required to process a

4.4. RQ 4: What practical challenges are faced in applying deep learning 
models for on-plant tomato fruit maturity classification, and what are the 
emerging future trends?
Regarding the classification of tomato fruit maturity levels, the 
mainstream methods currently include two-level classification [123], 
three-level classification [180], four-level classification [122], five-level 
classification [181], and six-level classification [54,67]. Because the 
maturity of tomato fruit determines the timing of harvesting, trans­
portation, and storage conditions, as well as the freshness and taste 
when eaten, timely and accurate classification of tomato maturity is 
particularly important [180].
4.4.1. Challenges in Practical Application
Current publicly available tomato datasets, due to their limited va­
riety of tomatoes and fixed lighting and background conditions (such as 
laboratory environments), often struggle to fully simulate the com­
plexities of field cultivation scenarios, including foliage shading, over­
lapping fruits, lighting variations, and changing fruit positions. This 
presents a significant challenge in dataset design. Addressing the issue of 
numerous datasets lacking sufficient scenario complexity and a unified 
standard is not only necessary but also urgent [182]. Since tomatoes are 
mainly divided into two categories—large tomatoes and cherry 
tomatoes—and each category has numerous different varieties, and 
different regions and production purposes lead to significant variations 
in tomato maturity classification standards, even within the same region 
and for the same variety, subjective differences in annotation can result 
in inconsistent training data labels, affecting model robustness and 
generalization performance [183]. Similarly, a large discrepancy be­
tween the number of mature and immature tomatoes in the dataset 
causes model skewness, distorted evaluation metrics, and performance 
degradation [184].
Many high-precision models (such as SegLoRA) are of high 
complexity, making them difficult to deploy on power-constrained edge 
devices. In practical applications, the gap between laboratory 
computing power and edge hardware creates an unavoidable conflict 
between real-time operation and low power consumption. Furthermore, 
most research has focused exclusively on the "recognition stage," lacking 
effective integration with drones or harvesting robots [185].
Meanwhile, trained models remain susceptible to shadow variations 
and foliage occlusion in real-world environments, leading to unstable 
performance [186]. Moreover, the color and texture differences between 
maturity stages are often subtle, and traditional convolutional features 
are sometimes insufficient to represent transitional phases. Table 7 il­
lustrates the mapping relationship between these practical challenges 
and the corresponding deep learning solutions.
4.4.2. Lessons Learned and Practical Adjustments
The transition from controlled laboratory environments to unpre­
dictable field conditions necessitates a fundamental shift in model pri­
orities. Our review of the 42 selected studies revealed three critical 
trade-offs:
Prioritizing Latency over Absolute Accuracy: While researchers often 
chase incremental mAP gains in labs, our synthesis indicates that 
inference latency is the primary bottleneck in the field. Studies that 
utilized tools like TensorRT or OpenVINO demonstrated that a slight 
reduction in precision (<2%) can yield a 3–5x increase in frame rates on 
hardware like NVIDIA Jetson Nano.
Architecture Selection for Edge Constraints: Although Transformer- 
based models excel at handling global context, their high parameter 
count makes them prone to thermal throttling. The literature suggested 
that hybrid models—utilizing lightweight backbones with a single 
Transformer layer—achieve the best balance between robustness and 
energy efficiency.
Hardware-Software Synergy for Illumination: Relying solely on data 
augmentation is often insufficient. Successful implementations 
frequently combined digital compensation (e.g., Lab color space) with 
hardware-level solutions like polarized filters or active LED strobes.
Constructing multimodal, large-scale open datasets is a key approach 
to addressing current data limitations. Integrating multi-source infor­
mation (e.g., RGB, depth, and thermal data) enhances model robustness. 
Furthermore, establishing standards by coupling colorimetric values 
with biochemical indicators reduces human subjectivity [187–189]. 
Additionally, in scenarios with insufficient samples or noisy labels, 
models can extract general features using self-supervised mechanisms 
(e.g., SimCLR, DINOv2), which can alleviate the problems of insufficient 
data and inconsistent labeling to some extent [190]. Rapidly and accu­
rately capturing the dynamic changes in tomato fruit characteristics is a 
prerequisite for more precise predictions of maturity stages. Using video 
sequences instead of static images has been shown to improve perfor­
mance by capturing dynamic changes [191–193].

5. Study Limitations
While this systematic review provides a comprehensive analysis of 
deep learning techniques used for maturity classification in tomato 
plants, several limitations remain.
Time Coverage: The literature search cutoff date was September 10, 
2025. Given the rapid advancements in deep learning, groundbreaking 
models published after this date (e.g., new iterations of YOLO or visual 
language models) were not included.
Language and Source Bias: This study only includes peer-reviewed 
journal and conference papers published in English. Relevant research 
published in other languages or "grey literature" (e.g., technical reports 
and patents) may have been omitted.
Keyword Limitations: Although a comprehensive set of keywords 
was used, research in specific areas might not have been captured due to 
variations in terminology—such as "maturity classification" versus 
"plant identification"—across different engineering and botanical 
disciplines.
Databases Scope: The databases utilized in this review are limited; 
therefore, future research can employ additional databases for supple­
mentary searches.
Finally, although this paper addresses and answers four specific 
research questions (RQs), many key challenges in this field remain to be 
explored, such as in-depth research on model interpretability. Tomato 
maturity classification is intrinsically linked to fruit identification, 
instance segmentation, and crop phenotyping. Future research should 
integrate these findings with relevant multidisciplinary studies to 
further advance the intelligentization of agricultural production.
6. Conclusion
This paper systematically reviews the research progress in tomato 
fruit maturity classification based on DL and CV technologies. Following 
PRISMA's rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria, 42 research papers 
were selected. The study designed four research questions and con­
ducted extensive comparisons to address them. This research aims to 
help researchers understand the latest advancements in the field through 
a systematic review of current literature (accessed up to September 
2025).
Overall, existing research has achieved a significant leap from 
traditional image processing to intelligent visual recognition. Specif­
ically, algorithms represented by the R-CNN series, YOLO series, and 
Transformer-based models have significantly improved the accuracy 
and real-time performance of maturity classification. Researchers have 
made substantial progress in lightweight network design (e.g., 
YOLO11n), attention mechanism fusion, multimodal feature extraction, 
and edge deployment.
CV has become a powerful tool in modern agriculture, fundamentally 
changing how crops are monitored and managed. It has evolved from 
traditional object detection to modern DL methods, such as CNNs and 
Transfer Learning [25,201–205]. DL-based tomato maturity classifica­
tion is gradually transitioning from "laboratory-scale optimization" to 
"intelligent deployment in real-world environments." Future research is 
expected to emphasize lightweight deployment, real-time performance, 
multimodal data fusion, and adaptive intelligence to achieve a system­
atic transition from manual detection to autonomous automation.
Tomato maturity is a key indicator of commercial value, influencing 
physicochemical properties including firmness, color uniformity, 
sweetness, and lycopene concentration [206]. Therefore, accurate 
classification is crucial for harvesting decisions, selective robotic pick­
ing, and yield estimation. Vision-based DL methods offer a 
non-destructive and cost-effective alternative to traditional chemical 
measurements. From a smart agriculture perspective, proper classifica­
tion in natural environments helps optimize pre-harvest planning and 
guides automated systems. Combining DL techniques with in-field 
phenotyping further highlights the importance of this research in the 
broader context of autonomous harvesting technologies.
