Abstract: Smart agriculture has the potential to solve labor shortages and improve pro-
duction eﬃciency and prices at the time of shipment. Predicting tomato yields during the 
cultivation period is crucial for planning shipment volumes and costs in advance. We pro-
pose technology that utilizes an AI camera to enable producers to predict yields more 
accurately, and we verify the eﬀectiveness of the developed system through experimental 
validation. Speciﬁcally, an AI-recognition camera was developed, utilizing You Only 
Look Once (YOLO) to detect individual tomatoes. The detected tomatoes are analyzed for 
size using point cloud data. Moreover, the AI-recognition camera performs to classify 
ripeness based on hue. This technology can achieve accurate ripeness classiﬁcation with-
out being dependent on the brightness of the greenhouse. To evaluate this AI classiﬁcation 
camera, the predicted yield obtained from the camera was compared with the actual har-
vested yield in the ﬁeld. The analysis showed an error rate of 6.85%, demonstrating suﬃ-
cient accuracy for practical implementation. By introducing this system, eﬃcient yield 
prediction can be achieved, leading to reduced labor costs, stable tomato supply, im-
proved quality, and optimized market distribution. As a result, it is expected to contribute 
to the beneﬁts of both shippers and consumers. 
Keywords: smart agriculture; LiDAR; point cloud; HSV color space; harvest prediction 
 
1. Introduction 
Issues that need to be solved in the social context of agriculture include labor savings and 
increased production volume owing to a decrease in the number of agricultural workers. 
Smart agriculture is a means to solve this problem, and agricultural methods that use Artificial 
Intelligence (AI), robots, and Internet of Things (IoT) sensors are attracting attention. 
This study proposes a technology to support fruit harvesting using greenhouse-
grown tomatoes as the target crop. By estimating the yield in advance, tomato growers 
can easily make appropriate supply plans for the markets and retailers to which they ship 
tomatoes. It also reduces the risk of over or under-supply relative to the demand, thus 
ensuring a stable supply. For example, if a large harvest is expected, proactive measures 
can be taken, such as arranging for processing and storage, to avoid a drop in market 
prices. Additionally, the labor and machinery schedules required for harvesting can be 
adjusted in advance to minimize waste costs and time. This proactive planning can also

Various approaches have been used in the technology and research related to tomato 
harvest predictions. For example, spectral images captured by a spectral camera have 
been analyzed using machine learning [1], and images have been analyzed using a ma-
chine learning Mask-CNN (Convolution Neural Network) to accurately determine the 
fruit-bunch condition and identify the diﬀerences in RGB [2,3]. Although these related 
studies have used methods to determine the degree of ripeness based on the color of to-
matoes by identifying the RGB values of the captured images, it remains challenging to 
accurately identify images due to varying light conditions inside a facility. On the other 
hand, a method has been proposed to correct the discrepancy based on the average num-
ber of days of harvest by quantitatively analyzing data such as total temperature, sunshine 
hours, and CO2 concentration, which growers have conventionally used to predict the 
harvest [4]. In related research, it is necessary to obtain data on many conditions, such as 
when sudden temperature changes occur or when it is diﬃcult to obtain data on the grow-
ing environment, which is a signiﬁcant hurdle in practical applications. 
Tomatoes are a high-demand and popular crop. They rank ﬁrst in global crop pro-
duction, with 1.8 million tons of tomatoes produced annually, followed by onions (1.04 
million tons) and cucumbers (0.91 million tons) [5]. Because tomatoes are the most popu-
lar crop, they were selected as the target for this study. 
In recent years, AI and machine learning-based harvest prediction technologies have 
aKracted aKention, especially in tomato cultivation. This technology analyzes environ-
mental data and crop growth conditions within greenhouses to predict yield and harvest 
timing. This allows farmers to easily plan their supply, preventing oversupply and excess 
inventory. AI technology is expanding beyond tomatoes to other crops such as strawber-
ries, cucumbers, and leKuce. For these crops, prediction using AI-based image analysis 
and environmental data is advancing [6,7]. For example, in strawberries and cucumbers, 
harvest prediction using AI has already been implemented. In particular, for strawberries, 
AI analyzes color, size, and ripeness to predict the optimal harvest time. In addition, pre-
diction technologies based on temperature, humidity, and soil components have been de-
veloped for cucumbers and leKuce [8,9]. This allows farmers to optimize harvest opera-
tions and maintain quality while ensuring a stable supply. 
In this study, we propose a technology for predicting the shipping period by acquir-
ing information on the size and ripeness of tomatoes. Furthermore, this technology aims 
to solve problems based on the direct requests of farmers, who were the inspiration for 
this research, as well as the background of the world, and it is highly necessary not only 
from an academic perspective but also from the perspective of social implementation, and 
this is the main motivation for this study.

3. Related Works and Issues 
Based on the ﬂowchart in Figure 1, we discuss the investigation and issues of each 
function of the tomato object veriﬁcation, size measurement, and ripeness color classiﬁca-
tion technologies. 
3.1. Challenges in Object Detection Technology 
To predict the harvest, it is ﬁrst necessary to detect tomatoes in camera images. Sim-
ple methods of detecting objects in images include edge detection, template matching, and 
machine learning. Template matching, including tomato size measurements, is discussed 
in Section 3.2. Machine learning includes Region-based Convolutional Neural Networks 
(R-CNN), Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD) [10], and YOLO. In this study, we used 
YOLO, which is the most popular and has a relatively fast detection speed and high de-
tection accuracy [11]. 
Figure 4 illustrates the detection process using YOLO. The input image is divided 
into an N × N grid, as shown in Figure 4A. Subsequently, as shown in (B), each grid cell 
predicts the presence of an object in that cell. This includes the coordinates of the bound-
ing box and the type of object. A conﬁdence score was calculated for the bounding box 
predicted for each grid cell. This score represents the probability of the object being inside 
the box and its accuracy. Because multiple bounding boxes are predicted for the same 
object, the most reliable box is speciﬁed, and duplicate boxes are removed. The location 
of the detected object and its class label are ﬁnally outpuKed, as shown in Figure 5 [12–
14]. YOLO has a low detection accuracy because false positives increase with low image 
quality or noise, as shown by the blue box in Figure 5 [11,15]. To improve the accuracy of 
tomato detection using YOLO, preprocessing such as resizing and random cropping 
based on the depth at the time of image capture is required, as well as optimizing pixels 
disturbed by moving speed and other factors. Annotation is also required to optimize the 
pixels disturbed by moving speed and other factors. Thus, it is necessary to solve the de-
tection accuracy problem for operational applications.

Template matching is a simple method for size measurement. Related research in 
template matching includes implementing a multi-scale template matching method on 
Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) to reduce computation time [16], utilizing GoogLeNet 
to improve computation speed [17], and devising a new similarity measure between tem-
plates and images, Global-Aware Diversity (GAD), to improve matching accuracy [18]. 
GAD is a new similarity measure between templates and images that improves matching 
accuracy [19]. These are studies on improving the performance and application of

method to extract the edges of an image obtained from binarization and template match-

because it requires constantly installing a yardstick for operational purposes.

3.3. Determination of Ripeness and Harvest Prediction 
The ripeness of tomatoes must be estimated to predict their harvest. The ripeness of 
tomatoes can be distinguished by a color change in the epidermis of the fruit: blue (un-
ripe), green (ripening), and red (fully ripe). The color can be identiﬁed using the RGB 
components of the tomato’s surface or by converting the color change into an index, such 
as the Hue Saturation Value (HSV). Another method is spectral analysis, which measures 
the reﬂectance of a surface at speciﬁc wavelengths using a spectrometer. Additionally, 
methods have been proposed to integrate and discriminate between RGB and spectral 
data using machine learning. 
An RGB camera is useful because it is relatively inexpensive, readily available, and 
has a low processing load [24–26]. However, they are easily aﬀected by ambient light 
(brightness and color temperature), and surface gloss and shadows interfere with accurate 
color determination. Therefore, color discrimination using sunlight, such as in green-
houses, is extremely diﬃcult to achieve. Spectral analysis [27,28] is less aﬀected by ambi-
ent light because it uses speciﬁc wavelengths. However, spectrometers and spectral anal-
ysis equipment are expensive, require calibration for initial installation, are complicated 
to operate, and have a large processing load. Machine learning [29] is beneﬁcial because 
it is versatile; however, it is diﬃcult to put it into practical use because it requires a large 
amount of data collection for learning and relearning when the light intensity or camera 
seKings change. On the other hand, HSV [30,31] is capable of making judgments that are 
close to those made by human senses and can quantify color diﬀerences by using hue, 
which is less aﬀected by changes in light intensity than RGB and is suﬃcient if ripeness is 
judged only by color. Based on this background, this study proposes using HSV for color 
discrimination of ripeness. 
4. Detection of Tomatoes in the Field 
4.1. Method of YOLO Detection 
As mentioned previously, YOLO was used to detect tomatoes. To set up the system, 
we installed libraries such as ultralytics, opencv-python, and numpy in a Python 3.9 en-
vironment. After that, we created our own training data for a dedicated tomato detection 
model. The process of creating training data consists of preparing tomato images and la-
beling them with annotations. In this study, we used the labelImg tool for annotation. 
After all images have been labeled, training was performed. The dataset for training is 
divided into training and test datasets, and the conﬁguration information is described in 
the data.yaml ﬁle. Next, we describe the preprocessing and training parameters. The im-
age data used in YOLO were actual images captured in the ﬁeld. First, multiple images of 
the object to be detected were prepared. In this study, 1166 images were obtained. To en-
hance detection accuracy even in the case of overlapping objects, low pixel counts, blurred 
objects, or overlapping leaves, as shown in Figure 5, we prepared images in various states. 
Next, as shown in Figure 7, the objects to be detected are surrounded by red arrows in the 
image and labeled with annotations. In this study, only tomatoes were annotated to detect 
tomatoes because the model was intended for tomato detection. The class label, in this 
case, was “tomato”. After all images were annotated, the last step was training. The train-
ing parameters were set to 315 test images, 100 training cycles, and a batch size of 32. 
Because it is common for the number of test images to be 2–30% of the number of training 
images, we set the number of test images to 315, which is approximately 30% of the 1166 
images. The number of training sessions was set as 100 to avoid overtraining. The batch 
size was set to 32 [32,33].

5. Tomato Size Measurement Method 
5.1. Method of Distance and Size Measurement by LiDAR 
The method for calculating size relative to distance is based on point cloud data. Point 
cloud data represents the three-dimensional conﬁguration of an object using a collection 
of points with XYZ coordinates, as shown in Figure 12 [39,40]. In this research, a depth 
camera is used instead of a 2D camera, and the depth camera can acquire depth images. 
Each pixel in the depth image contains information indicating the distance to the object, 
and this information can be used to estimate the position of the object in 3D space. The 
coordinates corresponding to each pixel in the depth image are calculated, and the three-
dimensional shape of the object is represented as a point cloud. 
 
Figure 12. Image of point cloud. 
To measure the size, the normal vector of a point cloud was used to obtain the point 
cloud of the tomato edge area. Figures 13 and 14 show that the normal vector indicates 
the direction perpendicular to the surface at a point on a certain plane or curved surface 
[41–43]. When measurements are taken on a horizontal surface, such as a desk, as shown 
in Figure 13A, the points appear on a plane, as shown in Figure 13B, and the normal vector 
is perpendicular to the plane. Thus, they all point in the same direction. Figure 14 shows 
the results for the curved surface. When measuring a curved surface such as a tomato, as 
shown in Figure 14A, a point cloud appeared on the surface, as shown in Figure 14B. The 
normal vector is perpendicular to each point cloud on the surface. Therefore, the direction 
varies from place to place.

6.2. Results of Hue Discrimination 
The hue discrimination was evaluated using red, orange, and green tomatoes. To 
conﬁrm the change in hue according to light intensity, we compared the results in dark 
and bright environments by changing the light intensity. The light intensity was set to 
1200 cd in light and 5 cd in the dark. These were the maximum and minimum values of 
the variable light intensity range of the LEDs used in this experiment. Figures 19–21 show 
the cumulative hue values for each pixel within the red, orange, and green images. Iden-
tiﬁcation was performed by selecting the peak value at which the hue was the highest. 
For example, the red pixel in Figure 19 is three in the dark and zero in light. The 
orange color in Figure 20 is 11 under both dark and light conditions. Green in Figure 21 
has a hue value of 30 in the light and 31 in the dark, and all colors have approximately the 
same hue value, regardless of the light or dark changes. Applying the hue values of each 
color to the color thresholds in Table 7 we observe that the hue values in Figure 19 are 3 
and 0, which correspond to the 0–6 range for red, and thus are assumed to be red. Figure 
20 shows that the hue value is 11, corresponding to 11–15 for orange. Figure 21 shows that 
the hue values are 30 and 31, corresponding to light green to yellow green, and thus, esti-
mated to be in the light green to yellow-green stage. 
Using this table as a reference, color discrimination was outpuKed as a judgment re-
sult based on the range of threshold values.

The progression of ripeness is inﬂuenced not only by the ripeness stage, which is the 
benchmark at harvest, but also by temperature changes and sunlight (light intensity) one 
week later. Predicting crop yield more accurately is possible by considering how these 
factors aﬀect growth and ripeness. Therefore, it is important to utilize data that includes 
these conditions in future research. In this study, the analysis results were based on quan-
titative data, so we believe that this technology can be used in a wide range of applications 
in other regions and environments. However, the experimental results were based on op-
erations at a single farm, and cultivation methods, optimal harvest times, shipping

22 of 25 
 
 
methods, etc., may diﬀer from country to country and region to region. Therefore, we 
believe that the usefulness of this engineering technology can be demonstrated by apply-
ing the harvest prediction method to the conditions of each region. 
However, there were some areas where our technology was insuﬃcient for veriﬁca-
tion, and we were unable to successfully apply the harvest prediction method to the meas-
urement of size and volume in the harvesting procedures and shipping systems of the 
farmers who conducted the demonstration experiment. In the future, we will aim to 
achieve a more accurate harvest prediction method by further researching the relationship 
between ripeness and size and volume to address this issue. 
The experiment conﬁrmed that hue discrimination could be performed with high ac-
curacy and that the hue one week before harvest could be based on “Yellow Green to 
Orange” and “Yellow Green”. However, further improvements in accuracy are required 
to minimize these errors. Additionally, although this experiment was conducted during 
the winter season, harvest timing is expected to be adjusted based on earlier ripening 
stages, such as “Light Green,” since ripening occurs earlier after harvest in the summer. 
The selection of appropriate harvest criteria for diﬀerent seasons will be the subject of 
future research. 
8. Summary 
In this study, we propose a yield estimation technique that enables growers to deter-
mine the yield of tomatoes in advance. Additionally, we develop an AI identiﬁcation cam-
era. The camera detects tomatoes using the YOLO machine learning model and calculates 
their size and volume using point cloud data. It is also capable of accurately identifying 
the ripeness of tomatoes using hue analysis, which is independent of light intensity. Fur-
thermore, the AI identiﬁcation camera was operated in an actual ﬁeld, and the results 
were compared with the yield data. Consequently, the error rate was conﬁrmed as 6.85%, 
which is a level that can withstand practical applications. 
These results allow the grower to estimate the yield in advance of harvesting and 
shipping and to adjust the labor and machinery operation schedule for harvesting opera-
tions in advance. In addition, it is easier to make appropriate supply plans with markets 
and retailers, and when a large harvest is expected and a surplus is anticipated, it is pos-
sible to reduce the risk of oversupply or shortage in relation to demand by reducing waste 
and deterioration losses for tomatoes when processing to avoid a decline in market prices 
and by arranging storage to realize a stable supply. This can reduce the risk of oversupply 
or shortage in relation to demand and ensure a stable supply. 
The greenhouse experiment was conducted in winter, but it is anticipated that har-
vest timing will need to be adjusted based on earlier ripening stages, such as “Light 
Green,” since ripening occurs more quickly after harvest in the summer. The selection of 
appropriate harvesting criteria for diﬀerent seasons will be the subject of future research. 
If these issues are resolved, growers can optimize harvest timing, improving eﬃciency, 
reducing costs, and maximizing proﬁts. 
The harvest forecasting technology developed in this study provides beneﬁts not 
only to growers but also to shippers and consumers. Speciﬁcally, it is expected to ensure 
a stable supply of tomatoes and improve their quality, thereby contributing to the in-
creased eﬃciency and sustainability of the entire agricultural industry.
