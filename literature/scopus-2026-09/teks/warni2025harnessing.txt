Abstract—This study explores the use of the YOLOv5 
algorithm to detect loose fruits palm oil in plantations, improve 
collection efficiency, and reduce reliance on manual labor. 
Loose fruits have high economic value due to their oil content, 
but they are often not collected, resulting in financial losses and 
the growth of weeds around oil palm trees. Conventional 
manual methods are often ineffective; thus, automatic detection 
is required. The dataset comprises 525 images of loose fruits, 
covering complex conditions on plantations, and secondary 
data from Roboflow, including nontarget objects, such as rocks 
and dry leaves, to train the model to accurately distinguish loose 
fruits. During training, several parameters were optimized: 
batch size, learning rate, optimizer type, weight decay, and 
momentum, which showed that small batch sizes provided 
higher mAP50 values, whereas large batches increased 
mAP50:95, indicating the ability to detect objects at higher 
levels of complexity. Testing in a scenario with loose fruits 
clearly visible and partially covered with leaves or grass showed 
that YOLOv5 could detect loose fruits well despite some 
constraints in the obstructed environment. The results of this 
study show that, with appropriate parameter settings, YOLOv5 
can be a reliable basis for automated loose fruit collection 
systems, potentially improving operational efficiency in oil 
palm plantations and contributing to the development of 
automation in the agricultural sector. 
Keywords— deep learning, loose fruits, object detection, palm 
oil, YOLO  
I. 
INTRODUCTION  
The oil palm is a palm tree that produces fruit rich in palm 
oil and is one of the largest sources of vegetable oil in the 
world. As a crucial agricultural product, oil palm oil plays a 
significant role in Indonesia’s agricultural and economic 
sectors. In 2022, the Central Statistics Agency (BPS) 
reported that Indonesia produced 45.58 million tons of palm 
oil, marking a 1.02% increase over the previous year’s 45.12 
million tons. This high production volume highlights 
Indonesia’s position as one of the world’s largest producers 
of palm oil. Indonesia’s palm oil is primarily derived from 
cultivation and processing activities on plantations, 
especially during the harvesting process [1]. 
The harvesting process in oil palm plantations collects 
fresh and loose palm fruits from the fields and palm oil 
processing mills [2]. The process generally involves cutting 
ripe fruit bunches, collecting loose fruit, pruning fronds, and 
transporting the harvested fruits. During harvesting, oil palm 
trees release loose fruits that account for 12.5% to 75% of 
the total fruit yield from a fresh fruit bunch [3]. 
Loose palm fruits are particularly rich in oil content. If 
not managed properly, these loose fruits become waste and 
encourage weed growth around oil palm trees [4]. Losses, 
defined as uncollected products from the harvesting process 
(in the form of fresh fruit bunches, loose fruits, or oil), 
represent significant economic setbacks for plantations. 
Thus, collecting loose fruits is an essential step in harvesting 
because it prevents weed growth around trees and reduces 
potential profit losses from unharvested loose fruits [5]. 
In practice, plantation workers still rely on traditional 
methods to collect loose fruits that fall to the ground [4]. 
Manual loose fruit collection is often inefficient, subjective, 
and slow [6]. This activity typically involves workers in 
crouching or bending postures, which increases physical 
strain, particularly on the lower back [7]. 
Despite the critical need for efficient loose fruit 
collection, existing automated solutions remain limited. 
Previous studies have explored various image processing and 
machine learning techniques for fruit detection in 
agricultural settings. Traditional methods, such as color 
thresholding and simple object recognition algorithms, have 
been applied to detect fruits; however, these approaches 
often suffer from low accuracy and are unable to handle the 
complex backgrounds and varying lighting conditions 
typical in oil palm plantations [8], [9]. Recent advancements 
have introduced deep learning-based methods, including 
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which have shown 
improved performance in object detection tasks [10]. 
Nevertheless, these models require extensive computational 
resources and may not achieve real-time processing speeds 
necessary 
for 
practical 
deployment 
in 
plantation 
environments. 
Advances in artificial intelligence offer promising 
solutions, with plans to develop detection technology 
specifically for loose palm fruits using the YOLO (You Only 
Look Once) method [11], [12], [13], [14]. An additional 
challenge in developing a loose fruit detection system for oil 
palm plantations is distinguishing loose fruits from similarly 
shaped or colored objects. Stones and other objects must also 
be detected simultaneously to prevent the system from

This study introduces a novel application of the YOLO

II. 
METHOD 
A. Dataset  
The data used in this study consisted of images of loose 
oil palm fruit within plantation environments, with 525 
images divided into 461 training, 32 validation, and 32 test 
images. Primary data were collected directly from the field, 
capturing various conditions such as camera heights of 0.5 
and 1 meter, loose fruits in states of clear visibility, partial 
obstruction by leaves, or clusters. Additionally, secondary 
data sourced from Roboflow were incorporated to balance 
the dataset’s classes, which comprised images of dry leaves 
and stones. These two classes represent objects commonly 
found in oil palm plantations, with shapes and colors similar 
to loose palm fruit. The following examples illustrate both 
the primary and secondary data samples collected for the 
study. 
This balanced dataset ensures that the detection model 
encounters realistic variations in object visibility and 
similarity, enhancing its ability to distinguish between loose 
fruits and visually similar non target objects within the 
complex environment of a palm plantation. Examples of the 
primary and secondary data are shown in Figure 1.

B. Yolov5 
At this stage, the system is trained using YOLOv5 
algorithm in the architecture shown in Figure 2. 
 
Fig. 2. The YOLOv5 algorithm architecture [15] 
In YOLOv5, the backbone is responsible for extracting 
essential features from input images using the CSPDarknet 
architecture, which is a modified version of Darknet 
designed to enhance efficiency and accuracy. This backbone 
comprises several stages, each containing convolutional and 
C3 layers. The backbone begins by applying convolution to 
the input image, followed by the C3 layer, which adds 
truncation and expansion to enrich the feature representation. 
This process can be repeated at various image resolutions 
(e.g., 640x640, 320x320, 160x160, etc.). In each stage, 
multiscale features are captured by downsampling. In the 
final backbone stage, the SPPFBottleneck layer consolidates 
spatial information from multiple feature scales using fixed-
size pooling before passing it to the neck [16]. 
The neck, which employs YOLOv5PAFPN (Path 
Aggregation Network), functions to merge features from 
various scales through a combination of top down and 
bottom-up processes, resulting in rich, diverse feature 
representations with minimal redundancy. In the head 
section, YOLOv5 is responsible for object detection, 
processing the extracted and refined features through the 
backbone and neck. The proposed head includes several 
convolutional layers that detect various object classes and 
estimate bounding boxes. The coupled head system in 
YOLOv5 integrates bounding box predictions with object-
class predictions in a single prediction process, producing 
bounding box coordinates, confidence scores, and object 
classifications [17]. 
C. System Design 
The object detection model for loose fruits undergoes a 
training and testing process, as shown in Figure 3. The 
process is separated into two primary phases: training and 
evaluation. The initial step in the training process involves 
the input of data, followed by the assignment of labels to 
identify key attributes or entities. The data is divided into 
training and validation subsets to guarantee model reliability. 
At the preprocessing stage, all images were resized to a 640 
x 640 resolution to ensure a consistent input size for the 
YOLO algorithm. This process involves incorporating data 
manipulation 
techniques 
such 
as 
mirror 
images 
(horizontal/vertical flips), adjustments to brightness, 
saturation, and exposure to enhance variation and the model's 
ability to withstand changes in lighting conditions, 
orientation, and environment. Following preprocessing, the 
YOLOv5 model is trained, resulting in a YOLOv5 model 
that is subsequently exported and applied during the testing 
phase.

A. Model Training Results 
The training process is divided into four stages, namely, 
training to find the optimal batch size and epoch, and training 
to find optimizers and learning rates. Then, the optimal 
weight decay and momentum values for the YOLOv5 
algorithm are determined. 
Training with batch size and epoch scenarios, each 
YOLO algorithm was trained on epoch 500 and batch sizes 
4, 8, 16, 32, and 64. In this section, we will analyze the values 
of mAP50 and mAP50:95 and see at what batch size the 
algorithm works best and at what epoch value the training 
results do not show significant changes. The YOLOv5 model 
showed relatively stable mAP50 values across batch sizes, 
whereas mAP50:95 increased when the batch size was 
enlarged to 64. The highest mAP50:95 in this model was 
achieved at a batch size of 64 (0.614), as shown in Table 1.

B.  Testing Result 
To evaluate the performance of the detection model, two 
testing scenarios were designed to align with the objectives 
of this study and simulate practical applications in real world 
settings. 
Scenario 1 focused on detecting loose fruits in ideal 
conditions where fruits were not obstructed by other objects 
such as leaves or grass. This scenario aimed to establish the 
baseline performance of the YOLOv5 model under 
controlled conditions. In this way, we wanted to validate the 
model basic accuracy in identifying target objects without 
external interference. The results from this scenario provide 
important insights into the model detection capabilities when 
applied in simple and predictable environments, as shown in 
Figure 4.  
Scenario 2 was designed to test the model’s robustness 
under more complex and realistic conditions. In this 
scenario, loose fruits were partially obstructed by natural 
elements such as leaves or grass, mimicking common 
challenges encountered in oil palm plantations. This setup 
directly reflects the practical application of the detection 
system, where loose fruits are often hidden or camouflaged 
by plant debris. The results from this scenario demonstrate 
the model ability to adapt to various levels of complexity, 
ensuring its reliability and effectiveness for use in automated 
fruit collection systems. 
These two scenarios together demonstrate the model 
readiness for real world applications by testing both its basic 
accuracy and its robustness in challenging environments. 
The results highlight the potential of the YOLOv5 based 
detection system to improve operational efficiency in oil 
palm plantations, particularly in automating loose fruits 
collection.

IV. 
CONCLUSION  
This study explores the use of the YOLOv5 algorithm to 
detect loose fruits in oil palm plantations. By utilizing a 
dataset that encompasses various environmental conditions, 
the model was successfully trained to distinguish loose fruits 
from non-target objects such as stones and dry leaves. The 
research results indicate that specific parameter settings, 
such as a small batch size that increases mAP50 and a large 
batch size that enhances mAP50:95, provide optimal 
performance. Additionally, the combination of the SGD 
optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01, weight decay of 
0.0005, and momentum of 0.9 yielded the most accurate 
detections. Testing the model in two scenarios demonstrated 
YOLOv5 capability to detect loose fruits, although 
challenges remain in more complex environmental 
conditions. Based on these findings, the study concludes that 
the YOLOv5 algorithm can serve as the foundation for an 
automated loose fruits collection system, potentially 
increasing 
operational 
efficiency 
and 
supporting 
advancements in the agricultural sector. 
 
As a follow up, this research will focus on developing an 
automated collection system by integrating the YOLOv5
