Towards Image-Based Measurement of Accurate Apple Size 
and Yield Using Stereo Vision Cameras 
Omeed Mirbod, Daeun Choi, Paul Heinemann, Richard Marini

2020 ASABE Annual International Meeting

Omaha, Nebraska 
July 12–15, 2020 
ABSTRACT. Accurate measurement of fruit size in apple orchards before harvest can have important implications on profits 
and management practices. Obtaining a distribution of fruit size can be labor intensive for a large orchard and therefore 
requires an automated system that can quickly and accurately size fruit on every tree. This study proposes an automated 
imaging system that uses stereo vision for finding the metric surface area of apples. Deep convolutional neural network 
models were utilized to classify apples as ideal candidates for sizing based on their orientation and visibility in an image. 
The results produced a correlation of apple size to apple weight of R2=0.69 making the system capable of capturing 
variability in fruit diameter distribution that ranges by 1cm (or equivalently 60 grams). There was also an improvement in 
correlation to yield when combining fruit size with fruit count than when utilizing fruit count alone. 
Keywords. 3D Reconstruction, Computer Vision, Deep Learning, Image Segmentation, Precision Agriculture, Yield 
Estimation 
1. Introduction 
Apple growers are interested in having an estimate on the quality of their crop each season before harvest and delivery 
to the packing house. Profit is associated with yield of quality fruit, but the value of fruit bound for fresh market versus 
processing is quite different. For example, in 2017 the U.S. average price for fresh-market apples was 40.7 cents per pound 
while the average price for processing was 12.4 cents per pound (USDA, 2019). What determines this distinction is primarily 
the size and color of the apple, with larger rich colored apples bound for fresh market and smaller ones for processing. 
Currently growers do not have a method of obtaining an on-tree fruit size estimate. Such a method can help improve budget 
and sales forecasting before harvest time and allow growers to have a metric for comparing their own harvest profit estimates 
with that of the pack-house. It can also give the growers a chance early in the season to modify their management practices 
on fruit thinning and irrigation to influence the size of fruit at harvest. 
One of the challenges in developing an in-field machine vision system for apple sizing is overcoming fruit occlusion in 
images as apples can be occluded by branches, leaves, or other apples. Another challenge is sizing the different varieties of 
apples, some which can be round, conical, or oblong. A rotated oblong or conical apple in a 2D image, even if fully visible, 
could misrepresent the true fruit size. Good accuracy in apple sizing has been achieved for enclosed machine vision systems 
in which individual apples were imaged on a moving bi-cone conveyor (Mizushima & Lu, 2013). Many fruit packers rely 
on such imaging systems for quality grading of incoming crop from growers, however such systems are not capable of

estimating fruit size before harvest. With the emergence of convolutional neural networks (CNN) and other deep learning 
models, in-field analysis of apples has become more prevalent with an increase in fruit detection accuracy. More recent deep 
learning algorithms, like Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017), can perform semantic segmentation of objects, which allows 
obtaining the corresponding area of detected objects. This algorithm has been used for segmenting apples in field images 
that may be partially occluded by the tree canopy or other apples (Jia et al., 2020). A similar algorithm, DaSNet-V2, by Kang 
& Chen (2020) achieved high accuracy in instance segmentation of tree branches and fruits to be used for 3D visualization 
of apple orchards for a robotic harvester. These recent works however were not focused on the sizing of the apples or the 
effect of occlusion on the instance segmentation of the fruit for sizing. Butters et al., (2019) used Mask R-CNN to obtain a 
distribution of diameter sizes based on harvested apples laying on top of a wooden crate. They used the known dimensions 
of the crate to transform pixel coordinates to world coordinates. Their work was meant to give growers a fast estimation of 
apple size distribution using a small sample of harvested apples.   
In this study a method for sizing apples in images was introduced using deep convolutional neural networks to selectively 
choose the best apples for sizing. The hardware used for data acquisition was a field deployable stereo vision system with 
high resolution cameras and active lighting. The stereo camera pair allows for retrieving depth information on each 
individual apple and therefore accurate conversion from pixel coordinates to metric coordinate system. Instead of attempting 
to find the diameter of apples for sizing, the Mask R-CNN model was used to find the metric surface area of each fruit on 
trees during the growing season. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1 Data Acquisition 
Image data for this study was collected at the Russell E. Larson Agricultural Research Center in Pennsylvania Furnace, 
PA on September 18, 2019 at 7pm. Ground truth diameter measurement data was collected on September 20, 2019 and 
harvest data was collected on October 1, 2019. For ground truth data, diameters of 10 apples per tree were measured using 
an electronic fruit size measure (EFM) device (UP GmbH Inc., Ibbenbüren, Germany) to determine the average fruit 
diameter per tree. Apples were also harvested and weighed for each tree to acquire an average fruit weight per tree. The 
machine vision system used for image acquisition included two FLIR Blackfly S cameras (model BFS-U3-88S6C-C, FLIR 
Systems, Wilsonville, OR) mounted in a stereo configuration and an active flash system made with Cree LEDs (model 
CMA3090, Cree Inc, Durham, NC) which provided artificial illumination. The rated output of each LED was 174W and 
12372 lumens. Each LED was strobed instead of being continuously on, therefore the power output was increased to 1.5kW 
for the strobe duration. Twelve of these LEDs were installed for the flash system. Both cameras were calibrated before image 
acquisition to obtain the camera intrinsic parameters and remove any distortion from the images. The equipment was 
mounted on a cart and dragged through two rows of an orchard collecting data from both sides of tree canopies. The cart 
moved at a rate of about 1 m/s and collected images at 3Hz for a total of 380 images for 30 trees. The artificial lighting 
strobe was adopted for two reasons. First, a constant illumination condition in images is preferable to the variable natural 
sunlight when training machine learning and neural network classification models. Second, capturing images on a moving 
platform can cause motion blur. Since the ground truth data on apple diameters showed only a small variability (about 1cm), 
it was important to capture well defined boundaries of each fruit for the 3D reconstruction phase and high-precision sizing. 
The LED strobe allowed to shorten the shutter duration and therefore lessen the motion blur effect.  
2.2 Apple Sizing Pipeline 
The apple sizing pipeline consisted of four stages: fruit detection, classification of fruit as a viable candidate for sizing, 
2D apple mask generation, and surface area calculation. Fruit detection was done using a random forest classifier to detect 
the fruit from the background in an image. Classification of the fruit as a potential candidate for fruit sizing was conducted 
using transfer learning from a pretrained convolutional neural network model, VGG-16 (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2015), and 
adapted to the binary classification task. Once candidates were identified, an image mask of each fruit was generated using 
Mask R-CNN followed by pixel mask to metric surface area conversion using stereo vision.  
2.2.1 Apple Detection 
The pipeline for apple detection was adopted from (Nuske et al., 2014) for grape detection in vineyards. First a set of 
dense circular cues were detected in an image, some containing true apple locations and others as background objects. SIFT 
descriptors of labeled data from the same image along with the circular cues were extracted and trained on a random forest 
classifier to build an apple detector.  On a test image, SIFT descriptors from a dense set of circular cues were classified as 
apple or not using the random forest classifier (Figure 1).

 2.2.2 Apple Candidate Identification for Sizing 
Once all apples were detected, patches of 128x128 pixels for each apple were extracted and fed to a deep convolutional 
neural network to be a classified as a good candidate for sizing or not. A “good candidate” apple for sizing was identified as 
one that is fully visible to the camera without being partially occluded by any parts of the canopy or by other apples. The 
apple must also be oriented in a way that maximizes its 2D surface area. This orientation is usually the side-view of the 
apple, not the top where the stem is, or the bottom where the calyx is visible. A few examples of good and inadequate 
candidates are given in Figure 2 which were used as training data. The training data consisted of 105 good apple candidates 
and 90 inadequate apple candidates. They included variations of occlusion by stem, leaves, and other fruit as well as different 
apple orientations. The datasets were expanded through image augmentation by rotating each image patch incrementally by 
15 degrees. This gave a total combined training dataset of 4,600 apple image patches. 
 
Figure 2. Samples of good versus inadequate candidates for apple sizing with (A) (B) (C) (D) representing apples that should not be used for 
sizing and (E) (F) (G) (H) representing candidates that should be used. 
It is difficult to find an abundance of fully visible apples in an image, therefore the CNN and mask generation network 
were both trained to allow room for some occlusion. For example, in Figure 2, patch (B) had severe occlusion by the 
surrounding leaves. This would be labeled as an inadequate candidate for sizing since the mask generation would have 
difficulty in interpolating the rest of the apple boundary, however in patch (F) there was minor overlap of the leaf on the 
apple. With enough training data, the mask generation network would be able to interpolate the boundary of the occluded 
apple region.

3. Results and Discussions 
The accuracy of the apple sizing method was evaluated on two ground truth data: comparison of apple surface area with 
apple diameters using an EFM device and comparison of apple surface area with harvest apple weight. The correlation 
analysis was performed on 30 trees with two trees excluded. One tree had minimal harvest and heavy occlusion therefore 
only one apple candidate could be found. The second exclusion was an outlier observed from the harvest weight data. For 
the ground truth data on apple diameters, ten apples were sampled from each tree and their diameters measured using the 
EFM device. The mean of the ten measurements then represented the average fruit diameter per tree. The comparison results 
are shown in Figure 4.  
 
Figure 4: Correlation between apple fruit size using surface area calculation and ground truth apple diameters 
Comparison to harvest data was made by taking the total yield of each tree and dividing by the number of picked apples 
for each tree, this resulted in an average fruit weight per tree which was then compared to the average fruit surface area 
measurement per tree. Figure 5A shows the correlation of manually picked apple candidates to harvest data and figure 5B 
shows the deep learning model picking the apple candidates for sizing. The results show that correlation to yield with 
manually selected apple candidates had a slightly higher coefficient of determination value (0.75) compared to automatically 
selected candidates by the neural network model (0.69). Two conclusions can be drawn from these plots. First, improvements 
still need to be made to the neural network model to match the performance of the manually picked results. Second, if the 
manually picked results were meant to simulate optimal selection of apple candidates for sizing, then one reason the 
correlation coefficient to the true harvest weight of the apples was not higher is due to the low number of apple candidates 
(a low sample size) used to estimate the average fruit surface area per tree. 
 
Figure 5. Correlation between average fruit weight per tree at harvest and average fruit size per tree. (A) represents obtained fruit size when 
apple candidates were handpicked compared to (B) which the deep learning network chose apple candidates for sizing. 
A comparison was also made to determine how much improvement the incorporation of fruit size has on yield. Figure 6 
shows the correlation between harvest fruit count and harvest weight per tree compared to total fruit surface area and harvest 
weight per tree. Both plots use harvest fruit count to determine the effect of incorporating fruit size for yield accuracy. In 
this case the improvement was minimal as can be seen by the r-squared values. The improvement can be dependent on how 
much fruit size variability exists in an orchard. An orchard experiencing high variability in fruit size can benefit from a 
machine vision system capable of capturing that variability. In an orchard where fruit size is almost identical throughout the 
field, knowledge on the variability would provide little additional information when estimating yield.

4. Conclusion 
A method for on-tree image-based sizing of apples was demonstrated. It was shown that instead of attempting to size 
every apple which can introduce errors due to fruit occlusion, it is possible to selectively size apple candidates that are fully 
visible and still obtain good correlation to the harvest weight. The apple candidates were selected by training a deep neural 
network which can distinguish a fully visible apple with proper orientation from one that is occluded or undergone spatial 
distortion. The result from this study can help growers determine before harvest how much of their fruit is likely to be packed 
for fresh market versus processing. A future work will include comparing the current imaging system with the grading system 
used by a fruit packer. Further tests will also include using the fruit sizing method on other apple varieties and canopies such 
as tall spindle. To improve the correlation of fruit weight with surface area, a sequence of images for each tree at different 
viewpoints will be analyzed which should help make more apples fully visible and therefore increase the number of apple 
candidates for sizing.
