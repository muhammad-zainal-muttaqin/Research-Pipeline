A B S T R A C T
Yield prediction has long been a valuable tool for farmers seeking to enhance crop production. Among the many 
ways to predict yield, the integration of machine learning (ML) techniques is becoming more common for 
refining prediction methodologies. This study highlights the current landscape of remote sensing and ML tech­
niques employed in predicting tree crop yield while also identifying critical gaps and areas for further explo­
ration. Studies with limited datasets for training often use simpler models such as linear regression, while studies 
with larger datasets use more complex models, including techniques such as deep learning, ensemble methods, 
and hyperparameter tuning; in these cases, the performance evaluation tends to be more sophisticated. Yield 
prediction using ML has demonstrated accuracy levels ranging from 50 % to 99 %. Studies using smaller datasets 
consistently demonstrate higher accuracy rates. While ML techniques can enhance yield prediction, their 
effectiveness depends on strategic data collection and a multi-factor and multi-method approach. Integration of 
various data sources, including weather, soil, and plant data, could enhance model resilience and applicability. 
Enhancing research in this field could be achieved through overcoming challenges in accurate data collection and 
fostering the development of open datasets. This comprehensive analysis lays the groundwork for future research 
endeavors aimed at refining and advancing the application of remote sensing and ML techniques in accurately 
predicting tree crop yield.
Introduction
Yield prediction is a critical task in crop production and precision 
agriculture (PA), directly impacting food security, as well as social and 
economic stability. Predicting crop yield before harvesting helps farmers 
plan and manage production risks across the production system, 
including logistics and sales [1,2]. Accurate yield prediction enhances 
operational efficiency, reduces food waste, and promotes sustainable 
agriculture. Additionally, it supports all aspects of cultivation-related 
management, reflecting its board impact on precision agriculture.
Yield estimation and prediction methods vary across academic and 
industrial settings, depending on factors such as the region, cultural 
context, and available resources. In the 1980s, yield prediction meth­
odologies utilized regression models by incorporating climatological 
data, crop management practices (fertilization, irrigation, etc.), and data 
from previous years [3–6]. As data availability and volume increase, the 
opportunity to develop more accurate yield prediction methodologies 
also expands. Therefore, adopting big data management techniques and 
artificial intelligence (AI) has proven to be an effective approach for 
managing this extensive volume of data. Recently, AI has gained notable 
attention in precision agriculture due to its ability to harness large 
amounts of data [7–9]. Machine learning (ML), a branch of AI, is a 
practical approach that can provide better crop yield prediction based 
on various features. Machine learning can identify patterns and corre­
lations, and extract knowledge from datasets based on datasets trained 
using experience-based results [10,11].
Publications using remote sensing and AI for data collection and 
analysis date back to the early 1980s [12,13]. However, the adoption of 
these techniques and tools for agricultural applications has notably 
increased since 2010 [14]. However, understanding how these tools are 
used, which techniques are most used, and how evaluations are con­
ducted is crucial, especially in guiding future research and development

provide comprehensive summaries of the existing knowledge in a

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the current land­
scape of remote sensing and machine learning (ML) techniques in tree 
yield estimation and prediction, highlighting the most used methods 
while identifying critical gaps and areas for further research. By 
analyzing the challenges associated with data collection and model 
development, this study emphasizes the importance of integrating 
multiple techniques and variables to improve the accuracy and reli­
ability of yield prediction models. Through this analysis, the research 
outlines a clear path forward to guide future efforts to develop more 
robust and adaptable models that can meet the diverse needs of farmers, 
growers, and researchers. Continued innovation and interdisciplinary 
collaboration demonstrate the growing potential of remote sensing and 
ML to advance sustainable agriculture and food security. This study not 
only contributes to the theoretical understanding of yield estimation and 
prediction, but also lays the groundwork for practical innovations that 
could significantly impact agricultural practices.
Material and methods
Several established guidelines for systematic reviews, including 
SALSA and PRISMA [15,16], were consulted in constructing the research 
methodology and the subsequent development of this study.
Data search and eligibility criteria
The data search was tied to specific criteria to identify research pa­
pers that closely match the objectives of this study. The following da­
tabases were searched to identify and select the relevant studies: Google 
Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science. Databases have been selected 
based on the extent of their relevance and coverage of journals and 
research; they serve as sources for searching publications and include 
materials such as articles, papers, technical reports, and preprints, 
among others. Each of these databases covers a different source, such as 
websites, journals, and libraries, but some of these are repeated between 
databases. The scientific papers in this study underwent a complete se­
lection process through a database search using the keywords listed in 
Table 1. The keywords were selected and written based on the guidelines 
of each database and the Boolean operators.
The search was conducted between June and December 2023, 
following predefined criteria for inclusion in the subsequent phase of the 
research; these criteria being: the papers were sorted by relevance in the 
database, published between 2010 and 2024, classified as preprints or 
research papers (excluding reviews or similar publications), and the­
matic relevance to the specified keywords (Table 1). The criteria were 
selected based on the growing research involving ML techniques and 
remote sensing for crop yield prediction purposes, especially since 2010. 
In recent years, several yield sensing, monitoring, and mapping tech­
nologies and techniques have been developed for tree crops [17,18]; 
however, these studies are not included in this systematic review, which 
emphasizes on yield prediction.
Data filtering, selection, and analysis
After filtering the publications in each database, the papers were 
classified as “Not selected” if they did not meet the selection criteria; and 
“Selected” if they met all criteria. The selection of all factors (keywords, 
criteria, papers) was carefully aligned with the research objectives, 
which encompassed both the goals and the purpose of this study; in this 
phase, the title of the papers, the keywords, and the abstracts were the 
source for the selection or not. Based on the selected results, after all the 
filtering, the papers were downloaded and underwent a more detailed 
evaluation. This evaluation consisted of reading the papers and 
searching them for information such as which crop was studied, yield 
prediction methodology, year of publication, the platform for data 
acquisition, sensors and vegetation indices used, ML techniques, and 
achieved accuracy/performance of the proposed method.
The data obtained in the spreadsheets were used for analysis and 
subsequently to construct the results of this systematic review using 
graphs, tables, figures, and others. The entire process of database se­
lection, keyword searches, data collection, and analysis used to produce 
this review article can be seen in the flowchart in Fig. 1.
Results and discussion
At the end of the search, from all papers found in each database, only 
42 from Google Scholar (GS), 26 from Scopus (Sc), and 22 from Web of 
Science (WoS) were matched against the pre-selected criteria. Among 
the selected papers, there was a duplication of 11 papers (GS and Sc: 6; 
GS, and WoS: 3; Sc and WoS: 2), and 3 papers were found in all databases 
(GS, Sc, and WoS).
The significant variance between the papers initially identified 
through filtering and those ultimately included in the research was 
primarily due to several factors. Despite meticulous filtering criteria 
applied across the databases, numerous papers contained significant 
deficiencies, including a preponderance of review and case study papers, 
papers focusing on crops other than trees, lack of utilization of ML 
techniques or remote sensing tools for yield prediction, and frequent 
deviation from topics related to yield prediction.
General overview
Since 2017, there has been a noticeable increase in research using ML 
techniques for yield prediction (Fig. 2a). This trend can be attributed to 
the increasing amount of data generated from a variety of sources, 
including unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), ground sensors, smart 
agricultural machinery, weather stations, soil parameters, and precision 
irrigation systems. However, it is worth noting a notable decrease in the 
number of publications observed in 2021, which can be attributed to the 
impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on academic production [19]. This 
decline may also be due to the common practice of conducting research 
one to two years before final publication, a process that, as noted above, 
was affected by the pandemic.
Following the pattern of previous years (except for 2021), the 
number of studies using AI methods to improve agricultural practices, 
including yield prediction, is expected to increase significantly by 2024.
From the survey data, the leading countries in research focused on 
predicting tree yield using remote sensing and ML are China (eight), 
followed by Brazil (seven), and the United States and Spain with six 
research, respectively (Fig. 2b). This pattern of crop research is closely 
linked to these countries’ position as some of the world’s leading

This information is consistent with the results of this review, which 
found that the most studied crop was apple (22 %), followed by coffee 
and citrus (14 % each) (Fig. 3). It should be noted that the scope of the

Compared to regular or annual agronomic crops such as soybean, 
corn, and wheat, studies with trees require more time because orchards 
or groves take longer to start producing; as a result, more than fifty 
percent of these crops have been studied in commercial fields (52 %), 
while the rest have been studied in experimental settings (42 %), and a 
small fraction of research has been conducted in both contexts (6 %). 
However, estimating the yield of tree crops is just as important as any 
other crop because yield estimation is required not only for predicting 
market dynamics and investments throughout the crop cycle but also for 
estimating the labor required to harvest the crop. In Florida citrus 
groves, for example, the typical approach to yield prediction involves 
sending workers into the fields to randomly select trees, count the 
number of fruits on each tree, harvest some of them, and then measure 
their weight. This methodology is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and 
sample-based and has been applied similarly to other tree crops. This 
underscores the need for more accurate and non-destructive techniques 
for tree crop yield prediction.
Predicting yield from field data has evolved significantly, with 
numerous methods available, including biomass analysis, fruit counting, 
yield monitors, and weather data [23,24]. More recently, advances in 
sensor technology, such as cell phones and high-quality cameras [25], as 
well as the integration of sensors aboard satellites, aircraft, and UAVs 
[26], have revolutionized the ability to collect precise and comprehen­
sive data, enabling more accurate and insightful crop yield prediction. 
According to this review analysis, the predominant techniques used to 
predict tree yield included various data collection methods. Field data 
was the most common approach at 32 %, followed by ground-based 
methods at 22 %. In addition, combinations of field data with satellite 
accounted for 13 %, while field data using UAV technology accounted 
for 9 %, and satellite and UAV approaches each accounted for 7 % 
(Fig. 4a). In this review, “field data” refers to information collected 
directly from the field using traditional methods, such as measured plant 
size, soil parameters, weather, previous yield, and biomass. These 
methods have already been applied to yield prediction before advances 
in remote sensing and artificial intelligence. “Ground-based” includes all 
data collected in the field using remote sensors, including cameras, cell 
phones, and LiDAR.
Landsat stands out as the most frequently used satellite for data 
collection (six research papers), followed by Sentinel (five research pa­
pers) (Fig. 4b). Landsat is one of the oldest satellite missions, with the 
latest version being launched in 2021. However, despite the low tem­
poral (16 days) and spatial resolution (30 m), the time series provided by 
the Landsat satellites allows a temporal evaluation of more than 20 years 
and provides a free high spectral resolution compared to other satellites, 
such as PlanetScop that contains four spectral bands, and it is free just 
for education purpose [27]. It was found that more than 80 % of the data 
collected by satellites was on commercial orchards. This finding explains 
the higher prevalence of lower resolution satellites, such as Landsat and 
Sentinel, which are easily accessible and free to use.
Among UAVs, Phantom 4 and Phantom 3 are the most frequently 
used, appearing in four and three research papers, respectively. Their 
popularity is due to their cost-effectiveness and compatibility with a 
wide range of sensors. Additionally, their compact size, ease of use, and 
other features meet the requirements of efficient data collection for 
research purposes. Mobile phones are also widely used for data collec­
tion and R&D (Fig. 4b). They serve as excellent, low-cost, and efficient 
tools for collecting data. However, due to certain limitations, such as the 
need for an operator to take the images, their use is primarily limited to

Nevertheless, it is important to note that the platforms themselves 
only support data collection, with the primary role being played by the 
sensors. These sensors are responsible for capturing the vector or raster 
data from the field and providing information about the trees. In the 
systematic review, sensors that generate images in the visible spectrum 
(RGB) were the most used (54 %); this type of sensor provides high 
resolution outputs that allow visual assessment of trees (Fig. 4c). As a 
result, these sensors are primarily used for tasks such as image seg­
mentation and/or classification [28]. Multispectral sensors, used in 21 
% of the research studies, typically include more than four bands of the 
spectrum, and in almost all cases, they were used to collect vegetation 
information about the field or trees, such as vegetation indices and the 
values from the specific wavelength bands including Near Infrared (NIR) 
and Red Edge [29].
LiDAR is also an important tool for yield prediction; it can provide 3D 
information about trees that helps characterize and reconstruct the 
plant. Approximately 14 % of the research studies used this technology 
for yield prediction (Fig. 4c). These sensors offer significant advantages 
regardless of lighting conditions, overcoming a common limitation in 
fruit detection and yield monitoring [30]. Traditional passive sensors, 
such as RGB and multispectral cameras, often face challenges in varying 
lighting conditions. However, the use of LiDAR can be limited in part by 
its higher cost compared to alternative sensors that also offer the 
advantage of capturing color data.

Machine learning techniques for yield prediction
Analysis of the ML models used in the studies revealed that 41 % of 
the studies used only one type of model to predict yield. In addition, 28 
% of the studies used two different models, 12 % used three, and 17 % 
used four or more models. Notably, about 2 % of the research studies did 
not use a predictive model to estimate yield. Instead, they used remote 
sensing techniques to collect data without applying ML techniques to 
predict yield. Random forest (RF) has emerged as the predominant ML 
technique used in these studies (13 studies), followed by multiple linear 
regression (8 studies), convolutional neural network (CNN; 8 studies), 
support vector machine (SVM; 6 studies), artificial neural network 
(ANN; 6 studies), support vector regression (SVR; 5 studies), and linear 
regression (5 studies) (Fig. 5).
The predominant categories of tree yield prediction methods were 
ensemble methods (e.g., random forest - RF, AdaBoost, gradient boost­
ing - GB), deep learning (e.g., convolutional neural network - CNN, 
artificial neural network - ANN, U-Net), supervised learning (e.g., sup­
port vector machine - SVM, K-nearest neighbor - KNN), regression (e.g., 
Lasso, multiple and linear regression – MLR, LR), and decision trees. 
Each of these categories has been released with specific objectives and 
uses different approaches to achieve the results based on the data 
collected in the field.
Ensemble methods, or multiple classifier systems, are strategies that 
generate multiple models and then consolidate them to improve results. 
Originally designed to reduce variance and improve model accuracy, 
these methods have also grown due to their ability to effectively respond 
to a wide range of challenges related to data analysis [31]. The pre­
dominant model used in this ML model is the RF, which is widely used 
for yield prediction.

Deep learning (DL) is a methodology based on the use of multi-layer 
neural networks to process data, typically involving hierarchical struc­
tures that include multiple layers of information processing stages to 
facilitate the learning process. It involves the successive application of 
multiple layers or stages through which data undergoes processing to 
construct a data-driven model [36,37]. This methodology has limita­
tions too, including the need for large amounts of data, the risk of 
overfitting, the significant computational resources required to train 
deep neural networks (including GPUs and TPUs), and sensitivity to 
hyperparameters and initialization. The dominant model used in this ML 
approach is the CNN.

feature representations using multiple convolutional kernels, 
reducing model complexity. The hierarchical model suggests a 
structured hierarchy in the visual cortex, with higher-order cells 
showing selective responses to complex features [38,39]. This model 
is widely used in yield prediction, as shown in the examples below:

In supervised learning, a relationship is established between a set of 
input variables (X) and an output variable (Y), allowing for the predic­
tion of outputs for unseen data. The algorithms used generate functions 
that map inputs to desired outputs. A common supervised learning 
scenario is the classification problem, where the goal is to approximate a 
function that assigns a vector to one of several predefined classes guided 
by input-output examples. These models are then evaluated for their 
predictive accuracy relative to the variability inherent in the dataset 
[41,42]. Some models based on this methodology require labeled data 
and may face scalability issues with large datasets. They may also 
struggle to generalize to data that differs significantly from the training 
dataset. Additionally, traditional supervised learning algorithms often 
require careful and manual feature engineering to achieve good per­
formance [43]. These issues can be drawbacks when choosing a model. 
The predominant models used in this ML models are SVM and KNN.
• SVM is an innovative approach to binary classification. It nonlinearly 
maps input vectors to a high-dimensional feature space, constructing 
a linear decision surface with robust generalization properties. 
Initially designed for separable data, it is extended to non-separable 
cases using polynomial transformations. Its effectiveness is demon­
strated through high generalization performance and comparative 
analysis with classical algorithms, as seen in optical character 
recognition studies [44]. Examples of the use of these models in yield 
prediction include:
➢ The SVM model was utilized for yield prediction of individual

kg, and a normalized RMSE of 18.68 % [45]. The model was likely 
selected for its ability to handle diverse data types, maximize 
prediction accuracy, provide insight into trait contributions, and 
ensure robust performance in predicting apple tree yields for 
precision orchard management.
➢ The SVM model was employed for jujube yield prediction based 
on field data, such as water and fertilizer applications, achieving 
an R2 of 0.81 and an RMSE of 1193.6 kg/hm2 [46]. In this study, 
SVM was probably used for its ability to handle complex re­
lationships and high-dimensional data.

Regression analysis is used to explore potential causal relationships 
between independent and dependent variables under certain conditions, 
using the independent variables to predict the dependent variables. 
While simple linear regression addresses situations with a single inde­
pendent variable, MLR extends this notion to predict the outcome of a 
response variable from a collection of independent variables [49,50]. 
These methods are susceptible to several challenges, including sensi­
bility to outliers and multicollinearity. They also assume homoscedas­
ticity and can suffer from overfitting and underfitting the data. The 
primary models used for regression analysis are multiple and linear 
regression.
• Multiple and Linear regression: LR is a modeling approach used to 
analyze data for predictive purposes. In simple linear regression, a 
bivariate model is constructed to predict a response variable (y) 
based on a single explanatory variable (x). On the other hand, MLR is 
the technique that extends the model to include more than one 
explanatory variable (x1, x2, xn), resulting in a multivariate model 
[51]. Examples of applications of these models in yield prediction 
research include:
➢ Yield prediction models (RF and MLR) were trained, based on a 
time series of satellite imagery (spectral bands and VIs) and high- 
density yield monitor data, to predict coffee yield with an R2 of 
0.88 [52]. Regression models were likely chosen in this study 
because of their ability to model relationships between different 
data sets, avoid overfitting, achieve high predictive accuracy, and 
provide insight into the importance of variables.
➢ Gonzalez Nieto et al. [53] evaluated the accuracy of several 
commercial computer vision systems and computer applications 
for estimating trunk cross-sectional area, number of flower clus­
ters, thinning efficacy, and yield estimation for apple trees. The 
linear regression used had a performance of 0.61 R2 and 37 kg/ha 
RMSE, and the quadratic regression had R2 of 0.66 and RMSE of 
36.9 kg/ha. Linear regression was likely chosen for its simplicity, 
interpretability, and efficiency, providing a straightforward way 
to model the relationship between variables.
The decision tree approach involves non-incremental learning from 
examples, where systems are provided with a collection of cases relevant 
to a classification assignment. They are built from the “top-down” 
approach, driven by the frequency data in the examples rather than their 
specific presentation order. In this hierarchical structure, class names 
are assigned to the “leaves”, while other nodes denote attribute-based 
tests that cover all possible outcomes. To classify an object, the pro­
cedure starts at the “root of the tree”, where the test is evaluated, and the 
appropriate branch is selected based on the result [54]. These ML 
models are prone to overfitting, sensitive to data changes, and tend to 
favor dominant classes in unbalanced datasets. Additionally, they may 
not effectively capture linear relationships between features and the 
target variable and may not perform reliably over the range of the 
training data. An example of the use of this model in yield prediction 
include:
➢ To evaluate the effectiveness of remote sensing and vegetation 
indices obtained by UAV-based multispectral imagery in detecting 
changes in canopy structure due to water stress and estimating the 
yield of adult sweet cherry trees, a decision tree model was trained. 
The model achieved a precision of 0.87 in classifying trees according 
to their yield [55]. The authors probably selected DT for its ability to 
handle complex datasets derived from multispectral UAS imagery 
and to integrate multiple vegetation indices effectively.
The metrics used to assess the accuracy and performance of ML 
models in tree crop yield prediction revealed several patterns. Among 
them, 28 % used two metrics to evaluate the accuracy or performance of 
the ML models, while 25 % relied on a single evaluation metric. In 
addition, 38 % used three or more metrics for evaluation, while only 9 % 
did not use any evaluation. The predominant metrics for assessing the 
effectiveness and data fit of ML models include the coefficient of 
determination (R2), which is used in 12 research studies, closely fol­
lowed by the RMSE and the mean square error (MSE), each of which was 
used in 11 studies (Fig. 6). The mean absolute error (MAE) is also 
common, appearing in 7 studies, along with mean absolute percentage 
error (MAPE) and recall, each appearing in 4 studies. In addition, a 
variety of other parameters, comprising 29 different metrics, were used 
in three or fewer studies.
The evaluation metrics can be categorized into two distinct groups: 
regression and classification metrics. These categories are customized to 
suit the unique requirements and objectives of individual studies, 
ensuring that the metrics selected can provide valuable insights into the 
performance of the ML models used in yield prediction studies.
Regression metrics
Regression models produce continuous outputs that require metrics 
to measure the closeness between predicted and ground truth values. 
These metrics typically assess the degree of deviation or distance be­
tween predicted and actual values, providing valuable insight into the 
accuracy and effectiveness of the regression model [56,57]. The most 
used regression performance/accuracy metrics in the studied papers 
were:
• Mean Squared Error is a fundamental metric for regression tasks. It 
computes the average of the squared differences between predicted 
and actual values, providing a measure of the overall model accu­
racy. Its differentiability facilitates optimization during model 
training through techniques like gradient descent. The squaring of 
errors in MSE amplifies the influence of outliers, highlighting their 
impact on the model’s performance. Understanding this emphasis on 
outliers is crucial for interpreting MSR values accurately [57–59].
• Mean absolute error calculates the average absolute differences be­
tween predicted and actual values, offering a robust measure of 
model accuracy in regression tasks. Unlike MSE, MAE is less sensitive 
to outliers because it does not square the errors, which can dispro­
portionately impact the metric. However, MAE does not indicate the 
direction of errors (whether they are underprediction or over­
prediction) since it uses absolute values. MAE’s interpretation aligns 
seamlessly with the original variable scale, but it lacks differentia­
bility, which affects the optimization landscape in regression tasks 
(during model training) [57–59].
• Root mean squared error is the square root of the average of the 
squared differences between predicted and actual values, essentially 
the square root of MSE. It inherits the differentiability of MSE, which 
helps in optimization. RMSE tends to disproportionately penalize 
smaller errors by normalizing the scale of error interpretation to that 
of the variable through square root transformation. This normaliza­
tion enhances RMSE’s robustness to outliers since outliers are eval­
uated uniformly across the dataset [57–59].
• Coefficient of determination (R2) quantifies the proportion of total 
variation in the target variable (Y) that is explained by the regression

line (X). Derived from the squared errors, R2 ranges from negative 
infinity to 1. When the sum of squared errors is minimal, R2 ap­

100 % of the variance. Conversely, when the sum of squared errors is 
large, R2 tends towards 0, indicating that the model has failed to 
capture significant variance. While R2 intuitively ranges from 0 to 1, 
its actual range extends to negative infinity due to the possibility of 
squared errors exceeding those of the mean [57–59].
Classification metrics
Classification is widely studied worldwide, with applications span­
ning both research and industry. From face recognition to object 
detection, classification models are indispensable in many scenarios. 
The discrete nature of their outputs requires metrics that can effectively 
compare discrete classes. These metrics are critical for evaluating model 
performance and understanding the effectiveness of the classification 
process, which is why they are used to predict the yield of trees. Each 
metric evaluates performance differently and provides different per­
spectives on the classification results [56,57]; in the case of the tech­
niques used in the surveys studied, the most used evaluation method was 
recall (Fig. 6).
The recall measures the ratio of true positives to all true positives. It 
focuses on Type II errors, which involve misclassifying a negative 
diagnosis as a positive diagnosis. A high recall value, closer to 1, in­
dicates effective identification of true positives. However, recall does 
not account for Type I errors (false positives). A low recall, typically 
below 0.5, indicates a high false negative rate, which can be caused by 
class imbalance or suboptimal model parameters. Techniques such as 
over/under sampling or focal loss can help to address the imbalance and 
enhance classifier performance [60].
Machine learning model accuracy
After conducting a comprehensive review of the literature and 
related datasets, it becomes apparent that the accuracy of the models 
utilized is strongly associated with the sample size. The reported accu­
racy of the best models in yield prediction research varied widely, 
ranging from 50 % to 99 %. It is noteworthy that in many cases, the 
highest accuracy values were achieved on smaller datasets, typically 
encompassing fewer than a thousand trees or small experimental plots. 
However, as the evaluated fields scaled up to commercial or larger sizes, 
there tends to be a decline in the accuracy of yield prediction models.
Another critical consideration is the timing of field data collection for 
training and testing ML models. Data collected too close to or too far 
from harvest can pose challenges depending on the research objectives. 
For example, if farmers require yield predictions to determine input 
investments, relying on data collected very close to harvest to develop

Research gaps and insights
Each ML technique offers researchers and developers unique capa­
bilities that complement each other to provide different insights into the 
task of yield prediction. Some techniques may excel at handling certain 
types of data or capturing certain patterns in the dataset, while others 
may offer better interpretability or scalability. By integrating multiple 
ML models, researchers and farmers can leverage the strengths of each 
approach to gain a more complete understanding of yield prediction and 
make more accurate and informed decisions. Ensuring the accuracy and 
representation of collected data for training yield prediction models is 
critical. Proper preprocessing, such as data cleaning and normalization, 
is essential to maintain the quality of the input data. Feature engineer­
ing, which involves the selection and transformation of relevant vari­
ables, can significantly improve model performance. In addition, 
integrating domain knowledge into the modeling process can improve 
the interpretability and reliability of predictions. A multifaceted 
approach, using multiple methods or variables to predict tree yield, in­
creases the robustness of the predictions and allows a comprehensive 
evaluation of these techniques using real field data (ground truth). 
Integrating different methods and multiple data sources and considering 
different factors that influence yield allows researchers to better capture 
the complexity of agricultural systems and improve the reliability of 
yield predictions. This includes combining input data from multiple 
sources, such as weather, soil, sensor data (aerial and ground), and other 
relevant information. This multi-method approach helps identify the 
most appropriate technique for each situation, increases the variability 
and amount of data collected, and improves the robustness of ML 
models, making them applicable across years and locations. Combining 
different models, known as ensemble learning, can mitigate the weak­
nesses of individual models and often produce better results. Continuous 
model evaluation and validation using cross-validation techniques help 
maintain robustness and prevent overfitting. As ML continues to evolve, 
the incorporation of advanced techniques such as deep learning and 
transfer learning holds promise for more accurate and scalable yield 
prediction models. Ultimately, the successful application of ML to tree 
yield prediction depends on a holistic approach that includes data 
quality, model selection, and rigorous evaluation practices. By inte­
grating multiple ML models, ensuring data quality, and employing 
advanced modeling strategies, researchers and farmers in the agricul­
tural industry can gain more reliable and actionable insights for effective 
decision-making.
The size of the fields from which data is collected and the data 
collection tools used are also important when employing ML techniques 
for yield prediction. The sheer size of the field has a significant impact 
on the accuracy and reliability of the results derived from the data 
collected and, therefore, the effectiveness of the yield prediction model. 
In addition, the timing of data collection plays a critical role in ensuring 
the accuracy of the model. Collecting data at the right time (e.g., in 
specific growth stages) is important to developing an accurate and 
reliable yield prediction model. However, collecting data in a variety of 
situations and under different crop and weather conditions is also crit­
ical to achieving the best yield prediction ML models. Ultimately, these 
factors highlight the importance of careful planning and strategic data 
collection practices as they directly impact the success and efficiency of 
farm operations. Agricultural environments are highly variable due to 
factors such as weather, soil conditions, and pest presence, among other 
factors. Models must be robust enough to handle this variability and 
make accurate predictions under different conditions.
There are also challenges to using ML techniques for yield prediction 
purposes. These include data availability, as robust models require huge 
amounts of data to avoid under or over-fitting results. Additionally, the 
infrastructure needed to collect high-quality data, build the models, and 
analyze them is critical, including sensors, IoT devices, and computa­
tional resources. The cost and complexity of building and maintaining 
this infrastructure can be unaffordable, especially in developing regions. 
Specialized professionals with expertise in both agriculture and data 
science are needed not only to collect and process the data but also to 
interpret and know how to apply the results generated. These pro­
fessionals must not only understand the nuances of agricultural practices 
but also have the technical skills to apply ML techniques effectively. 
Selecting the appropriate ML models is crucial as it must align not only 
with the research objectives but also with the data collection method­
ology and strategy, and the intended application. Improper model se­
lection can lead to inaccurate predictions and ineffective solutions. 
Implementing ML solutions can be expensive and resource-intensive. 
The cost of hardware, software, and skilled personnel can be a barrier, 
especially for small farms or research centers. Generative AI can be used 
to address the challenges of data set size and robustness. Generative AI 
techniques can create new, synthetic data derived from existing data­
sets, expanding the overall dataset and providing more training exam­
ples for models. This can help in situations where acquiring real-world 
data is difficult or expensive. Overcoming these challenges will require a 
multifaceted approach that integrates technological advances, interdis­
ciplinary collaboration, and strategic planning.
Some studies have not clearly explained their methods for model 
generation and data analysis. A clear presentation of the methods used 
for model generation and data analysis is important in replicating the 
research in different settings. When researchers thoroughly document 
their procedures, it ensures that other researchers can understand and 
follow the same procedures, facilitating reproducibility and promoting 
the advancement of knowledge across different settings. Insufficient 
explanation of these processes can make it difficult for other researchers 
to understand how results were obtained, making it difficult to validate 
and extend findings. Therefore, it is crucial for researchers to thoroughly 
document their steps to enhance transparency and facilitate reproduc­
ibility, ultimately improving the quality and impact of future research 
endeavors.
Finally, efforts should be made to develop open datasets, user- 
friendly, and easy-to-use decision support systems that incorporate 
these ML models for empowering farmers with data-driven knowledge. 
Simplifying the implementation and interpretation of these techniques 
will enable farmers to make informed decisions about crop manage­
ment, resource allocation, and overall farm operations. By providing 
user-friendly interfaces and clear documentation, farmers could easily 
leverage these tools to enhance productivity, sustainability, and profit­
ability in their farming practices. This user-centric approach ensures 
that the benefits of advanced analytics and ML are accessible to all 
farmers, fostering innovation and resilience in agriculture.
Conclusions
Despite the use of precise search strategies such as Boolean operators 
and relevant keywords in accordance with database guidelines, many 
unrelated papers were retrieved, making it difficult to identify the 
relevant research among the numerous results that did not meet the 
objectives of this specific study. This discrepancy may be due to inap­
propriate terminology used by some authors or differences in database 
methodologies that are not adequately explained to the users. Clarifying 
and standardizing terminology, as well as providing clearer guidance on 
database search methodologies, can help mitigate this issue and improve 
the efficiency of literature searches. The limited number of selected 
papers in this systematic review may be due to the specific focus of this 
research. During the filtering process, it was found that many papers use

ML techniques for precision agriculture purposes, such as fruit and

directly related to the scope of this review, focused on tree crop yield

the review.
Studies with limited datasets for training often use simpler models 
such as linear regression, while studies with larger datasets use more 
complex models, including techniques such as deep learning, ensemble 
methods, and hyperparameter tuning, and performance evaluation 
tends to be more sophisticated. Accuracy levels of the utilized ML 
models range from 50 % to 99 %, with studies with smaller datasets 
often achieving higher accuracy. While ML techniques provide valuable 
insights for yield prediction, their effectiveness depends on accurate 
field data, strategic data collection, and a multi-method approach. 
Combining multiple data sources such as weather, soil, and plant in­
formation increases model robustness and applicability. Overcoming 
challenges such as data availability and the need for specialized exper­
tise in developing robust ML models is critical. Finally, the development 
of user-friendly decision support systems will enable farmers to use these 
advanced techniques to improve agricultural productivity, sustainabil­
ity, and profitability.
