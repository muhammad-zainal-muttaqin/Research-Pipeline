A B S T R A C T
Extracting phenotypic traits of grape bunch is crucial for accurately monitoring grape quality, health, and yield 
estimation. This is important for optimising resources, enhancing marketing strategies, and boosting overall 
agricultural productivity. While most research concentrates on data processing algorithms, this study focused on 
the preceding step: collecting reliable data. Object detection and tracking enable precise monitoring and 
quantification of fruit, facilitating agricultural management. This study compares two data acquisition meth­
odologies for grape bunch detection and tracking in a commercial vineyard where leaf removal was not per­
formed: a traditional single-view approach and a multiple-viewing method designed to mitigate fruit occlusion 
issues. The PointTrack algorithm, trained and validated using MOTS annotations, was employed to evaluate 
detection and tracking performance through metrics of three trials. The multiple-view method achieved i) higher 
ratio between tracked and GT detections of 74 % compared to 23 % for the single-view approach and ii) 
enhanced tracking metrics, with the multiple viewing trials metrics ranging from −1.35 to 3.84 for MOTSA 
(Multiple Object Tracking and Segmentation Accuracy) and sMOTSA (soft MOTSA), and iii) higher correlation 
and lower RMSE of grape bunch phenotypic traits (OIV codes 202 and 203) compared to ground truth mea­
surements (R2 = 0.53, RMSE = 19.13). Nonetheless, the multi-view technique was compromised by motion blur 
due to UAV movements, complicating the tracking process. This study underscores the importance of strategic 
data acquisition in improving performance for fruit detection and tracking. Future work should extend this 
methodology to other fruit varieties and environments to validate its broader applicability, enhancing the reli­
ability of yield estimation in precision agriculture.
1. Introduction
Deep Learning (DL) has been applied in recent years in the agricul­
tural domain, for instance, in fruit detection and tracking [1,2], wit­
nessing notable advancements and offering promising research lines for 
yield prediction [3–5] and fruit growth monitoring [6,7], among others. 
Within DL, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have proven to be 
particularly effective since they learn complex features from images and 
videos, which allow accurate fruit recognition in complex field envi­
ronments, such as challenging illumination conditions [8]. These tech­
nologies offer great potential in the agricultural sector for fruit counting, 
phenotyping, and consequent yield prediction, abating inspection time 
and reducing labour costs.
One of the advantages of Deep Learning is domain adaptation [9,10], 
which consists of applying the knowledge gained in one specific field to 
another domain. For instance Ref. [11], developed a fruit flower 
detection for multiple species trained on apple flower images, and tested 
on apple, peach, and pear flowers. Further [12], implemented Faster 
R-CNN for multi-class fruit detection, including apple, mango, and or­
ange labels, which benefits from a common characteristic between those 
fruits: their circularity.
Despite the potential of DL in fruit counting [13], challenges can lead 
to under or overestimation, for instance, occlusion [14,15]. Occlusion 
occurs when fruits are partially or fully hidden, typically by leaves and 
branches, complicating the detection and tracking task [16]. Fully hid­
den fruits, enclosed by leaves, are not detectable from any perspective,

Furthermore, detection and tracking metrics suffer from fruit oc­

[20–22]. Nevertheless, the focus can also point to the optimal acquisi­
tion of the datasets by considering multi-view perceptions [23] and the 
specific knowledge domain for which they are being acquired [24]. 
Therefore, while most research targets data processing algorithms [18,
19], this study targets the initial step of gathering accurate data.
To detect partially occluded fruits, multi-view strategies have 
increasingly gained attention in agricultural research since their po­
tential to enhance object detection and tracking has been shown. For 
example [25], developed a theoretical framework for UAVs to acquire 
images or videos from the side of the canopy considering multiple views 
(frontal, left side, and right side) to increase the chance of observing the 
fruit at its full size. [26,27], have further demonstrated the advantages 
of multi-view approaches for improving the detection and counting of 
fruits and flowers.
Following this rationale, this study validates the theoretical frame­
work proposed by Ref. [25] in a real-world experiment conducted in a 
commercial vineyard with leaf occlusion. Unlike previous work, which 
primarily focused on theoretical modeling, this study provides an 
empirical assessment by comparing the results with those obtained using 
a traditional single-view methodology for data acquisition. It analyses 
key metrics such as grape bunch detection and tracking accuracy, fruit 
counting, and correlation with ground truth (GT) measurements. This 
study makes a unique contribution by contrasting the outcomes when 
fruit detection is performed with regard to the data acquisition method 
versus actively optimising data collection through the use of multiple 
views. By bridging the gap between theoretical modelling and practical 
application, the findings not only validate the proposed framework but 
also offer insights for improving fruit detection and counting in com­
mercial agricultural settings.
2. Materials and methods
The research process that was carried out is outlined in Fig. 1, 
illustrating the employed workflow. The focus of attention was driven 
already to the first step: data acquisition. Two different methodologies 
were employed to gather the necessary videos in MP4 format. The first 
method used the traditional method that consisted of flying the UAV to 
record videos of the side of the canopy without considering any 
enhancement, similar to Ref. [28]. The second implemented a 
multi-view viewing approach (Fig. 2), which involves collecting data 
from various perspectives to reduce errors in fruit detection caused by 
leaves and branches blocking the view. It was designed specifically for 
woody crop environments, such as taking into account the biophysical 
characteristics of the field.
For both methods, the UAV used was a DJI Phantom 4 RTK (DJI 
Sciences and Technologies Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) that has 
the sensor integrated. Flights were conducted at an altitude of 3 m above 
ground level (AGL), and the speed was not stable, ranging between 0.1 
and 1 m/s. Videos were captured between September 19 and September 
20, 2023, under sunny conditions with wind speeds below 0.5 m/s. This 
experimental setup aimed to test the hypothesis that the multi-view 
approach is more effective for object detection and tracking while 
addressing occlusion issues. For more details on the experimental setup, 
refer to Ref. [29].

and were composed of cleaning the videos, annotation of the grape 
bunches, and training/validating the PointTrack [30] algorithm. A total

2.1. PointTrack implementation and metrics
The implementation of the PointTrack algorithm in this study was 
largely similar to that in Ariza-Sentís et al. [32], except for image res­
olution differences due to the utilisation of different sensors. Transfer 
learning [33] was employed to train the Spatial Embeddings (SE) model. 
An Adam optimiser [34] with a learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁵ and a finetuning 
rate of 5 × 10⁻⁶ was used. The SE model [35] was trained with a batch 
size of 20 and 1200 epochs. Hyperparameters were optimized by 
adjusting their values up and down until no further improvement in 
performance or overfitting was detected. During SE training, image 
crops were vertically oriented to match the grape bunches’ shape, with 
resolutions of 256 x 512 and 512 x 1024 pixels for initial training and 
finetuning, respectively. The PointTrack tracking component was 
trained with a batch size of 32 and 100 epochs.
Several metrics were computed to assess their ability to detect and 
track grape bunches to compare the two proposed methodologies and 
determine the most valid method for object detection and tracking in 
vineyards without leaf removal. Two detection performance metrics, 
MOTSP (Multiple Object Tracking and Segmentation Precision) and 
MODSP (Multiple Object Detection and Segmentation Precision) were 
calculated using Equations (1) and (2). Additionally, MOTSA (Multiple 
Object Tracking and Segmentation Accuracy) and sMOTSA (soft 
MOTSA) were obtained as detection metrics by applying Equations (3) 
and (4). The higher the metrics the more accurate on performance.

2.2. Grape bunch measurements
The International Organization of Vine and Wine (OIV) [36] has 
established several standards for characterising the phenotyping of 
grape bunches. For example, bunch length and width correspond to the 
descriptor codes OIV 202 and 203, respectively. According to the OIV 
guidelines, a sample size of 10 bunches is required to determine these 
descriptors. Therefore, this study analysed ten bunches to compare the 
phenotyping traits (grape bunch length and width) between annotated 
and detected bunches. Measuring these descriptors is crucial for gaining 
insights into the physical dimensions of the bunches, which serve as 
indicators of fruit quality and vine health [37]. Moreover, the size of 
grape bunches is directly related to the harvest yield, thus enabling more 
precise yield estimations [38].
3. Results
A series of experiments were conducted to evaluate the performance 
of the algorithm. The most effective trials were as follows. 
- Trial 1: training the algorithm exclusively with multiple view data
- Trial 2: training the algorithm exclusively with single-view data
- Trial 3: training the algorithm with a combination of multiple and 
single view data
3.1. Detection and tracking metrics
Table 1 presents the detection and tracking metrics for each trial. The 
video number remained consistent across trials. Some values are nega­
tive due to the occurrence of false positives. It was observed that for the 
single-view dataset, Trial 2 achieved the highest detection and tracking 
metrics (sMOTSA, MOTSA and MODSA equal to −0.08), while for the 
multiple-view dataset, Trial 3 exhibited the highest metrics (positive 
values for all sMOTSA, MOTSA and MODSA). This can be attributed to 
the fact that Trial 2 included only single-view data, allowing the algo­
rithm to learn the patterns of that specific dataset effectively. In contrast, 
Trial 3, which incorporated both multiple and single-view data, enabled 
the algorithm to generalise better for the multiple-view scenario.
Fig. 3 provides a visual comparison of the sMOTSA metrics across 
trials for each category (single and multiple angle).
3.2. Grape bunch counting
In addition to the detection and tracking metrics, the algorithm’s 
ability to count the number of grape bunches was evaluated. Fig. 4
shows the relative difference between the ground truth number of grape 
bunches present in each video and the predicted number. In most cases, 
the value is negative, indicating undercounting. The closer the value is 
to 0, the more accurate the detection count. Positive values indicate 
overcounting of the predicted grape bunches.
3.3. Single and multliple views to avoid occlusion
Furthermore, to visually validate the importance of occlusion, 
several images of the same plant, both with single and multiple views, 
are presented in Fig. 5. It can be observed that incorporating multiple 
points of view reduces the occlusion of grape bunches, making them 
easier to detect and track.
3.4. Phenotypic assessment
Table 2 displays the annotated and detected masks of 10 grape 
bunches for both single-view and multiple-view perspectives. It can be 
observed that the sizes and shapes differ between single and multiple 
views, even for the same grape bunch GT, due to fruit occlusion, as 
highlighted in Fig. 5, which plays a critical role in hiding full shapes. In 
addition, grape bunches appear more accurately shaped in the multiple- 
view masks since they are completely visible from at least one of the

4. Discussion
Fruit occlusion presents a significant challenge in object detection 
and tracking [14,39,40]. A multiple-view method was implemented to 
address this issue, capturing images from various viewpoints to reduce 
occlusion and enhance visibility. Although the detection and tracking 
metrics were not the primary focus, the observed limitations, such as 
false positives due to colour similarities and varying illumination 
conditions [41,42], were similar to the problem presented by Ref. [32].
Despite these challenges, the multiple-view approach demonstrated 
a more accurate count of grape bunches compared to single-view 
methods. Fig. 4 showed that the relative difference between the actual 
and predicted number of grape bunches was more accurate with mul­
tiple views, despite significant undercounting in most cases. This can be 
expected because of occlusions, making underestimation more likely 
than overestimation, except in cases where there are a lot of FPs. The 
ratio between Tracked and GT values for single view ranged from 13 % 
to 23 %, while multiple views consistently outperformed, ranging from 
27 % to 74 %, with the highest metrics observed in the second trial.
Illumination conditions varied across trials and datasets, necessi­
tating validation to ensure shadows did not significantly impact detec­
tion. Table 2 remarks on the shape differences between single-view and 
multiple-view detections, with the latter providing a more realistic 
representation of grape bunches. This improvement in morphology, as 
shown in Table 2, highlights the ability of multiple views to better 
capture the shape of grape bunches, aligning them more closely with

4.1. Limitations and future research
Several limitations were encountered during the development of the 
research. One significant challenge was the difficulty in labelling the 
videos due to the homogeneity between white grape bunches and the 
surrounding green canopy. This issue was further complicated by chal­
lenging illumination conditions, which often made it hard to distinguish 
between fruit and shadows. Moreover, the process of annotating videos 
captured from multiple angles presented difficulties in identifying the 
same grape bunch across different frames. Despite these obstacles, the 
labelling was successfully completed, demonstrating resilience to the 
inherent environmental variability encountered in agricultural settings.
No specific limitations were observed regarding the selected UAV, 
which was chosen for its affordability and suitability for farmers. While 
more advanced sensors could have been employed, an integrated sensor 
was selected to enhance reproducibility. The UAV and sensor setup 
proved sufficient accuracy for the objectives of this study.
During the grape bunch annotation process, some challenges were 
found. Continuity in tracking annotated grape bunches, especially when 
they disappeared for several frames, was complicated but crucial for 
maintaining accurate metrics. We followed a similar annotation pro­
cedure as described in Ref. [43], ensuring that grape bunches reap­
pearing in subsequent frames retained the same ID. While some datasets, 
allow for discontinuous tracks [44], we found that this approach led to 
lower tracking metrics in our study. Maintaining continuity proved 
essential for accurate detection and tracking in our MOT-based 
framework.
Future research should include vineyards from different latitudes 
and varieties to train the object detection and tracking algorithm in 
other environments. Expanding the dataset to include diverse latitudes 
would enhance the algorithm’s robustness and generalisation. Although 
the current study utilised a significant volume of video data, incorpo­
rating additional variations would provide further improvements. 
Furthermore, the methodology could be extended to red grape varieties 
and other fruit species to validate the results and broaden the scope of 
application.
The replication of experiments across single and multiple perspec­
tives validated the added value of having multi-view strategies in 
commercial vineyard conditions, further supporting the theoretical 
framework. Despite these challenges, the improvement achieved by 
including multiple views over a single view emphasises the importance 
of a well-structured data acquisition plan. Proper planning can signifi­
cantly enhance the accuracy of fruit detection and tracking, even under 
non-ideal conditions. This study demonstrates that multiple views are a

5. Conclusions
This study demonstrates that employing multiple perspectives for 
fruit detection and tracking is more effective than relying on a single 
perspective, as it minimises occlusion and enhances the robustness of the 
algorithm. In addition, it highlights the significance of strategic data 
acquisition in improving deep learning models for object detection and 
tracking. The PointTrack algorithm was utilised to gather detection and 
tracking metrics, along with assessing phenotypic traits in grape di­
mensions (OIV codes 202 and 203), which were then compared to 
ground truth values. Using a multi-view approach significantly reduced 
occlusion, enhancing the visibility and counting accuracy of grape 
bunches compared to conventional single-view methods, leading to 
more precise phenotyping and, consequently, better yield predictions. 
The multi-view approach achieved a ratio between tracked and GT de­
tections of 74 %, while the single-view method only identified 23 % of 
the present grape bunches. Moreover, incorporating multiple perspec­
tives improved the correlation with ground truth measurements, 
increasing the R2 from 0.39 with a single view to 0.53 with multiple 
views, and reducing the RMSE from 28.05 to 19.13. Future research 
should expand this methodology to other types of fruit and settings to 
confirm its wider applicability.
