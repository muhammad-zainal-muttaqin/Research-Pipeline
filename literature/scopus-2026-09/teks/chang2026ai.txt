A B S T R A C T
Three-dimensional point cloud (3DPC) data capture detailed geometric and structural plant traits beyond the 
capability of 2D imaging. When combined with artificial intelligence (AI), it offers a powerful, non-invasive tool 
for plant phenotyping, which is crucial for driving advancements in plant breeding and agriculture. However, 
challenges related to data complexity, limited datasets, and model generalization hinder 3DPC’s widespread 
adoption. To provide a comprehensive overview and guide future research in this area, we conducted a sys­
tematic literature review (SLR) following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses 
(PRISMA) guidelines by analysing 381 papers published between January 2017 and October 2025 from major 
databases. Our review examines the advantages, current status, limitations, and future directions of AI appli­
cations in 3DPC-based plant phenotyping. Our findings indicate a rapid increase in publications since 2022, with 
deep learning (DL) methods, especially pointwise MLP-based networks, driving much of this growth, with a 
notable recent surge in Transformer-based, Graph-based, and particularly Hybrid models that combine their 
strengths. Furthermore, novel methods like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) are 
emerging as powerful tools for 3D reconstruction and scene synthesis. Time-of-Flight (ToF) and Structure from 
Motion and Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS) technologies remain the predominant 3DPC data acquisition tech­
niques. Research in this area focuses on trees/shrubs and cereals, typically involving single-species studies. 
Although the overall use of public datasets remains low (18.9%), their adoption has significantly increased since 
2020. Key limitations identified include: (1) a lack of standardized data collection and formats, (2) insufficient 
model robustness and generalization, especially from lab to field, (3) high computational demands, and (4) a 
reliance on species-specific models. The future of AI-driven 3DPC phenotyping hinges on overcoming these 
bottlenecks. Priority should be given to: developing field-deployable, computationally efficient models; exploring 
the potential of the foundation model; establishing diverse and standardized public datasets; and strengthening 
the integration of 3D phenomics with genomics to bridge the genotype-to-phenotype gap. This review provides a 
foundational roadmap to guide research in plant phenomics, crop breeding, and plant science.
1. Introduction
Plant phenomics, the high-throughput acquisition and analysis of 
multi-dimensional phenotypes across a plant’s lifespan, is fundamental 
to modern plant biology and crop improvement [1]. The plant pheno­
type is the dynamic expression of the plant genotype as it interacts with 
its surrounding environment during the growth cycle [2]. In phenomics 
research, the phenotype refers to the measurable aspects of plant traits, 
including growth, development, and physiology [3–5]. Therefore, 
accurate phenotypic analysis is essential to plant breeding programs [6,
7], enabling the selection of superior genotypes and providing crucial 
insights into plant growth and development mechanisms [8].
In recent years, advances in plant phenotyping have begun to 
reshape plant science and agricultural research, with three-dimensional 
methods emerging as a powerful tool [9]. A key part of this trans­
formation involves 3DPC analysis, enabling the capture of plant geo­
metric information beyond the reach of traditional 2D imaging. In plant 
phenotyping, 3DPC are typically acquired using either active sensing

passive image-based approaches that reconstruct 3D geometry from 
multi-view RGB images via SfM-MVS pipelines [11]. Unlike 2D imaging, 
a 3DPC consists of countless spatial points—each defined by X, Y, and Z 
coordinates—offering a detailed, three-dimensional view of a plant. 
Beyond just shape, 3DPC can integrate additional data, such as color or 
spectral information, making it a versatile platform for combining 
multiple types of measurements. By revealing full spatial details, 3DPC 
yields more precise and reliable estimates for traits such as leaf structure 
and size or stem diameter [12,13].
Leveraging the detailed geometric information provided by 3DPC 
analysis, artificial intelligence (AI) has become an effective tool in plant 
phenotyping. This allows for the interpretation of intricate plant 3DPC 
data with far greater precision and efficiency [14]. Early research 
employed a range of traditional machine learning (ML) techniques, 
including supervised methods such as support vector machines (SVM) 
[15], random forests (RF) [16], decision tree (DT) [17], K-Nearest 
Neighbour (KNN) [19] etc., which relied on hand-engineered features 
for tasks like object detection [20], segmentation [21], and classification 
[22]. Subsequently, more automated local descriptors emerged, such as 
including surface feature histograms (SFH), point feature histograms 
(PFH), and fast point feature histograms (FPFH). These methods 
generate multi-dimensional representations of neighborhood geometry 
through histogram binning of normal relationships [23–25]. Meanwhile, 
unsupervised ML techniques were widely adopted for direct 3DPC 
analysis in plant phenotyping, such as density-based spatial clustering of 
applications with noise (DBSCAN) [26], K-means clustering [27] and 
hierarchical clustering [28], self-organized maps (SOM) [18], among 
others. These unsupervised methods were typically used for point cloud 
segmentation and clustering to identify plant structures, such as leaves, 
stems, or branches. However, both supervised and unsupervised ap­
proaches based on hand-crafted features tend to be poorly adaptive, 
susceptible to noise, lack the expressive power required for complex 
scenes, limit the accuracy and generalization of the models, and require 
significant expertise and experience from researchers [29–31].
Prior to the widespread adoption of deep learning (DL) for 3DPC, 
researchers typically converted point clouds into structured formats to 
leverage existing DL frameworks, such as traditional convolutional 
neural networks (CNNs) [32], using two primary approaches: voxeli­
zation, which partitions point clouds into three-dimensional grids 
(voxels) for processing with 3D CNNs, as exemplified by VoxNet [33], 
and multi-view projection, which projects point clouds into 2D images 
or voxels from multiple angles for analysis with 2D CNNs, as seen in 
MVCNN [34]. Although voxelization is intuitive, it is computationally 
intensive and memory-inefficient, particularly at high resolutions, 
where sparse point clouds generate numerous empty voxels. Multi-view 
projection, though effective for tasks like classification, sacrifices some 
three-dimensional geometric information. While these methods intro­
duced DL to 3DPC analysis, they processed data indirectly, thereby 
failing to fully exploit the intrinsic properties of raw point clouds [35]. 
These limitations spurred the adoption of DL in point cloud analysis, 
enabling advanced computational techniques to process raw data 
directly. For instance, PointNet [36],a pioneering DL network designed 
to directly process point clouds, demonstrated the transformative po­
tential of AI in this domain by focusing on global features, despite 
overlooking local geometric relationships between points. Since then, 
DL has surged in this area, opening the door to quantifying traits such as 
leaf angle and other detailed phenotypes with unprecedented accuracy 
[37,38]. Building on PointNet’s success, PointNet++ [39] addressed the 
absence of local feature extraction, while the Dynamic Graph CNN 
(DGCNN) [40] advanced point cloud processing into the realm of graph 
neural network (GNN), marking a shift from static local feature extrac­
tion to dynamic relational modeling. Driven by evolving needs, re­
searchers began to prioritize computational efficiency, sparsity 
handling, and enhanced geometric representation. Sparse convolution 
methods, such as MinkowskiNet [41], combined voxel-based 
representations with sparse convolution to optimize efficiency for sparse 
point clouds, proving particularly effective for segmentation of large 
trees [42]. After 2021, transformer-based approaches, such as Point 
Transformer [43] and PCT (Point Cloud Transformer) [44], leveraged 
self-attention mechanisms to capture both global and local relationships, 
further improving performance [45,46]. Lightweight models, like 
PointMLP [47], employed simpler multilayer perceptron (MLP) struc­
tures to achieve high performance with reduced computational 
complexity [48]. Recently, NeRF [49] and its variants have risen to 
prominence, introducing innovative approaches for reconstructing 
detailed three-dimensional scenes from two-dimensional plant images 
[50,51]. At the same time, 3DGS [52] and its generative extensions have 
driven a paradigm shift from geometric reconstruction to generative 3D 
scene synthesis. Compared with NeRF, 3DGS technologies demonstrate 
stronger generalisation and flexibility, enabling high-quality 3D recon­
struction or novel view synthesis from limited observations[53,54]. 
Such approaches are increasingly being explored for realistic 3D plant 
scene generation, visual content creation, and digital twin ecosystems, 
bridging the gap between precision geometry and creative scene 
modelling. Based on this, the research direction is gradually shifting 
from the simple geometric precise reconstruction to more generative 3D 
scene modelling [53,55]. Unlike NeRF, which mainly relies on 
multi-view images for high-precision reconstruction, the core advantage 
of 3DGS lies in its unique fusion of the advantages of differentiable 
pipelines and point-based rendering techniques. By representing the 
scene with a learnable 3D Gaussian function, it preserves the ideal 
properties of continuous volumetric radiation fields, which is crucial for 
high-quality image synthesis, while avoiding the computational over­
head associated with rendering blank spaces [56,57]. Such methods 
often build upon or complement the geometric foundations provided by 
SfM-MVS. Now, numerous studies have been exploring their potential to 
enhance visual quality and enable applications like novel view synthesis 
and scene editing [51,58].
Despite progress, integrating AI with 3DPC analysis for plant phe­
notyping still faces significant challenges. For example, point cloud 
generating sensors like Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) remain 
both costly and complex, limiting their use in resource-constrained 
settings like small farms and research institutes [59]. Processing 
large-scale point clouds demands substantial computing power and 
memory, straining infrastructure and hindering real-time or field-based 
applications. Additionally, processing 3DPC data requires staff with 
deep computer science expertise. Moreover, current methods often lack 
generality, being tailored to specific plant species and controlled indoor 
environments, which restricts their effectiveness in outdoor breeding 
programs and real-world agricultural settings. This gap slows the po­
tential of 3D phenotyping to enhance crop development. While datasets 
like ROSE-X [60], Pheno4D [61] have eased data scarcity, their limited 
diversity and coverage fall short of supporting robust AI model training 
across varied species and conditions.
Literature reviews offer valuable new perspectives and potential 
solutions for challenges within a specific research area [62]. Although 
several narrative reviews on plant 3DPC are available [9,12,63–67], and 
one previous study has applied quantitative analysis in this field [14], 
existing reviews mainly focus on limited aspects such as individual 
acquisition methods or specific applications. To date, a comprehensive 
quantitative review that systematically integrates 3DPC acquisition 
methods, plant groups, algorithms, and datasets is still lacking. There­
fore, we conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) focused on 
applying AI methods to 3DPC data processing for plant phenotyping 
across these key dimensions to fill this gap. To ensure rigor and repro­
ducibility, our SLR adheres to the PRISMA guidelines [68], encom­
passing publications from 2017 to October 2025 across eight major 
databases (PubMed, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Scopus, 
SpringerLink, Wiley Online Library, and ACM Digital Library). Our 
methodology begins with defining three core research questions (RQs) 
to address critical gaps in the field, namely: (1) Compared with 2D RGB

2. Methodology
2.1. Review objectives and research questions
The primary objective of this review is to conduct a meta-analysis of 
current research on AI applications in 3DPC-based in plant phenotyping. 
To achieve this, we formulated a focused set of RQs to guide a detailed 
assessment of relevant literature [70]. These RQs provided the founda­
tion for our SLR and facilitated the collection of pertinent publications. 
RQ1: Compared with 2D RGB etc. images, what are the advantages 
of 3DPC in plant phenotyping? What metrics can be used?
RQ2: What AI algorithms are used to analyze 3DPC? What available 
datasets can be found?
RQ3: What are the current trends and limitations of AI and 3DPC in 
analyzing plant phenotypic traits? What strategies can be imple­
mented to improve its effectiveness?
2.2. Literature search strategy
When conducting a SLR, the selection process must follow a rigorous 
and unbiased search strategy aimed at bringing together the broadest 
possible range of original studies that provide a solid evidence base to 
directly answer the posed RQs [62,71,72]. Therefore, building upon 
effective methodologies identified in previous studies [73], the search 
and analysis process was meticulously designed to comprehensively 
incorporate all published research results. To ensure both comprehen­
siveness and depth, our literature search and analysis followed a struc­
tured three-stage approach (Fig. 1). 
Step 1: Keyword Refinement and Initial Scoping
We began with a preliminary investigation using Google Scholar 
(https://scholar.google.com/) to identify relevant review articles and 
refine our search terms. Our initial search string was: ((“Plant” OR 
“Crop”) AND (“Point Cloud” OR “Dense cloud”) AND (“Artificial Intel­
ligence” OR “Deep Learning” OR “Machine Learning”)). From the re­
sults, we selected review articles published between 2010 and the 
present to identify high-performing keywords.
This process led to the development of five keyword sets: 
(1) Root Key 1: “Point Cloud” OR “Dense Cloud” (2 terms, 1 rows)
(2) Root Key 2: “Plant” OR “Crop” (2 terms, 1 rows)
(3) Data Key: “LiDAR”, “RGB-D”, “SfM”, “ToF”, “Laser Triangula­
tion” etc. (a total of 26 terms, 17 rows)
(4) Phenotype Key: “Leaf”, “Stem”, “Height”, “3D” etc. (a total of 25 
terms, 13 rows)
(5) Method Key: “AI”, “PointNet”, “SVM” etc. (a total of 37 terms, 32 
rows)
(6) “Root Key 1″ and “Root Key 2″ ensure that the data focuses on the 
3DPC data of plants or crops. After being connected by “AND”, 
they are respectively combined with “Data Key” and “Phenotype 
Key” through “AND” operation. The resulting set is then con­
nected with “Method Key” through “AND” operation to filter for 
ML methods (Supplementary Figure 1). Within each set, similar 
terms were combined with “OR” to broaden the category in a 
row, ensuring all relevant variations are captured in a single row 
(e.g., “RGB” OR “Red-Green-Blue” in the Data Key) (see Supple­
mentary File1 for the full list).
Step 2: Database Searching and De-duplication
We selected eight databases based on their update frequency, 
accessibility, and the quality and credibility of their content: PubMed 
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Web of Science (https://www. 
webofscience.com/), IEEE Xplore (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org), Scien­
ceDirect (https://www.sciencedirect.com/), Scopus (https://www. 
elsevier.com/products/scopus), SpringerLink (https://www.springer. 
com/cn), ACM Digital Library (https://dl.acm.org/), and Wiley Online 
Library (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/). Then, an automated Python 
program systematically combined keyword sets to generate 960 unique 
keyword strings (derived from two groups of combinations, e.g., 17×32 
and 13×32). These were searched across eight databases, finally 
resulting in a total of 7680 valid searches.
Subsequently, duplicate studies were automatically removed using 
the Zotero plugin, Zotlicate (https://github.com/ChenglongMa/zoplicat 
e), which is designed to detect and manage duplicate items in Zotero. 
The remaining papers then underwent manual screening using ASRe­
views. Irrelevant articles were excluded based on pre-defined inclusion 
and exclusion criteria (detailed in Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2). Concur­
rently, we manually searched and reviewed additional articles related to 
three-dimensional plant 3DPC in the Google Scholar database. We 
meticulously cross-referenced our collected papers with the references 
in these reviews to ensure the comprehensiveness of our literature 
search. Finally, the final set of relevant studies was exported as an Excel 
file.
Step 3: bibliometric analysis
We performed a bibliometric analysis by extracting the following 
information from each publication: (1) Publication details: Title, Item 
Type, Author, Year of Study, Institution, DOI, and Citation Count (as of 
Oct 2025); (2) Study characteristics: Study Purpose, Plant Type, Number 
of Plants, Environment (Field/Controlled), Country, and Parameters 
Measured; (3) Data and methods: Dataset Description, Publicly Avail­
able Data, Software/Hardware Used, Data Collection Sensors, Point 
Cloud Equipment, Methodology, and Machine Learning Algorithms 
(including those for 3DPC and non-3DPC); and (4) Results: Algorithm 
Performance, Evaluation Metrics, and Key Findings, along with the 
Harvard Citation. RStudio was used to perform the statistical and visual 
analyses, and figures were generated with R and the ggplot2 package 
[74].

external factors may still lead to the inclusion of irrelevant papers into 
our database [75]. Therefore, we have established clear inclusion and 
exclusion criteria to prevent bias in our results.

3.2. AI research on plant 3DPC has increased
To analyse the publication trends of research combining AI and 
3DPC, we visualized the number of 381 selected articles by year. Our 
analysis revealed a significant and accelerating growth in publications 
integrating AI and 3DPC, particularly in recent years (Fig. 3). In 2017, 
the number of publication was small, with only 3 articles published in 
this field (Supplementary Table 1). A discernible upward trend emerged 
in 2018 with 9 publications, followed by 17 in 2019, and a marked 
escalation thereafter, with publication counts rising to 21 in 2020, 26 in 
2021, 41 in 2022, 72 in 2023, 88 in 2024, and 104 in 2025 (October). 
This trajectory reflects a burgeoning interest in applying AI to 3DPC 
analysis in plant phenotyping, with over half of the included studies 
published from 2022 onwards.
Additionally, the analysis of publication types further reinforces the 
view that this research area is both maturing and dynamic. As shown in 
Supplementary Figure 2, most of the articles are journal articles (n =
342, 89.8 %), with conference papers accounting for 39 articles (10.2 
%). Building on this, we also presented the top ten journals by publi­
cation volume in our dataset for reference (Fig. 4).
3.3. Emphasis on trees/shrubs and cereals in AI point cloud research
To characterize the distribution of plant groups in studied in research 
combining AI and 3DPC for plant phenotyping, we categorized species 
into six major plant groups (trees/shrubs, cereals, Solanaceae, Fabaceae, 
Brassicaceae and other species) and analyzed the distribution of 
research across different plant groupsinvestigated per publication. Our 
analysis revealed a varied representation of plant groups (Fig. 5A), with 
trees/shrubs being the most frequently studied (n = 132), followed by 
cereals (n = 112). Solanaceae (n = 76), other species (n = 73), Fabaceae 
(n = 36), and Brassicaceae (n = 28) were represented to a lesser extent. 
Despite the significant increase in the scale of research, the distribution 
pattern of research focus has remained highly consistent, and the 
research proportion of each major plant group has not fundamentally 
shifted (Fig. 5B–C). Concurrently, a strong tendency towards single- 
species studies was observed (Supplementary Table 2). The vast ma­
jority of publications (n = 329, 86.4 %) focused on only one plant spe­
cies, with a steep decline in the number of studies investigating Multiple 
species.
Overall, the combination of AI and 3DPC in plant phenotyping 
studies exhibits a markedly unbalanced yet highly stable research 
pattern across different plant groups.

3.5. Dominance and shifts in 3DPC acquisition methods preferences for 
plant 3DPC analysis
To determine the prevalent 3DPC acquisition methods employed in 
studies combining AI and 3DPC for plant phenotyping, we first catego­
rized methods based on their 3DPC acquisition principles, including 
ToF, Laser Triangulation, Structured light, SfM-MVS, Learning-based 
(3DGS-based and NerF-based) methods, Stereo vision and Other type 
(Hyperspectral, Multispectral, X-ray, L-system, etc.). Then we visualized 
and analysed the distribution of these methods used for generating 3DPC 
in these publications, identifying trends in methods usage over time. Our 
analysis revealed that ToF and SfM-MVS methods, as expected, were the 
most frequently utilized technologies (Fig. 7), with ToF being the most 
common (n = 163), followed by SfM-MVS (n = 117), learning-based 
methods (n = 36), stereo vision (n = 34), other type (n = 33), Laser 
Triangulation (n = 24), and structured light (n = 23).
The temporal analysis (Fig. 7) illustrated the temporal evolution of 
sensor type usage in 3D plant phenotyping studies from 2017 to 2025. 
While early studies between 2017 and 2018 relied heavily on SfM-MVS 
and Stereo Vision, and the field has diversified after 2018. Notably, ToF 
sensors entered a period of exponential growth around 2022, emerging 
as the dominant technology by 2025. Concurrently, the 2023–2025 
period marks a significant surge in Learning-based methods, under­
scoring the integration of AI and deep learning in data perception. 
Although Structured Light and Laser Triangulation maintain a smaller 
footprint, their steady presence indicates specialized utility. Overall, 
these results reveal a paradigm shift from conventional geometric vision 
toward advanced ToF sensing and AI-driven 3DPC reconstruction 
frameworks.

while DL is increasingly prevalent in all scenarios, its dominance is more 
pronounced in controlled/semi-controlled environments (laboratory,

demonstrate strong adaptability in complex field scenarios and have not

4.1.1. Advantages
Compared with projection-based 2D imaging approaches, a key 
advantage of 3DPC is its ability to capture the complete spatial archi­
tecture of plants, including dimensions like height, width, and the 
volumetric distribution of organs such as leaves and stems (Supple­
mentary File2). Unlike 2D imaging methods, which often struggle with 
occlusion and fail to represent hidden structures in dense canopies[155], 
3DPC significantly helps mitigate occlusion issues, enabling the analysis 
of complex branching patterns and dense canopies [156]. Additionally, 
3DPC is more robust to complex illumination conditions, as it relies 
primarily on geometric information rather than reflectance intensity, 
whereas factors such as varying light or shadows often affect 2D 
methods like RGB or multispectral imaging, making it more reliable for 
both controlled and field environments [157]. The non-invasive nature 
of 3DPC acquisition methods, such as LiDAR or structured light scan­
ning, allows for repeated measurements without disturbing the plant, 
making it ideal for longitudinal studies of growth dynamics or stress 
responses[64,158]. Furthermore, the high resolution of 3DPC, often at 
sub-millimeter precision, enables detailed organ-level analysis by dis­
tinguishing individual organs like leaves and stems, which is challenging 
for 2D techniques [24]. Importantly, this capability allows the tracking 
of growth dynamics of individual organs over time, such as the expan­
sion of single leaves or the elongation of individual stems, making 3DPC 
particularly valuable for detailed organ-level growth analysis and dy­
namic phenotyping studies [159,160]. Nevertheless, practical applica­
tions of 3DPC may still be influenced by factors such as point cloud 
noise, incomplete occlusion handling in complex canopies, segmenta­
tion and registration inaccuracies, and the computational demands of 
large-scale point cloud processing [63,161].

4.1.2. Metrics
In terms of metrics, 3DPC provides a wide range of phenotypic traits 
for quantification. Morphological parameters like true width [162], 
stem diameter [163], crown diameter [86], and total volume [164] 
serve as direct indicators of growth and biomass accumulation. Shape 
descriptors, such as branching angles [165], path curvature [166], and 
leaf inclination angles [167], provide insights into structural complexity 
and light interception efficiency. Metrics like leaf area [168] and 
branching node [108] derived from point cloud data are effective for 
evaluating photosynthetic capacity and developmental patterns, 
respectively. Additionally, 3DPC supports time-series analysis by accu­
rately measuring dynamic changes, such as growth rates [169] or organ 
movements [170], which are crucial for studying plant responses to 
environmental factors. Volumetric metrics, including hull volume [171] 
and alpha volume [172], further aid in assessing biomass and canopy 
structure. However, the choice of metrics should align with specific 
research goals, as their relevance can vary depending on the plant spe­
cies and experimental context, and 3DPC has limitations in providing 
physiological or biochemical information, such as nitrogen or chloro­
phyll content, which may require integration with other data types.
In summary, compared to 2D imaging, 3DPC delivers a richer, 
geometrically precise three-dimensional representation of plants. It 
overcomes, to some extent, the limitations of 2D techniques, such as 
view dependency, occlusion, and difficulties in representing complex 
structures or directly measuring volume, enabling the extraction of a 
broader, more accurate, and biologically meaningful set of phenotypic 
traits. The advantages of 3DPC lie in its ability to provide detailed 3D 
geometric data, mitigate occlusion, estimate volume, and analyze 
complex structures. However, it comes with challenges, including 
higher equipment costs, more intricate data collection, point-level 
annotation requirements and processing workflows, and larger data 
volumes. Whether to use 2D, 3D, or a combination depends on the 
phenotyping goals, required precision, budget, throughput needs, and 
available resources. For studies focused on growth rates, plant archi­
tecture, biomass (via volume proxies), or organ dimensions, 3DPC 
stands out as the preferred choice.

4.2. For RQ2: what ML algorithms are used to analyse 3DPC? what 
available datasets can be found?
In our SLR, we categorized AI algorithms for plant 3D point cloud 
analysis and listed relevant datasets. To answer RQ2, we will proceed 
with discussions based on the established algorithm classifications and 
conduct an in-depth analysis of public datasets.
4.2.1. Traditional ML methods
Traditional ML approaches, rely on hand-engineered features to 
process 3DPC data including filtering, denoising, segmentation, feature 
extraction, registration, and classification. However, these traditional 
ML methods often struggle with the unstructured nature of 3DPC, 
resulting in limited adaptability and increased sensitivity to noise, 
which restricts their performance in complex phenotyping tasks[29]. 
Nevertheless, it is important to note that these ML techniques remain 
highly valuable (Supplementary Figure 3). For example, DBSCAN is 
often used for point cloud denoising and initial clustering to separate 
plant organs from background noise[173]; QuickShift and KNN play a 
role in feature extraction or nearest neighbor search, helping to handle 
the uneven density of point clouds[151,174]; SOM (Self-Organizing 
Map) is particularly suitable for downsampling and structure-aware 
sampling of plant point clouds[152,153].
4.2.2. Pointwise MLP-based methods
Pointwise MLP-based methods usually use shared MLP as the basic 
unit in models for high efficiency. Since 2017, Pointwise MLP-based 
networks began to emerge, with the introduction of models like Point­
Net pioneering the direct processing of raw point cloud data [80]. Sri­
vastava et al. (2017) introduced an end-to-end pipeline using PointNet 
for drought stress classification in crops, marking its debut in plant 
phenotyping [106]. However, PointNet lacks the ability to capture local 
geometric structures, which are crucial for modeling the complex and 
irregular morphology of plant organs. To address this limitation, 
PointNet++ was introduced into the field of plant phenotyping research 
in 2021, as it hierarchically aggregates local features and captures 
multi-scale geometric information, making it more suitable for 
fine-grained structural tasks. Typical works include Masuda et al.’s al­
gorithm for estimating leaf area of tomato plant point clouds and Hu 
et al.'s method for segmenting rapeseed field point clouds[175,176]. 
Subsequently, some improved algorithms based on the PointNet family 
began to emerge, such as CropPointNet[177] for crop segmentation, 
PartNet[111] and PlantNet [110] for instance segmentation, BioPM 
[178] for biomass prediction..
Overall, Pointwise MLP-based methods have been widely adopted in 
plant phenotyping, offering efficient processing of raw point clouds.
4.2.3. CNN-based methods
CNN-based methods in point cloud analysis used convolutional op­
erations to extract features from three-dimensional data. Since raw point 
clouds are irregular and unordered, these methods typically first trans­
form the point cloud into a regular representation, such as voxels or 2D 
projections, to make it compatible with convolutional networks. In 
2020, Jin et al. developed a CNN-based method (VCNN) for segmenting 
maize stems and leaves from ground-based LiDAR data [107], which 
means voxel-based methods have come into focus. Following this trend, 
Farah Saeed et al. employed Point-Voxel CNN, which integrates point 
and voxel data representations, for organ segmentation [108]. Gong 
et al. proposed Panicle-3D, a CNN-based model with PointConv module 
[109]. This model combines the local feature extraction capability of 
convolutional networks with the geometric structure information of 
point clouds and is suitable for semantic segmentation of plant point 
clouds. Li et al. developed a novel 3D plant point cloud completion 
method, PlantPCC, capable of handling complex geometric shapes and 
intricate edge features[179]. While still present in recent year, CNNs 
seem to be less dominant in the later years compared to newer 
architectures like Transformers and Hybrid models, though they might 
be used as components within more complex systems.
4.2.4. Graph-based methods
Graph-based methods are a class of techniques that convert unor­
dered, irregular 3DPC into a graph structure (points as nodes, neigh­
borhood relations as edges), leveraging graph theory and graph neural 
networks to capture local geometry, global relationships, and spatial 
topology. Our results showed although graph-based work emerged in 
2022, it increased significantly in 2023 and 2024, such as PlantNet 
(2022) which incorporated EdgeConv [40], a convolutional block based 
on a dynamic GNN, into its main dual-function architecture to improve 
accuracy [110]. PlantSegNet (2024) learns a latent space through a se­
ries of EdgeConv operations, in which points belonging to the same 
instance are close to each other [119]. Zhang et al. (2024) introduced a 
geometric graph encoder (GGE) into the SN-MGGE network to capture 
local geometric features and hierarchical information in both Euclidean 
and hyperbolic spaces, thereby leveraging the advantages of Euclidean 
and hyperbolic geometry to enhance the understanding and represen­
tation of complex geometric data [120].
4.2.5. Transformer-based methods
Transformer-based Networks saw a notable rise after 2022 in our 
analysis result, reflecting their ability to capture long-range de­
pendencies in point cloud data, which is critical for reconstructing 
complex plant structures like tree branching patterns [112]. For 
example, Li et al. (2022) proposed the MASPC_Transform network 
framework based on Point Transformer, which achieved good segmen­
tation results on the ROSE_X dataset[113]. Kaya et al. (2022) proposed 
RoseSegNet, an attention-based DL architecture for organ segmentation 
of plants, which outperforms the widely-used architecture PointNet++
by 4 % in terms of MIoU metric on the public data set [114]. Yang et al. 
(2022) proposed SAFF-Net, which is a Semantic Segmentation Network 
of Noisy Plant Point Cloud based on Self-Attention Feature Fusion[115]. 
Du et al. (2023) proposed a PST-based method that accomplished an 
accurate segmentation of 3DPC in the rapeseed pod stage [116]. Huang 
et al. (2024) proposed a GeoTransformer enhancement method based on 
Gaussian curvature for registering plant point clouds with low overlap 
[117]. Ma et al. (2024) proposed the plant segmentation transformer 
network (PSTNet), an end-to-end model for semantic segmentation of 
eggplant plant point clouds [118].
4.2.6. Hybrid methods
It is worth noting that, compared to previous years, hybrid archi­
tecture experienced explosive growth in 2025. Researchers are no longer 
confined to single network paradigms but are instead developing 
combinatorial approaches that combine the advantages of multiple ar­
chitectures to achieve higher accuracy and stronger generalization ca­
pabilities in point cloud analysis. For example, the combination of GNN 
and Transformers can simultaneously capture local geometric relation­
ships and global contextual information [121]; while the fusion of CNNs 
and Graphs introduces a structured topology-aware mechanism while 
maintaining the efficient feature extraction capabilities of convolutions. 
TPointNetPlus proposed by Liu et al. (2025) integrated PointNet++
with Transformer modules, enabling robust cotton organ segmentation 
across different growth stages [122]. The Plant-MAE network (2025), 
based on self-supervised learning, includes a kernel-based point con­
volutional embedding module and an attention-based multi-angle 
feature extraction module (MAFEB), exhibiting excellent generalization 
capabilities. After minor fine-tuning, this framework can be applied to 
other crop types [123]. The growth of hybrid architecture signifies a 
trend of combining different architectural components to leverage their 
respective strengths.

For example, Zhang et al. (2024) proposed NerF-ag for efficient, accu­

can significantly reduce computational costs while maintaining high 
reconstruction accuracy [52]. This method represents the scene as a 
Gaussian point cloud and performs efficient volume rendering in screen 
space, thereby achieving real-time or near real-time 3D visualization 
[132]. Recent studies have begun to explore the application of 3D 
Gaussian Splatting in plant phenotyping, exemplified by the Plant­
Gaussian framework (2025) that enables high-quality 3D visualization 
and morphological analysis across time and diverse scenes[53]. In 
addition, 3DGS has been combined with stereo matching networks to 
improve depth estimation accuracy for leafy vegetables, demonstrating 
its potential in precise 3D phenotyping [55].
4.2.8. Datasets
Publicly available datasets are increasingly critical for AI-driven 
3DPC plant phenotyping, supporting reproducibility, cross-study com­
parison, and methodological innovation. In terms of species, the datasets 
span a broad range, from single-species datasets such as ROSE-X (Rosa 
rugosa), Soybean-MVS (Glycine max), and Wheat3D PartNet (Triticum 
aestivum L.) to multi-species collections like Plant 3D, TrackPlant3D, and 
Crops3D, which together encompass major crops including maize, to­
mato, sorghum, rice, and cotton. Synthetic datasets such as ModelNet 
and ShapeNet further extend these resources by offering large-scale 3D 
models of generic objects, including plant-like forms, useful for pre­
training and geometric analysis. Environmentally, these datasets cover 
both controlled indoor and field conditions. Indoor datasets (e.g., ROSE- 
X, Wheat3D PartNet, PLANesT-3D, 3DPhenoMVS) emphasize precise 
plant geometry under stable lighting, while field datasets (e.g., Soybean- 
MVS, PFuji-Size, Crops3D) capture realistic environmental variability, 
supporting robustness evaluation under natural conditions. Some data­
sets, such as Pheno4D and Plant 3D, include both settings for cross- 
environment generalization. The data formats range from 3DPC and 
meshes (ModelNet, ShapeNet, Plant 3D, Chickpea 3D) to volumetric 
reconstructions (ROSE-X) and hybrid forms integrating raw RGB images 
(PFuji-Size). Correspondingly, acquisition methods include synthetic 
rendering (ModelNet, ShapeNet), LiDAR scanning (Plant 3D, Crops3D, 
TrackPlant3D), structure-from-motion and multi-view stereo (SfM-MVS) 
(Soybean-MVS, PFuji-Size, PLANesT-3D, Wheat3D PartNet), structured- 
light (Crops3D), and X-ray tomography (ROSE-X). These methods differ 
in precision, scalability, and suitability for different plant morphologies. 
Dataset sizes vary widely, from small, high-fidelity sets such as 
Label3DMaize (12 point clouds) and ROSE-X (11 volumetric models) to 
large-scale benchmarks like Crops3D (1910 point clouds) and ShapeNet 
(over 51,000 models). Labeling granularity also spans multiple levels, 
from object-category and part-level annotations (ModelNet, ShapeNet) 
to detailed plant organ, instance, and metric-level labels (e.g., Pheno4D, 
PFuji-Size, Crops3D, Wheat3D PartNet). Some datasets, such as

Overall, various AI approaches have been applied to plant 3D point 
cloud analysis. Traditional ML methods are still valuable for pre­
processing or postprocessing. Pointwise MLP-based, CNN-based, graph- 
based, and transformer-based methods have progressively enhanced the 
ability to capture local and global structures in 3DPC data. More recent 
NeRF-based and 3DGS-based approaches have substantially improved 
reconstruction fidelity, particularly for complex plant geometries and 
fine-scale structures. In parallel, hybrid methods that integrate different 
architectures further enhance segmentation accuracy and improve 
generalization across different plant species and growth stages. At the 
same time, these datasets also provided support for researchers with 
limited resources.
4.3. For RQ3: what are the current trends and limitations of ML and 
3DPC in analysing plant phenotypic traits? What strategies can be 
implemented to improve its effectiveness?
To address RQ3, Our SLR highlights significant current trends, key 
limitations, and potential improvement strategies of ML and 3DPC in 
analysing plant phenotypic traits. Particularly these aspects are exam­
ined from the perspectives of publications, species, datasets, data 
acquisition methods, and algorithms.
4.3.1. Publications
In our SLR, we speculated the rapid increase in publications in recent 
years. This trend is unlikely to be coincidental. Rather, it likely reflects 
the convergence of multiple technological and research-driven factors. 
From a demand perspective, plant researchers are increasingly seeking 
detailed phenotypic data. At the same time, the rapid advances of 3D 
data acquisition sensors and technologies in recent years, such as LiDAR 
[77], depth camera [78], and SfM-MVS [63], have provided a critical 
data foundation for this research expansion [79]. Concurrently, break­
throughs in DL, notably with architectures such as PointNet [80], which 
was introduced around 2017, provided robust methodologies for pro­
cessing and interpreting the complex, unstructured nature of 3DPC data 
[81]. Furthermore, the increasing availability of open-source tools and 
datasets, such as those hosted on platforms like the ROSE-X dataset [60] 
or Pheno4D dataset [61], etc., has lowered entry barriers, fostering 
broader participation and accelerating research progress. Powerful 
hardware, such as graphics processing units (GPUs) and tensor pro­
cessing units (TPUs), has further enabled the efficient training of these 
computationally intensive models [82]. These synergistic factors might 
collectively underpin the rapid development observed in this field. The 
prevalence of journal articles, which typically undergo rigorous peer 
review and reflect well-validated findings, suggests the field is consoli­
dating its knowledge base. The smaller share of conference papers may 
indicate that, while rapidly evolving, this domain is transitioning from 
preliminary explorations to more refined outputs suitable for journal 
publication [83]. However, this distribution could also partly reflect 
publication preferences in AI subfields, where conferences sometimes 
serve as primary venues for cutting-edge work in computer science. The 
absence of book sections aligns with the field’s relatively recent emer­
gence, as comprehensive syntheses in book form are more typical of 
disciplines with decades of accumulated research. Despite this rapid 
growth in publications, only a few studies to date have explicitly linked 
3D phenotypic data to genotypic information [180–184]. This gap 
highlights a major opportunity for future research: combining 
high-resolution 3DPC phenomics with genomics through methods such 
as GWAS, QTL mapping, or genome-assisted breeding can provide a 
deeper understanding of plant growth, yield, and stress resistance, and 
ultimately accelerate breeding programs [185].
In summary, the number of publications in this field has increased 
rapidly in recent years and is likely to continue growing. While most 
studies have focused on 3D phenotyping alone, future work is expected 
to explore the promising integration of genotypic data, bridging the

strategies.
4.3.2. Species
Our analysis of plant groups highlighted the dominance of trees/ 
shrubs and cereals. The prominence of trees and shrubs likely stems from 
their structural complexity and larger size, which make them ideal 
candidates for 3D analysis, as well as the availability of extensive LiDAR 
datasets from forestry applications [84–88]. The focus on cereals is likely 
driven by their economic importance and the resulting investment in 
high-throughput phenotyping research for these major crops [89–91]. 
Therefore, a relatively mature sensor–algorithm–trait framework has 
been established for rice and wheat [186,187].At the same time, we also 
found that most papers only study a single species. This phenomenon 
may reflect the current stage of methodological development in this 
field, where algorithm optimization and validation are usually carried 
out on a species-by-species basis. Furthermore, the inherent biological 
specificity of many phenotyping studies, often driven by questions 
related to particular species or cultivars, contributes to this trend. Data 
standardization challenges and computational costs associated with 
analyzing large, multi-species datasets may also limit the prevalence of 
comparative studies. These findings highlight the dominance of trees/­
shrubs and cereals in AI and 3DPC phenotyping research, driven by their 
structural and economic significance. The focus on single-species studies 
reflects the current stage of methodological refinement, particularly for 
these groups. However, this trend also offers new perspectives for future 
research in this field. For example, models developed for cereal crops 
like wheat could potentially be extended to other monocot species such 
as Oryza sativa L. (rice) or Hordeum vulgare L. (barley), .etc. Similarly, 
tree segmentation algorithms designed for various woody species could 
be refined to achieve broader applicability. These advancements may 
enhance the universality of phenotyping tools across a wider range of 
plant taxa, thereby providing promising directions for future research.
4.3.3. Datasets
Our analysis for dataset usage found that although only a few studies 
use public datasets, their adoption has risen significantly with the 
growing availability of such data, our result shows that a marked in­
crease in dataset usage in 2020, a shift likely driven by the COVID-19 
pandemic. Restrictions on fieldwork and laboratory access starting in 
early 2020 may have prompted researchers to turn to these resources to 
sustain progress. This upward trend, though emerging from a modest 
baseline, highlights the growing significance of high-quality, accessible 
datasets in facilitating methodological advancements and expanding 
taxonomic applications within the field. Datasets vary in species 
coverage, environment, acquisition method, data format, and annota­
tion detail. Single-species datasets (ROSE-X, Soybean-MVS, Wheat3D 
PartNet, PFuji-Size) provide organ- or trait-level labels, while multi- 
species collections (Plant 3D, TrackPlant3D, Crops3D) cover major 
crops such as maize, tomato, rice, sorghum, and cotton. Synthetic 
datasets (ModelNet, ShapeNet) extend scale and geometric diversity. 
Indoor datasets enable precise geometry capture, whereas field datasets 
reflect realistic variability. Acquisition approaches include LiDAR, 
structured light, multi-view stereo, X-ray tomography, and synthetic 
rendering, producing point clouds, meshes, volumetric reconstructions, 
or hybrids.
Despite this diversity, many datasets are small, incompletely anno­
tated, or lack cross-environment consistency, limiting generalization. To 
overcome these challenges, future efforts should prioritize a unified 
3DPC data framework, including standardized naming conventions, file 
structures, minimum resolution thresholds, and comprehensive meta­
data (e.g., light, temperature, and temporal markers). Drawing from 
ImageNet’s impact on AI vision and genomics’ success with FASTA 
formats, a freely accessible, globally curated 3DPC phenotyping data­
base could drive innovation in phenomics, enhance machine learning 
model generalization, and enable integration with climate models. Such 
a platform would allow researchers to evaluate model performance 
using diverse, high-quality datasets covering crops, growth stages, and 
environmental conditions, ensuring robustness and fairness in pheno­
typing applications. However, achieving consensus may face challenges, 
such as hardware variability (e.g., ToF vs. stereo vision) and adoption 
costs. Collaborative initiatives led by academic consortia related to plant 
phenotyping, plant phenomics or plant science should define these 
standards, develop conversion tools for legacy data, and curate diverse 
datasets spanning crops, environments, and growth stages.
4.3.4. Data acquisition methods
The observed dominance of ToF and SfM-MVS-based approaches 
reflects both technological maturity and practical considerations in 
plant phenotyping. SfM-MVS has historically served as a cost-effective 
and flexible solution, leveraging widely available RGB cameras to 
reconstruct 3D point clouds without specialized hardware. This explains 
its prevalent use in early studies, particularly between 2017 and 2018, 
when deep learning techniques and dedicated depth sensors were still 
emerging. In contrast, the rapid growth of ToF-based methods after 2022 
highlights a shift toward sensor-driven depth acquisition. Modern ToF 
sensors offer direct depth measurements, improved robustness to com­
plex canopy structures, and compatibility with real-time and high- 
throughput phenotyping platforms. These characteristics align well 
with the increasing demand for automated, scalable, and field- 
deployable phenotyping systems. The exponential rise of ToF usage by 
2025 suggests that it has become a preferred solution for integrating 3D 
perception with AI-based analysis pipelines. Notably, the recent surge in 
learning-based 3D reconstruction methods between 2023 and 2025 
marks a notable paradigm shift. Unlike traditional geometric ap­
proaches, learning-based techniques such as NeRF and 3D Gaussian 
Splatting integrate data-driven priors into the reconstruction process, 
enabling improved handling of occlusions, sparse views, and complex 
plant geometries. This trend indicates that AI is no longer limited to 
downstream trait extraction but is increasingly embedded in the 
perception and reconstruction stages themselves. Although Structured 
Light and Laser Triangulation account for a smaller proportion of 
studies, their sustained usage suggests continued relevance in controlled 
environments where high-precision measurements are required. Simi­
larly, the presence of alternative acquisition modalities, including 
hyperspectral and multispectral systems, reflects growing interest in 
multimodal phenotyping that combines structural and physiological 
information.
Overall, these findings reveal a clear transition from traditional 
geometry-based vision methods toward advanced ToF sensing and AI- 
driven reconstruction frameworks. Future advancements could involve 
multimodal sensor fusion (e.g., LiDAR+MSI) and the development of 
low-cost, portable devices (e.g., smartphone-based) to improve data 
quality and accessibility [10,147,188]. In addition to mobile and 
low-cost devices, recent advances in ToF-based depth sensors and 
AI-enabled edge sensing platforms provide new opportunities for 
real-time, robust 3D data acquisition [189,190]. These technologies 
enable on-sensor processing, representing an important emerging trend 
beyond traditional LiDAR and RGB systems [191].

innovations in local feature modeling, neighborhood reasoning, and

enabling real-time, field-based high-throughput phenotypic analysis and

inspired by the field of computer vision, exploring fundamental models

the scarcity of labeled data [197,198], self-supervised pre-training on

learning transferable, biologically meaningful geometric representa­

reliance on labeled data and improves the model's generalization ability 
across different species, growth stages, and environments, laying the 
foundation for building a scalable, general, and biologically consistent 
analytical framework.
4.4. Comparison with existing reviews
In recent years, several review articles have summarized the appli­
cation of three-dimensional technologies in plant phenotyping from 
different perspectives (Table 2). Early reviews, such as Paulus et al. 
(2019) [12], mainly focused on geometry-based 3D measurement 
techniques and their biological applications, with limited attention to 
AI-driven analysis. More recent studies, including Akhtar et al. (2024) 
[14] and Zuo et al. (2024) [66], reviewed the complete 3D plant phe­
notyping workflow from data acquisition to trait extraction. However, 
AI methods were discussed as auxiliary tools rather than a central 
analytical paradigm. Some reviews have concentrated on specific tasks 
or methodologies. For example, Song et al. (2025) [63] provided a 
comprehensive overview of plant point cloud segmentation techniques, 
while Nethala et al. (2024) [65] emphasized multi-scale feature 
extraction and segmentation using remote and proximal sensing data. 
Although these studies offer in-depth insights into particular problems, 
they do not present a unified perspective on AI-enabled 3D point cloud 
analysis across the entire phenotyping pipeline. Other reviews adopted a 
deep learning–centric perspective. Jin et al. (2025) [64] systematically 
summarized the application of deep learning in 3D plant phenotyping, 
highlighting data representation, model design, and interpretability. Li 
et al. (2025) [67] further introduced emerging 3D reconstruction tech­
niques, such as NeRF and 3DGS, into plant phenotyping research. 
Nevertheless, these studies primarily focus on deep learning models or 
reconstruction techniques, rather than treating 3D point clouds as a core 
data modality integrated with AI throughout acquisition, processing, 
analysis, and application stages.
In contrast, our work provides a PRISMA-based SLR of 381 studies, 
offering the most comprehensive synthesis to date of AI-driven 3D point 
cloud analysis in plant science. Unlike previous narrative reviews, we 
explicitly position 3D point clouds as the central data representation and 
quantitatively examine advantages of 3DPC, metrics, species, dataset, 
data acquisition, algorithms. Moreover, we systematically summarize 
research trends, key challenges and future directions for AI-based 3D 
plant phenotyping.

5. Limitations of this study
This SLR also has several limitations that should be acknowledged. 
Firstly, the search was confined to a specific date range (from January 
2017 to October 2025), potentially excluding relevant studies published 
before or beyond this period. For instance, a surge of research papers on 
point clouds may emerge at the end of 2025, which could be missed. 
However, such a time limit is necessary because it is a common practice 
in systematic literature reviews to ensure feasibility and timeliness. 
Moreover, the number of papers published before 2017 was limited. 
Secondly, despite efforts to incorporate diverse sources, we just focus on 
open-access and English-written literature. The reason why we adopted 
this approach is to ensure that the included studies are accessible to the 
most of the target academic audience. Thirdly, we also did not collect 
literature from arXiv or bioRxiv because these papers have not under­
gone peer review even though it can still indicate emerging research 
directions. We prioritize peer-reviewed literature to base our conclu­
sions on validated research findings, thereby enhancing the reliability of 
our comprehensive analysis. These limitations underscore the need for 
cautious interpretation of the findings and highlight the importance of 
future reviews to address these gaps.
6. Conclusion
This paper presents a systematic review of AI-driven 3D point cloud 
analysis techniques in plant phenotyping, covering data acquisition 
methods, algorithmic evolution, application species, and publicly 
available datasets, along with a comprehensive classification and dis­
cussion of these aspects, highlighting current research trends, limita­
tions, and future directions.
