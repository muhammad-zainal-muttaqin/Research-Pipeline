A B S T R A C T  
Oil palm bunches, which are the raw materials for several oil palm products, are traded to a crude palm oil (CPO) 
factory or a middleman. Since high-oil-content palm bunches are desired to produce CPO, the factories and 
middlemen traditionally have several experts hired for grading FFBs. We have found that colour, shape and size 
of FFBs are three parameters that need to be considered for classification. Shape and colour data can be collected 
using RGB and IR sensors, while size data can be collected using IR and weight sensors. In this paper, we propose 
a deep learning-based model working with three sensors (RGB camera, infrared sensor, and a load cell) for 
classifying a palm bunch. The classifying framework of the algorithm is a multi-input and multi-label con­
volutional neural network. The multi-label classifies the 4 grades of FFBs (overripe, ripe, under ripe and unripe). 
Based on 1,575 images for 14 varieties of palm bunches collected from four trading sites, the sensors were 
installed, the algorithm was trained, validated, and tested with 70%, 20% and 10% of the overall datasets, 
respectively. The general images that are preprocessed after training model are applied and compared between 
original and preprocess images by Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM). The cross-entropy 
loss function was applied to investigate the probability error between the target and predicted label during the 
training process. Based on the testing datasets results, the proposed model achieves an accuracy of 90.26%, 
precision of 89.86%, recall of 89.54%, and an F1-score of 89.68%.   
1. Introduction 
In recent years, the global crude palm oil (CPO) market has expanded 
due to the increasing demand from several industrial sectors such as 
food, cosmetics, and bioenergy. In the agricultural production stage, the 
farmer sells the harvested fresh fruit bunches (FFBs) to the middleman 
or CPO factories. The quality of CPO known as the oil extraction rate 
(OER) is related to oil quantity in the FFBs, which is determined by 
variety, fertilizer doses, and agronomical practices (Barcelos et al., 
2015). Since CPO factories demand excellent quality (high-OER) FFBs, 
they intentionally set a buying price to obtain high OER FFBs. Experts 
are hired by the CPO factory and middleman to choose high quality 
FFBs. The experts combine and apply several methods including 
counting dropped fruit numbers, observing the cleanliness, colour and 
size of FFBs, and checking the moisture of FFBs. 
The grading methodology is very biased and unreliable due to its 
reliance on humans. There is no rapid and reliable method yet to classify 
high OER FFBs. The chemical method of Soxhlet extraction (L´opez- 
Basc´on and Luque de Castro, 2020) is the standard method for 
measuring OER. The method is complicated, time-consuming, and 
invasive, hence it is inappropriate in FFB trading sites where it requires a 
rapid test. Several feasible methods were proposed, most of which were 
based on determining the ripeness degree of FFBs. The ripeness degree is 
only part of the high-grade of FFBs but grading by experts is more 
concerned about conditions such as shape, cleanliness, and the variety of 
FFBs. Hyperspectral imaging technique working with invisible light and 
NIR sensors were used for classifying palm bunch (Junkwon et al., 
2009). A portable NIR spectrometer was proposed for estimating ripe­
ness degree and oil content in palm bunch. A neural network and sum of 
weighted-wavelength reflectance (Makky and Soni, 2014).

Based on previously proposed works, the methods can be categorized 
into contact and non-contact techniques. The contact techniques mean 
the sample body was invaded or touched physically, and vice versa for 
the non-contact one. The techniques based on contact types are not 
appropriate due to their slow process (Minakata et al., 2018; Abbas 
et al., 2005; Minakata et al., 2018; Abbas et al., 2005). The techniques 
based on non-contact types are more appropriate. The reflection at 630 
nm wavelength from the palm bunch sample is sensed and converted to 
voltage for classifying palm bunches (Utom et al., 2018). However, the 
obtained classification error was still large. A degree of RGB colour in­
tensity was proposed to predict the ripeness degree of palm bunches 
(Alfatni et al., 2008). A digital camera was used in this research, and the 
mean value was calculated from the digital number. The technique was 
limited to a single palm variety. In (Fadilah et al., 2012), proposed the 
principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce features and use a multi- 
layer perceptron to determine the ripeness degree. Thus, the relation­
ship between colour and the ripeness degree of palm bunch images was 
developed. The proposed algorithm showed several small features and 
high accuracy. 
A technique using a commercial digital camera was proposed 
(Pamornnak et al., 2015). The algorithm was implemented as a mobile 
application and compared with the standard OER method, and it was 
claimed that the algorithm achieved a mean error of less than 2.2%. 
However, the algorithm needs to be developed further to be applied to 
the whole bunch. In (Fahmi et al., 2018), two grades of palm bunches 
(ripe and unripe) were classified from RGB palm bunch images using the 
linear equations based on backpropagation and Learning Vector Quan­
tization (LVQ). However, the technique may not apply in a trading site 
with several varieties of traded palm bunches. Based on RGB image 
classification, a machine learning (ML) technique was proposed (Sep­
tiarini et al., 2019). An edge detection algorithm was applied to obtain 
an image region of the palm fruit. 
The study of the various computerized models based on computer 
vision to address issues. In the agricultural sector occupied a large 
portion of the time of many researchers. To create these models, a broad 
variety of technologies must be used, including machine learning, con­
volutional neural networks, deep learning, and transfer learning (Li and 
Yang, 2020). Based on our review, there are several endeavors from 
numerous researchers to demonstrate the deep learning network models 
based visible sensor for agricultural application. Algorithms developed 
using CNN were applied to detect banana leaf diseases, sweet peppers, 
and FFBs. Banana leaf classification for disease isolation using deep 
learning techniques based on LeNet architecture and a stochastic 
gradient descent (SGD) optimizer (Amara et al., 2017). According to our 
review there is on research work using the three simultaneous sensors 
for classification. For example, the RGB and near-infrared (NIR) images 
were proposed for classifying fruit for 7 classes (Sa et al., 2016). An 
algorithm for detecting sweet peppers uses a faster region-based CNN 
(Faster R-CNN) to process RGB and near-infrared (NIR) images. Large 
data sets are often required by convolutional neural networks. When 
detecting plant locations at the pixel level and manually annotating an 
empirical data set consisting of 30 images, the fine-tuned VGG-16 
network was shown to be the most effective method for identifying plant 
locations. To solve different agricultural issues, computer vision and 
deep learning methods have been shown to be state-of-the-art (Hasan 
et al., 2020). The multi-crop CNN integrates both image and categorical 
data (Picon et al., 2019) for 17 diseases and 5 crops (wheat, barley, corn, 
rice, and rapeseed). A CNN framework built on top of the ResNet50 
neural network is proposed. The non-image dataset from the training 
process (Picon et al., 2019) was added to the convolutional neural 
network using RGB images and crop ID information. This model has an 
accuracy of 98% in real field environments. However, this technique 
was demonstrated and proved with a single palm variety. For our work, 
the main novelty using images and non-image of varieties FFBs for 
classification framework. 
The deep learning (DL) technique was proposed to classify the 
ripeness degree of the palm fruit. AlexNet and DenseNet frameworks 
were applied to the RGB image of the palm fruit. The accuracy of the 
results obtained for several varieties was low. Based on an RGB image of 
FFBs, the research applied pre-training convolution neural network 
(CNN) and the AlexNet framework to classify the bunches. High classi­
fication accuracy was obtained, but this was proven with a single variety 
of FFBs. In Saleh and Liansitim, 2020, a new CNN model was proposed to 
classify two ripeness degrees of RGB palm bunches. However, this 
technique was applied to a single palm variety. Work on classifying FFBs 
using two simultaneous RGB and IR sensors and an algorithm based on 
ML technique was proposed (Pamornnak et al., 2017) and the technique 
was limited to a single variety of FFBs. Table 1 for summary of the 
relevant research works. 
In our main novelty, we used three data readings, from three 
different sensors, to form the multi-input model for classification of 
FFBs. We proposed a multi-sensor technique for collecting multi-input 
and classifying a wide range of FFB in real-world conditions in this 
paper. The model, implementing a convolutional neural network back­
bone VGG and AlexNet techniques by integrating images and non- 
image, is developed as a classifying framework. The main advance of 
the idea is a triple-input parallel deep learning mode for classifying 14 
different varieties of FFBs. This is an improved automatic system for 
classifying FFBs which is the first system that can classify different FFBs. 
The contributions of our proposed work are as shown below. 
Images and weights of FFB from four trading sites using multi-sensor 
technology were collected and stored on a cloud server. 
Multi-input and multi-label grading are developed by using CNN, 
which integrates images and non-images data. FFBs varieties total 
approximately fourteen classifications when evaluated in a real-field 
environment. 
The test case was compared to the performance in five cases: RGB 
image, only an IR image, RGB + IR image including the weight of the 
FFBs, AlexNet model and VGG-19 model.

2. Materials and methods 
This section presents our research methodology as shown in Fig. 1. 
The flow diagram shows the process step of our methodology. A station 
called “PalmSnap” is designed for collecting data. The dataset manage­
ment software based on a web service is implemented. The web service 
contains a module for transferring FFBs data from the station to the 
cloud server. Four stations in the South of Thailand were installed at the 
trading sites. The data collecting process at four trading sites was 
planned to be collecte for 3 months. The collected datasets were stored 
in the data cloud. The preprocessing image is applied to resize the image 
data and reduce noise from several sources such as lighting, hardware 
walls, water, etc. The image datasets are labelled by the experts at the 
sites (all experts at these trading sites have at least 5 years of FFBs 
grading experience) and split into three groups: training, validating, and 
testing the dataset. The DL model is developed and trained with the 
dataset. The output cross-entropy selectively weighs some features to 
prevent overfitting and to ensure robustness. Finally, the model is 
validated and tested.

2.1. Design of data collecting equipment and web service 
This subsection describes the system design of PalmSnap. The station 
is to be installed at the trading sites, so the station should be small, and 
the weight should be light. To support cloud computing, the station 
should connect to the application server via the internet. The application 
server processes the data and returns the classification result to the 
PalmSnap unit. Fig. 2 shows the drawing of the PalmSnap unit, which 
consists of a light-controlled cabinet, a multi-sensor unit, conveyor, and 
a control module including a computing and communication unit. A 
multi-sensor unit consists of a multi-dimensional image sensor and a 
load cell. A Kinect V2 camera is used as the RGB and IR sensor due to its 
capabilities for collecting visible and nonvisible wavelength dataset and 
for its affordability. A Kinect V2 camera contains RGB and IR (infrared) 
sensors. An RGB image has a maximum data size of 1920 × 1080 pixels 
while an IR image has a maximum data size of 512 × 424 pixels. The 
visible sensor covers 400 to 430 nm for purple wavelength, 500 to 570 
nm for green wavelength, 670 to 750 nm for deep red wavelength, 660 
to 700 nm for yellow to orange wavelength, and >750 nm for IR 
wavelength. A load cell (f) chosen as a weight sensor is installed under 
the roller wheel as shown in Fig. 2. The sensing area is protected from 
external disturbance by installing aluminum housing. This housing is 
formed with two sidewalls and a top wall. The a-b and c-d, which are 
120 and 160 cm, respectively, are chosen for loading FFBs. During 
testing, the front and back sides of the cabinet are open for feeding FFB 
in and out. Two ribbon curtains are installed at the front and back sides 
to minimize ambient light from the housing exterior. LED lamps are 
installed in the housing to provide optimum illumination conditions. 
The finished volume of the housing is 0.90 m3. In Fig. 2, the Kinect V2 
camera is installed at the height of 85 cm from the conveyor. This is a 
suitable range for capturing an entire image of FFB. The computing unit 
is implemented with a personal computer and the algorithm is devel­
oped using C# language. The air interface uses a 4G mobile network due 
to network robustness and reliability. The recorded RGB image, IR im­
ages and the weight obtained from the station are transferred to the web 
service for developing the model further. 
Each PalmSnap unit communicates with the web service server via a 
4G mobile channel. Fig. 3 shows the web service that consists of the 
front-end and back-end services. The front-end service is a web-based 
service developed using HTML and JavaScript, whereas the back-end 
service is based on the NoSQL Google Firebase database system. The 
client application was developed based on Microsoft Visual Studio 2015 
(Visual C#.NET). A Kinect software development kit (Kinect SDK) was 
installed in the PalmSnap. With the client application, the images and the 
weight of the FFB are transferred to the cloud database using a design for 
the JavaScript object notation (JSON) format based on the hypertext 
transfer protocol (HTTP). The web service consists of three main

2.2. Implementing and installing the equipment 
As shown in Fig. 1, sufficiently large datasets are required for 
training the model. Therefore, several image and weight datasets of 
different varieties of FFBs are collected from the trading sites. The South 
of Thailand was chosen because it is the largest palm harvesting area in 
Thailand. We selected the trading sites by site surveying, interviewing 
managers, as well as reviewing and analyzing their trading history. The 
key factors for site selection are the records of the number of sellers, total 
tons of selling FFBs, the number of varieties of FFBs, number of classi­
fication degrees, the expert experience, and the standard OER labora­
tory. In this paper, four trading sites owned by two companies were 
selected. We found that all experts at these trading sites have at least 5 
years of FFB grading experience. Fig. 4 shows one of the trading sites 
where the PalmSnap unit (the orange box) was installed. As shown in 
Fig. 4, the expert (yellow T-shirt) loaded the sample onto the PalmSnap 
unit. Data collection results, RGB, IR image, and weight, are displayed in 
the small window (bottom picture). 
Four grades of FFBs, which are grade A, B, C and F, are collected. The 
grades A, B and C mean overripe, ripe and under ripe FFB, respectively, 
while grade F means unripe FFB that is normally rejected at the trading 
sites. The plan was to collect at least 300 samples of FFBs for each grade. 
The expert determines the grade and price of each FFB sample at each 
site based on the variety and ripeness of FFBs. In the data collection 
process, the sample will be fed to the PalmSnap unit to collect the images 
and weight of FFBs. Each dataset for each sample is labelled and 
transferred to the cloud server using the web service. The image, weight, 
variety, and ripeness grade determined by the experts were collected. 
The data collection duration was 90 days during August and October. 
Table 2 summarizes the data results collected from 4 trading sites 
(Site 1–4). There are 1,575 FFBs from 14 varieties. The number of 
trading sites was two. There were only 2 varieties of oil palm bunches, 
but some trading sites have more than 13 varieties. The varieties of oil 
palm bunches vary according to the effectiveness of the plan and the 
environmental suitability of the area. As a result, the characteristics of 
the varieties are different such as the colour of ripeness, seed thickness, 
%OER and so on. The expert grading has unique complexity in classi­
fication for each oil palm bunch. The experts will be able to grade the oil 
palm bunches using the dataset by categorizing them into four grades. 
According to the collected dataset, trading site 4 had the largest number 
of varieties compared to the other sites. The average weight of FFBs that 
underwent testing was 11.1 ± 6.7 kg. Many physical features such as 
colour, weight, and shape were collected using PalmSnap units.

2.4. Define classification model 
This subsection describes the mechanism of the DL classification 
model. The parallel CNN is chosen as the core classification framework. 
A multi-input architecture was created to maximize the relationship 
between the acquired examination and training the model with as much 
data as possible, depending on the trainable parameters and number of 
layers. The proposed model can grade the FFBs with learnable param­
eters and number of layers, that use only 21 M and 77 layers. On the 
other hand, the standard models such as AlexNet, VGG-19, ResNet-50 
and DenseNet-201 used learnable parameters of 60.9 M, 143.6 M, 25.5 
M and 20 M respectively. And the number of layers of AlexNet, VGG-19, 
ResNet-50 and DenseNet-20 used 25 layers, 47 layers, 177 layers and 
708 layers. More learnable parameters are suitable for the number of 
classifications. In addition, the variety of classification in this research 
has only four grades of FFBs, so the high learnable parameters are un­
necessary. This architecture contains three more parallel sub- 
architectures than the previous single architecture. Fig. 7(a) shows the 
block diagram of the classification model. Each multidimensional 
dataset, which contains a 227 × 227 RGB image, a 227 × 227 IR image, 
and FFB’s weight, is applied to the classification model. The design of 
layers and sub-blocks depends on parameters such as activation, learn­
able properties, and total learnable using the “Analyzed” function. For 
this study, multi-input was combined with VGG and AlexNet model as 
the backbone CNN. The input size is 227 × 227 from the AlexNet model 
because this model is a larger size than another model. The larger size 
was depended on accuracy of classification (Cho et al., 2015). However, 
we will need to implement incremental processing and fine-tuning 
within every layer, which is very complex. On the other hand, the 
smaller size affected the number of pixels and resolution in the image 
dataset. The RGB and IR image is applied to the convolution block where 
the weight data is applied to the concatenation block. Fig. 7(b) shows 
the layer detail of the model. Because the development is less complex, 
the convolution block reference from the AlexNet model has 5 sub- 
blocks. Each sub-block consists of two convolution layers using the 
activation function “ReLU” The first convolutional layer of a CNN is 
essentially a standard image filter. Its goal is to get a raw image and 
extract basic features from the edges and corners. Instead of accepting 
the raw image as input, the second convolutional layer gets the features 
extracted by the first layer. This allows it to combine these basic shapes 
into more complex features. The batch normalization (BN) layer, and a 
max-pooling layer. In these five sub-blocks, the number of filters is 32, 
64, 128, 256, and 512, respectively, where the parameters of the BN and 
the max-pooling layers are the same in each sub-block. Each BN and the 
max-pooling unit compute the maximum of a local patch in the feature 
maps. This is performed on the local patches of the feature maps in the 
sliding window. The max-polling layer helps in dimensionality reduc­
tion and creates invariance for small distortions and shifts. The structure 
of the fully connected block shown in Fig. 7(b) consists of two layers, the 
fully connected (FC) and the dropout layers. The FC layer contains 1000 
channels, and the activation function “ReLU” is introduced to aggregate 
information. This function will output the result directly if it is non- 
negative; otherwise, it will output a zero. A 50% dropout is chosen to 
prevent overfitting, and each of the auxiliary features is masked. Out­
puts of the FC block and the weight data are applied to the concatenation 
block, as shown in Fig. 7(b). This concatenation block is used to generate 
output of the same type. In the concatenation block, four channels of the 
FC layer and the activation function “Softmax” are introduced. This 
block is helping to generate the main output and calculate the loss. The 
output branch concatenates feature masks and performs convolution 
followed by the softmax classification multi-label to get the segmenta­
tion output. The softmax function is the probability member for each 
multi-label.

of dimensions, rotations, and lighting conditions to improve the model 
performance when training. The dataset is essential when using deep 
learning to solve the image classification. Firstly, based on the hypoth­

3.2.2. Loss curves 
The loss curves during training and validating are presented in this 
section. In Fig. 10, the loss curves of the multi-input model after training 
and validating processes were computed using the loss cross-entropy 
formula. The loss is plotted in the y-axis where the iteration number is 
plotted in the x-axis. The loss curve of training is plotted with a red line, 
while the loss curve of validating is plotted with a blue line. The gap 
between these two curves indicates the learning process of the model. 
There are three characteristics of the loss curve: under-fitting, over- 
fitting, and good fitting. An under-fitting model occurs when the model 
is not able to obtain a sufficiently low loss during the training and 
validating processes (Zhang et al., 2019). If the gap between two loss 
curves is large, the model is “over-fitting” (Jabbar and Khan, 2014; van 
der Aalst et al., 2008). A good-fitting model is one in which the gap 
between the two curves is small. As shown in Fig. 10. 
The loss curves in Fig. 10 show the overall model learning behaviors. 
The loss functions are ending approximately 14,000. The comparison 
between RGB-input model, IR-input model, VGG-19 model, AlexNet 
model and multi-input model are evaluated. The investigation shows 
that RGB-input model and VGG-19 model training loss curves approach 
steady state (less than 0.05) in faster than 4,000 iterations. However, IR- 
input model, AlexNet model and multi-input model eventually decrease 
to a stable state (<0.05) after nearly 12,500, 13,000 and 8,950 itera­
tions, respectively. The validating loss curve is a bit less smooth than the 
training one when the model converges due to the smaller dataset for 
validation. The gap between the training and validating loss curves of 
RGB-input model, IR-input model, VGG-19 model, AlexNet model and 
multi-input model achieves a loss value 0.53, 0.53, 1.6, 0.8 and 0.39 
respectively.

4. Conclusions 
A multi-input and multi-label classification DL model for grading 
several varieties of FFBs has been presented. The collecting unit called 
PalmSnap has been proposed as a multi-sensor data collecting unit. It 
consists of a Kinect camera and a load cell. Four collecting units were 
installed at four trading sites for collecting FFBs. The FFBs datasets were 
transferred to the cloud server to develop the multi-input DL model. 
Before the training process, the data preprocessing process was applied 
for noise cleansing and resizing. Three different data types (RGB, IR, 
weight) were applied to train the multi-input model. It has been found 
that the model, which composes of five convolution sub blocks, two fully 
connected sub blocks and one concatenation sub block, is able to classify 
4 grades of 14 varieties of FFBs with 90.26% accuracy, 89.86% preci­
sion, 89.54% recall, and an 89.68% F1-score. The performances of the 
multi-input model were better than those of the single-input models. 
The limitation of the proposed model is its more complexity as 
compared with the single-input model. Another issue is the dataset 
preparation dataset which contains RGB, IR and weight. The overall 
processing time of the system needs to be improved to match the ca­
pacity of a palm oil refinery plant. Currently, the whole processing time 
of our system is approximately 30 s per FFB, with 20 s used for moving 
the FFB using the conveyor belt and 3 s for the software process, 
including the setup and computing. If we assume that the average 
weight of a FFB is 25 kg, the processing capacity of the system in 
continuous mode is 30 tons. With the average production capacity of the 
palm oil refinery plants in the south of Thailand, 31.3 tons, the pro­
cessing time of the proposed system needs to be reduced to be relevant to 
the production capacity. If it succeeds, we will install the improved 
model in PalmSnap and deploy them in palm trading sites. We strongly 
believe that this approach will create an efficient palm ecosystem for all 
key stakeholders.
