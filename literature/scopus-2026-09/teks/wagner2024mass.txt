Mass Estimation of Soft Fruit via Oscillatory Plant Dynamics
Nikolaus Wagner and Grzegorz Cielniak1
Abstract— Yield forecasting is an essential task in modern
agriculture, as it enables farmers and food economists to
manage crop and its distribution precisely and effectively.
Traditionally, most methods for yield forecasting are based on
historical data and yield estimates from manually collected
samples. More modern approaches often rely on computer
vision-based fruit counting algorithms, which do not take
individual crop weights into account.
In this paper, we propose a novel, non-destructive method to
estimate the mass of individual pieces of fruit by exploiting the
dynamic properties of plants. By observing short-term oscilla-
tory plant motion through RGB-D video data, we formulate
an approach for mass estimation based on determining the
parameters of a damped harmonic oscillator model.
We test the proposed algorithm by collecting a dataset of
around 300 video samples of strawberries on a real strawberry
farm and apply our method. With a semi-automated toolchain,
capable of inferring the key parameters from video data and
calculating the mass of individual berries from those, we were
able to estimate the mass of all berries in our dataset with a
median error of 1.16 g, outperforming a baseline utilising vision-
based volume estimation to infer the mass. These insights hold
valuable improvements for the development of yield forecasting
systems and selective harvesters, which help to address the
sustainability of food production and labour shortages.
I. INTRODUCTION
Strawberries are an increasingly important crop, with
426,000 tons produced in the UK alone in 2022. At an
average farm gate price of 2,600 £ per ton this equals a total
value of roughly 1.1 billion pounds for UK farmers per year,
with further growth expected [1]. This amount of produce re-
quires fast and precise handling, as strawberries are soft fruit
which spoil quickly. In order to manage the complex tasks of
storing, shipping, pricing, stocking and selling the crop it is
therefore essential to have an accurate model of the expected
amount of harvest per day. Automated yield forecasting of
strawberries has been addressed previously, e.g. at a larger
scale through satellite imagery [2] or more precisely at the
individual plant level [3]. The latter approaches generally
rely on mass measurements of individual fruits, which are
usually inferred indirectly through automated vision-based
detectors and volumetric approximations.
In many cooler climates such as the UK’s, the preferred
method for growing strawberries at scale is by using tabletop
systems in polytunnels (see Fig. 1), which provide protection
against the elements and cooler nights. In these growing
systems the strawberries hang down the sides of the beds,
where they ripen and can be picked more easily, speeding up
harvesting processes. This system gives the hanging berries

When exposed to wind or rain, plants experience various
kinds of movement. They sway or shake under external
influences, leaves flutter in the wind and branches bend under
the strain of rain or snow. This type of oscillatory motion can
be exploited in order to infer internal mechanical properties
of plants [4], which is impossible with classical vision-based
systems alone. With our approach, we intend to bridge this
gap by exploiting the dynamic motion of plants with the help
of state-of-the-art (SOTA) machine vision solutions in order
to gain a better understanding of the internal mechanical
parameters of plants. We thus aim to improve upon existing
solutions, gaining deeper insights into plant health, irrigation
and crop state with the help of modern machine vision
systems. Specifically, the previously mentioned swinging
motion is what we exploit to analyse the internal properties
like the mass of the fruit. To this end, we introduce the
following key contributions in this paper:
1) an application of estimating the mass of soft fruit from
dynamic plant behaviour, observed with a video-based
position tracking system, in a non-destructive manner;
2) an approach of using Harmonic Inversion to deduce the
key oscillatory parameters needed for this application;
3) a dataset containing around 300 annotated video sam-
ples of strawberries exhibiting the relevant dynamic
behaviours with an evaluation of our approach on this
dataset, compared to an established baseline.

II. RELATED WORK
Robotics and computer vision-based applications in agri-
culture have grown rapidly over the last decade. Robotics-
assisted monitoring of plants [5], selective spraying of in-
secticides [6], selective harvesting [7] and also more general
precision crop care [8] are all fields that have benefited from
recent technological advancements massively. Furthermore,
not only areas directly linked to in-the-field plant care utilise
these emerging technologies, but botanists and horticulturists
increasingly also employ modern technologies for things like
phenotyping and breeding as well [9][10][3][11][12]. Most
of the features considered for these applications are how-
ever solely geometric or colour-based measurements [10].
Dynamic behaviours however, especially those exhibiting
movement in the range of seconds instead of minutes or
hours, are closely related to the internal mechanical parame-
ters of plants, hence studying those enables an estimation of
characteristics not accessible by vision sensors alone. Due
to the static but motile nature of plants, most of their move-
ments are oscillatory, leading us to identify key parameters
defining these motions [13]. Some recent work in this area
has already highlighted the significance of motion parameters
for plant health [14], irrigation state [15] and specifically
also for automated phenotyping [16]. In this paper, we utilise
these insights into automated weight estimation for tasks like
crop mass estimation and therefore also yield forecasting, an
increasingly important area in modern agriculture [17].
The significance of having an understanding of the amount
and mass of your crop is essential for planning harvesting
tasks, storage logistics as well as financial and economic
strategies. Estimating the mass of crop which can be har-
vested at a certain point in time needs to be automated,
preferably in a non-destructive manner. This currently does
not align with the SOTA, which is why new methods
to improve upon this are in huge demand. Most existing
methods rely on single-image-based analyses [3] or other
more empirical methods [18]. Plants however are dynamic
structures and exhibit various kinds of motion, which we
can exploit to gain an understanding of the mass of the
harvestable crop. Furthermore, purely vision-based meth-
ods suffer from another significant downside: Even if very
precise volume estimates can be obtained, one still needs
accurate density information to be able to effectively use the
data for mass estimation, which is virtually impossible to
obtain in a quick and non-destructive manner. It becomes
apparent that a non-destructive method, utilising vision data,
enhanced with a minimal amount of interactive perception,
capable of estimating the mass of crop in the field is required.
III. MATERIALS AND METHODS
A. Scenario
In our method, we make use of the oscillatory motion of
free-standing or hanging plant parts. We exploit this motion
to infer the mass of strawberries, although the same princi-
ples apply to many similar scenarios. This process, utilising
techniques from classical mechanics, signal processing &
computer vision, consists of the following general steps:
1) Applying a controlled stimulus to the berries to induce
motion, monitoring the resulting behaviour with a
semi-automated vision system;
2) Extracting the dynamic characteristics from the ob-
served movement;
3) Calculating mechanical parameters like mass, damping
and compliance from the obtained characteristics.

C. Motion Tracking
To obtain the raw motion information itself, we use visual
data recorded with the help of an RGB & depth (RGB-D)
camera, providing high-resolution images at sufficiently high
frame rates of 30 Hz. We then detect and track the individual
pieces of fruit utilising a strawberry detection and tracking
framework, consisting of a custom implementation of Mask
R-CNN [19] trained on segmentation-annotated strawberry

F. Dataset
To evaluate the performance of our method, we collect a
dataset of strawberries in motion to estimate the mass of each
fruit based on oscillatory behaviour. We record data samples
of the oscillatory reaction obtained by applying a controlled
stimulus onto individual strawberries. These data samples
combine video and manually obtained sensor measurements,
which were performed destructively to obtain GT values after
acquiring the non-destructive motion samples for the dataset.
Each sample thus contains the excitation force applied to
the berry, a 10 s long RGB-D video monitoring its ringdown
behaviour, the mass and length of the pedicel (i.e. the directly
connected stem) of each berry. Furthermore, we classify the
ripeness into one of four stages: unripe-small, unripe-fullsize,
half-ripe and fully-ripe berries (see Fig. 3 for illustration).
We then evaluate the motion obtained from the video sam-
ples as described in Section III-C with the tools introduced in
Section III-D to furthermore obtain eigenfrequency f, decay
rate β, initial amplitude x0 and phase information ϕ. For this
dataset, this annotation step was semi-automated with manual
augmentations and corrections to ensure consistent data
quality for evaluation, however, this can be fully automated
to work in a completely autonomous system.
We collect 295 such samples from 2 varieties (Driscoll’s
Zara & Driscoll’s Katrina) using a Pasco PS-2189 force
sensor with a resolution of 0.002 N [23], a Precision Scale
G&G LS200 with a resolution of 0.01 g [24] and a Microsoft
Azure Kinect RGB-D camera with an image resolution
of 1920 x 1080 pixels, a frame rate of 30 Hz and a depth
resolution of 1 mm provided by the camera’s time-of-flight
(TOF)-sensor [25]. We place the camera roughly 1 m away

V. DISCUSSION
Our method outperforms purely vision-based approaches
due to not relying on prior knowledge like fruit density.
This makes it transferable to any plant of similar structure,
regardless of size or shape of fruit. We require an interaction,
which is often undesirable since it might damage the crop,
but this can be mitigated by using a combined approach
of our method to infer the mass and a purely vision-based
approach to estimate fruit volume. That way, an estimate of
density can be obtained from small sample sets, allowing for
knowledge transfer without needing further interactions. This
improves the scalability of our method and could make its
performance comparable to purely vision-based approaches.
Furthermore, if no interactions with the plant are possible,
we can utilise known average values for the stiffness k,
eliminating the need to collect force and displacement data
(see Eq. (2)). This reduces accuracy, as the stiffness varies
greatly across different plants (cf. Fig. 5), however by using
a mean value for k across all samples, the mean error for
all mass estimates is still below 25 %. External factors like
wind or rain may affect data collection in outdoor settings,
however this offers the potential for collecting motion data
without interactions, relying on the natural movement of
plants, which we aim to explore in future research.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
We have successfully demonstrated a semi-automated ap-
proach for obtaining dynamic motion parameters of plants
from RGB-D vision data and minimal interactions. We have
shown how these parameters can be used for mass estimation
of strawberries and thus how the method applies to modern-
day problems like yield forecasting in fruit production. We
have introduced a dataset consisting of around 300 video
samples which have been used to test the method and
compare it to a purely vision-based, volumetric baseline. Our
method performs consistently better, reaching median errors
of 1.16 g across all samples. Our method is not yet fully
automated, as precise and delicate manipulation of soft fruit
is needed, however this can be achieved using SOTA robotics
easily. For the data discussed in this paper, we utilised
isolated pieces of fruit, although in real-world environments
strawberries often appear in clusters. In future work, this
needs to be taken into account and modelled appropriately.
It is also not yet fully clear how well the method generalises
in other scenarios as it has only been tested in this specific
setting, however, the same principles apply and we look
forward to experimenting with another crop in the future.
