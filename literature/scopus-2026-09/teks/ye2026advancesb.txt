Abstract
Orchard production involves intensive labor requirements, limited operational periods,
and highly dynamic and complex working environments. Consequently, the development
of intelligent orchard robots has become a important approach to enhancing production
efficiency and reducing reliance on manual operations. This review focuses on the demands
of autonomous robotic systems operating in challenging orchard scenarios and provides
a comprehensive overview of key technologies, including environmental perception and
semantic cognition, autonomous navigation and environmental modeling, intelligent task
execution, and collaborative robotic systems. Recent advances in fruit and blossom detec-
tion, branch and canopy structure perception, multi-modal sensor fusion for localization,
semantic mapping, robotic harvesting control, variable-rate spraying, precision pollination,
and autonomous intra-row weed management are systematically discussed. Furthermore,
emerging technologies such as multi-robot coordination, robot–UAV cooperation, large
language models (LLMs), and vision-language models (VLMs) for enhancing decision-
making capabilities in agricultural robotics are reviewed. Finally, the existing challenges
of orchard robots in terms of perception reliability, long-term autonomous navigation,
operational robustness, system-level integration, and standardized performance evaluation
are analyzed, followed by discussions on potential future research directions.
Keywords: intelligent orchard robots; environmental sensing; autonomous navigation;
precision agricultural operations; multi-robot collaboration; intelligent agriculture
1. Introduction
Orchard production is one of the most labor-intensive components of agricultural
production systems and involves diverse operations, including blossom pollination, pest
and disease control, weeding, inspection, harvesting, and transportation. Compared with
field-crop production, orchard operations are highly seasonal, have narrow operational
windows, involve spatially dispersed targets, and remain heavily dependent on manual
labor. In recent years, rising labor costs and a declining agricultural workforce have placed
substantial pressure on conventional orchard-management practices. In particular, reliance
on manual labor alone may be increasingly insufficient to meet the requirements of large-
scale, refined, and highly efficient production in harvesting, spraying, pollination, and
weeding. Accordingly, the use of robots, sensors, autonomous navigation, machine vision,
and intelligent optimization algorithms to increase orchard automation has become a major
direction in the development of smart orchards [1–3].

With the development of smart and digital agriculture, agricultural robots are evolving
from single-purpose mechanized equipment into intelligent systems capable of environmen-
tal perception, autonomous mobility, precision execution, and task-level decision-making.
Previous studies have indicated that autonomous driving systems, intelligent agricultural
robots, and multi-robot systems are increasingly being applied to conventionally manual
tasks such as seeding, spraying, and harvesting, while agricultural production is progres-
sively moving toward unmanned and intelligent operation [4]. In orchard environments
in particular, robotic technologies have been used for fruit harvesting, flower pollination,
variable-rate spraying, intra-row weeding, environmental monitoring, and yield estima-
tion, thereby offering technical pathways to alleviate labor shortages, improve operational
efficiency, and reduce resource consumption [5,6].
Existing studies have demonstrated a broad range of orchard-robot applications. Har-
vesting robots mainly address fruit recognition, three-dimensional localization, manipulator
path planning, and end-effector control [7]. Spraying robots emphasize target recognition,
variable-rate pesticide application, and improved utilization of spray liquid. Pollination
robots focus on flower detection, identification of suitable pollination windows, and precise
pollen delivery. Weeding robots primarily address weed recognition between rows or
individual trees, path tracking, and operational safety [8,9]. For example, research on
spraying robots for jujube orchards has noted the large scale of China’s jujube industry
and the important role of high-density dwarf jujube orchards in Xinjiang. Mid-season
management in these orchards still depends heavily on manual labor and is affected by
pronounced seasonal and structural labor shortages, creating a clear practical demand for
automated orchard robots [10].
At the same time, the capabilities of a single robot the capabilities of a single robot
are no longer sufficient to meet the production requirements of large orchards. In exten-
sive orchards, densely distributed task locations, or geographically distributed orchard
systems, a single robot often suffers from low operational efficiency, limited coverage,
and long task-completion times. Multi-robot collaboration, robot-UAV collaboration, and
cross-orchard task scheduling have therefore emerged as new research directions in smart
orchards [11,12]. Related studies have shown that agricultural robots and UAVs can be
applied to pollination, harvesting, spraying, and monitoring. Ground robots are well suited
to close-range precision operations, whereas UAVs are advantageous for aerial spraying
and inspection. Coordinated use of the two platforms can exploit their complementary
strengths and improve the efficiency of complex agricultural operations [13–15].
Consequently, research on intelligent orchard robots is no longer confined to a single
mechanical structure or a single-purpose machine. Instead, it is increasingly oriented to-
ward an integrated technological system comprising multimodal perception, autonomous
navigation, precision operation execution, task scheduling, and intelligent decision-making.
Achieving robust perception, reliable mobility, accurate execution, and efficient collabo-
ration in complex orchard environments has become a key requirement for advancing
orchard robots from experimental validation to practical deployment [16–18].
Orchard environments combine semi-structured and unstructured characteristics.
Fruit trees are generally planted in rows and therefore exhibit a degree of spatial regularity;
however, canopy morphology, foliage density, fruit distribution, terrain undulation, weed
cover, and working-lane width remain highly variable. These environmental characteristics
create substantial challenges for orchard robots in perception, localization, navigation, and
operation execution [19]. In contrast to indoor or standardized industrial environments,
orchard robots must not only identify operation targets but also understand tree structure,
traversable areas, obstacle locations, and the spatial relationships between operation targets
and robotic actuators.

First, complex orchard environments impose major challenges on perception systems.
Fruits, flowers, branches, trunks, leaves, and weeds vary considerably in color, morphology,
and spatial distribution and are frequently affected by occlusion, overlap, illumination
variation, and background interference. For harvesting robots, fruit-localization errors
directly affect grasping success and fruit-damage rates. For pollination robots, the accuracy
of flower recognition and pollination-position control influences pollination performance.
For weeding and spraying robots, accurate recognition of trunks, weeds, canopies, and
target plants is essential for operational safety and resource-use efficiency [20,21]. Studies on
semantic mapping have shown that conventional simultaneous localization and mapping
(SLAM) can provide relatively accurate geometric maps, but geometric information alone
is insufficient to support autonomous orchard operations. Robots must also understand
semantic information, such as trees, terrain, and traversable regions, within the map.
Second, orchard localization and navigation are challenged by unstable global navigation
satellite system (GNSS) signals, sensor degradation, and dynamic environmental changes [22].
GNSS performs well in open farmland, but canopy occlusion, multipath interference, and
terrain shielding in orchards can reduce positioning accuracy or cause signal loss. Inertial
navigation can provide continuous short-term pose estimates but accumulates drift. Vision-
based navigation provides rich environmental information but is vulnerable to illumination,
weather, and motion blur. Two-dimensional LiDAR is relatively insensitive to lighting but
captures only planar information and may provide insufficient geometry in complex canopies
and undulating terrain [23]. To address these issues, current studies increasingly employ
multi-sensor fusion involving LiDAR, RGB-D cameras, inertial measurement units (IMUs),
wheel odometry, and adaptive Monte Carlo localization (AMCL) to improve localization
stability and path-tracking performance in GNSS-constrained orchard environments.
Orchard operations also impose stringent requirements on navigation and execution
accuracy. Spraying, pollination, weeding, and harvesting cannot be completed merely
by reaching a target area; rather, robots must operate stably along specified paths and at
prescribed distances and attitudes [24]. For example, a spraying robot in a high-density
jujube orchard must follow predefined operation paths with high-precision cruising and
point-specific stopping. Studies have implemented spray-route navigation using LiDAR
SLAM, IMU-based inertial navigation, extended Kalman filtering (EKF), AMCL local-
ization, A* path planning, and dynamic window approach (DWA)-based local obstacle
avoidance [25]. Field experiments reported navigation-error indices for straight-line travel,
point stopping, turning, and S-shaped path tracking, demonstrating explicit requirements
for lateral-deviation and heading-angle control in practical orchard operation. Meanwhile,
orchard-robot tasks are expanding from single-robot, single-task operation toward multi-
robot, multi-task, and multi-scenario systems. Although multi-robot systems can improve
operational efficiency, they introduce complex challenges involving task allocation, path
conflicts, energy constraints, load balancing, and operation-time limits. Existing studies
have emphasized the inherent coupling between task allocation and path planning in
agricultural multi-robot operations. If task allocation is considered without accounting
for obstacles, terrain constraints, and dynamic interactions among robots, the resulting
solution may be difficult to execute effectively in real environments [26]. Joint optimization
of task allocation and path planning is therefore an important direction for improving the
feasibility and robustness of collaborative agricultural-robot operations.
Collaborative scheduling becomes even more complex in distributed or multi-orchard
scenarios. Orchards may be geographically dispersed and differ in area, task density,
and environmental conditions. In addition, robot and UAV operations may be subject
to precedence constraints. For example, in the task-allocation problem involving pollina-
tion robots and spraying UAVs across multiple orchards, robots must complete pollina-

tion before UAV spraying begins, and this precedence requirement markedly increases

Overall, autonomous operation in complex orchard environments faces four principal
challenges. First, perceptual targets are complex: fruits, flowers, branches, trunks, and
weeds are readily affected by occlusion and environmental variation. Second, the navi-
gation environment is difficult: GNSS constraints, uneven terrain, and canopy occlusion
reduce localization stability. Third, operation execution requires high precision: robots
must perform accurate, efficient, safe, and minimally damaging operations within confined
spaces. Fourth, multi-robot collaborative scheduling is highly complex and must simultane-
ously account for task duration, path cost, load balance, energy constraints, and precedence
relationships. Together, these challenges require orchard-robot research to progress from
isolated module optimization toward system-level co-design.
To address the requirements of autonomous agricultural-robot operation in complex
orchard environments, this review is organized around the technical chain of environmen-
tal perception, autonomous navigation, precision execution, and collaborative decision-
making. In contrast to reviews focused on a single robot category or a single operation
technology, this article emphasizes the technical interrelationships among the perception,
mobility, execution, and decision layers and seeks to capture the overall trend toward
systematic development of intelligent orchard robots.
The review first summarizes environmental-perception and semantic-understanding
technologies for orchard robots. This section examines methods for recognizing and localiz-
ing representative objects, including fruits, flowers, branches, trunks, weeds, and obstacles,
with particular emphasis on applications of machine vision, RGB-D sensing, stereo vision,
LiDAR, point-cloud processing, instance segmentation, semantic segmentation, and VLMs
in complex orchard scenes. Existing studies show that geometric maps alone cannot satisfy
the requirements of autonomous operations. Semantic maps provide high-level information
on tree layouts, target instances, and traversable regions and thus constitute an important
foundation for autonomous navigation and task execution.
Second, this review summarizes localization, navigation, and scene-mapping meth-
ods for orchard robots. With emphasis on autonomous mobility in GNSS-constrained
environments, this section discusses visual navigation, LiDAR navigation, SLAM-based
mapping, multi-sensor fusion localization, semantic map construction, path planning,
and path-tracking control. Related studies indicate that multi-sensor fusion can partially
compensate for the limitations of individual sensors and improve navigation reliability in
complex, occluded, and unstructured orchard scenes.
Third, this review summarizes precision-execution technologies for representative
orchard operations, including harvesting, spraying, pollination, and weeding. Key issues
include operation-target localization, manipulator control, variable-rate spraying, liquid
pollination, intra-row weeding, wind compensation, error calibration, and system eval-
uation. Comparison across tasks shows that precision execution depends not only on
actuator performance but also on close coordination among front-end perception accuracy,
navigation stability, and operation-control strategies. Finally, multi-robot collaborative
scheduling and intelligent decision-making are reviewed, with emphasis on multi-robot
task allocation, joint task-path optimization, multi-trip harvesting schedules, robot-UAV
collaboration, multi-orchard operation management, and the use of LLMs and VLMs in
robotic task planning. Existing research suggests that single-robot systems are insufficient
for large-scale orchard operations. Although multi-robot systems can alleviate efficiency
bottlenecks, they also require effective solutions to task allocation, path planning, load

For the purpose of this review, a smart orchard robot is defined as an embodied or
physically deployed robotic system designed to operate in orchard environments and capa-
ble of implementing at least part of an integrated perception–decision–action loop. Such
systems may perform autonomous or semi-autonomous functions related to orchard inspec-
tion, navigation, harvesting, spraying, pollination, weeding, transportation, or coordinated
task execution.
Ground mobile robots constitute the primary focus of this review. Aerial robots are
included only when they directly perform orchard operations or cooperate with ground
robots in sensing, task planning, scheduling, or execution. Fixed sensing platforms are
not regarded as robots but are considered enabling infrastructure when their outputs are
directly integrated into robotic perception, localization, planning, or control. Agricultural
machinery is included only when it incorporates orchard-specific autonomous sensing,
localization, decision-making, or operation-control capabilities.
Individual sensors, perception algorithms, manipulators, end effectors, and control
modules are treated as robotic enabling subsystems rather than independent orchard robots.
They are included when they have a demonstrated or clearly defined role within a robotic
operation pipeline. Similarly, LLMs, VLMs, and other foundation models are considered
only when they directly support robot perception, semantic reasoning, task planning, behav-
ior generation, or control. General-purpose artificial-intelligence applications, conventional
mechanized equipment, standalone remote-sensing studies, and broader smart-farming

To improve the transparency and reproducibility of this review, a structured litera-
ture search and study-selection process was conducted. The search was completed on
15 June 2026 using SPIS, covering publications from 2002 to 15 June 2026. Search terms
covered orchard environments, robotic platforms, and key functions, including perception,
navigation, mapping, harvesting, spraying, pollination, weeding, manipulation, multi-
robot systems, robot–UAV cooperation, and vision-language models. Search syntax was
adapted for each database, and reference lists of relevant reviews and primary studies were
also examined.
A PRISMA-type procedure was used for study selection. The initial search identified
215 records. After duplicate removal, titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened. Studies
were included if they investigated orchard robots or enabling technologies with a clear role
in a robotic perception–decision–execution pipeline and reported sufficiently detailed or
quantitative results. Studies focused solely on conventional machinery, standalone remote
sensing, general smart-farming management, or algorithms without a clear robotic applica-
tion were excluded. Editorials, conference abstracts without full texts, non-peer-reviewed
reports, and studies lacking sufficient methodological or experimental information were
also excluded. In total, 154 records were excluded and 61 core studies were retained.
The selected studies were classified into four categories: environmental perception and
semantic understanding, autonomous navigation and scene mapping, precision operation
execution, and collaborative scheduling and intelligent decision-making. Studies covering
multiple categories were classified according to their main contribution.
Because of substantial differences in crops, orchard structures, robotic platforms,
experimental conditions, and evaluation metrics, a formal meta-analysis was not per-
formed. Instead, the studies were qualitatively assessed in terms of quantitative evidence,
technological maturity, field-validation scale, environmental robustness, reproducibility,
computational requirements, and application relevance. Unreported information was
marked as “NR” rather than inferred.

2. Orchard Environmental Perception and Semantic Understanding
Stable and accurate perception and semantic understanding of complex orchard en-
vironments are prerequisites for autonomous robotic operation [29]. Unlike industrial
environments, orchard scenes contain intertwined fruits, flowers, branches, trunks, leaves,
weeds, and ground surfaces and are frequently characterized by occlusion, overlap, illu-
mination variation, scale differences, and depth noise. Perception systems must not only
recognize targets but also provide reliable spatial information for manipulator control, preci-
sion spraying, liquid pollination, intra-row weeding, path planning, and obstacle avoidance.
Consequently, orchard perception is evolving from conventional two-dimensional image

2.1. Recognition of Fruits, Flowers, and Operation Targets
Fruits, flowers, and canopies are the most direct operation targets for orchard
robots [33,34]. Harvesting robots require three-dimensional fruit positions; pollination
robots must identify flowers and determine spray locations; and spraying robots must
extract target plants or canopy structures from complex backgrounds. Because orchard
scenes are affected by foliage occlusion, fruit overlap, and illumination variation, conven-
tional two-dimensional detection alone is insufficient for execution-layer requirements. The
integration of object detection, instance segmentation, and three-dimensional localization
has therefore become an important trend [35–37].
To address apple localization in complex orchards, Bhattacharya et al. proposed a three-
dimensional localization framework based on detection, segmentation, and clustering [38].
Apples are first detected using a VLM, after which instance segmentation is used to obtain
apple pixel regions. The pixels are subsequently projected into three-dimensional space,
and density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) is applied to
filter depth noise [38]. The method was evaluated on an RGB-D image dataset containing
219 apples and including indoor and outdoor scenes as well as real and simulated fruit.
The mean absolute localization error was 7.7 mm, representing a 74.3% improvement over
the best baseline. The approach was also deployed on a field harvesting robot, where
it exhibited favorable real-time performance and robustness to occlusion. The principal
contribution of this study is the integration of two-dimensional semantic recognition with
three-dimensional geometric localization, enabling perception outputs to be used directly
for robotic harvesting. Nevertheless, the sample size remains limited, and generalization
across cultivars, seasons, and heavily occluded scenes requires further validation.
Hao et al. developed a gripping-assisted flower-detection and wind-compensated
pollination robot for precision liquid pollination in kiwifruit orchards. The system used
CLIP-assisted automatic annotation to construct a flower-detection dataset and trained a
lightweight YOLO model for real-time recognition. It also integrated liquid-spray control
and wind-speed and wind-direction compensation modules [39]. Field trials conducted
during 2024–2025 showed that, after wind compensation and a second supplemental spray,
the fruit-set rate exceeded 82% in all test areas and reached a maximum of 96.0%. This
study demonstrates that flower recognition should not be evaluated solely in terms of
detection accuracy but should also be jointly assessed with pollination performance, pollen
consumption, and compensation for environmental disturbances. A remaining limitation
is that mechanical pollination did not fully match manual pollination for some fruit-quality
indicators, indicating the need for coordinated optimization of flower perception, spraying
mechanisms, and operation strategies.
For spray-target recognition, Hui Liu et al. developed an intelligent multivariable
spraying robot for orchards and nurseries. The robot used three-dimensional LiDAR to
acquire point clouds of target plants and an improved DBSCAN algorithm to extract plant
information. Spray flow rate, air volume, droplet diameter, and spraying direction were
then controlled according to target position, shape, and volume [40]. In field trials with
canopy diameters of 0.8–1.5 m, tree heights of 1.5–1.8 m, and inter-tree spacing of 2 m,
conventional spraying consumed 180–220 g of liquid per nozzle, whereas multivariable
spraying consumed 30–35 g, corresponding to an average pesticide-saving rate of an aver-
age spray-liquid reduction of approximately 83%. The study shows that three-dimensional
target recognition can be used not only for localization but also to derive spray-control
parameters, thereby closing the loop from target recognition to variable execution.

2.2. Perception of Branches, Canopies, and Tree Structure
Branches, canopies, and overall tree structure provide essential information for orchard
robots to understand their surroundings [41,42]. Branches may occlude fruit and interfere
with harvesting paths, trunks can serve as spatial references for navigation and weeding,
and canopy structure determines spray coverage and liquid deposition. Perception of
tree structure is therefore an important link between environmental understanding and
precision operation [42–45].
To address semantic segmentation of apple-tree branches under complex orchard
conditions, Zhengyang Liu et al. proposed EDSC-HRAFNet. Built on HRNet, the model
introduced an enhanced dynamic snake-convolution module to capture branch bifurcations
and elongated topological features, together with HeteroFPN and Parallel-M4 decoding
modules to strengthen multiscale feature representation [46]. The authors constructed a
multi-scene in situ apple-tree dataset and evaluated the method under eight complex orchard
conditions. Precision, recall, Dice, intersection over union (IoU), mean IoU (mIoU), and mean
pixel accuracy (MPA) reached 91.50%, 91.71%, 91.60%, 84.51%, 91.72%, and 95.58%, respec-
tively. Relative to HRNet, these metrics increased by 6.98%, 10.09%, 9.21%, 13.50%, 7.25%, and
5.24%, respectively. The method specifically addressed the discontinuous segmentation of
slender branches and bifurcated structures and is therefore relevant to obstacle avoidance in
harvesting and to robotic pruning. However, its frame rate of 25.1 frames per second (FPS),
although sufficient for low-speed apple-harvesting robots, imposes a greater computational
burden than lightweight models. Performance also declined in scenes containing yellow
apples and under adverse weather conditions, indicating that complex illumination and
background similarity continue to affect branch-recognition stability.
From the perspective of trunk perception, Sampurno et al. developed an orchard
robot for single-tree-row tracking during intra-row weeding. The robot used a three-
dimensional camera and a lightweight YOLO instance-segmentation model to detect trunks
and generated a path with a fixed lateral offset relative to the tree row. LiDAR was
simultaneously used to trigger a laterally extending weeding mechanism [47]. Experiments
showed a mean lateral-tracking root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.329 m and a mean
heading RMSE of 4.45 degrees, which were adequate for low-speed weeding navigation.
This study demonstrates that trunk detection is not merely a perception task but directly
determines the robot trajectory and the safety of the operation mechanism.
Perception of branches, canopies, and tree structure must balance semantic accuracy,
topological continuity, and real-time performance [48,49]. Existing methods can recognize
complex branch and trunk structures to some extent, but robustness across cultivars,
seasons, severe occlusion, and adverse weather remains a major research priority [50,51].

2.3. Detection of Weeds, Obstacles, and Traversable Areas
Detection of weeds, obstacles, and traversable areas is essential for safe mobility and
precision operation of orchard robots [52,53]. Orchard floors commonly contain weeds,
fallen branches, stones, irrigation facilities, and uneven terrain. Weeds are operation targets
for weeding robots, whereas weeds and other obstacles can compromise driving stability
and operational safety for harvesting, spraying, and inspection robots [54–56].
To address uncut weeds remaining between trees during intra-row orchard mowing,
Sampurno et al. designed a compact intra-row weeding robot. Machine stereo vision was used
to detect trunks and achieve single-row tracking, while LiDAR triggered a lateral mechanism
that removed weeds between trees [47]. At a low speed of 0.5 m/s, the lateral weeding
mechanism achieved an operation accuracy of 84.04%; when the speed increased to 0.8 m/s,
accuracy decreased to 76.85%. The authors attributed this reduction to delays between real-
time LiDAR point-cloud processing and actuator motion. These results show that weed and
obstacle detection must be assessed not only in terms of recognition accuracy but also with
respect to temporal synchronization among perception, decision-making, and execution.
Pan et al. addressed obstacle and traversable-area detection from a navigation per-
spective through an orchard perception and semantic-mapping method. LiDAR SLAM
was used to construct an orchard point-cloud map, a three-dimensional object-detection
network (3D-ODN) extracted instance-level semantic information such as fruit trees, and
terrain analysis was combined with these results to construct a visibility-graph map for
global path planning [57]. Tests in a greenhouse, an unstructured apple orchard, and an out-
door litchi orchard showed that the method could support orchard monitoring and selective
harvesting. Further experiments found that the viewpoint feature histogram (VFH) feature
achieved an IoU of 0.824 but required 2.13 s for feature extraction. By contrast, the proposed
three-channel feature method achieved an mIoU of 0.812, with a feature-extraction time of
0.1681 s and a prediction time of 0.0268 s. Thus, a small reduction in accuracy produced
a substantial gain in real-time performance, making the method more suitable for online
perception and mapping by mobile robots.
The purpose of traversable-area detection is not simply to segment the ground but to
transform tree rows, obstacles, and vehicle-motion constraints into a path representation
that a robot can execute. Pan et al. used semantic results to identify tree rows and construct a
visibility graph. Compared with the timed elastic band planner (TEB-planner), the method
achieved shorter global-planning times and more reasonable paths and could define turning
regions for Ackermann-steered vehicles [57]. This study indicates that orchard perception
should advance from identifying environmental elements toward generating executable
navigation representations.
Detection of weeds, obstacles, and traversable regions is a critical step in the tran-
sition from environmental perception to autonomous mobility. Future studies should
further improve stability under dense weed cover, muddy ground, occlusion by orchard
infrastructure, and dynamic obstacles [58–60].
2.4. Multimodal Fusion and Three-Dimensional Semantic Representation
No single sensor often cannot fully satisfy all perceptual requirements of orchard
robots [61]. RGB cameras provide color and texture information but are highly sensitive
to illumination. RGB-D and stereo-vision systems provide depth but are constrained by
sensing range and noise. LiDAR is robust to illumination changes and is suitable for
mapping and structural extraction, but it lacks texture semantics. Multimodal fusion has
therefore become an important direction for orchard-environment understanding [62–64].
The semantic-mapping method of Pan et al. illustrates the advantages of integrating Li-

2.5. Foundation-Model-Enabled Understanding of Orchard Scenes
Vision-language models and LLMs provide new technical pathways for orchard-scene
understanding [65,66]. Conventional vision models generally depend on large manually
annotated datasets, support fixed task categories, and have limited cross-scene transferability.
Foundation models offer stronger semantic representation and transfer capabilities and can
reduce annotation costs, enable open-vocabulary recognition, and support more flexible task
planning when integrated with robotic-control frameworks [67,68].
Bhattacharya et al. used a foundation VLM for apple detection and combined it
with instance segmentation and DBSCAN clustering for three-dimensional localization,
ultimately achieving a mean absolute localization error of 7.7 mm [38]. This result shows
that VLMs are not limited to coarse-grained semantic recognition; when combined with
geometric computation, they can also support high-precision localization for harvesting.
Hao et al. used CLIP to assist in constructing a kiwifruit-flower detection dataset and then
trained a lightweight YOLO model for real-time recognition, thereby reducing manual
annotation requirements. Field trials indicate that the value of foundation models in
agriculture lies not only in direct target recognition but also in improving the efficiency of
data preparation and model development.
Although the AGRI-BT robot proposed by Salman et al. was designed for green-
house operation, its technical architecture is informative for intelligent orchard robots.
The authors constructed 1187 pairs of natural-language agricultural task descriptions
and expert-validated XML behavior trees and applied low-rank adaptation (LoRA) to
CodeLlama-7B for domain adaptation, enabling the model to convert natural-language
tasks into executable behavior trees. CLIP was integrated for visual-semantic judgment [69].
ROS 2 simulations showed 100% XML structural validity, 87.5% semantic correctness, 82.0%
task alignment, and an overall weighted validation score of 91.4%. For navigation tasks,
specific task descriptions reduced tracking error from 0.208 to 0.135 m, a decrease of 35.1%.
This study indicates that foundation models can support not only perception but also task
planning and perception-driven decision-making.
Nevertheless, substantial limitations remain in the application of foundation mod-
els to agricultural robots [70]. Salman et al. reported that task alignment for complex
multistage tasks was lower than both structural validity and semantic correctness, pri-
marily because complex agricultural tasks accounted for only a small proportion of the
dataset. CLIP achieved a confidence of 84.1% on natural plant images but only 25–30% on
Gazebo-simulated greenhouse images, revealing a domain gap between simulated and
real agricultural imagery [71,72]. Foundation models therefore cannot be separated from
agriculture-specific data, lightweight deployment, and constraints imposed by robotic
execution. A more practical future direction is to combine the semantic generalization
capabilities of foundation models with specialized detectors, three-dimensional point-
cloud processing, semantic maps, and behavior-tree control to establish orchard-scene
understanding systems that are verifiable, deployable, and closed-loop.

3. Autonomous Navigation and Scene Mapping for Orchard Robots
Autonomous navigation and scene mapping provide the basis for orchard robots to
progress from isolated point operations to continuous field operations. Orchard robots
can perform spraying, weeding, harvesting, inspection, and transportation only when
they can reliably estimate their own pose, understand orchard structure, and generate
executable paths. Compared with open farmland, orchards are characterized by canopy
occlusion, narrow inter-row spaces, irregular tree arrangements, uneven terrain, foliage
obstruction, and unstable GNSS signals. Navigation systems must therefore achieve not
only localization and obstacle avoidance but also task-specific control of path, attitude, and
working distance. Accordingly, orchard navigation research has evolved from single-sensor
localization toward autonomous mobility systems jointly supported by vision, LiDAR,
IMUs, wheel odometry, and semantic maps [73–77].
3.1. Environmental Characteristics and Technical Requirements of Orchard Navigation
Orchard environments are generally semi-structured: tree rows exhibit a degree of regu-
larity, whereas canopies, branches, ground surfaces, and obstacles remain highly uncertain.
When operating in orchards, robots must maintain a stable heading within narrow rows and
switch paths between trees, at row ends, or within turning areas [78,79]. For spraying robots,
navigation errors alter the distance between nozzles and the canopy and consequently affect
spray deposition. For weeding robots, excessive lateral deviation may result in missed weeds
or tree damage. For harvesting and inspection robots, path stability directly influences the
manipulator workspace and the consistency of data acquisition [80–82].
Research on spraying robots for high-density jujube orchards clearly illustrates these
requirements [83,84]. For predefined route cruising in densely planted jujube orchards,
researchers developed an autonomous navigation system based on LiDAR SLAM and
IMU-based inertial navigation and fused IMU and LiDAR localization information using
an EKF [85]. LiDAR was used for orchard perception and SLAM mapping, AMCL localized
the robot within the map, and A* and DWA algorithms were used to generate and track
spraying paths. Field experiments showed that, during travel along a 15 m straight path, the
mean deviation along the X-axis was 3.41 cm and the mean heading angle was 1.25 degrees.
Within a 1.5 m point-stopping region, the mean X-direction deviation was 1.98 cm and the
mean heading angle was 0.77 degrees. During a turn with a radius of 2 m, the mean X-axis
deviation was 1.17 cm, the parallel-trajectory distance deviation was 4.79 cm, and the mean
heading angle was 3.31 degrees. Along an S-shaped path, the mean X-axis deviation was
1.8 cm and the mean heading angle was 1.6 degrees [86–88]. These results demonstrate that
orchard navigation systems must simultaneously satisfy the requirements of straight-line
cruising, point stopping, turning, and complex path tracking; arrival at a target point alone
is not an adequate evaluation criterion [89,90].
Weeding scenarios impose additional navigation requirements [91]. Intra-row weeding
robots must maintain a fixed lateral offset from a single tree row and control laterally
moving actuators when approaching trunks [92–94]. The single-tree-row-tracking robot
developed by Sampurno et al. used a three-dimensional camera and a lightweight YOLOv8
instance-segmentation model to detect trunks, extract a forward navigation path, and
maintain a lateral offset of 0.8 m from the tree row [47]. Simulation and field experiments
yielded a navigation RMSE of 0.329 m and demonstrated intra-row weeding navigation
without GNSS. This study shows that orchard navigation is not solely a global-localization
problem but is also closely related to control of the relative position between the robot and
its operation mechanism.
The technical requirements of orchard navigation systems can be summarized as
follows: stable localization in GNSS-constrained environments; reliable path extraction

and maintenance of task-appropriate lateral distance, heading angle, and operating attitude.

3.2. Vision and LiDAR-Based Navigation Methods
Vision and LiDAR are the two most widely used sensing modalities for orchard-robot
navigation [95]. Vision sensors provide rich color, texture, and semantic information and are
therefore suitable for trunk recognition, tree-row extraction, and object detection. LiDAR
provides stable range measurements and geometric-structure information, is insensitive to
illumination changes, and is well suited to mapping, obstacle avoidance, and extraction of
inter-row structure. Each modality offers distinct advantages and limitations [96,97].
For vision-based navigation, Sampurno et al. combined a three-dimensional camera
with a lightweight YOLOv8 instance-segmentation model for trunk detection and single-
row tracking [47]. The method identified trunks on one side, extracted the tree row ahead of
the robot, and generated a trajectory with a fixed lateral offset. The study also compared the
performance of visual-detection models on an edge device. The lightweight YOLOv8p-seg
model achieved a mean average precision (mAP) of 87.6% and 39.07 FPS for trunk detection,
satisfying real-time navigation requirements. End-to-end latency from visual perception
to steering control was approximately 27.64–30.64 ms, demonstrating the feasibility of
vision-based navigation for low-speed orchard operations. The principal advantage is that
semantic trunk recognition is directly converted into a navigation-control input, making the
method suitable for intra-row operation where GNSS is unavailable and tree-row structure
is evident. However, the visual system remains sensitive to illumination, occlusion, and
variations in trunk appearance, and actuator accuracy decreases at higher travel speeds.
LiDAR navigation exhibited high stability in the jujube-orchard spraying robot. LiDAR
was used for environmental perception, SLAM mapping, extraction of inter-row operation
points, and localization assistance [98]. Inter-row points detected by LiDAR were clustered and
fitted to extract operation paths; A* performed global path search, while DWA handled local
path tracking and obstacle avoidance. In field trials, the robot maintained centimeter-level
lateral deviations on straight, point-stopping, turning, and S-shaped paths, demonstrating the
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16168046

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 8046
15 of 39
advantages of LiDAR for high-precision cruising in densely planted orchards. A limitation is
that two-dimensional or low-channel-count LiDAR provides insufficient representation of
canopies, uneven ground, and three-dimensional obstacles. Future systems should therefore
incorporate richer three-dimensional point-cloud information [99,100].
In general, vision-based navigation is better suited to extracting semantic targets and
tree-row features, whereas LiDAR is more suitable for ranging, mapping, and obstacle
avoidance. No single approach can cover all real orchard conditions. Vision offers a cost
advantage under stable lighting and when trunks have distinctive features; LiDAR is
more robust when illumination varies substantially, environmental structure is complex, or
high-precision maps are required. Future studies should emphasize complementary fusion
of visual semantics and LiDAR geometry.
3.3. Multi-Sensor Fusion Localization and SLAM
Canopy and terrain occlusion reduce GNSS reliability in orchards, making multi-sensor
fusion localization and SLAM important directions for autonomous navigation [101,102].
Vision alone is vulnerable to illumination changes, LiDAR alone may degrade in areas with
limited geometric features, and inertial navigation alone accumulates drift [103]. Fusion
of IMUs, wheel odometry, RGB-D sensing, LiDAR, and AMCL can improve localization
continuity and mapping consistency [104–106].
The jujube-orchard spraying-navigation system combined LiDAR SLAM, IMU-based
inertial navigation, EKF, and AMCL localization [107–109]. The IMU provided short-term
attitude and motion information, LiDAR supplied environmental geometric constraints, the
EKF fused localization information, and AMCL performed global localization in a previously
constructed map. Field results showed that the system met the cruising-accuracy requirements
of spraying in densely planted jujube orchards [110,111]. Its significance lies in the integration
of multi-source localization, path planning, and operation-path tracking within a complete
spraying-robot system rather than in validation of an isolated localization algorithm.
Chang et al. proposed a low-cost multi-sensor fusion SLAM navigation system for an
autonomous orchard-weeding robot [60]. The system fused RGB-D point-cloud information
with two-dimensional LiDAR to construct a two-dimensional occupancy grid and tightly
coupled visual-inertial odometry with wheel odometry to mitigate feature degradation in
unstructured environments. AMCL was introduced to improve global-localization stability,
and Cartographer enhanced mapping accuracy and long-term consistency. In real-orchard
experiments, the mean absolute pose error was 0.486 m with a standard deviation of
0.328 m. At travel speeds of 0.5–1.5 m/s, the mean heading deviation remained below
7 degrees, while longitudinal and lateral localization errors were limited to 8 and 7 cm,
respectively. These results show that multi-sensor fusion can provide usable navigation in
GNSS-denied orchards and is particularly suitable for low-cost weeding robots. However,
the absolute pose error remained close to 0.5 m; high-precision harvesting or near-tree
operations therefore require additional correction through local visual servoing or close-
range perception.
Pan et al. used LiDAR and an IMU with LIO-SAM to construct an orchard point-cloud
map and deliberately excluded GPS during mapping because orchard obstacles reduce GPS
stability and accuracy [57]. A mobile robot first collected LiDAR data and generated the
point-cloud map; fruit-tree detection, terrain analysis, and semantic processing were then
performed on the map to create a high-level semantic representation for navigation. In con-
trast to SLAM systems that output only geometric occupancy grids, this approach further
transformed the SLAM result into a scene structure suitable for autonomous operation.
Multi-sensor fusion localization and SLAM improve navigation continuity and resis-
tance to disturbance in orchards but also increase the burden of sensor calibration, temporal

synchronization, computation, and system complexity [112–114]. Future system design
should therefore select an appropriate fusion level according to task requirements. General
inspection and weeding may use low-cost fusion of RGB-D sensing, two-dimensional Li-
DAR, and wheel odometry, whereas high-precision harvesting and complex scene mapping
are better served by three-dimensional LiDAR and semantic SLAM.
3.4. Semantic Map Construction and Path Planning
Conventional SLAM primarily provides geometric maps, whereas orchard robots need
to know not only where obstacles are located but also where tree rows and traversable
areas are situated, how each tree can be accessed, and in which direction operations should
be performed [115–117]. Semantic maps therefore serve as an important intermediate layer
linking perception, navigation, and operation tasks.
The orchard-perception and semantic-mapping framework proposed by Pan et al.
is representative [57]. The framework comprises mapping, perception, and semantic-
processing stages. First, LiDAR and LIO-SAM are used to construct an orchard point-cloud
map. Second, 3D-ODN detects and localizes fruit trees within the map. Finally, cloth
simulation filtering (CSF) extracts terrain information, and the fruit-tree detections are
used to classify the point cloud into fruit trees, traversable terrain, and other obstacles.
A semantic map containing tree distribution, row structure, and access nodes is then
constructed. Because the method directly processes a three-dimensional point-cloud map,
it avoids reprojection of two-dimensional image semantics into three-dimensional space
and reduces the errors and computational overhead associated with additional calibration
and data conversion [118,119].
For path planning, the study used the Hough transform to detect tree columns, de-
termined inter-row corridors from their positions, and constructed a visibility graph as a
high-level navigation graph [120]. Because vehicles cannot easily turn within narrow rows,
one-way travel constraints were assigned within the corridors, and access points with opposite
orientations were generated on both sides of each tree column, enabling the robot to form
feasible routes around the rows. Experiments were performed in a greenhouse, an apple
orchard, and a litchi orchard [121]. With a 10 m × 10 m local map and a 128 × 128 feature map,
the method achieved an mIoU of 0.812, a feature-extraction time of 0.1681 s, and a prediction
time of 0.0268 s. Compared with the 2.13 s processing time of the VFH feature, the proposed
approach substantially improved efficiency while retaining good accuracy.
The semantic map was also compared with TEB-planner for path planning. In the
apple orchard, the visibility graph required an average of 4 ms, compared with 34 ms
for TEB-planner; the corresponding times were 6 and 45 ms in the greenhouse and 3 and
31 ms in the litchi orchard [122,123]. In addition to faster planning, the visibility graph
accounted for the vehicle orientation at the target and produced paths better aligned with
orchard-operation directions. These results show that path planning for orchard operations
should not simply apply a general-purpose local planner; tree-row structure, vehicle-
motion direction, and the sequence of operation visits should be incorporated into the map
representation. To enhance the robustness and adaptability of the improved algorithm in
the complex environment of the orchard, after the initial path is generated, the path quality

is becoming increasingly important for orchard robots operating on sloped, uneven, muddy,
or vegetation-covered ground. Recent approaches use three-dimensional LiDAR, stereo
vision, or RGB-D sensing to construct local elevation and traversability maps. Terrain
properties such as slope, surface roughness, step height, ground clearance, and disconti-

that may cause rollover, chassis collision, excessive vibration, or loss of traction. Semantic
information can further distinguish apparently similar geometric surfaces, such as firm

Terrain assessment based solely on external perception may nevertheless be unreliable
when vegetation conceals the ground or when soil conditions change after rainfall. There-
fore, newer approaches increasingly combine exteroceptive sensing with proprioceptive
information from IMUs, wheel encoders, motor current, suspension displacement, or con-
tact and force sensors. Disagreement between commanded and measured motion can be
used to estimate wheel slip, sinkage, or reduced traction. This information can update the
traversability map online and support adaptive velocity control, traction control, and local
replanning. Such perception–mobility feedback is particularly relevant to orchard spraying
and harvesting platforms, whose stability may be affected by high centers of gravity, liquid
movement, or changing payloads.
Risk-aware planning and control provide another direction for mobility in challenging
environments. Instead of selecting the geometrically shortest route, terrain-aware plan-
ners incorporate slope, roughness, predicted slip, energy consumption, vehicle stability,
and uncertainty into the path cost. Model predictive control and adaptive path-tracking
methods can then adjust travel speed, steering commands, and vehicle attitude according
to local terrain conditions. At the platform level, tracked mechanisms, independently
driven wheels, active or articulated suspension, and adjustable chassis structures may
improve mobility over uneven ground, although they also increase mechanical complexity,
power consumption, and maintenance requirements. Future studies should evaluate these
approaches through long-duration trials across different soil moisture conditions, slopes,
seasons, and orchard structures and should report not only localization and tracking errors
but also slip ratio, vibration, energy use, intervention frequency, stability margins, and
mission-completion reliability.
Semantic map construction converts a complex orchard into a structured representa-
tion that robots can understand, plan within, and execute. A limitation is that parameter
settings remain environment dependent. For example, local-map size, feature-map resolu-
tion, and ground-segmentation parameters all affect detection accuracy and computational
efficiency. Stronger adaptive capabilities are required for different tree species, row spacings,
and terrain conditions.
3.5. Robustness, Real-Time Performance, and Generalization of Navigation Systems
Orchard-robot navigation systems must ultimately operate reliably over long periods in
real environments; robustness, real-time performance, and generalization are therefore critical
evaluation criteria [124]. Robustness refers to the ability to maintain localization and path
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16168046

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 8046
18 of 39
tracking under changes in illumination, canopy occlusion, uneven terrain, and sensor noise.
Real-time performance requires perception, localization, planning, and control to be updated
promptly while the robot is moving. Generalization determines whether a system can transfer
from one orchard to different species, tree forms, and cultivation systems [125,126].
Existing experiments indicate that multi-sensor fusion is the primary means of improv-
ing robustness. The jujube-orchard spraying robot combined LiDAR, an IMU, EKF, and
AMCL to achieve centimeter-level path tracking. The orchard-weeding robot fused RGB-
D, two-dimensional LiDAR, visual-inertial odometry, wheel odometry, and AMCL and
maintained longitudinal and lateral errors below 10 cm at 0.5–1.5 m/s. The single-tree-row-
tracking robot used three-dimensional vision for trunk detection and LiDAR to trigger the
operation mechanism, enabling near-tree operation without GNSS. Collectively, these stud-
ies show that orchard navigation should not rely on a single information source but should
exploit the complementarity of geometric, semantic, and motion information [127–129].
Different studies have adopted different trade-offs to achieve real-time performance.
The lightweight YOLOv8p-seg model used by Sampurno et al. achieved 39.07 FPS, with a
perception-to-control delay of approximately 27.64–30.64 ms, making it suitable for low-
speed weeding navigation [47]. The three-channel point-cloud feature method of Pan et al.
required 0.1949 s; although slower than pure vision in frame rate, it provided efficient
point-cloud semantic mapping [57]. Visibility-graph path planning required only 3–6 ms
across different orchard environments, substantially less than the 31–45 ms required by
TEB-planner. These results indicate that real-time optimization depends not only on model
lightweighting but also on an appropriate representation of the task.
Generalization remains a weakness of current systems. Although Pan et al. validated
their semantic-mapping framework in a greenhouse, an apple orchard, and a litchi orchard,
they noted that local-map size and feature-map resolution may require adaptive adjustment
for different orchard environments and recommended extending the method to vineyards
and field crops. The single-tree-row-tracking method of Sampurno et al. is suitable for
weeding where rows are clear and the operation offset is fixed, but visual recognition and
actuation timing may become unstable under severe trunk occlusion, irregular rows, or
high-speed travel.
Autonomous navigation for orchard robots is progressing from localization and obstacle
avoidance toward scene understanding and operation-oriented path planning [130,131]. Fu-
ture research should strengthen three areas: improving the stability of multi-sensor fusion
systems under severe occlusion, sloped terrain, and dynamic obstacles; reducing the computa-
tional cost of semantic mapping and path planning to support edge-computing platforms; and
establishing cross-orchard, cross-species, and cross-season navigation benchmarks to promote
the transition from single-scene experiments to long-term deployment [132,133].
Overall, autonomous orchard-navigation technologies have developed along multiple
pathways, including multi-sensor fusion localization and semantic-map-driven planning,
with substantial differences in localization accuracy, hardware cost, and applicable scenar-
ios. Table 3 summarizes representative studies to clarify the technical characteristics and

4. Precision Operation Technologies for Orchard Robots
Precision operation execution is the ultimate implementation stage of environmental
perception, autonomous navigation, and task control in orchard robots [134]. Unlike ordi-
nary mobile platforms, operation-oriented orchard robots must not only reach target areas
but also perform specific tasks, including harvesting, spraying, pollination, and weeding,
within complex canopies, narrow rows, and unstable external environments. Operation
outcomes are jointly affected by target-recognition accuracy, robot-localization error, ac-
tuator response speed, manipulator-control precision, and environmental disturbances.
Precision operation should therefore not be interpreted as the design of an isolated actuator
but as a closed-loop system encompassing perception and localization, motion control,
operation mechanisms, and performance evaluation.
4.1. Perception, Localization, and Manipulator Control for Harvesting Robots
Harvesting is among the orchard-robot tasks with the most stringent requirements for
perception, localization, and manipulator control. Fruits are often occluded by branches
and leaves and distributed irregularly in three-dimensional space. The end effector must
approach, grasp, detach, and avoid obstacles within a constrained workspace [135,136]. If
three-dimensional fruit localization is inaccurate, stable harvesting cannot be achieved even
with a highly precise manipulator. Conversely, if manipulator positioning error is large, the
visually estimated target position will not coincide with the actual end-effector position [137].
For fruit localization, Bhattacharya et al. proposed a detection-segmentation-clustering
framework for three-dimensional apple localization in complex orchards. Apples were
first detected using a VLM, instance segmentation was then used to obtain fruit-pixel
regions, and the pixels were projected into three-dimensional space. DBSCAN point-cloud
clustering was subsequently applied to suppress depth noise [38]. The dataset contained
219 apples and covered indoor and outdoor scenes as well as real and simulated fruit. The
method achieved a mean absolute localization error of 7.7 mm, a 74.3% improvement over
the best baseline, and was deployed on a field harvesting robot. This study demonstrates
that harvesting perception should not stop at two-dimensional bounding boxes but should
output three-dimensional coordinates directly usable by the manipulator. However, the
limited sample size means that generalization across cultivars, dense fruit occlusion, and
seasonal variation requires further validation.
Regarding manipulator-control accuracy, Qiao et al. proposed a rigid-flexible coupled
error-calibration method for a long-reach apple-harvesting manipulator. They attributed
manipulator errors to manufacturing and assembly deviations, compliance deformation
induced by self-weight and payload, and nonstructural errors such as backlash [138]. A
rigid-flexible coupled pose-error model was established by integrating systematic error,
self-weight compliance error, and payload compliance error. Iterative least squares and a
residual neural network were then used for parameter identification and nonstructural-
error estimation, respectively. In simulation, the identification accuracy of the model
after removal of redundant parameters reached 93.478%. Physical experiments showed
that, after compensation, the mean position error and position RMSE were 0.278 and
0.202 mm, respectively, while the mean orientation error and orientation RMSE were 0.230
and 0.178 degrees, respectively. Relative to the uncalibrated system, the mean position error
and position RMSE decreased by 95.221% and 95.182%, and the mean orientation error
and orientation RMSE decreased by 94.080% and 93.541%, respectively. These findings
show that high-precision control of orchard-harvesting manipulators depends not only on
kinematic models but also on compensation for flexible deformation and nonstructural
errors introduced by lightweight structures. The method provides high-precision error
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16168046

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 8046
21 of 39
compensation for harvesting robots, although practical orchard harvesting still requires
integration with visual servoing, obstacle-avoidance planning, and adaptive end effectors.
Overall, the core of precision harvesting lies in coordination between three-dimensional
target localization and high-accuracy manipulator control. Future studies should evaluate the
relationships among fruit-localization error, manipulator-compensation error, and harvesting
success rather than assessing vision algorithms and mechanical systems separately.
4.2. Variable-Rate Application and Targeted Control for Spraying Robots
Spraying is one of the most widely implemented robotic tasks in orchard management.
Its objectives are to reduce pesticide waste, improve the uniformity of canopy deposition, and
decrease environmental contamination. Conventional continuous spraying generally uses
fixed flow and air rates and readily causes overapplication, spray drift, and off-target depo-
sition. Variable-rate spraying robots must instead adjust spraying parameters dynamically
according to canopy morphology, target volume, distance, and operation zone [139,140].
Hui Liu et al. developed an intelligent multivariable spraying robot for orchards
and nurseries. The system comprised a tracked mobile chassis, a navigation system, four
independent spraying units, four centrifugal nozzles, four air-assisted units, a 75 L chemical
tank, a three-dimensional LiDAR target-detection unit, and a central processing unit [40].
Three-dimensional LiDAR was used to acquire orchard point clouds, and an improved
DBSCAN algorithm extracted target plants. A multivariable spraying model was then
established from target position, shape, and volume to independently control spray flow
rate, air volume, droplet diameter, and spraying direction. The oscillating-fan mechanism
provided a maximum swing angle of 30 degrees, thereby increasing the spray range.
Field trials conducted with canopy diameters of 0.8–1.5 m, tree heights of 1.5–1.8 m,
and inter-tree spacing of 2 m showed that conventional spraying consumed 180–220 g of
liquid per nozzle, whereas multivariable spraying consumed only 30–35 g, corresponding
to an average pesticide-saving rate of 83%. The study further reported that the coverage
rate of multivariable spraying was lower than that of conventional spraying but higher
than that of flow-only variable-rate spraying. At the same liquid application rate, variable
air volume and adjustable droplet size improved penetration and reduced drift, resulting
in more uniform coverage than flow-only variable spraying. The principal contribution of
this study was the direct conversion of three-dimensional point-cloud target recognition
into coordinated control of flow, air volume, droplet size, and spray direction, thereby
establishing a relatively complete precision-spraying loop [141,142]. Remaining limitations
include the sensitivity of canopy-volume estimation and liquid-deposition patterns to
tree morphology, airflow, and sensor resolution. More accurate canopy modeling and
deposition feedback are needed.
4.3. Flower Detection and Precision Operation for Pollination Robots
Pollination has strict timeliness and accuracy requirements. In kiwifruit, for example,
flowers are distributed beneath the trellis, exhibit diverse orientations, and are frequently
occluded by the canopy. Pollination performance is also affected by wind speed, wind di-
rection, and spray volume [143]. Pollination robots must therefore simultaneously address
flower recognition, spray localization, quantitative liquid control, and compensation for
environmental disturbances [144].
Hao et al. developed a precision liquid-pollination robot for kiwifruit orchards. The
system used a CLIP-assisted automatic-annotation method to construct a kiwifruit-flower
detection dataset and trained a lightweight YOLO model for real-time recognition. A liquid-
spray control system and a wind-compensation system were also designed to adjust spray
offset and pollen dosage according to meteorological data [39]. The control architecture

compensation, and programmable logic controller (PLC)-based quantitative liquid spraying.
Wind-tunnel experiments were used to establish models of spray-center displacement and

Field trials conducted during 2024–2025 showed that the robot achieved commercial-

in pollen conservation and pollination precision but also identify operation efficiency as
a major constraint on large-scale application. The authors further noted that the current
manipulator arrangement produced an ineffective workspace approximately 220 mm in
diameter at the center of each operation zone, potentially leaving some flowers untreated.
Raised beds around kiwifruit roots and fruiting branches near the main trunk also increased
the difficulty of treating peripheral flowers. Overall, the study integrated flower detection,
localized manipulator spraying, and wind compensation into an engineering-complete
precision-pollination system, although manipulator coverage, mobile-operation efficiency,
and fruit-quality evaluation require further improvement.
The application of multi-objective optimization in spraying systems remains in its
infancy. Most existing studies aggregate multiple objectives into a single weighted target for
resolution; while this approach simplifies the computational process, it risks discarding crit-
ical information along the Pareto frontier. Establishing systematic trade-off analyses among
competing objectives—such as maximizing deposition, minimizing drift, and curtailing
economic costs—constitutes a crucial trajectory for future exploration.
4.4. Intra-Row Navigation and Actuator Control for Weeding Robots
Intra-row orchard weeding is a representative near-tree operation. Conventional mowers
cannot easily access the area immediately surrounding trunks, and residual weeds often
require manual removal. Conversely, poorly controlled mechanical weeding mechanisms
may damage trunks and roots [145,146]. Intra-row weeding robots must therefore coordinate
tree-row tracking, trunk detection, lateral-mechanism control, and safe obstacle avoidance.

Sampurno et al. designed a single-tree-row-tracking robot for intra-row orchard
weeding. The compact platform had a mass of 25 kg excluding payload, was powered by a
200 W portable battery, and used four-wheel independent drive with parallel front-wheel
steering to operate beneath low canopies and in confined spaces [47]. A three-dimensional
camera and a lightweight YOLO instance-segmentation model detected trunks in real time
and generated a path with a lateral offset of 0.8 m from the tree row. LiDAR simultaneously
triggered a laterally extending mowing mechanism, allowing the cutter to reach uncut
weeds between trees. Figure 5 shows the working mechanism of a real-time, laser-based

These results show that the key challenge in intra-row weeding is not simply trunk or weed
recognition but temporal matching among perception, control, and mechanical action. The
main advantage of the study was the integration of visual navigation and LiDAR-triggered
actuation on a single platform, providing a complete solution for intra-row weeding.
However, lateral-tracking error remained relatively large, and orchards with young trees or
narrow inter-tree spacing require more precise near-tree safety control.

4.5. Operation-Accuracy Calibration, Performance Evaluation, and System Integration
Operation accuracy in orchard robots is jointly determined by perceptual localiza-
tion, navigation error, mechanical execution, and environmental disturbances and cannot
be evaluated using a single algorithmic metric [147]. For harvesting robots, Qiao et al.
established a rigid-flexible coupled pose-error model for a long-reach harvesting manip-
ulator and incorporated manufacturing and assembly errors, self-weight deformation,
payload-induced compliance deformation, and nonstructural errors into the compensation
framework [138]. After compensation, the mean manipulator position error was 0.278 mm
and the mean orientation error was 0.230 degrees; relative to the uncalibrated system,
these errors decreased by 95.221% and 94.080%, respectively. These results indicate that
lightweight manipulators used in orchards must account for structural flexibility and load
variation. Otherwise, even accurate fruit localization may not enable the end effector to
reach the target reliably.
For spraying and pollination robots, evaluation should place greater emphasis on agri-
cultural outcomes. The multivariable spraying robot of Hui Liu et al. used three-dimensional
LiDAR to extract target-plant information and controlled flow rate, air volume, droplet diame-
ter, and spraying direction [40]. In field trials, conventional spraying consumed 180–220 g of
liquid per nozzle, whereas multivariable spraying consumed 30–35 g, yielding an average
pesticide-saving rate of 83%. The kiwifruit-pollination robot of Hao et al. integrated flower
detection, wind compensation, and quantitative liquid spraying on a single platform and
achieved a field fruit-set rate above 85% with pollen consumption of approximately 200 g/ha.
These results demonstrate that precision-operation evaluation should extend beyond detection
accuracy to include resource consumption and agronomic effectiveness [148].
Weeding robots further illustrate the influence of system response on operation
accuracy [149]. The intra-row weeding robot of Sampurno et al. achieved 84.04% lateral-
weeding accuracy at 0.5 m/s, but accuracy decreased to 76.85% when speed increased to
0.8 m/s, primarily because of delays in point-cloud processing and mechanism actuation.
Evaluation of intra-row operation should therefore consider not only path tracking but also
actuator response, the risk of missed treatment, and trunk safety.
Collectively, existing studies suggest that orchard-robot performance evaluation
should include module-level, operation-level, and production-level metrics. Module-level
metrics include detection accuracy, localization error, and control latency. Operation-level
metrics include harvesting success, pesticide savings, fruit-set rate, and weeding accuracy.
Production-level metrics include operation time per unit area, resource consumption, and
long-term stability [150]. An integrated assessment is more likely to provide a comprehen-
sive evaluation field applicability.
5. Current Challenges and Future Prospects
5.1. Robustness Challenges in Perception, Navigation, and Operation Under Complex
Orchard Conditions
Existing studies demonstrate substantial progress in target recognition, localization
and navigation, and operation execution; nevertheless, complex orchard environments
remain the principal barrier to stable deployment. Fruits, flowers, branches, trunks, and
weeds are frequently affected by occlusion, overlap, and illumination variation, resulting
in inconsistent perception across tree forms, cultivars, and seasons. Bhattacharya et al.
integrated a VLM, instance segmentation, and DBSCAN clustering for three-dimensional
apple localization and achieved a mean absolute error of 7.7 mm, a 74.3% improvement over
the best baseline. This result confirms that multimodal perception can improve localization
accuracy in complex environments. However, the dataset contained only 219 apples, and
generalization across orchards, cultivars, and severe occlusion remains to be validated.
https://doi.org/10.3390/app16168046

Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 8046
26 of 39
For navigation, canopy occlusion and terrain undulation reduce GNSS reliability and
compel robots to rely on onboard vision, LiDAR, IMUs, and odometry. The jujube-orchard
spraying robot fused LiDAR SLAM, an IMU, EKF, and AMCL and achieved a mean X-
axis deviation of 3.41 cm along a 15 m straight path and 1.8 cm along an S-shaped path,
demonstrating the effectiveness of multi-sensor fusion for cruising in densely planted or-
chards. However, long-term navigation stability has not been adequately validated in more
challenging environments involving sloped terrain, muddy ground, and dynamic obstacles.
Operation execution is likewise affected by environmental disturbances. The kiwifruit-
pollination robot combined flower detection, wind compensation, and quantitative liquid
spraying to achieve a field fruit-set rate above 85% with pollen consumption of approxi-
mately 200 g/ha. The study nevertheless reported an ineffective manipulator workspace
approximately 220 mm in diameter, which could leave some flowers untreated, and the
operation time remained excessive for large orchards. The weeding robot achieved 84.04%
lateral-weeding accuracy at 0.5 m/s, but accuracy decreased to 76.85% at 0.8 m/s, demon-
strating that perception-processing delay and actuator response directly affect operation
quality. Future research should therefore strengthen closed-loop coordination among
perception, navigation, and actuation to improve continuous operation in real orchards.
5.2. Bottlenecks in Multi-Robot Collaborative Scheduling and Intelligent Decision-Making
As orchard size increases, a single robot cannot satisfy the time-critical requirements
of many operations, making multi-robot and robot-UAV collaboration increasingly impor-
tant. However, most existing studies focus on static task allocation and simulation-based
optimization and do not adequately consider dynamic task changes, equipment failure,
battery replenishment, communication latency, or weather.
For multi-robot scheduling, Chen et al. investigated multi-trip task scheduling for
harvesting robots in smart orchards. They considered the relationships among robot self-
weight, real-time payload, and energy consumption and proposed an adaptive experience-
based discrete genetic algorithm. Tests on 18 newly generated orchard instances and 24
existing benchmark instances showed that the algorithm outperformed six advanced alter-
natives. The study realistically represented repeated unloading and re-entry of harvesting
robots, but validation remained predominantly instance- and simulation-based and did not
fully account for path blockage, robot failure, or temporary task changes in real orchards.
For robot-UAV collaboration, Wang et al. studied task allocation for pollination
robots and spraying UAVs in multi-orchard scenarios. They formulated a multi-orchard
robot-drone task-allocation (MORDTA) model that minimized the maximum completion
time across all orchards and proposed an improved evolutionary algorithm incorporating
problem-specific knowledge. A total of 500 instances were generated, of which 125 were
used for parameter calibration and 375 for testing; the proposed method outperformed
several comparison algorithms. The model incorporated the precedence constraint that
ground pollination must be completed before aerial spraying, providing strong scenario
specificity. However, it assumed constant robot and UAV speeds, normal equipment
operation, and negligible equipment-transport time, leaving a gap between the model and
practical deployment.
For intelligent decision-making, LLMs and VLMs offer a new route to natural-language
task planning. Salman et al. proposed the AGRI-BT robot framework, which used LoRA-
fine-tuned CodeLlama-7B to generate XML behavior trees and CLIP for visual-semantic
judgment. The system achieved 100% XML structural validity, 87.5% semantic correctness,
82.0% task alignment, and an overall weighted validation score of 91.4%. However, align-
ment on complex multistage tasks remained lower than structural validity, indicating that
foundation models in agricultural robotics must be combined with rule constraints, be-

havior trees, semantic maps, and safety-control mechanisms rather than directly replacing
verifiable robot-control systems. Multi-robot scheduling, ground-air collaboration, and
foundation-model-enabled decision-making constitute three major directions of collabo-
rative intelligence for orchard robots. Representative studies are compared in Table 5 to
summarize differences in problem formulation, solution methods, validation settings, and
current bottlenecks.
5.3. Data Benchmarks, System Integration, and Future Research Directions
Orchard-robot research currently lacks unified datasets, evaluation metrics, and long-
term field-testing benchmarks. Different studies use detection accuracy, localization error,
path deviation, pesticide-saving rate, fruit-set rate, weeding accuracy, and completion
time. Although these metrics characterize individual systems, they make cross-system
comparison difficult. Recent work has begun to emphasize data and benchmark devel-
opment. Chen et al. constructed 18 new orchard scheduling instances and combined
them with 24 existing benchmarks to evaluate multi-trip harvesting schedules. Orchard
size ranged from 20 m × 20 m to 70 m × 70 m, the number of trees ranged from 100 to
1225, and fruit-maturity ratios were set to 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8, providing a basis for algorithm
comparison. AGRI-BT robot established 1187 agricultural-task descriptions paired with
expert-validated behavior trees, and Hao et al. released code and data resources for their
kiwifruit-pollination robot. These efforts improve research reproducibility.
Robotic pruning, advanced manipulation, and Vision–Language–Action systems rep-
resent important emerging directions for orchard robotics. Compared with harvesting,
spraying, and weeding, robotic pruning requires a more detailed understanding of tree
architecture and agronomic objectives. A pruning robot must reconstruct branch topology,
identify appropriate pruning points, evaluate branch accessibility, generate collision-free
cutting trajectories, and accurately control the cutting tool without damaging retained
branches or nearby fruits. Variations in branch diameter, orientation, stiffness, and occlu-
sion further increase the difficulty of autonomous pruning. Future studies should therefore
integrate three-dimensional structural perception, agronomic decision rules, manipulator
planning, and cutting-quality evaluation within a unified closed-loop system.
Advanced manipulation is also essential for improving the adaptability of orchard
robots. Compliant control, force and tactile sensing, active perception, dexterous end
effectors, and coordinated multi-arm systems may enable robots to interact more safely
and effectively with fragile, deformable, and irregularly distributed biological targets. In
particular, the combination of visual feedback and contact information could help compen-
sate for localization errors and unexpected target motion during harvesting, pruning, and
flower manipulation. However, the increased mechanical and computational complexity of
such systems must be balanced against real-time performance, maintenance requirements,
and field reliability.
Beyond the use of LLMs and VLMs for semantic understanding and task planning,
emerging Vision–Language–Action systems provide a potential framework for mapping
visual observations and natural-language instructions directly to executable robotic actions.
Such systems may support more flexible task specification, cross-task knowledge transfer,
and adaptive decision-making in changing orchard environments. Nevertheless, orchard-
oriented VLA research remains at an early stage. Major challenges include the scarcity
of orchard-specific action datasets, domain shifts across cultivars, seasons, and orchard
structures, high computational requirements, limited interpretability of generated actions,
and the need to guarantee physical safety. Closed-loop field validation and the integration
of explicit geometric, agronomic, and safety constraints will therefore be necessary before
VLA systems can be reliably deployed in practical orchard operations.

Digital twins, realistic simulation environments, and sim-to-real learning are becoming
important tools for accelerating the development of orchard robots. A digital twin can provide
a virtual representation of the orchard, robotic platform, sensors, actuators, and operational
processes and may be updated using data collected from field robots and environmental-
monitoring systems. Such a framework can support robot design, mission planning, prediction
of operational performance, maintenance analysis, and evaluation of alternative control or
scheduling strategies before physical deployment. For large orchards and multi-robot systems,
digital twins may also facilitate fleet-level monitoring, resource allocation, and the analysis of
interactions among robots, crops, terrain, and environmental conditions.
Realistic simulation environments provide a complementary approach for developing
and testing robotic systems under repeatable and controllable conditions. Virtual orchards
can represent variations in tree architecture, canopy density, fruit and flower distribution,
illumination, weather, terrain slope, soil conditions, occlusion, and sensor noise. They can
therefore support large-scale generation of training data and systematic evaluation of per-
ception, navigation, manipulation, and collaborative-planning algorithms without the cost,
seasonal restrictions, or safety risks of continuous field experimentation. Simulation is particu-
larly valuable for rare or hazardous scenarios, such as localization failure, severe wheel slip,
manipulator collision, communication loss, and interactions between multiple robots.
However, models trained or validated in simulation often experience reduced perfor-
mance after deployment because virtual scenes cannot fully reproduce the visual appear-
ance, physical properties, and dynamic variability of real orchards. Sim-to-real learning
methods, including domain randomization, synthetic-data generation, transfer learning,
system identification, and progressive adaptation using limited field data, may help reduce
this gap. For manipulation tasks, accurate simulation of branch flexibility, fruit detachment,
contact forces, cutting processes, and end-effector interactions remains especially challeng-
ing. Similarly, terrain simulation must account for soil deformation, vegetation cover, wheel
slip, sinkage, and changing moisture conditions. Future research should therefore integrate
digital twins and simulation-based learning with hardware-in-the-loop experiments, real
sensor data, and staged field trials. Simulation should be treated as a tool for accelerating
development and risk reduction rather than as a substitute for long-term, multi-season
field validation.
Future orchard-robot research should prioritize three directions. First, multimodal
datasets should be established across crops, seasons, and orchards, covering images, point
clouds, semantic maps, robot trajectories, and operation outcomes. Second, a unified
system-level evaluation framework should integrate perception accuracy, navigation stabil-
ity, execution success, resource consumption, and agronomic effectiveness. Third, long-term
field validation of complete robotic systems should be strengthened, with particular at-
tention to stability under dust, post-rain ground conditions, canopy occlusion, dense fruit
clusters, and continuous operation. Orchard robots can progress from prototype valida-
tion toward scalable application only when algorithmic performance, operation quality,
and production benefits are evaluated together. Table 6 summarizes current bottlenecks
and potential research breakthroughs across six dimensions: environmental perception,
autonomous navigation, precision operation, multi-robot collaboration, intelligent decision-
making, and evaluation benchmarks.

specific evidence rather than as directly comparable benchmarks. The reviewed studies
differ substantially in crop species and cultivars, orchard architecture, sensing configuration,
dataset size, laboratory or field setting, illumination and occlusion conditions, terrain
complexity, robot travel speed, validation duration, and metric definitions.

5.4. Technology Readiness and Barriers to Commercial Deployment
The transition of orchard robots from research prototypes to commercially deployable
systems depends not only on algorithmic accuracy and operation performance but also on
technological maturity, economic feasibility, human factors, regulatory compliance, and
organizational capacity. The studies reviewed in this article exhibit substantially differ-
ent levels of maturity. To avoid assigning unsupported numerical technology-readiness
levels, the available evidence can be qualitatively divided into four stages: algorithm- or
simulation-level validation, controlled-environment prototype validation, single-orchard
field-prototype validation, and multi-orchard or long-term pre-commercial validation.
According to this framework, many fruit-, flower-, branch-, and trunk-perception meth-
ods have reached the controlled-environment or single-orchard prototype stage. Several
LiDAR-, vision-, and multisensor-based navigation systems have also been tested using
integrated mobile platforms under real orchard conditions. Nevertheless, their robustness
across cultivars, seasons, orchard architectures, terrain conditions, and prolonged operating
periods remains insufficiently demonstrated. Harvesting, pollination, spraying, and intra-row
weeding systems have progressed from isolated algorithm validation toward integrated field
prototypes, but their commercial readiness is still constrained by cycle time, actuator durability,
operation coverage, maintenance requirements, and the limited duration and scale of field
validation. Multi-robot coordination, robot–UAV scheduling, and foundation-model-assisted
decision-making generally remain at earlier maturity levels because much of the current evi-
dence is derived from optimization benchmarks, simulations, or constrained demonstrations
rather than long-term closed-loop operation in real orchards.
Economic feasibility is another major barrier to adoption. The total cost of an or-
chard robot extends beyond its initial purchase price and includes sensors, manipulators,
batteries, charging or refilling facilities, software, routine calibration, maintenance, com-
ponent replacement, technical support, and operator training. High-value sensors and
precision actuators may improve performance but also increase acquisition and repair
costs. Furthermore, orchard operations are strongly seasonal. A robot designed for only
one short-duration task may remain unused during much of the year, reducing equip-
ment utilization and delaying the recovery of capital investment. Consequently, return

on investment depends on orchard area, crop value, labor availability, annual operating

and medium-sized producers, who may have limited capital, technical personnel, repair
facilities, and access to specialized after-sales services.
Improving commercial feasibility will therefore require not only higher technical
performance but also modular and maintainable system design. Interchangeable opera-
tion modules may allow one mobile platform to support inspection, spraying, weeding,
transportation, or other tasks at different times of the production cycle, thereby increasing
seasonal utilization. Simplified calibration procedures, standardized interfaces, accessible
replacement parts, remote diagnostics, and local maintenance support may also reduce
downtime and lifecycle cost. Shared ownership, leasing, cooperative operation, and robot-
as-a-service models may provide alternative adoption pathways when individual farms
cannot justify purchasing and maintaining complete robotic systems. However, the eco-
nomic effectiveness of these models requires farm-level and region-specific validation
rather than inference from algorithmic performance alone.
Human factors and occupational safety must also be considered during system devel-
opment. Orchard robots may operate near workers, trees, irrigation equipment, vehicles,
and animals, while spraying robots additionally involve potential exposure to agricultural
chemicals. Practical systems should therefore provide reliable obstacle and human detec-
tion, emergency stopping, manual override, safe-state transitions, clear status indicators,
and procedures for recovery after sensor, actuator, communication, or localization failure.
Operator interfaces should be understandable to users without specialist robotics training,
and routine activities such as battery replacement, refilling, cleaning, calibration, and fault
diagnosis should be designed to minimize physical and cognitive burden. Field validation
should evaluate not only robot performance but also operator workload, required training
time, intervention frequency, near-miss events, and the safety of maintenance procedures.
Compatibility with existing orchard infrastructure constitutes an additional deploy-
ment constraint. Row spacing, canopy form, turning space, slope, ground condition,
irrigation pipelines, support structures, wireless coverage, and access to electricity or refill-
ing stations vary substantially among orchards. A robot that performs well in a modern
high-density orchard may be unsuitable for older orchards with irregular rows, narrow pas-
sages, steep terrain, or heterogeneous tree architecture. Commercial systems must therefore
either adapt to existing orchards or clearly specify the infrastructural conditions required
for deployment. Excessive dependence on orchard reconstruction would substantially
increase adoption costs, particularly for small producers.
Regulatory and organizational issues may further delay commercialization. Depend-
ing on the platform and operation, orchard robots may be subject to requirements concern-
ing agricultural-machinery safety, autonomous vehicle operation, pesticide application,
UAV flight, electromagnetic compatibility, data protection, and occupational health. Lia-
bility must also be clarified when damage results from perception errors, software failure,
communication loss, incorrect operator configuration, or interactions between multiple
autonomous machines. Because these requirements vary across jurisdictions, commer-
cialization requires cooperation among robot developers, orchard managers, machinery
manufacturers, service providers, insurers, and regulatory authorities. Traceable opera-
tion logs, defined human-supervision responsibilities, risk-assessment procedures, and
verifiable fail-safe mechanisms will be important for certification and accountability.
Overall, current orchard-robot research is advancing more rapidly in algorithmic
capability than in lifecycle cost assessment, maintainability, human-centered design, regu-
latory preparation, and organizational integration. Future field studies should therefore

6. Conclusions
This review examined the development of smart orchard robots through an integrated
technological chain comprising environmental perception, autonomous navigation, precision
operation, and collaborative decision-making. Its principal contribution is the establishment of
an engineering-oriented framework that connects sensing and semantic understanding with
robot mobility, task execution, and system-level coordination. Rather than treating harvesting,
spraying, pollination, weeding, and multi-robot operation as isolated applications, the review
demonstrates that their performance depends on the coordinated operation of perception,
localization, planning, actuation, and evaluation components.
The reviewed evidence indicates that different technological areas have reached
substantially different levels of maturity. Selected fruit-, flower-, trunk-, and canopy-
perception methods have been integrated with robotic platforms, while LiDAR-, vision-,
and multisensor-based navigation systems have achieved credible, although mostly site-
specific, field validation in GNSS-constrained orchards. Semantic mapping has also been
demonstrated in greenhouse, apple-orchard, and litchi-orchard environments. Among
operation technologies, variable-rate spraying and wind-compensated pollination have
produced measurable field outcomes, and several harvesting and intra-row weeding sys-
tems have progressed to integrated prototype demonstrations. Nevertheless, most of these
results were obtained using limited crops, orchard structures, seasons, operating speeds,
or validation periods. They should therefore be interpreted as evidence of task-specific
feasibility rather than proof of broad commercial readiness.
By contrast, heterogeneous multi-robot coordination, robot–UAV scheduling, large-
scale collaborative operation, and LLM- or VLM-assisted task planning remain predomi-
nantly exploratory. Much of the supporting evidence is derived from optimization bench-
marks, simulation, constrained datasets, or controlled demonstrations. These approaches
show potential for improving task allocation, semantic reasoning, and system flexibility,
but their reliability under communication loss, dynamic obstacles, equipment failure, en-
vironmental change, and long-term closed-loop operation has not yet been sufficiently
established. Foundation models should therefore be regarded as high-level enabling tools
rather than verified replacements for deterministic and safety-critical robotic control.
Based on the reviewed evidence, three priorities are particularly important. First, cross-
orchard, cross-cultivar, and cross-season datasets and standardized evaluation protocols
are required to support reproducible comparison and assess environmental generalization.
Second, future systems should prioritize long-term closed-loop integration of perception,
navigation, and actuation, including uncertainty estimation, real-time edge deployment,
failure detection, safe-state transition, and human intervention mechanisms. Third, col-
laborative and foundation-model-assisted systems must progress from simulation and
constrained demonstrations to physical validation involving heterogeneous platforms, real-
istic communication and energy constraints, dynamic task reassignment, and sufficiently
long operation in real orchards.
Overall, the transition from successful prototypes to dependable orchard-robot systems
will require a shift from isolated algorithm optimization toward system-level robustness,
standardised evaluation, operational safety, maintainability, and sustained field validation.
