A B S T R A C T
Deep learning has achieved promising performance for fruit and vegetable image analysis, by possessing 
strong representation power, and providing resilient generalization and broad transferability on large-scale 
data for classification, detection, and segmentation tasks, which is indispensable role in optimizing agricultural 
practices. This comprehensive survey reviews over 270 recent studies, offering a deep exploration of the 
key techniques and strategies, fundamental properties, and advancements and future directions according to 
different categories of deep learning methods for fruit and vegetable image analysis. Furthermore, this paper 
outlines the novelty and concept of fruit and vegetable image analysis, summarizes publicly available datasets, 
evaluation metrics, and discusses successful applications in disease detection, quality grading, yield estimation, 
localization, and multiple application integration. The survey emphasizes the need for processing large-scale 
datasets and exploring the potential of efficient deep learning for enhancing real-time applications and specific 
tasks. By comprehensively comparing and analyzing the fundamental attributes of the fruit and vegetable image 
analysis methods from a fresh perspective, this survey reveals the commonalities and disparities of divert 
techniques and guides researchers and practitioners toward developing more efficient and accurate solutions.

1. Introduction
Fruit and vegetable image analysis, involving the classification, de-
tection, and segmentation of agricultural imagery, is a critical compo-
nent of agricultural machine vision. This technology plays a significant 
role throughout the cultivation process of fruits and vegetables, such 
us cultivation practices, harvesting, transportation logistics, and sales 
operations. In recent years, deep learning-based methods for fruit and 
vegetable image analysis have gained widespread attention due to 
their unique advantages, such as reliability, objectivity, accuracy, cost-
effectiveness, and reduced time consumption. These learning mecha-
nisms have been extensively applied in various practical agricultural 
scenarios, demonstrating their broad applicability and effectiveness in 
real-world contexts.
Despite the increasing adoption of deep learning techniques in this 
domain, comprehensive reviews that provide a detailed overview of 
the current state-of-the-art and future trends are still lacking. Several 
recent surveys have focused on specific aspects, such as pest and disease 
detection (Liu and Wang, 2021; Li et al., 2021a; Gupta and Tripathi, 
2024), feature evaluation (Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018), qual-
ity evaluation (Chuquimarca et al., 2024), localization (Tang et al., 
2020; Fu et al., 2020b), and detection (Dalal et al., 2025; Chakrabarty 
et al., 2026) in the context of fruit and vegetable analysis. However, the 
emphasis of these review is paid on the deep learning technology for 
a single application scenario of fruit and vegetable image processing. 
The absence of in-depth technological analysis, coverage of recent 
advances, and discussion of emerging application trends leaves readers 
without a clear understanding of the current state and future direc-
tions of deep learning in fruit tree image processing. For this reason, 
this study conducted a systematic and comprehensive investigation 
on deep learning based fruit and vegetable image analysis methods, 
aimed at providing clear insights into the progress and potential future 
directions, enabling researchers to comprehensively grasp the concepts, 
categories, applications, and development directions of deep learn-
ing technology in fruit and vegetable image processing. The main 
contribution of this paper can be summarized as follows.
(1) Provide an accurate definition and basic principles of fruit and 
vegetable image analysis, introduce the detailed strategies of various 
deep learning methods for fruit and vegetable image analysis, and 
present diverse dataset and evaluation metrics for fruit and vegetable 
images.
(2) Sort out the commonalities and disparities of divert techniques 
of fruit and vegetable image analysis, discuss the fundamental proper-
ties in disease detection, quality grading, yield estimation, localization, 
and multiple application integration, as well as their development trend 
in these applied scenarios.
(3) Summarize the main application status and outline the applica-
tion trends of fruit and vegetable images analysis.
(4) Identify the development trends and goals of fruit and vegetable 
image analysis.
Specially, we conduct a comprehensive survey of over 270 research 
papers focused on deep learning-based fruit and vegetable image anal-
ysis, the detailed statistical information of these research papers is 
presented in Fig. 1. Preliminary selection of research papers from the 
past decade using keyword matching and limiting literature sources. 
Then select deep learning papers on fruit and vegetable image process-
ing. To comprehensively collect the relevant literature, we employed a 
combination of search key terms such as ‘‘fruit and vegetable’’, ‘‘deep 
learning’’, ‘‘classification’’, ‘‘detection’’, and ‘‘segmentation’’ along with 
alternative spellings and Boolean operators (AND, OR) to ensure com-
prehensive coverage. Convenient for more precise analysis from dif-
ferent perspectives, we also limit the period of publication time of 
the literature to be within the latest ten years, language to English 
literature. In addition, the paper must be published in influential jour-
nals and conferences, and the literature sources involve IEEE, Elsevier, 
Google Scholar, MDPI, Springer, and others.
The rest of this survey is organized as follows: Section 2 intro-
duces the definition of fruit and vegetable image analysis. Section 3 
summarizes publicly available datasets and existing evaluation metrics. 
Section 4 comparative analysis is provided on various approaches for 
fruit and vegetable image analysis related to classification, detection 
and segmentation task. Section 5 discusses specific applications of fruit 
and vegetable image analysis across different tasks. Finally, Section 6 
offers concluding remarks and highlights potential research trends for 
deep learning-based fruit and vegetable image analysis in the future 
research exploration. The overall structure of the survey is depicted in 
Fig. 2.

2.2. Definition of fruit and vegetable image analysis
Fruit and vegetable image analysis is an application of digital image 
processing technology in agriculture, designed to meet specific needs in 
production, transportation, and marketing. Generally, the methodolo-
gies used in fruit and vegetable image analysis often rely on human 
experience, rather than solely on mathematical definitions. Similar to 
other machine vision tasks, fruit and vegetable image analysis can be 
categorized into image classification, segmentation, and detection. As 
illustrated the types of tasks involved in fruit and vegetable image 
analysis in Fig. 3, these tasks typically involve identifying ‘‘what’’ is 
present in the image, delineating its spatial extent or ‘‘where’’, and 
understanding the characteristics or ‘‘how’’ it relates to the overall 
scene.
Classification is a fundamental aspect of fruit and vegetable image 
analysis, aiming to accurately determine the categories of these objects. 
It involves two main aspects: categorizing different types of fruits 
and vegetables and grading the same types based on their quality 
or attributes. The classification task is capable of providing valuable 
insights into the question of ‘‘what’’, contributing to identifying and 
differentiating various fruits and vegetables based on their specific 
characteristics and attributes.
Detection in fruit and vegetable image analysis, refers to localizing 
target objects within an image and simultaneously categorizing them. 
This process necessitates fine-grained classification to ensure precise 
identification of each fruit or vegetable type. Detection tasks necessitate 
pre-labeling the categories of fruits and vegetables and providing the 
specific locations of small or overlapping targets, aimed at answering 
the questions of ‘‘where’’ and ‘‘what’’.
Segmentation is crucial in fruit and vegetable image analysis as 
it performs the separation of branches, leaves, flowers, and fruits. Its 
primary purpose is to accurately monitor and measure the objects of 
interest within an image, providing objective information about them. 
Segmentation is also commonly used for more advanced tasks such as 
pose estimation and yield estimation. By answering the questions of 
‘‘where’’ and ‘‘how’’, segmentation task is capable of providing critical 
insights into the spatial distribution and structural characteristics of 
fruits and vegetables.

3. Datasets and evaluation metrics
3.1. Datasets
Fruit and vegetable datasets are fundamental to deep learning tech-
niques, as the quantity and quality of data directly impact the perfor-
mance of models. Training deep learning models with small datasets 
often leads to overfitting, whereas large datasets facilitate to enhance 
model generalization and accuracy. To this end, this section provides 
a systematic overview of fruit and vegetable datasets available in the 
publicly domain, with analysis covering their nomenclature, release 
date, scale, object categories, and acquisition methodologies.
To support research in this field, available datasets are system-
atically categorized according to their primary application domains, 
including classification, quality grading, yield estimation, target local-
ization, and disease detection. Such categorization not only provides 
a clearer understanding of dataset suitability for different tasks but 
also highlights their potential value in addressing specific research 
challenges. A comprehensive summary of these datasets is presented 
in Table 1.
Current public fruit and vegetable image datasets exhibit fundamen-
tal limitations – including restricted scale, limited taxonomic diversity, 
and uniform acquisition contexts – that hinder robust model devel-
opment. Single-cultivar datasets (e.g., PFuji-Size, MangoNet) prevent 
cross-varietal analysis, while controlled-environment acquisitions fail 
to capture the photometric complexity, occlusions, and environmental 
noise inherent to field conditions. These constraints critically limit 
model generalizability, causing performance deterioration in real-world 
applications. To advance agricultural computer vision, the commu-
nity requires either: (1) large-scale, multimodal, and geographically 
distributed benchmark datasets, or (2) integration of synthetic data 
augmentation, domain adaptation, and federated learning techniques 
to systematically address data scarcity and domain shift challenges.
3.2. Evaluation metrics
Methods for fruit and vegetable image analysis are mainly catego-
rized into classification, segmentation, and detection tasks. For each 
task, the selection of appropriate evaluation metrics is essential to 
ensure a fair and reliable assessment of model performance. In practice, 
a wide range of metrics has been employed in this field, including 
accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, L2 error, Intersection over Union 
(IoU), Mean Intersection over Union (MIoU), Root Mean Squared Error 
(RMSE), Mean Relative Error (MRE), mean Average Precision (mAP), 
and Dice coefficient (Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018; Koirala 
et al., 2019; Ukwuoma et al., 2022) , and the specific metrics used for 
each task are summarized in Table 2.

4.1.1. Basic CNN classification models
Basic CNN models, including AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al., 2017), 
VGG (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2014), and GoogLeNet (Szegedy et al., 
2015), provided the basis for subsequent advances in deep learning-
based image classification. Characterized by relatively simple architec-
tures and stable performance, these models have been widely adopted 
and adapted for fruit and vegetable classification with significant im-
provements in accuracy compared to traditional methods.
AlexNet. For fruit and vegetable classification, AlexNet is the ear-
liest deep learning model that has achieved better performance than 
traditional methods. Many scholars have modified AlexNet to be more 
favorable for fruit and vegetable feature extraction. For example, Liu 
(2020) proposed Interfruit network, an enhanced AlexNet with resid-
ual blocks and Inception modules incorporated, which demonstrated 
excellent performance in classifying diverse fruit images, achieving 
an impressive classification accuracy of 93.17%. To enhance accuracy 
and minimize training time, Dong et al. (2021) improved AlexNet 
by modifying its fully connected layer and utilizing ImageNet for 
pre-training. In strawberry disease classification task, the modified 
AlexNet model exhibited a significant improvement of 3.55% over the 
baseline, while reducing average training time by 44.3% and com-
pressing the model size to 12% of its original size. In the field of 
vegetable classification, Zhu et al. (2018) performed the classification 
task by employing image augmentation techniques to train AlexNet. 
The experiments achieved a classification accuracy of 92.1%, markedly 
surpassing the performance of the two traditional machine learning 
methods. Consequently, AlexNet has gained widespread adoption due 
to its superior performance in classifying small datasets. However, it is 
unfortunate that all the aforementioned models based on AlexNet suffer 
from issues such as inadequate sample size, limited generalization 
ability, and subpar accuracy. The root cause lies in the utilization of 
an 11 × 11 convolution kernel, which results in an excessive number 
of parameters. Consequently, the model exhibits poor classification 
performance when confronted with large datasets and intricate images.
VGG. In the light of the simplicity, ease of use, and superior 
performance of VGG over AlexNet. Researchers have employed VGG 
directly or utilized transfer learning-based VGG for fruit and vegetable 
classification tasks to enhance accuracy even further. Zeng (2017) 
adopted VGG to classify a mixed dataset of 26 vegetables and fruits 
with 95.6% of accuracy achieving. By adding five BN layers to the fully 
connected layers fc6 and fc7 of VGG, a different improved VGG model 
is proposed, which achieved an accuracy of 96.5% in the decile class 
experiment of vegetable. In virtue of dense and flatten layers, Sharmila 
et al. (2022) constructed another improved VGG16, which obtained 
97% classification accuracy. Similar research work demonstrated im-
pressive performance in literature (Das, 2023), in which they employed 
pre-trained VGG16 and VGG19 to classify bell pepper.
From the above analysis, VGG has demonstrated strong generaliza-
tion ability for image classification, though its training speed remains 
relatively limited. In response, transfer learning-based variants of VGG 
have achieved faster convergence and higher accuracy. For exam-
ple, Altaheri et al. (2019) employed pre-trained AlexNet and VGG16 for 
date bunch maturity classification, reporting accuracies of 96.51% and 
99.01%, respectively. Similarly, Osako et al. (2020) fine-tuned a VGG 
model pre-trained on ImageNet for lychee variety recognition, yielding 
an accuracy of 98.33%. Overall, VGG consistently surpasses AlexNet in 
classification accuracy by a considerable margin. Notably, it is worth to 
mention that due to its larger number of learnable parameters, VGG16 
requires a higher number of iterations to converge during the training 
phase. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that the VGG-based 
models mentioned above encounter challenges when dealing with lim-
ited datasets and a single representation of data content. Therefore, 
there is still a need to further improve the classification performance 
of VGG for complex images and large datasets.
GoogLeNet/Inception. GoogLeNet, also known as Inception V1, is

and Inception modules. Its notable advantage lies in its ability to

within the Inception modules, which leads to improved representation 
learning and enhanced classification performance. For instance, Pande 
et al. (2019) trained the Inception V3 classification model by using 
transfer learning with 90% of the accuracy for fruit classification. 
Based on the Inception modules, Nikhitha et al. (2019) proposed a

identified eight different phenological stages of apple based on transfer 
learning and the Inception V3 model and employed data augmen-

which effectively mitigated overfitting issues, resulting in a remarkable 
classification accuracy of 98%. Notably, this accuracy surpassed that

4.1.2. Residual-based CNN classification models
Residual-based CNN models, such as ResNet (He et al., 2016) and 
DenseNet (Huang et al., 2017), have been developed to address issues 
like gradient disappearance and overfitting in deep networks. These 
models have shown significant improvements in classification accuracy 
and feature representation, making them highly effective for fruit and 
vegetable image classification.
ResNet. To mitigate the issues of gradient disappearance and over-
fitting of ResNet, researchers have employed residual structures in 
the design of classification models by increasing the depth of the 
model to improve its performance. Comparative studies have been 
conducted, and the experiments consistently demonstrate that the en-
hanced ResNet achieves the highest classification accuracy when com-
pared to other CNN models. For example, Ponce et al. (2019) applied 
ResNet to olives classification task, in which the comparative experi-
ments with six CNN models illustrated that the Inception-ResNetV2 has 
the highest overall classification accuracy. ResNet18 architecture also 
can be utilized by Li et al. (2021c) for orange classification. Through 
comparative experiments involving five other CNN models, ResNet18 
demonstrated superior performance on the private orange dataset. 
ResNet also proved effective for freshness classification, where four 
CNN models were employed. In this experiment, ResNet18 achieved an 
impressive classification accuracy of 96% (Arunachalaeshwaran et al., 
2022). By fusing ResNet50 and ResNet101, Kang and Gwak (2022) 
successfully conducted the classification of hog plum fruits, accurately 
determining both their freshness and category. The freshness classifi-
cation achieved an impressive average accuracy of 98.50%, while the 
category classification exhibited a commendable average accuracy of 
97.43%. For the classification of multi-category fruit and vegetable, 
ResNet also performs well. For example, Wu et al. (2022) proposed 
an improved ResNet101 to classify fruit leaf diseases. By incorporating 
an attention mechanism, the feature extraction capability is enhanced, 
resulting in a notable improvement in classification accuracy. Specif-
ically, the model achieved an impressive accuracy of 85.90% when 
classifying 25 different types of diseases across six distinct plant species. 
Driven by the limitations in terms of speed and clarity associated with 
manually collected images, researchers have initiated investigations 
into the utilization of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) images as an 
alternative data source. For example, Tetila et al. (2020) evaluated 
the classification performance for soybean pests based on UAV images, 
achieving 93.82% accuracy with the Resnet50. Li et al. (2022) proposed 
an improved ResNet50 to classify freeze-tolerant rape and frostbitten 
oilseed rape based on UAV images with an accuracy of 93.33%. Though 
ResNet effectively addresses the issue of gradient disappearance and 
offers deeper network architectures, it is accompanied by drawbacks 
such as slow training speed and a tendency to overfit when the network 
depth increases.
DenseNet. Due to its impressive feature reuse capability, DenseNet 
is proved to be an effective solution in mitigating overfitting issues. 
In a study conducted by Verma and Verma (2022), DenseNet was 
utilized for fruit classification, yielding an impressive classification 
accuracy of 99.03%. Additionally, DenseNet exhibits remarkable classi-
fication accuracy even with unbalanced datasets. For example, Herman 
et al. (2021) applied DenseNet for seven maturity analysis of oil palm 
fruits. The experimental results implied that compared with previous 
methods, DenseNet can achieve a better classification performance of 
86% on the unbalanced dataset. In order to address the challenge of 
classifying fruit subspecies, Xue et al. (2020) introduced an innovative 
attention-based DenseNet approach, which successfully achieved a clas-
sification accuracy of 95.86%. As an improved DenseNet, it is proposed 
by Liu et al. (2020) to detect tomato maturity in complex environments 
on unbalanced datasets, and can reach an accuracy of 91%. Different 
from above approaches, Khan et al. (2022) employed DenseNet201 for 
the purpose of disease classification in cucumber leaves. Additionally, 
they applied the entropy elm technique, a method based on entropy and 
extreme learning machines, to enhance the classification performance 
in that the combined approach yielded an impressive accuracy rate of 
98.48%.
Residual-based models deepen networks via skip connections, mit-
igating gradient vanishing and boosting classification accuracy. How-
ever, information redundancy and low efficiency still curb their perfor-
mance on fruit and vegetable images classification.

4.2. Detection methods
Detection methods in fruit and vegetable image analysis are crucial 
for accurately localizing and classifying objects within images. These 
methods can be broadly categorized into two main types: two-stage 
detection models, exemplified by Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2017), and 
one-stage detection models, exemplified by SSD (Liu et al., 2016) and 
YOLO (Redmon et al., 2016). Each type of these models has its own 
strengths and is suited to different applications scenarios, these Deep

4.2.1. Two-stage detection models of fruit and vegetable
Two-stage detection approaches refer to a type of object detection 
method that involves two distinct stages: region proposal and object 
classification. In these approaches, the first stage is responsible for 
generating potential regions of interest (ROIs) in an image that may 
contain different objects. These proposals are typically generated using 
techniques like selective search or region proposal networks (RPNs). In 
the second stage, the proposed regions are fed into a classifier to deter-
mine the presence of objects and classify them into different categories. 
This stage involves features extraction from the proposed regions and 
classification task by recognizing the objects using different models 
like support vector machines (SVM) or convolution neural networks 
(CNNs). Two-stage detection approaches are known for their accuracy 
and robustness in detecting objects in complex scenes. They have been 
widely used in various computer vision applications, involving object 
detection in images and videos.
Currently, most fruit and vegetable detection models relied on two-
stage detection approaches such as Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN. 
Faster R-CNN, being the pioneering model for fruit and vegetable detec-
tion, was introduced in 2016. Sa et al. (2016) proposed a multimodal 
Faster R-CNN model for fruit detection by utilizing transfer learning 
to extract combined features from both RGB and near-infrared (NIR) 
images, and the detection accuracy can reach to 83.8%. Similarly, 
based on the Faster R-CNN, Wang et al. (2019) designed a window 
scaling method to match targets for fruit and vegetable detection, 
which was capable of automatically separating fruit and vegetable 
from the background with a detection accuracy of 96.33%. Further-
more, Faster R-CNN has also been utilized for multi-category detection, 
demonstrating a remarkable detection accuracy of no less than 85.8% 
specifically for apple detection (Gao et al., 2020a). To address the 
detection of multiple fruit categories, Wan and Goudos (2020) proposed 
an enhanced version of Faster R-CNN, resulting in improved detection 
capabilities since their approach achieved a mean average precision 
(mAP) of over 91%. In order to implement Faster R-CNN-based mod-
els into real environments, some researchers have pursued detection 
speed. Bargoti and Underwood (2017) proposed an improved Faster R-
CNN with 58 ms of the average detection speed for apples, mangoes, 
and pears in the orchard. Building upon Faster R-CNN, Zhong et al. 
(2022) proposed an improved approach by replacing RoI pooling with

RoI alignment to accurately pinpoint cluster peppers, with experiments 
demonstrating a detection time of 42 ms per frame.
Furthermore, Faster R-CNN can be extended to Mask R-CNN by 
incorporating a segmentation branch, which performs object detection 
and segmentation task for the target location in a unified frame-
work. Since then, diverse enhanced versions of Mask R-CNN have 
been specifically designed and tailored for fruit and vegetable de-
tection. By adapting Mask R-CNN, Shi and Wu (2019) pioneered a 
unified framework for tomato defect inspection, seamlessly integrating 
detection, classification, and high-resolution segmentation. Leveraging 
a dual-input Mask R-CNN, Chakraborty and Deka (2025) harnessed

multimodal data extracted from three sub modules: local–local feature

Based on the aforementioned summary, it is evident that the two-

face difficulties in detecting obscured targets, and their speed is rel-
atively sluggish, posing challenges for real-time operations. There-
fore, in applications that require high real-time performance, two-stage 
detection models are rarely used.
4.2.2. One-stage detection models of fruit and vegetable
YOLO-based Detection Models. YOLO is a one-stage detection 
approach that directly predicts bounding boxes and class probabilities 
in a single pass through the neural network. This simplicity leads to 
faster inference times and lower computational requirements compared 
to two-stage approaches. Due to the significant evolves and advances in 
the capability of YOLO in object detection, it has garnered widespread 
attention in fruit and vegetable detection across various baseline mod-
els. These improved models based on YOLO include: (1) Enhanced 
YOLO detection models — Researchers have imposed specific modifica-
tion on the baseline YOLO framework, enhancing its flexibility in fruit 
and vegetable detection. (2) Attention-based YOLO — YOLO has been 
augmented with attention mechanisms, which facilitates to enhancing 
feature expression power for relevant features during the detection pro-
cess in fruit and vegetable applications. (3) Lightweight YOLO detection 
models — Efforts have been made to develop lightweight versions of 
YOLO, aimed at ensuring efficient and effective performance for fruit 
and vegetable detection while minimizing computational requirements.
(1) Enhanced YOLO detection models. To improve the performance 
of YOLO-based detection models, researchers have focused their efforts 
on the following strategies for the detection of fruit and vegetable. 
For garlic detection, Yang et al. (2022a) introduced a refined version 
of the YOLOv2 model called IRM-YOLOv2, which is constructed by 
incorporating the inverse residual module and resulted in a mean 
accuracy of 99.2%. This enhancement exhibits the powerful strength of 
the IRM-YOLOv2 model, offering a more precise and reliable concept 
and patterns for garlic detection. Based on YOLOv3, Dewantara et al. 
(2021) developed a combined system for food material identification 
and quality measurement, and achieved the average detection accuracy 
of 83.33%. Indeed, YOLO also has stronger detection capability for 
obscured or small targets. For detecting small targets, Zhao et al. 
(2021a) put forward an enhanced YOLOv3 model as a detection method 
by incorporating the CSPNet and SPP module to extract both global and 
local features. The experimental results demonstrate an impressive mAP 
of 96.3% and an average detection speed of 27.8 FPS. These findings 
highlight the effectiveness of Dao’s approach in achieving accurate 
small object detection while maintaining efficient processing speed. 
Based on the YOLOv4, Wu et al. (2021) proposed a fig recognition 
method that can discern the obscured fruit with fast detection and 
high accuracy. Based on YOLOv4 and graph clustering algorithms, Ning 
et al. (2022) presented a novel method termed AYDY for the identifi-
cation of sweet peppers and the planning of picking sequences. This 
approach came true automated picking of clusters of sweet peppers 
with multiple nearby targets, which can further promote efficient and 
reliable harvesting processes. For detecting small target citrus in com-
plex backgrounds. Chen et al. (2020b) proposed an improved YOLOv4, 
which achieves an average higher detection improvement accuracy o 
3.15% than the original YOLOv4, YOLOv3, and Faster R-CNN. Due 
to its exceptional speed in detecting objects, YOLO has gained sig-
nificant attention in the context of automatic harvesting. Kong et al. 
(2021) employed YOLOv4 to successfully perform spatial localization 
task of fruits, which demonstrates remarkable stability and real-time 
performance. With an average localization accuracy of 89.55% and a 
collision-free harvesting success rate of 90.04%, their approach proved 
to be highly efficient and effective in enabling automated fruit har-
vesting. In their exploration of automatic oil palm fruit detection, Robi 
et al. (2022) carried out a multiple version by fusing YOLO, including 
YOLOv3, YOLOv3-tiny, YOLOv4, and YOLOv4-tiny, the comparative 
analysis of which revealed that YOLOv4 exhibited the highest level 
of accuracy, reaching an impressive maximum accuracy of 98.70%. 
This finding underscores the effectiveness of YOLOv4 in accurately 
identifying oil palm fruits, and showcases its potential for facilitating 
automated fruit detection in agricultural applications.
With the development of YOLO, higher versions of YOLO have also 
been used for fruit and vegetable detection. For example, real-time 
detection of strawberry growth status in YOLO v7 (Yang et al., 2025a). 
Use YOLO v8 for kiwi bud detection (Dang et al., 2025), passion fruit 
detection (Li et al., 2025a), eggplant recognition (Liu et al., 2025b),

(2) Attention-based YOLO. For the attention-based YOLO, researches

adaptive feature fusion model, with the objective of detecting the 
ripeness of wolfberries. By merging a spatial attention mechanism to 
a bidirectional feature pyramid network, employed adaptively spatial 
feature fusion techniques, the power of feature expression of this model

single image detection speed of 15 ms, demonstrating its efficiency

in recognizing tomato tasks under different lighting conditions such

4.2.3. Other detection models of fruit and vegetable
Zero-shot detection models and end-to-end detection models have 
also been applied in fruit and vegetable image analysis. SAM-DINO
(Paul and Machavaram, 2024), combining the Segment Anything Model 
(SAM) and Grounding DINO for chili-pepper detection. In the compar-
ison of the detection performance of single-shot detection (YOLOv10) 
and zero-shot detection (SAM-DINO) models for chili-pepper in green-
house environment, YOLOv10 showed excellent detection ability,
whereas SAM-DINO demonstrated the potential of zero-shot detection. 
YOLOvOVOD (Paul and Machavaram, 2025), a hybrid zero shot de-
tection model, integrated YOLO and DINO hybrid model and thus 
inherited the strengths of the high-accuracy detection capabilities of 
YOLOv9 with the open vocabulary prompting of Grounding DINO, 
Significantly improving the performance of the model for sweet pep-
per detection in nighttime greenhouse environments. The optimized 
deformable DETR, a green apple detection model, incorporated Trans-
former and end-to-end detection methods to solve the interference 
problem of green background on green apples (Liu et al., 2025a).
Otherwise, CenterNet transforms object detection tasks into key-
point detection problems, which avoids complex anchor mechanisms 
and non maximum suppression steps, significantly improving detection 
speed. Long et al. (2025) devised a tomato detection method based 
on Graph CenterNet, which enhances the flexibility of feature extrac-
tion by replacing traditional convolution operations with visual graph 
convolution (ViG) and exhibits significant accuracy and robustness in 
tomato recognition tasks in complex environments.
In summary, based on the above literature and Table 4, the follow-
ing information can be obtained. The mainstream approach for fruit 
and vegetable image detection is to design lightweight single-stage 
object detection models that combine attention mechanisms. Multi type 
object detection is still its weakness. The detection models often use 
derivative versions of YOLO, and non-mainstream detection models 
CenterNet and DETR have also been the development direction in this 
years.

4.3.1. Semantic-based segmentation models of fruit and vegetable
Semantic segmentation involves assigning a class label to each pixel 
in an image, performing the identification and delineation of different 
objects. Classical semantic segmentation models encompass FCN (Fully 
Convolutional Networks) (Long et al., 2015), U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 
2015), SegNet (Badrinarayanan et al., 2017), and DeepLab (Chen et al., 
2014).
FCN. FCN, known for its simplicity and excellent segmentation 
performance, was one of the earliest semantic segmentation mod-
els. Although both studies employed fully convolutional networks 
(FCNs), Zabawa et al. (2019) focused on detecting the edges of indi-
vidual grape edges in the context of grape detection and segmentation, 
whereas Li et al. (2017) applied FCNs to ground-based automatic 
segmentation of cotton in the field. Moreover, Lin et al. (2019a) 
and Kang et al. (2020) applied FCN to respectively segment guava fruits 
and estimate their poses based on the segmentation results, and grape 
and branch segmentation from RGB images, both of which facilitates 
robotic harvesting. Different from Kang’s method, Liu et al. (2022) 
utilized FCN to generate a mask that effectively distinguishes specific 
regions and subsequently performs the segmentation of overlapping 
fruits from a homochromatic background. Unfortunately, the structural 
limitations of FCNs impede model improvements and prevent higher 
segmentation accuracy, thereby restricting their practical applicability 
in more demanding scenarios. As a result, the U-Net architecture 
has gained widespread popularity as a scalable semantic segmenta-
tion model for fruit and vegetable segmentation. U-Net is primarily 
employed for such tasks as fruit and leaf segmentation.
U-Net. U-Net is a scalable and widely used model for semantic 
segmentation, particularly in medical imaging and agricultural ap-
plications. Roy et al. (2021) proposed an EnU-Net to segment the 
rotten part of the apple with a validation accuracy of 97.54%. Häni 
et al. (2020) used the U-Net to estimate apple orchard yield and 
obtained yield estimation accuracies ranging from 95.56% to 97.83%. 
For obscuring problems, Li et al. (2021b) proposed an improved U-Net, 
which fused residual blocks and gated convolution to obtain boundary 
information, and imposed ASPP to fuse edge features and high-level 
features. There are many improved U-Net being successfully used for 
leaf segmentation (Zhang et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2021a; Wspanialy 
and Moussa, 2020), and cucumber powdery mildew segmentation (Lin 
et al., 2019b). Therefore, U-Net has become a go-to architecture for 
fruit and vegetable image segmentation. Its encoder–decoder struc-
ture can be scaled and modified to handle different image resolutions 
and object scales, and it routinely delivers high segmentation accu-
racy, making it suitable for practical applications such as automated 
harvesting and quality inspection.
SegNet. SegNet is derived from VGG16 and is known for its simple 
structure and ease of implementation. As a variant of SegNet, ResSeg is 
proposed by Pinzón-Arenas et al. (2020) for food segmentation, achiev-
ing an accuracy of over 90% and highlighting the effectiveness of the 
SegNet architecture. Based on SegNet, Kerkech et al. (2020) proposed 
a novel grape disease detection method that identifies shaded, ground, 
healthy, and symptomatic grapes and merges these recognized results 
from visible and infrared images to produce a comprehensive disease 
map. Due to SegNet’s successful applications, SegNet was applied to 
distinguish weeds in field images (Rathore et al., 2022); isolate pear 
trees from their background for precision pesticide spraying (Kim et al., 
2020) and segment and localize tomatoes (Maheswari et al., 2022).
DeepLab. DeepLab is characterized by its ability to reduce the num-
ber of parameters and accelerate model training without compromising 
the mean intersection over union (MIoU) metric. In their study, He 
et al. (2020) selected Xception65 as the backbone for DeepLab, which 
demonstrated superior performance in terms of MIoU. Stasenko et al. 
(2021) trained DeepLab to detect rotten regions of apples, achieving 
an impressive MIoU of 99.99% during the testing phase. For grape 
bunch segmentation, Heras et al. (2021) evaluated three different 
models and found that DeepLab performed best, achieving an av-
erage segmentation accuracy of 84.37%. As many improved version 
of DeepLab came out, DeepLab-ResNet was designed for apple blos-
som segmentation by showing an outstanding F1 score of 89.6% (Sun 
et al., 2021), and DeepLabv3+ was incorporated feature pyramids to 
fuse feature maps from different levels, achieving an MIoU of 84.8% 
for grape leaf black rot spot segmentation (Yuan et al., 2022). As 
a lightweight network, MobileNetV2 is employed as the backbone 
network with coordinate attention (CA) module imposed to construct

DeeplabV3+model, which achieved the improved segmentation accu-
racy for rapeseed seedlings and field drainage ditches, and further 
provided theoretical basis and technical support for multi-stage growth

Although the above analysis further validates the performance of

4.3.2. Instance-based segmentation models of fruit and vegetable
Instance segmentation involves detecting and segmenting each in-
dividual object in an image, providing detailed information about the 
spatial distribution and structure of each object. Mask R-CNN (Mask 
Region-based Convolutional Neural Network) (He et al., 2017) is a 
prominent model in this category, aimed at detecting and segment-
ing each object in fruit and vegetable image, especially for maturity 
analysis of individual fruit. Selected ResNet-50-FPN as the backbone 
for ripe green tomato segmentation, the F1 score of the model reached 
92.0% (Zu et al., 2021). Mask RCNN can also be used for ripe straw-
berry detection with good segmentation of overlapping fruit (Yu et al., 
2019), or drawing support from modular scoring and edge to improve 
the segmentation performance of fruit and vegetable images in super-
market environments (Hameed et al., 2022). It is well known that 
more advanced models have enabled a wider range of applications 
for instance segmentation. Based on instance-based segmentation mod-
els, Gómez-Zamanillo et al. (2024) constructed a Mask R-CNN model as 
a variety of classification method, which is incorporated the strength 
of DenseNet121 and Vision Transformer (ViT) for instance segmenta-
tion, and employs XGBoost classifier to conduct classification task for 
pepper varieties, thus improving the efficiency and accuracy of pepper 
phenotype analysis. YOMASK (Zhao et al., 2025b), an improved model 
of YOLACT, optimized its performance by comparing different back-
bone networks, and combining feature fusion strategies and attention 
mechanisms. It demonstrated excellent segmentation performance and 
inference speed in lettuce instance segmentation tasks, providing strong 
support for high-throughput phenotype analysis. Based on improved 
YOLOv7 and HRNet, the multi category cherry tomato instance seg-
mentation model, Y-HRNet (Liu et al., 2024a), is capable of adaptively 
adopting detection strategy from ‘‘coarse detection, fine segmentation’’ 
to enhance the model’s adaptability for different maturity stages of 
cherry tomatoes.
4.3.3. Other segmentation models of fruit and vegetable
Apart from the aforementioned segmentation techniques, the fol-
lowing segmentation methods are also commonly used such as The Seg-
ment Anything Model (SAM), Strawberry Segmentation Model (SSM), 
CF-ASPM and FCOS-EAM. The SAM is a general image segmentation 
model proposed in recent years, and multiple fruit and vegetable image 
segmentation works have emerged based on this model. For example, 
based on SAM, a SSM is developed for color phenotype detection 
of strawberry germplasm resources using field robots and semantic 
segmentation (Yang et al., 2024). This method is mainly addressed the 
issue of uneven sample distribution by enhancing the loss function and 
introducing multiple loss methods (combined with category weights). 
As an automatic segmentation and phenotype quantification model for 
citrus flowers, CF-ASPM (Qiu et al., 2025) combined pre trained SAM 
and lightweight classification module and achieved superior ability to 
accurately identify and quantify the key structural features of citrus 
flowers.
To further improve the segmentation ability of green fruits in com-
plex backgrounds, multiple studies have designed complex model struc-
tures by incorporating attention mechanisms, loss functions, boundary 
enhancement, and other methods. For example, by adding boundary 
attention modules (BAM) and edge attention modules (EAM), FCOS-
EAM (Jia et al., 2024) is constructed and achieves high-precision 
segmentation of green fruits such as apples and persimmons by adding 
boundary attention modules (BAM) and edge attention modules (EAM). 
Similarly, by introducing texture consistency loss and reverse attention 
mechanism, the semantic segmentation accuracy greatly improved in 
green pomegranate segmentation task, especially in complex back-
grounds (Wang et al., 2025a).
As described in the literature and listed in Table 5, we can draw 
the following conclusions. Instance segmentation is a hot topic in fruit 
and vegetable image analysis, which will support downstream tasks 
such as counting, maturity estimation, phenotype analysis, and growth 
detection. The introduction of attention such as CA and Transformer 
is an effective means to improve segmentation performance. The use 
of emerging models and technologies such as SAM and Mamba will 
become mainstream in the future.
5. Applications
Deep learning models have revolutionized various aspects of fruit 
and vegetable production, distribution, and marketing by providing 
efficient and accurate solutions for tasks such as quality grading, dis-
ease detection, yield estimation, and localization. These successful 
applications not only improve operational efficiency but also support 
more informed decision-making across the agricultural scenarios.
5.1. Disease detection
The identification of fruit and vegetable diseases from acquired 
images, a technology for identifying diseases from acquired images, 
has been the emergence of Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based 
models as highly efficient solutions in recent years. Various successful 
methods for this task have been proposed, broadly categorizing into 
single-crop and multi-crop detection based on the scope of disease 
detection.
5.1.1. Single-crop disease detection
Single-crop disease diagnosis focuses on detecting diseases in a 
specific type of fruit or vegetable, typically achieving higher diagnostic 
capabilities.
Fruit Disease Detection. The primary emphasis in common fruit 
disease diagnosis is on apples, grapes, plums, and pomegranates, among 
other fruit species.
The primary concept for detecting apple leaf diseases is to con-
struct robust and accurate diagnostic models based on VGG architec-
ture (Yan et al., 2020; Sahu et al., 2023). To improve detection speed, 
some researchers have designed modified detection models based on 
MobileNet (Bi et al., 2022). It is well known that grape leaves are 
susceptible to various diseases such that the health of grapevines and 
grape yield is adversely affected. For this purpose, a Grape Leaf Disease 
Dataset (GLDD) has been constructed to facilitate the detection of these 
diseases (Xie et al., 2020). To achieve a good balance between high 
precision and low-parameter training, Lin et al. (2022) proposed a 
lightweight grape leaf detection model called GrapeNet, in which the 
integration of lightweight convolutional layers and SE blocks into the 
backbone network of YOLOv7 is conducive to enhancing the model’s 
generalization ability and noise data processing capability (Khan et al.,

demonstrate significantly superior performance compared to traditional 
convolutional neural networks. Also, a comparison of machine learning

deployment on resource-constrained edge devices (Wang et al., 2025b).
In addition, various other fruit disease diagnosis methods such as 
mango leaf disease diagnosis based on transfer learning and fine-tuning 
techniques (Chen et al., 2024b), high-precision banana nematode de-

citrus leaf disease detection and classification using YOLOv8 (Frederick

in disease diagnosis task.
Vegetable Disease Detection. Tomato diseases are a primary cause

mildew. Given the small size of the cucumber leaf disease dataset, 
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), as a data augmentation tech-

performance (Zhang et al., 2021). Moreover, these models such as 
modified versions of AlexNet (Zhang et al., 2019a), Inception (Zhang 
et al., 2021), and EfficientNet (Zhang et al., 2020b) also achieved a 
relatively better effect for cucumber leaf disease detection with the

5.1.2. Multi-crop disease detection
Multi-crop disease detection aims to identify diseases across various 
crops by focusing on generic disease names rather than specific diseases 
of individual crops. Research by Toda and Okura (2019) found that 
extracting crop-related features, as opposed to disease-specific features, 
may not yield optimal results. This approach eliminates the need 
for designing crop-specific networks and employs Convolution Neural 
Networks (CNNs) to extract interpretable features.
The Plant Village (PV) dataset is one of the largest and most exten-
sive multi-crop disease datasets, containing 54,303 images of healthy 
and diseased plants across 38 different categories. Currently, most 
disease detection methods rely on the PV dataset, on which remarkable 
results can be achieved by employing pre-trained models for crop 
disease detection. For instance, Hassan et al. (2021) introduced a novel 
technique by using pre-trained MobileNet-V2 with depthwise separable 
convolutions replacing standard convolutions, which facilitates to re-
ducing the number of parameters while achieving high accuracy. The 
pre-trained EfficientNet Mobile and Net-V2 are implemented respec-
tively for leaf disease diagnosis (Atila et al., 2021) and the detection of 
subtle disease symptoms (Chen et al., 2020a). Among these methods, 
the former added an additional mechanism, data augmentation strate-
gies, achieving an accuracy of 99.1%. Overall, based on CNN models, 
researchers have explored various detection methods, and achieved 
sequential improvement in disease diagnosis (Mohanty et al., 2016; Too 
et al., 2019; Arsenovic et al., 2019; Vallabhajosyula et al., 2022).
In terms of test datasets, some studies have also constructed private 
datasets for this field. For example, Zhou et al. (2021) developed the 
PRP-Net, specifically for detecting diseases in six types of vegetables in 
complex environments. Barbedo (2019) reclassified 79 diseases across 
14 different plants in the PV dataset, enabling the detection of multiple 
diseases on a single leaf. Turkoglu et al. (2022) created the Turkish 
Plant Dataset with 15 plant diseases containing, on which six pre-
trained CNN models are conducted comparative experiments, achieving 
an accuracy rate of 97.56% in the best case. On a comprehensive 
dataset containing 23 categories of healthy and diseased plant im-
ages, a lightweight model is developed for detecting diseases in key 
vegetable crops such as eggplant, potato, tomato, and soybean (Bhola 
and Kumar, 2025). For greenhouse vegetable diseases, Liu and Wang 
(2024) proposed a vegetable disease detection method based on multi-
source information fusion, which greatly enhances the detection and 
recognition capabilities of disease diagnosis.
5.2. Quality grading
Accurate quality grading is essential for determining the market 
value of fruits and vegetables. Quality is influenced by multiple factors, 
including ripeness, shape, size, color, and appearance (Masuda et al., 
2023). Deep learning techniques have been widely applied to standard-
ize and automate this process through maturity analysis and quality 
evaluation.
5.2.1. Maturity analysis
Maturity analysis aims to determine the degree of ripeness of fruit 
and vegetable to facilitate harvesting and quality assurance (Tem-
pelaere et al., 2023). The maturity analysis can be accomplished 
through direct detection or by initially segmenting the specific area 
and subsequently evaluating the level of ripeness. To guarantee that 
consumers obtain high-quality products, it is crucial to consider time of 
harvesting of products. Premature harvesting results in unpalatable or 
unripe produce, whereas delayed harvesting leads to over-ripening or 
spoilage (Huang et al., 2020). Additionally, for different products and 
destinations, the length of transport and the speed of maturation must 
be considered when determining the maturity of the harvest (Huang 
et al., 2020).
For assessing the maturity and quantity of various fruits and vegeta-
bles, researchers have made significant advancements in using image 
processing techniques. For instance, Halstead et al. (2018) built upon 
the DeepFruits framework (Sa et al., 2016) and introduced a pre-
trained Faster R-CNN model to estimate the maturity and quantity 
of bell peppers. This approach involved classifying the foreground 
or background and subsequently analyzing the foreground region for 
ripeness classification. In the case of tomatoes, Afonso et al. (2020) 
performed two process, i.e. semantic segmenting the tomato image and 
classifying the segmented regions based on ripeness to differentiate 
between ripe and raw tomatoes. Huang et al. (2020) employed instance 
segmentation to classify tomatoes into four categories: unripe, broken, 
pre-harvest, and harvested, with precise identification and classification 
of individual tomatoes based on their specific characteristics taking 
into consideration. Moreover, the integration of advanced algorithms

like R-YOLO ensures efficient and accurate detection of maturity levels, 
which can enhance the overall productivity and quality of fruit-related 
operations (Yu et al., 2020). Furthermore, YOLOv5 was also applied to

CML (Chen et al., 2024a) with lightweight faster block implanting

for color changing melon flipping. Similarly, Khamis et al. (2022)

segmentation and classification of ripe and raw strawberry also can be 
found in some literature (Yu et al., 2019; Ge et al., 2019).
For solving the problem of limited annotated data, a prediction 
method using WGAN to generate synthetic data has emerged (Lyu 
et al., 2025). Although this method reduces annotations, the generated 
images still differ from the actual images, thus resulting in poor per-

fundamentally solve the problem, self-supervised methods have become 
a research direction in maturity analysis. For example, Zhu et al. 
(2025b) and Zhao et al. (2025c) employed self-supervised method to 
respectively predict the maturity of apples based on the zero detection 
model Grounding DINO, and solve the problem of determining the 
maturity of apples in orchards when they are obstructed. Although self-
supervised methods save training time to an extent, their detection 
performance is limited. Meanwhile, there are studies with the aim 
of exploring maturity analysis by conducting segmentation methods. 
For example, the segmentation framework SegLoRA (Pisharody et al.,

Adaptation (LoRA) module, and achieves accurate tomato counting 
while estimating maturity. A two-stage model for predicting strawberry 
harvest dates was developed using UNet and Triple-Branch Attention 
Fusion to achieve strawberry maturity and harvest date prediction in 
plant factories with artificial lighting (Lin et al., 2025).
Similarly, Yuan and Chen (2024) proposed a freshness detection 
method for fruits and vegetables based on deep features and prin-
cipal component analysis, in which three pre-trained deep learning

module, thus enhancing fine-grained feature extraction capability and 
achieving weightlighting with a parameter size of 0.37M. Addition-
ally, implementing a lightweight dragon fruit maturity classification 
application deployed on Android devices. Notably, based on the Mo-
bileNet V2 architecture, a two models system is capable of performing 
multi-tasks, i.e. one is for the classification of 32 classes of fruits and 
vegetables, and another is for the determination of the ripeness of 6 
classes (Mukhiddinov et al., 2022).
In brief, the above studies demonstrate the effectiveness of image 
processing techniques in accurately assessing the maturity and quan-
tity of fruits and vegetables. By leveraging advanced algorithms and 
models, researchers are able to develop various systems that provide 
detailed classifications and estimations for different produce, and offer 
better decision-making and optimization in agricultural processes.
5.2.2. Quality evaluation
Automated quality evaluation using computer vision has signif-
icantly improved efficiency, accuracy, and cost-effectiveness in the 
agricultural industry (Ge et al., 2019; Altaheri et al., 2019). Researchers 
have successfully applied deep learning techniques to the quality eval-
uation of fruits and vegetables, yielding satisfactory results. For in-
stance, Zhang et al. (2018) applied deep learning to classify tomatoes 
into five different classes, and Hu et al. (2020) employed VGG19 
and Inception V3 to grade apples based on their level of damage. 
Furthermore, for grading apples and bananas, Ismail and Malik (2022) 
utilized a pre-trained, EfficientNet, to develop a real-time automatic 
detection system, in which apples were classified as either healthy or 
defective, while bananas were classified into four categories. To classify 
different grades of mangoes (Wu et al., 2020) employed pre-trained 
models such as AlexNet, VGG, and ResNet, in which each individual 
mango was categorized into one of three grades, namely A, B, and C, 
based on two key factors: the uniformity of color and the presence and 
severity of defects or diseases. By analyzing the color distribution and 
identifying any visible defects or diseases, the networks were able to 
accurately classify each mango into its appropriate grade. Overall, the 
utilization of pre-trained networks in mango grading demonstrates the 
potential of computer vision techniques to automate and enhance the 
accuracy of quality evaluation in the agricultural sector. Some research 
works also provided different solutions for apple sorting by classifying 
apple into four classes based on their size, surface defects, color, and 
shape(Yang et al. 2021b), automatically classifying fresh date into high 
and low quality (Yang et al., 2021b), automatically classifying fresh 
date into high and low quality (Cao et al., 2021), and for pre-harvest 
and post-harvest detection of chili fruits (Faqeerzada et al., 2024).
In recent years, the fusion of spectroscopy and deep learning has 
proven to be a powerful tool for nondestructive detection of the quality 
and state of fruit and vegetable, allowing for efficient and accurate eval-
uation without damaging the produce. Varga et al. (2021) conducted a 
hyperspectral imaging to create a dataset of avocados and kiwis based 
on the dimensions of hardness, sweetness, in which overall ripeness the 
fruits are classified into three categories.
Meanwhile, by employing a hyperspectral convolutional neural 
network, they achieved successful predictions. Similarly, Gao et al. 
(2020b) conducted a pre-trained AlexNet to rapidly classify the straw-
berries, which focused on strawberries and collected hyperspectral 
images, both in field and laboratory settings. For bananas classifica-
tion, Mesa and Chiang (2021) developed a triple-classification system 
by using RGB images and hyperspectral images, constructed a dataset 
and classified bananas based on size and quality. By employing hy-
perspectral images, valuable reflectance values closely associated with 
internal features can be obtained. The combination of hyperspectral 
imaging and deep learning techniques facilitates a comprehensive 
analysis of the internal properties of fruits and vegetables. This en-
ables accurate and objective classification based on specific quality 
attributes, thereby enhancing decision-making processes related to fruit 
sorting, distribution, and market positioning.
Notably, the lightweight quality evaluation method has a strong 
demand for application in fields and large-scale production. There-
fore, Wang et al. (2025c) proposed a lettuce quality detection system 
based on FPGA, which uses lightweight Lit-FasterNet to reduce the 
dimensionality of hyperspectral image features. The detection time for 
a single hyperspectral image can reach to 0.083 s, and the power con-
sumption is only 6.323 watts. To estimate the quality attributes and nu-
tritional components of sweet melon fruits, Shah et al. (2025) proposed 
a model based on MobileNet-v3 to achieving rapid and non-destructive 
field detection of sweet melons.
In conclusion, the combination of spectroscopy and deep learning 
offers great potential for nondestructive evaluation of produce, and 
further provides valuable insights into the internal characteristics and 
quality of fruits and vegetables.

automated yield estimation methods based on deep learning highly 
desirable. Researchers have developed various models to address this 
challenge. These models can be broadly categorized into two types: 
direct counting-based methods and indirect counting-based methods.

Indirect counting-based methods typically involve the initial identi-
fication of ROI, followed by counting based on the information derived 
from these regions. Detection and segmentation networks are com-
monly employed to identify and delineate these ROI. For apple counting 
in continuous image sequences, Jarvinen et al. (2018) utilized the 
Faster R-CNN for optical flow-based tracking of apples in orchards. 
Similarly, Vasconez et al. (2020) employed the Faster R-CNN and 
SSD models to detect and count three different types of fruits. In 
the domain of mango counting, Kestur et al. (2019) introduced a 
semantic segmentation network called MangoNet, which is capable 
of accurately segmenting mangoes in images of varying sizes, and its 
execution time remains unaffected by the number of mangoes present 
in the image. Yao et al. (2025) proposed a tracking method based on 
extended Kalman filter for accurate estimation of mango yield, in which 
YOLOv8s is responsible for mango detection, extended Kalman filter 
acts for tracking to solve the problems of inconsistent fruit detection 
and duplicate counting in videos. For drone imaging, a yield estimation 
framework is designed to implement detection, health assessment, and 
yield quality estimation of tropical fruits such as peaches, apples, and 
citrus (Ahmad et al., 2024). Giménez-Gallego et al. (2024) devised a 
novel model by fusing Mask R-CNN and Vision Transformer models for 
pixel-based fruit size estimation, and another novel model to estimate 
and monitor the fruit size in pixel units from the automated masks.
In a similar vein, Mask R-CNN model was applied to perform seg-
mentation of blueberries based on their ripeness, and thus the number 
of blueberries was determined by analyzing the generated mask (Ni 
et al., 2020). MANet also was applied to perform semantic segmen-
tation on RGB images, and then identified and separated individual 
grape clusters through depth gradient information, volume and weight 
in reconstructed three-dimensional shape of grape clusters (Devanna 
et al., 2025).
To meet the high-precision requirements of yield estimation, a real-
time fruit volume estimation algorithm named RTFFE-YOLOv9, was 
designed with binocular stereo vision technology, in which these mod-
ules, the innovative Dual-Scale and Global–Local Sequence (DSGLSeq) 
module and Multi-Head and Multi-Scale Self-Interaction (MHMSI) mod-
ule, were merged to improve the discriminative power of model for 
occluded fruits and the detection accuracy of small target fruits (Yi 
et al., 2025).
Due to variations in shading, yield estimation often suffer from 
inaccuracies and typically require correction. One common method 
involves using a correction factor, and the simplest approach with the 
use of a separate correction factor for each orchard. For instance, a 
correction factor is calculated by comparing the actual number of man-
ually harvested fruits to the number of fruits detected in the image. This 
correction factor is then applied to adjust all predictions (Koirala et al., 
2019). Obviously, this approach assumes a consistent tree structure 
across the entire orchard, which results in recalculating the correction 
factor when the orchard’s location changes. Consequently, even though 
they managed to identify a greater number of fruits, the detected count 
remained significantly lower than the true number. This emphasizes the 
ongoing research challenge of achieving exhaustive and accurate fruit 
counts.
5.4. Localization
Localization of fruits and vegetables is essential for robotic pick-
ing and harvesting operations, so research in this area focuses on 
using depth information and point cloud data to achieve accurate 3D 
positioning. Depth images assign pixel values based on the distance 
between the sensor and the target in the scene. Typically, depth images 
are combined with RGB images to form RGB-D images for accurate fruit 
and vegetable localization, such that RGB images enable the extrac-
tion of features through two-dimensional target detection, while depth 
images provide the power to perceive and understand the physical 
world by capturing spatial geometric structures. For instance, Yang 
et al. (2019) utilized Kinect v2 to convert the 2D centroid coordinates 
of citrus into highly accurate 3D coordinates, thus it can achieve a 
localization error of merely 5.9 mm. For detection of bell peppers, Arad 
et al. (2020) employed a Fotonic F80 depth camera to extract depth 
information such that it can result in an average fruit localization time 
of 3.7 s. These localized fruit and vegetable coordinates are then uti-
lized for robotic picking. In the above mentioned, the harvesting robot 
demonstrated an impressive picking success rate of 61%, indicating 
its remarkable efficiency and effectiveness in completing the task. To 
clearly illustrate these methods, an overview of the different types of 
RGB-D sensors employed and the corresponding localization methods 
utilized as shown in Table 6.
Some studies have concentrated on generating point clouds of the 
entire scene by combining RGB information with depth information. 
For instance, Mai et al. (2015) utilized segmented point clouds of fruit 
regions to determine their precise 3D locations of the fruit. Nguyen 
et al. (2016) adopted a distance filter and a color filter as preprocessing 
techniques to enhance the quality of the point cloud data. Moreover, 
they applied a Euclidean clustering algorithm to segment the point 
clouds, effectively separating and combining the clusters corresponding 
to each individual apple. By implementing this approach, they achieved 
a remarkable position estimation error of less than 10 mm. Different 
from Nguyen’s method, Zhang et al. (2019b) employed the Kinect 
v2 sensor to capture point cloud images of apple trees, and subse-
quently constructed a point cloud segmentation algorithm to accurately 
segment and localize the regions containing the apples.
Based on the analysis conducted above, it can be inferred that depth 
images and point cloud images generated by RGB-cameras achieve 
superior accuracy in capturing 3D position information. Neverthe-
less, these data types introduce additional dimensions that result in 
increased computational and memory resource demands, making it 
challenging to achieve real-time processing. Additionally, depth and 
point cloud images typically exhibit lower resolution compared to 
RGB images, and further improvements are necessary to enhance their 
recognition accuracy.
In recent years, to reduce the complexity of models, 2D image-
based localization systems have become a research hotspot due that 
they have fast and accurate inference speeds. These research work 
related with 2D image-based localization systems cover cabbage local-
ization model (Qiu et al., 2024a; Zheng et al., 2024), tomato local-
ization model (Liu et al., 2024b; Gao et al., 2025), grape localization 
model (Li et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025b), strawberry localization

5.5. Multiple application integration
The widespread promotion of a single application has led to the 
flourishing development of fruit and vegetable image analysis. In recent 
years, the work of multi application integration has been increasing, 
mainly including multi task integration and multi crop processing.
5.5.1. Integration of multiple analytical tasks
Multi tasking integrates multiple applications into one method. 
These applications are usually similar, such as detection and counting, 
quality identification and disease diagnosis, quality classification and 
bruising detection, etc.
In the past two years, researchers have conducted beneficial ex-
plorations. For example: fruit testing, disease diagnosis, and yield 
estimation (Ahmad et al., 2024), Litchi detection and counting (Lu 
et al., 2025), LGENetB4CA realizes pepper variety identification and 
leaf disease classification (Van et al., 2025), bruise detection and length 
classification of asparagus (Chuang et al., 2025).
5.5.2. Cross-crop image processing
Multi crop processing is the use of a model to complete processing 
tasks for multiple crops, changing the limitations of using different 
methods for different crops and improving the efficiency of field opera-
tions. These crops are often fruits and vegetables of the same or similar 
types. For example, leafy vegetables, fruit vegetables, and fruits.
The research work in the past two years mainly includes: EA-CNN 
for fruit and vegetable classification on the Fruit-360 dataset (Khan

6. Research directions
The field of deep learning-based fruit and vegetable image anal-
ysis is rapidly evolving, with several promising research directions 
emerging to address current challenges and expand its applications. 
These directions include the construction of large-scale datasets, on-site 
applications, the development of large models, and the integration of 
artificial intelligence generated content (AIGC).
6.1. Construction of large-scale datasets
The availability of publicly accessible, extensive, and high-quality 
datasets is a significant bottleneck in the advancement of fruit and 
vegetable image analysis. Most existing datasets are privately owned 
and suffer from limitations such as insufficient sample sizes, lack of 
diversity, and inaccurate annotations (Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú, 
2018). Though these techniques like transfer learning and generative 
models can partially alleviate these issues, they do not provide a fun-
damental solution. Therefore, the construction of large-scale datasets 
is crucial for the development of this field. Large datasets not only im-
prove model performance but also facilitate the training of more robust 
and generalizable models. Collaborative efforts between researchers, 
industry stakeholders, and data collection initiatives can facilitate to 
create comprehensive datasets that cover a wide range of fruit and 
vegetable types, growth stages, and environmental conditions. The 
strategy for constructing the dataset can be:
(1) Establish a multimodal dataset that includes images, text, and 
videos.
(2) The test database should include fruit and vegetable of var-
ious types, varieties, production stages, lighting conditions, origins, 
occlusion, and scales.
(3) Expand the number of datasets using image generation tech-
niques such as GAN, Diffusion, Flow Matching, etc.
6.2. On site applications
Overcoming Background Interference. Fruit and vegetable im-
ages often include complex backgrounds with elements such as leaves, 
stems, branches, flowers, sky, and soil, which can interfere with and 
obscure the foreground objects. Developing models that can effectively 
handle background interference is essential for accurate image analysis. 
Techniques such as background subtraction, attention mechanisms, and 
multi-modal data fusion (e.g., combining RGB images with depth or 
thermal images) can enhance the robustness of models in real-world 
scenarios.
Lightweight Model. Despite the remarkable achievements of deep 
learning in fruit and vegetable image analysis, most models are com-
putationally intensive and require significant memory and processing 
power. This limits their deployment on mobile devices and resource-
constrained environments. Developing lightweight models that main-
tain high accuracy while reducing computational complexity is a criti-
cal research direction. In addition, computational efficiency is also an 
important indicator and research direction for lightweight. In many 
cases, lightweighting can often lead to improved computational effi-
ciency. Techniques such as model compression, pruning, quantization, 
and the use of efficient architectures (e.g., MobileNet, EfficientNet) can 
help create models that are suitable for real-time applications on edge 
devices.
Application of Self-propelled Unmanned Equipment. The inte-
gration of self-propelled unmanned equipment (e.g., UAVs, autonomous 
robots) in agricultural operations offers new opportunities for real-
time image collection and analysis. These devices can be equipped 
with advanced sensors to capture high-resolution images and other 
relevant data, enabling tasks such as quality grading, yield estimation, 
and target localization. Developing novelty models that can process 
data from these devices in real-time and guide automated operations 
(e.g., robotic harvesting, pesticide spraying, fruit thinning, bagging, 
and picking) is an important area of research.
Multi task model. A single fruit and vegetable image processing 
model can only provide a single processing task and cannot meet the 
complex task requirements of actual production. In grape picking, it is 
necessary to simultaneously complete tasks such as detection, classifi-
cation, positioning, and grasping, as well as downstream tasks such as 
yield estimation, quality grading, and maturity analysis. Existing meth-
ods are difficult to meet this complex requirement. Therefore, models 
that integrate detection, classification, and segmentation tasks will 
become the development direction. At the same time, the integration of 
upstream and downstream tasks will also promote the development of 
fruit and vegetable image processing technology. Upstream tasks usu-
ally integrate classification, detection, and segmentation models, while 
downstream tasks require the integration of growth status analysis, 
disease detection, maturity analysis, yield estimation, quality grading, 
growth monitoring, phenotype analysis, thinning, picking, and other 
models.
Multi crop analysis. The universal analysis model that does not dis-
tinguish crop types has the ability to identify multiple crops, has strong 
generalization ability, greatly facilitates users, and reduces the burden 
of deploying models based on crop types. It is of great significance to 
improve the application and promotion scope of fruit and vegetable 
image analysis. The multi crop analysis model has the ability to analyze 
multiple types of fruits and vegetables, which can be the same type but 
different varieties, or different types but different varieties.

6.3. Multi-modal large model for fruit and vegetable
The development of large models has significantly advanced natural 
language processing, computer vision, and multimodal applications. 
Similarly, large models tailored for fruit and vegetable image analysis 
can unlock new possibilities and improve performance across various 
tasks. Researchers can leverage existing large models and fine-tune 
them on specific fruit and vegetable datasets. Additionally, developing 
specialized large models from scratch, trained on extensive and diverse 
datasets, can lead to more accurate and robust solutions. These models 
can incorporate multi-modal data (e.g., combining visual, spectral, and 
temporal information) to provide a comprehensive understanding of 
fruit and vegetable characteristics.
6.4. Artificial intelligence generated content (AIGC) in fruit and vegetable 
image generation
The integration of multimodal large models, pre-training tech-
niques, and generative algorithms has given rise to AIGC, which holds 
great potential for fruit and vegetable image analysis. AIGC can gen-
erate high-quality and creative content related to fruits and vegetables 
based on input conditions or guidance. For example, it can generate 
articles, images, videos, and even provide detailed information about 
the nutritional value, yield, and quality of produce. AIGC can also 
analyze images to identify and provide information on diseases or 
defects, along with potential treatment plans. This technology can 
enhance research and applications in areas such as virtual farming, 
agricultural education, and consumer engagement.
7. Conclusions
This survey provides a comprehensive review of the latest research 
in deep learning-based fruit and vegetable image analysis. We have 
covered essential background knowledge and summarized over 270 
relevant studies, categorizing them based on the type of task they 
address. Our review highlights the significant advancements in classifi-
cation, detection, segmentation, and other applications such as quality 
grading, yield estimation, and fruit and vegetable localization. These 
achievements demonstrate the potential of deep learning techniques to 
revolutionize agricultural practices by enhancing efficiency, accuracy, 
and decision-making processes. In addition, we conducted an in-depth 
analysis of the development trends and application prospects of fruit 
and vegetable image analysis technology, and pointed out future appli-
cation directions. I hope that through these analyses, researchers can 
understand the deficiencies and development directions of technology, 
and provide effective research ideas for them.
Despite these advancements, several challenges remain. (1) The 
need for construction of large-scale, high-quality datasets is urgent for 
further improving model performance and generalization. (2) Over-
coming background interference and developing lightweight models 
are essential and imperative for real-time applications in resource-
constrained environments. (3) The application of self-propelled un-
manned equipment, such as UAVs and autonomous robots, offers new 
opportunities and challenges for real-time image collection and anal-
ysis. (4) Though development of large models and the integration of 
artificial intelligence generated content (AIGC) hold great potential for 
advancing this field, how to achieve a balance between efficiency and 
computing power is a tricky problem.
In summary, deep learning-based fruit and vegetable image anal-
ysis has made significant progress, but continued research is needed 
to address existing challenges and explore new opportunities. Future 
work should focus on building comprehensive datasets, developing 
efficient and lightweight models, and leveraging advanced technologies 
to enhance agricultural productivity and sustainability.
