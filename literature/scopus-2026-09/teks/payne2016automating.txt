Automating mango crop yield estimation 
A.B. Payne, K.B. Walsh and P.P. Subedi 
Central Queensland University, North Rockhampton, Queensland, Australia. 
Abstract 
Several imaging technologies were assessed for determination of mango crop 
load, including hyperspectral and thermal imaging, but with preference given to RGB 
imaging. Sets of RGB images of mango fruits on trees were collected under a range of 
orchard conditions over five seasons. A number of different image processing 
algorithms were evaluated, and positive identification rates of up to 80% were 
achieved. Approaches included colour and texture filters, contour detection and pixel 
classification using various approaches. Issues that affect load estimates, occlusion of 
fruits by leaves and flower stalks, fruit bunching, shadowing, triggering, and 
insufficient lighting are discussed. These image sets are available on request for 
comparative work using other image processing algorithms. 
Keywords: fruit, processing, colour, texture, segmentation, count 
INTRODUCTION 
The use of machine vision in crop yield estimation for fruit trees is an area of growing 
interest in horticulture, involving technologies that will ultimately underpin automated 
harvesting. However, despite advances in digital imaging technology and machine vision 
algorithms, the detection of fruits on trees remains a challenging problem. The application is 
problematic due to the unstructured nature of the field environment and the variation in 
attributes (of fruit and of background). 
A number of imaging technologies have potential for fruit detection and counting on 
canopy, from LIDAR to thermal imaging. However, as this application can be achieved using 
human vision, the use of RGB imaging appears appropriate and cost effective (e.g., see 
review by Payne and Walsh, 2014). 
RGB images of mango fruits on tree have been collected across a range of ambient 
conditions, orchard environments and seasons. The utility of night imaging over day imaging, 
and a filtering algorithm primarily based on colour and texture was described in Payne et al. 
(2013, 2014). In a collaborative work with the groups of Daley (Thailand) and Linker (Israel), 
the use of a K‐nearest neighbour and Support Vector Machine (SVM) pixel classification 
based on colour and smoothness, and fruit detection based on an elliptical shape model 
(Linker et al., 2012; Cohen et al., 2014), and SVM classification using super‐pixel over‐
segmentation and clustering of Scale Invariant Feature Transform features (Qureshi et al., 
2014) have also been attempted (Qureshi et al., 2015). Although this approach yielded 
results on par with other published results (up to 80% true positive fruit identification of 
fruit in images not used in calibration), results were not sufficient to advocate commercial 
viability. 
Most papers on the application of machine vision to automated counting of fruit on 
tree report calibration results. That is, results are reported of the application of a model 
whose parameters have been adjusted to achieve optimal results on the same or a similar set 
of images are reported. Such values are optimistic. Instead, results of model use in prediction 
of images not used in the calibration process, i.e., a validation set, are required. Such images 
can be, in order of increasing rigour, of different trees in the same orchard as the calibration 
set, of the same trees as used in the calibration set but collected on different days, of trees 
from different orchards and/or seasons to those imaged in the calibration sets. 
Through this paper we would encourage interested researchers to try new machine 
vision approaches with the image sets of this application.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
In preliminary work in 2009, thermal, hyperspectral, RGB, and bandwidth filtered 
(visual & NIR) technologies were assessed to detect mango fruits. Video, 3D (e.g., LIDAR) 
technologies were not tested. It was decided to use RGB technology to collect images since 
there was a significant additional cost with the other collection approaches, and none, other 
than hyperspectral, had resulted in marked improvements in fruit detection over RGB 
imaging. Further work focussed on the development of the camera and lighting rig. 
Mango images were collected of field orchards at night between 2010 and 2013, 
covering four seasons of mango fruit production (Tables 1 and 2). 
Method optimisation was based on images in the calibration set only, without recourse 
to the validation sets. The validation sets varied in the night and season of image acquisition, 
the trees used, the orchard, the number of floodlights in the illumination rig and the distance 
from camera to the tree trunk (Tables 1 and 2). Orchards varied in tree size, such that images 
did not capture the entire tree. In this case, imaging was repeated with the camera located 
further from the tree, allowing the full tree canopy to be imaged (but reducing the pixel 
resolution of the fruit regions).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Criteria for success in machine vision estimation of fruit number in image 
Prediction results should be reported in terms of a common set of statistical terms, to 
allow easier comparison of methods. We recommend reporting of the bias adjusted root 
mean square error of prediction, the bias and the correlation coefficient of determination 
(R2) of the predicted to manual counts of fruit in images of tree canopies, and discriminant 
analysis parameters such as sensitivity, S = TP/(TP+FN), precision, P = TP/(TP+FP) = 1‐FER, 
false error rate, FER = FP/(TP+FP), F1 statistics (harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity 
(where TP is true positive detections of fruit, FP is false positive detections, FN is the 
number of false negative detections). 
For example, for a set of validation images (74 images with 3148 fruits, mean of 42.5 
and SD of 13.3 fruit image‐1) the approach of Payne et al. (2013), Linker et al. (2012) and 
Qureshi et al. (2014) returned a R2 of 0.63, 0.67 and 0.57 and a slope of 1.06, 1.16 and 0.85, 
and a RMSEP of 11.0, 9.1, 11.3, respectively. In terms of discrimination analysis parameters, 
the three approaches returned a S of 57, 63 and 54, a FER of 17.7, 4.0 and 10.2, and a F1 of 
0.67, 0.76 and 0.68, respectively. For this validation set, the best result was obtained using 
the method of Linker et al. (2012). However, the other methods returned better results for 
some validation sets (as explored by Qureshi et al., 2015). A key issue is consistency of 
prediction across image sets, i.e., if the machine count is always 50% of manual count, an 
adjustment factor can be added. In general, the approaches of Linker et al. (2012) and 
Qureshi et al. (2014) resulted in the reduction of false positives to almost insignificant levels, 
but false negatives (i.e., missed fruit) remained significant and a barrier to accurate crop 
yield prediction (Table 3).

Causes of misclassification 
Daylight imaging involves variable lighting, especially under the strong tropical 
sunlight associated with mango production (Figure 1a). Common problems included 
significant shadows on fruit (Figure 1a), with consequent misidentification of either well or 
unlit areas of the background as fruit. As the background was well illuminated, fruit was 
often detected on adjoining trees. Imaging under overcast conditions proved the most 
successful for day operation, with reduced shadowing. Floodlights and flash illumination 
were tried to augment the variable natural sunlight (Figure 1c), but this approach still 
resulted in variation in image illumination intensity due to variation in sunlight. 
Errors encountered in daytime imaging included falsely detected fragments of white 
signs, fruit on background trees, second detections of a fruit split by a leaf or stem and 
misdetections of two or more fruit as a single fruit.

Night time imaging is recommended. This approach almost completely eliminated 
false detection of background features and also of fruit on other trees. By underexposing 
images, fruit contrast relative to foliage was increased (Figure 1b). However, shadowing 
remained an issue, and variation in shadowing between trees occurred as a consequence of 
differences in canopy structure. Night time imaging changed the profile of image errors, with 
no detections of fruit from background trees occurring and the rate of detection of multiple 
fruit as a single fruit decreased significantly, however, occlusion and fruit splitting remained 
significant (Payne et al., 2014). 
The false negative (missed fruit) issue was due to fruit occlusion by a leaf or stem, 
leaving visible fruit areas too small for machine vision recognition, although these areas 
were still recognisable as fruit to a human observer. New machine vision approaches are 
required to address this issue. 
Each season introduced new types of errors. For example, in one imaging session, a 
large number of moths were present following a heavy rainfall event, and were attracted to 
the artificial lighting. The moths created false positives. Also causing issues were tree trunk 
painting, red tagging or labelling, undertaken for orchard management (Figure 2). For 
example, in one orchard red ribbons were present on trees. Where the ribbons crossed the 
trunk of the tree it created ‘mango sized’ false positives. During the course of the project, 
each of these items was distinguished from fruit through the inclusion of additional filters. 
Other events assisted fruit pixel segmentation, e.g., the presence of strong colouring on the 
fruit, or the formation of a dew on fruit, creating light backscatter when illuminated (Figure 
1b).

Integration into an on-farm system 
Adoption of a fruit detection technique by the mango industry will require (a) ease of 
use, and (b) a system that delivers management benefit. For example, a system is required 
which geo‐locates the images. In our work we employed a sonar sensor to trigger image 
acquisition based on alignment of the imaging equipment with the trunk of the tree. This 
approach proved difficult to implement in practice as tree trunks were often obscured by 
foliage from the tree itself, or too many additional trigger points existed such as long grass 
and irrigations systems (Figure 3). Trees could be passive RFID tagged, with detection 
technology added to the imaging vehicle. However, such tags cost ca $5 each, a high expense 
for a large orchard. It seems likely that future systems will use a geo‐location system based 
on GNSS or radio technologies.

Instant, in‐field, image processing is not required for this application, unlike a fruit 
harvesting application. Images could be captured, returned to a central server, and 
processed at leisure. Data (yield) could be displayed on a map relative to tree or row. Yield 
over subsequent seasons could be reviewed, and the orchard managed at a tree level (like a 
dairy herd, with underperforming individuals removed, and over performing individuals 
selected for breeding). Conversely, it could be useful to consider the display of partial results 
for review and feedback by the human operator of the system. In‐field recalibration or 
adjustment of machine vision parameters may also be viable. 
Note that in mature orchards, the tree canopy closes along the row. In this 
circumstance, imaging of a single canopy is not possible. Fruit count data can be allocated to 
the identity of the nearest tree trunk (Figure 4). 
 
Figure 4. Orchard maturity: (a) mature, (b) immature. 
In addition to the machine vision problem of identification of fruit within an image, 
there also exists the issue of relating fruit count in canopy images to the actual fruit load on 
the tree. This relationship will be a function of fruit load and canopy architecture (both 
affecting the number of fruit hidden from view in an image). Prediction statistics should thus 
also be presented on the relationship of image count to tree or orchard fruit count. Of course 
an espalier (planar) format would best suit imaging. Fruits within canopy are of lower 
quality, so in one sense a fruit count that excludes these fruits may be useful. 
RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE STUDY 
Further work is required in the development of algorithms for fruit segmentation and 
detection, augmented by: 
• 3D collection and modelling of the fruit and trees, possibly using video, stereovision 
or LIDAR 
• hyper‐spectral imaging to improve segmentation of fruit pixels. 
• self‐calibration based on inputs from the local environment (e.g., lighting conditions) 
• comparison of algorithms using the same calibration and validation sets – the image 
sets of the current study are available on request for comparative evaluation of 
methods.
