A B S T R A C T
Fruit is a vital component of the human diet, and its quality attributes are of major concern to consumers. Fruit 
quality is primarily enhanced during the on-tree growth phase. In-field fruit quality detection provides critical 
data for precision orchard management, thereby enhancing fruit quality at the source of the supply chain. Driven 
by growing demand, research on in-field fruit quality detection is rapidly expanding. Therefore, a comprehensive 
review is essential to track the state-of-the-art inspection technologies and devices tailored to orchard envi­
ronments. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in promising in-field fruit quality detection 
technologies, which primarily rely on instrumentation based on mechanical, acoustic vibrational, optical, and 
electrochemical principles. It evaluates the application scenarios and performance of current portable devices, 
wearable sensors, noncontact devices, and flexible robotic manipulators integrated with quality sensing capa­
bilities. Furthermore, potential pathways are outlined to facilitate the transition of fruit quality detection 
technologies from postharvest applications to field-based implementation. Different detection technologies and 
devices are suited to specific application scenarios. Portable devices leverage miniaturization and cost- 
effectiveness for routine spot checks. Wearable sensors enable continuous monitoring of long-term fruit qual­
ity changes. Noncontact devices achieve orchard-scale assessments through broad spatial coverage. Flexible 
robotic manipulators with quality sensing capabilities allow integrated harvesting and grading operations. 
However, challenges remain in terms of robustness, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness under real orchard con­
ditions. Future developments will focus on achieving intelligent and automatic in-field fruit quality detection 
through innovations in micro-nano fabrication, structural design, advanced algorithms, and the integration of 
multiple sensors.
1. Introduction
Fresh fruits are indispensable foods in promoting human health as 
they are rich in vitamins, trace elements, antioxidants, minerals, and 
dietary fibre (Sharma & Deutsch, 2023). As people increasingly pursue 
quality-conscious lifestyles, the demand for high-quality fruits has 
increased significantly. Currently, the demand for high-quality fruits 
exceeds the supply, and their prices have hit record highs. In contrast, 
poor-quality fruits are unmarketable. Hence, the fruit industry must 
prioritize quality over quantity. Key quality attributes of fruits encom­
pass appearance, flavor, texture, and nutrients (Gao et al., 2025; Hussain 
et al., 2025). Appearance is mainly determined by the size, shape, 
wholeness, defects, gloss, and color, while flavor is a complex and 
diverse trait and is mainly composed of aroma, sweetness, acidity, 
bitterness, and astringency (Shi et al., 2025). Fruit texture represents the 
combination of mechanical, structural, and surface characteristics 
perceived by visual, auditory, kinesthetic, and tactile senses (Li, Sun, 
et al., 2024). Firmness, crispness, toughness, mealiness, and juiciness are 
common terms to describe fruit texture.
Fruit quality is fundamentally determined during preharvest devel­
opment and remains largely immutable after harvest (Lohner et al., 
2021). Consequently, in-field quality detection has emerged as a pivotal 
strategy for quality enhancement at the source of the supply chain. The 
use of nondestructive detection technologies during the fruit growth

stage enables precision orchard management and provides critical data 
for harvest decisions. Accurate assessment of maturity is crucial, as 
premature harvest compromises flavor and storability, whereas delayed 
harvest increases susceptibility to damage and spoilage, leading to sig­
nificant postharvest losses (Walsh et al., 2020). Fruits of different 
qualities are mixed after picking. During transportation and storage, 
diseased fruit may rot and contaminate surrounding fruit, rendering all 
fruit unmarketable due to food safety concerns. Real-time detection

for the fresh market and those of low quality for processing before 
transportation and storage (Yu et al., 2024). This could reduce fruit 
losses and help growers significantly reduce postharvest handling costs. 
Therefore, it is necessary to accurately monitor the quality information 
of growing fruits to ensure that highly nutritious foods are delivered to 
consumers at low costs (Van Beers et al., 2015).
While numerous nondestructive techniques have been developed for 
fruit quality assessment (Al-Dairi et al., 2023), existing reviews have 
predominantly focused on their postharvest and indoor applications 
(Akter et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2021; Mohd Ali et al., 2023; Porep et al., 
2015). Among them, mechanical, acoustic vibrational, optical, and 
electrochemical techniques dominate the field of fruit quality detection. 
Each technique offers distinct advantages. Mechanical methods are 
generally applied to evaluate a fruit’s localized firmness. Acoustic vi­
bration techniques, on the other hand, are advantageous in assessing the 
global firmness and internal defects of the fruit. Optical techniques allow 
nondestructive evaluation of multiple fruit quality attributes, mainly 
including soluble solids content (SSC), acidity, and dry matter content 
(DMC). Electrochemical techniques are advantageous due to high 
sensitivity and correlation with the physiological status of fruits. How­
ever, transferring these technologies to the orchard environment poses 
significant challenges. Orchards are unstructured environments with 
highly variable light, temperature, and humidity (Bechar & Cl´ement, 
2017), which adversely affect detection accuracy and demand far 
greater device and algorithm ruggedness than controlled laboratory 
settings. Consequently, conventional benchtop and online systems are 
often ill-suited for field deployment due to their bulky size and opera­
tional complexity.
In response, research efforts are increasingly toward the develop­
ment of adaptable platforms and algorithms for specific in-field 
scenarios (Fig. 1). The emerging platforms can be categorized into four 
distinct pathways based on the application scenarios, including portable 
devices for spot-check sampling, wearable sensors for continuous in-situ 
monitoring, noncontact systems for spatial-scale mapping, and flexible 
robotic manipulators that integrate detection and harvesting functions. 
However, in-field detection of fruit quality in orchard environments is a 
relatively emerging field. A comprehensive review in this domain is still 
lacking, which poses a significant challenge for introductory re­
searchers. This paper systematically reviews recent advances in fruit 
quality inspection technologies and related devices developed for or­
chard scenarios. The specific objectives are to 1) elucidate the funda­
mental principles and in-field applicability of mechanical, acoustic 
vibrational, optical, and electrochemical sensing technologies, 2) criti­
cally review the instrumentation and data analytics employed in 
portable, wearable, noncontact, and sensing-enabled robotic systems, 3) 
discuss the application scenarios, merits, and limitations of each tech­
nological pathway, and 4) outline future perspectives for enhancing the 
robustness and intelligence of in-field fruit quality detection.
2. Promising technologies for in-field assessment of fruit quality
2.1. Mechanical technology
Fruits are non-homogeneous composites composed of water, inor­
ganic salts, and organic matter that exhibit viscoelastic behavior over 
time due to compositional and structural variations. Mechanical tech­
nology has been applied for many years to nondestructively obtain in­
ternal quality information of fruits, usually focusing on the assessment 
of fruit firmness and ripeness (both related). In general, mechanical 
technology can be classified into two broad categories according to the 
detection principle.
(1) Microforce-deformation methods. The Magness-Taylor (MT) 
method is the gold standard for measuring fruit firmness. A probe pen­
etrates a specified location on peeled fruit at a certain depth to measure 
the rupture force as an indicator of fruit firmness. However, the rupture 
force is obtained in an invasive manner. For nondestructive measure­
ments, several means have been reported. These methods involve 
applying microforces within the fruit’s elastic range and then extracting 
firmness-related data from the resulting force or deformation (Wang

2.2. Acoustic vibrational technology

vibration response of a fruit is directly related to its overall physical

with mass m can be simplified as a spring-mass-damped system. When 
the system vibrates under the excitation of external force f(t), its vi­

tural characteristics and internal components. By measuring the reflec­
tance, absorption, and transmittance of fruits to light, quality attributes

can be assessed nondestructively. The visible/near-infrared (Vis/NIR) 
spectra at 380–2500 nm band have been widely used for fruit quality 
assessment because of their good penetration ability and relatively low- 
cost instrumentation (Walsh et al., 2020). Vis spectroscopy provides 
information about the pigmentation of fruits, such as chlorophyll, ca­
rotenoids, and anthocyanins. As fruit ripens, its firmness decreases, and 
pigmentation changes (Hertog et al., 2025). Thus, Vis spectroscopy 
indirectly conveys information about fruit ripeness and firmness. Com­
ponents of water, pectin in the cell wall, cellulose, and hemicellulose are 
correlated with fruit quality. The C–H and O–H bonds within those 
components result in absorption in the NIR region (Gao et al., 2025), so 
that fruits of different qualities show differences in the NIR spectra. 
Therefore, quantitative or qualitative fruit quality prediction models can 
be established based on NIR spectral data.
As shown in Fig. 3, when incident light hits the fruit, a portion of the 
light undergoes specular reflection on the surface, carrying the surface 
information of the fruit. Since the internal properties of fruits are related 
to their surface properties, the internal quality attributes of fruits can be 
assessed indirectly through the specular reflection mode. The remaining 
light penetrates the fruit surface and is scattered by the internal tissues 
in indeterminate directions. The light that exits the fruit on the same side 
as the incident light is regarded as the diffuse reflection light. Diffuse 
light penetrates the fruit typically to a depth of less than 10 mm from the 
exocarp, so it does not directly carry optical information about the 
centre region of the fruit.
At the same time, part of the light is absorbed by the fruit tissue and 
converted into molecular vibrational energy or thermal energy, leading 
to the attenuation of light intensity. The rest of the transmitted light 
penetrates the entire fruit and carries optical information of the central 
flesh tissue, thus providing insights into the internal quality of the fruit. 
Optical techniques provide flexible instrument configurations through 
specular, diffuse, and transmitted modes. In specular and diffuse 
reflection modes, the light source and the sensor are placed on the same 
side of the sample. In transmission mode, the light source and sensor are 
configured on both sides of the sample to meet different inspection 
needs.
2.4. Electrochemical technology
For on-site fruit quality assessment in orchards, electrochemical 
technology is advantageous due to its high sensitivity and correlation 
with the physiological status of fruits. It is primarily divided into two 
categories: one based on detecting the intrinsic electrical properties of 
fruit tissues, and the other relying on chemical sensing mechanisms to 
identify volatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted by the fruit.
Fruit quality detection methods based on electrical properties enable 
nondestructive, in-situ assessment of internal quality and physiological 
status. This is usually achieved through the measurement of dielectric 
properties, impedance spectra, and tissue conductivity (Feng et al., 
2024; Nayak et al., 2025). In the process of fruit growth, maturation, 
damage, physical injury, or decay, accompanying biochemical reactions 
alter the distribution and intensity of internal bioelectric fields (Lin, 
Chen, et al., 2024). These microscopic changes are macroscopically 
observable as variations in the dielectric properties of the fruit. Dielec­
tric property detection primarily characterizes a material’s polarization 
behavior and energy dissipation under an external electric field. This is 
commonly quantified via the dielectric constant and loss factor, which 
exhibit high correlations with moisture content and density of fruit tis­
sues (Liu, Ma, et al., 2025). Hence, this relationship provides both the 
theoretical foundation and practical utility for dielectric techniques in 
determining the ripeness of apples (Guo et al., 2011) and bananas 
(Soltani et al., 2011), the water content of sugarcane (Naderi-Boldaji 
et al., 2015), and the SSC and firmness of pears (Guo et al., 2015) and 
kiwifruit (Fazayeli et al., 2019). Impedance spectroscopy analysis fol­
lows a similar principle. It employs a low-amplitude alternating current 
across a broad frequency range to the fruit (Cheng et al., 2022). Then, 
impedance modulus and phase angle are measured, facilitating the 
construction of equivalent circuit models that reflect cellular membrane 
integrity and intracellular ion distribution. This approach is highly 
sensitive to structural changes at the cellular level resulting from 
ripening, chilling injury, or disease manifestations (Feng, Sui, et al., 
2025; Li et al., 2019). Conductivity measurement offers a more 
streamlined alternative. By assessing ionic conductivity at specific fre­
quencies, it effectively identifies stress-induced damage or spoilage- 
related softening in fruits (Justicia et al., 2017). When integrated with 
portable electrodes, conductivity measurement allows for real-time, in-

In-field chemical sensing methods mainly detect VOCs released 
during fruit growth and metabolism. Each variety of fruit has a unique 
odour conferred by emitted VOCs (e.g., esters, alcohols, and ketones), 
which act as key indicators for assessing fruit quality (Li, Liu, et al., 
2025; Zhao, Liu, & Jiang, 2024). Electronic nose (e-nose) is a sensory 
bionic instrument developed by imitating the human olfactory mecha­
nism and is capable of detecting VOCs from fruits (Zhong et al., 2024). 
As shown in Fig. 4, a typical e-nose is composed of a sampling unit, a 
detection unit, and a data processing unit (Mohd Ali et al., 2020). The 
sampling unit often uses static headspace sampling to collect and deliver 
VOCs to the detection unit (Khlaif et al., 2024). The detection unit is the 
core of the system. It contains an array of cross-sensitive gas sensors 
housed in a sealed gas chamber. Common sensor types include piezo­
electric, electrochemical, optical, and thermal conductivity sensors 
(Peris & Escuder-Gilabert, 2009). The data processing unit analyses 
sensor signals using pattern recognition algorithms and outputs the re­
sults (Li, Qi, et al., 2025). Among these, metal-oxide semiconductor 
(MOS) gas sensors are classical electrochemical sensors that are widely 
used for fruit VOC detection. The working mechanism of the MOS gas 
sensor involves redox reactions between gas molecules and the sensor 
surface (Zhang, Liu, & Wei, 2025). These reactions alter the charge 
distribution and carrier concentration in the metal oxide layer, leading 
to measurable changes in electrical resistance (Liu, Zheng, et al., 2025). 
Such sensors can be incorporated into portable e-nose systems, which 
are suitable for in-field monitoring of fruit ripeness and early spoilage in 
orchards.
Olfactory visualization is another emerging e-nose technology, 
which was first proposed in 2000 (Rakow & Suslick, 2000). Olfactory 
visualization technology utilizes sensor arrays made of chemically sen­
sitive dyes (Arslan et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2024). These dyes bind to 
small molecules in the VOCs through intermolecular interactions (e.g., 
π-π bonds, metal bonds, hydrogen bonds) and undergo a color change. 
Selecting suitable color-sensitive materials requires balancing sensi­
tivity and selectivity toward VOCs. Commonly used materials include 
Brønsted acid/base dyes (e.g., pH indicators), Lewis acid/base dyes (e.g., 
metal complexes with open ligand sites or color-emitting agents con­
taining metal ions), redox dyes, as well as solvatochromic and vapor­
chromic dyes (Li, da Monteiro, et al., 2025; Zhao, Zhang, et al., 2024). 
However, dyes tend to produce strong signals only for gases with redox 
properties or acid/base properties, and are not sensitive to gases lacking 
these characteristics, such as ethanol, acetone, and toluene. In recent 
years, researchers have combined functional materials with dyes to 
improve cross-sensitivity. Composite color-sensitive materials can be 
prepared by combining metal-organic frameworks, polymers, and gold 
nanoparticles with dyes for a wider range of gas components (Chen 
et al., 2022). Color information before and after the reaction is captured 
using smartphones or other imaging devices (Li et al., 2020). Then, color 
features are extracted for the identification of VOCs and the determi­
nation of their concentrations (Lu, Chen, et al., 2022). The use of color 
change as a direct sensing signal reduces device complexity and cost, 
making the technology well-suited for distributed deployment in or­
chards and large-scale VOC screening. It is noteworthy that both MOS 
sensors and olfactory visualization sensors rely on specific chemical 
reactions between sensing materials and gas molecules. These systems 
share common fundamental principles in sensitive material design and 
contribute to dynamic and visual monitoring of fruit quality in orchards.
2.5. Comparative analysis of different technologies
As shown in Table 1, a comparative assessment of the major 
nondestructive techniques for fruit quality evaluation reveals consider­
able differences in detection principles, measurable parameters, and 
practical applicability under field conditions.
Mechanical techniques, which rely on measurements of deformation, 
resistive force, and elasticity, are primarily used to quantify firmness- 
related properties such as elastic modulus and compressive strength. 
These approaches provide direct and accurate textural assessments and 
demonstrate consistent accuracy. A key limitation is the low throughput 
and the need for direct contact, which poses a risk of micro-damage to 
delicate fruit tissues. Acoustic vibration techniques utilize time-domain 
or frequency-domain response to infer physical quality attributes such as 
firmness and structural integrity. These systems exhibit high sensitivity 
to changes in tissue elasticity and structural integrity. However, their 
performance is susceptible to environmental noise and vibration, and 
measurement stability is highly dependent on equipment precision, 
potentially compromising reliability in open orchard environments.
Machine vision systems assess appearance quality, size classification, 
and surface defects by analyzing color, shape, size, and texture. These 
systems offer high throughput, potential for automation, and compati­
bility with field-deployable setups. A principal constraint is their reli­
ance on external features; internal quality attributes remain beyond 
their evaluation scope. Vis/NIR spectroscopy is suitable for estimating 
internal chemical indicators such as SSC and moisture levels. These 
methods achieve high precision and are particularly suitable for rapid 
in-situ measurement. Nevertheless, estimation accuracy can be affected 
by variations in fruit surface properties (e.g., peel color, roughness). 
Hyperspectral imaging extends optical sensing by integrating spatial and 
spectral information, enabling spatial-scale quality and compositional 
evaluation. Despite its comprehensive capabilities, high equipment 
costs, substantial data volumes, and computationally intensive pro­
cessing hinder its adoption for high-throughput field applications.

System performance is also contingent upon controlled illumination and

Electrochemical techniques, typified by e-nose systems, detect VOC 
fingerprints to evaluate maturity and incipient decay. They are suitable 
for monitoring field environments. Yet, these systems often exhibit semi- 
quantitative repeatability and are highly sensitive to ambient tempera­
ture, humidity, and background volatiles, which complicates quantita­
tive analysis. Olfactory visualization devices, which utilize colorimetric 
sensor arrays, offer a low-cost alternative for screening, despite their 
generally limited sensor stability and slow response times. Electrical 
impedance techniques analyze impedance spectra, phase angles, and 
amplitude characteristics to estimate moisture content, firmness, and 
maturity. Principal advantages include instrumental simplicity, low 
operational cost, and suitability for integration with flexible robotic 
manipulators. However, impedance measurements are significantly 
influenced by fruit moisture content, temperature fluctuations, as well 
as by variations in electrode contact pressure.
In summary, the presented nondestructive techniques enable com­
plementary assessment strategies for various field scenarios, including 
portable spot-checking (S1), continuous in-situ monitoring (S2), non- 
contact spatial mapping (S3), and integrated sensing-harvesting ma­
nipulators (S4). Thus, the strategic fusion of these modalities is crucial 
for developing robust, accurate, and scalable systems that can underpin 
intelligent, data-driven quality management in the fruit industry.
3. Technological pathways for in-field detection of fruit quality
3.1. Spot-checking with portable devices
Portable devices are suitable for spot checks of fruit quality in the 
orchard due to operational simplicity, compact size, and cost- 
effectiveness (Cavallaro et al., 2024; Rabasco-Vílchez et al., 2024). 
Among the key quality attributes targeted by portable devices, firmness 
and SSC represent critical market-driven metrics, which can be assessed 
through mechanical, acoustic vibration, and optical techniques.
Mechanical portable devices are particularly effective for evaluating 
quality indicators related to the physical properties of fruits. Timm et al. 
(1996) designed a portable device to automatically measure fruit firm­
ness. The fruit was placed between two parallel plates and gently com­
pressed at a constant speed driven by a motor. The initial slope of the 
force displacement curve was found to be the best indicator to charac­
terize fruit firmness. The bio-yield force is another nondestructive index 
of fruit firmness. A portable apparatus for acquiring the fruit bioyield 
force was reported (Imanpanah et al., 2015). The volume of the appa­
ratus was 500 mm × 200 mm × 550 mm. For miniaturization, Wang 
et al. (2020) developed a handheld prototype with a volume of 66 mm ×
63 mm × 192 mm to detect peach firmness by sensing the resistance of 
fruits under micro deformation (Fig. 5(a)). The results showed that there 
was a logarithmic correlation between prototype readings and MT 
firmness, and the coefficient of determination (R2) was 0.6822. In 
addition, a portable device based on the impacted method was reported 
(Slaughter et al., 2009). A low-mass tip fitted with an accelerometer 
tapped the pear at a constant velocity. The peak deceleration (A) and the 
time (t) to reach A were extracted from the impact response curve to 
calculate the fruit firmness indicator as shown in Eq. (4). The mea­
surement speed of the impact method is obviously faster than that of the 
microforce-deformation method. However, the deformation of fruit 
upon impact decreases with increasing firmness, resulting in shorter 
contact times and lower signal-to-noise ratios. Besides, the above 
methods can only measure the localized fruit firmness.

overall quality of fruits. The acoustic vibration response of a fruit can be 
acquired by contact (accelerometers and piezoelectric transducers) or 
noncontact sensors (microphones and laser Doppler vibrometers). For 
miniaturization and low cost, portable acoustic vibration devices often 
use microphones as vibration pickup transducers. As shown in Fig. 5(b), 
the apple resonant frequency was measured with a portable Aweta™ 
AFS sensor (AWETA, Pijnacker, Netherlands). The device determined 
fruit weight, gently taped the fruit, and analyzed the resulting sound to

Optical devices are prone to perform better than mechanical and 
acoustic vibrational devices in predicting chemically relevant quality 
attributes of fruits. NIR spectra acquired in diffuse reflection mode are 
directly related to the internal quality of fruits. The performance of a 
handheld NIR spectroscopy device was verified in predicting moisture 
contents and wet fat of on-tree olives (M´arquez & Maza, 2023). The 
acquired spectra in the wavelength range of 900–1700 nm, combined 
with partial least squares regression (PLSR) models, allowed for moni­
toring the ripening evolution of olives and determining the optimum 
harvest time. The above study employed a commercial portable spec­
trometer to obtain spectra of fruits. Wide spectral wavelength ranges 
increased prices and data processing costs. As shown in Fig. 5(c), a light- 
emitting diode (LED) and a Si photodiode detector provided a low-cost 
solution to design an NIR diffuse reflectance device (Yao et al., 2023). 
Since the depth of diffuse light penetrating the fruit is usually within 10 
mm, the diffuse reflected spectrum carries the quality information of 
localized flesh tissues and is difficult to reflect the quality of the whole 
fruit. In contrast, transmitted light that penetrates the whole fruit can be 
used to assess quality information in the central region of the fruit. 
Therefore, portable devices in transmission mode are able to assess the 
whole fruit quality (Wang et al., 2017). But the light source needs to 
penetrate the whole fruit to reach the detector during measurement, so 
the larger and denser the fruit, the higher the power of the light source 
required. A spectrometer is the core sensing component of portable 
optical devices, and its miniaturization has emerged as a technological 
trend. Advances in sensing principles, functional materials, and fabri­
cation processes continue to drive performance optimization of 
application-specific spectrometers, leading to enhanced overall capa­
bilities. For instance, Zhao et al. (2025) proposed a method for fabri­
cating miniaturized spectrometers with nanometer resolution based on 
electrohydrodynamic printing, which holds promise for substantially 
reducing device size while maintaining high sensing accuracy.
3.2. Continuous in-situ monitoring with wearable sensors
While portable devices are widely used for spot-checking fruit 
quality, they are generally unsuitable for continuous monitoring in or­
chards. Recent advances in material science and electronics have facil­
itated the development of wearable sensors, which offer a promising 
alternative for the in-situ and continuous assessment of fruit quality 
during growth. These sensors facilitate accurate tracking of multiple 
physiological parameters, providing valuable insights into fruit devel­
opment, supporting cultivation management, and potentially enhancing 
yield. Current wearable sensors are primarily applied to long-term 
physiological monitoring of climacteric fruits, with an emphasis on 
ripeness-related biomarkers.
The earliest wearable sensors for fruit quality assessment were pre­
dominantly rigid. A wearable vibration device in the use of a piezo­
electric generator and detector was constructed to measure acoustic 
vibration responses of grapes (Takahashi et al., 2010). The second 
resonance frequency (f2) was extracted from the response signal and 
combined with the grape diameter (d) to calculate the elasticity index 
(EI, d2f2
2 ) for the characterization of fruit firmness. A similar sandwich- 
type wearable vibrometer was demonstrated for detecting firmness and 
split-pit defects of on-tree peaches (Kawai et al., 2018). Experimental 
results showed that f2 and the third resonance frequency (f3) had a linear 
relationship with the reference firmness value, with R2 of 0.825 and 
0.819, respectively. The ratio of f3 to f2 was calculated to discern split-pit 
peaches.
In contrast to the rigid devices mentioned above, flexible wearable 
sensors exhibit superior mechanical compliance and flexibility. They can 
be laminated to the irregular surfaces of fruits, thus improving the 
detection accuracy. In terms of mechanical sensing, a stretchable strain 
sensor based on graphite and carbon nanotubes (CNT) was prepared 
(Tang et al., 2019). The synergistic effect between the two-dimensional 
graphite sheets and the one-dimensional CNTs allows the CNTs to bridge 
cracks in the graphite sheets under strain, thereby enhancing the sen­
sor’s tensile performance. The strain sensor can be fixed on a zucchini to 
reflect the fruit’s expansion process by detecting the resistance change of 
the sensor.
For chemical sensing, flexible wearable ethylene gas sensors are a 
typical representative. A wearable chemoreceptive ethylene gas sensor 
was fabricated based on a nanocomposite (SPM) consisting of single- 
walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), palladium nanoparticles (PdNPs), 
and a copper-based metal-organic framework (Cu-MOF-74) (Yan et al., 
2024). The nanocomposite was attached to a fork finger electrode on a 
flexible PET substrate (AIEFP) prepared by laser direct writing. The 
sensor can be directly attached to the surface of kiwifruit to realize 
dynamic in-situ monitoring of ethylene gas release and effectively track 
the fruit’s ripening process. To make the detection results more intui­
tive, You et al. (2024) achieved the visualization of gas sensors (Fig. 6
(a)). Based on electrostatic spinning and vacuum filtration techniques, 
colorimetric chips were prepared using eight dye/Metal-organic 
framework (MOF) composites and two Pd2+/dye/NH2-UiO-66 com­
posites. Polydimethylsiloxane materials with microfluidic channels 
were prepared and integrated with the colorimetric chip to construct a 
flexible wearable olfactory visualization sensor. The feasibility of this 
wearable olfactory visualization sensor for monitoring the fruit ripening 
process was demonstrated by continuously measuring VOC emissions

measurements demonstrated that decreased fruit firmness correlated 
with a significant reduction in both the impedance magnitude (|Z|) and 
its imaginary component (Zi). These findings confirmed the sensor’s 
efficacy for reliable firmness evaluation. However, there are still several 
factors that limit the practical application of wearable sensors in or­

in complex unstructured environments still need to be systematically 
verified.
Wearable sensing technologies have developed more quickly in 
biomedicine than in food science. For example, Mei et al. (2025)
designed a wearable 3D paper-based microfluidic electrochemical 
platform for in-situ sweat monitoring, which demonstrated the potential 
of flexible, biocompatible interfaces for continuous biomarker detection. 
Zhang, Li, et al. (2025) developed a fully integrated multimodal wear­
able system that enabled simultaneous metabolite and temperature 
sensing, underscoring progress in real-time, multi-parameter data 
acquisition. Moreover, Jeon et al. (2025) reported a molecularly 
imprinted polymer biosensor integrated with a deep learning framework 
for salivary biomarker analysis, reflecting the growing integration be­
tween intelligent algorithms and sensing hardware. Collectively, ad­
vances in soft materials, system-level integration, and AI-powered signal 
interpretation demonstrate significant potential for translation to the in- 
situ monitoring of fruit quality during growth.
3.3. Spatial-scale mapping with noncontact devices
Manually assessing fruit quality across entire orchards is time- 
consuming and labor-intensive, whether using portable devices or 
attaching wearable sensors directly to the fruit. The height of fruit 
canopies further complicates manual operation. Noncontact sensing 
technologies have emerged as a viable solution for spatial-scale mapping 
by shifting the focus from individual fruit quality assessment to the 
overall spatial distribution of quality parameters across the orchard. 
These technologies are capable of simultaneously evaluating multiple 
fruits, targeting externally visible or spectrally active quality indicators, 
such as color, size, SSC, and firmness.
Machine vision enables noncontact acquisition of image information 
from multiple on-tree fruits in a target area for size, color, and ripeness 
assessment. Maceachern et al. (2023) used a digital camera to take im­
ages of blueberries in an orchard. Multiple YOLO versions were used to 
assess blueberry ripeness. Among them, YOLOv4 Small achieved the 
best triple classification results with a mean average precision (MAP) of 
79.79 %. Compared with digital cameras, smartphone is another prac­
tical tool for acquiring fruit images due to their low cost and easy 
operation. The YOLOv4 model with an integrated convolutional block 
attention module (CBAM) was implemented to evaluate apple size and 
maturity using images that were captured by a smartphone (Lu, Lu, 
et al., 2022). The CBAM was able to adaptively adjust the weights of the 
channel and spatial information in the images to efficiently focus on the 
key features related to ripeness, thereby improving the detection accu­
racy. The accuracy of this model for triple classification of apple 
maturity on two varieties ranged from 71.0 % to 92.6 %, and the R2 for 
fruit height and width prediction were 0.68 and 0.66, respectively. To 
further address fruit occlusion and overlap problems in complex orchard 
environments, a model for detecting olive fruit ripeness was developed 
based on an improved weighted bi-directional feature pyramid network 
that incorporated a CBAM (Zhu et al., 2024). The established model can 
still classify olive fruits into six ripeness levels under the condition of 
occlusion and overlapping. The MAP for four varieties of olive fruits is 
above 93 %.
However, external image information of fruits is only indirectly 
related to their internal qualities. In order to obtain directly the internal 
characteristics of fruits, scholars have developed a variety of noncontact 
detection devices. To perform real-time in-field measurements, a self- 
developed e-nose device containing 13 MOS gas sensors was reported 
for monitoring the growth of peaches (Voss et al., 2020). The e-nose was 
placed at the bottom of a peach tree in the middle of the orchard. Air 
containing the fruit volatile compounds was drawn into the sensor 
chamber using a fan. The peach growth stages were divided into 4 levels 
using a random forest (RF) classification algorithm combined with linear 
discriminant analysis (LDA), demonstrating the feasibility of the 
developed e-nose for fruit maturity assessment in the orchard. Although 
e-noses can detect a variety of VOCs, they often exhibit limited speci­
ficity when exposed to chemically similar species or interfering gases. 
Such interferences introduce signal noise that undermines sensor 
selectivity, increasing the risk of false positives or negatives and thereby 
compromising the accurate identification of target analytes. To address 
these limitations, recent research has focused on tailoring sensing ma­
terial architectures to enhance selectivity. A notable development is the 
adoption of bilayer sensor designs that incorporate a functional over­
layer or filter to selectively screen out interferents. As shown in Fig. 7(a), 
Rh-SnO₂ sensing films combined with a catalytic CeO₂ overlayer enabled 
rapid, cost-effective, and highly selective detection of trace aromatic 
hydrocarbons in complex gas mixtures (Jeong et al., 2023).

quality can provide more accurate information. As shown in Fig. 7(b), a 
noncontact acoustic vibration method was proposed for in-situ

3.4. Integrated detection and harvesting with flexible robotic 
manipulators
Harvesting accounts for more than 40 % of the total labor cost in fruit 
production (Hern et al., 2023). To address this, robotic harvesters have 
been introduced in modern orchards. However, conventional rigid ma­
nipulators primarily function as end-effectors for gripping and lack in­
tegrated fruit quality assessment capabilities. This limitation leads to 
two main issues. One is the high rate of fruit crush damage. The second is 
that fruit sorting needs to rely on additional downstream equipment. As 
a result, it is difficult to realize in-field sorting simultaneously with 
harvesting, which ultimately reduces the commodity rate of high-value 
fruits. In contrast, flexible manipulators equipped with tactile, optical, 
or electrochemical sensors can assess properties such as firmness and 
SSC during gripping. The integrated approach is particularly suitable for 
fruits where in-field sorting during harvest is commercially viable and 
for quality indicators that can be reliably estimated within the short 
timeframe of robotic manipulation. By providing real-time data on fruit 
quality attributes, flexible robotic manipulators enable dynamic 
adjustment of gripping force and support immediate in-field grading, 
thereby mitigating postharvest losses and enhancing the overall effi­
ciency of fruit production.
In terms of tactile perception, a dual-sensing flexible manipulator 
was developed (Jin et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 8(a), a force-sensitive 
resistor sensor and a bending degree sensor were pasted on each side of 
the finger. The stiffness index H (H=F/θ) was constructed by measuring 
the contact force (F) and bending angle (θ) during grasping the sample. 
PLSR method combined with competitive adaptive reweighted sampling 
was used to establish a kiwifruit firmness prediction model. The R2
P was 
0.813, and the root mean square error of prediction set (RMSEP) was 
0.369 N/mm. However, the reported manipulator took 5 s to detect a 
fruit. To improve efficiency, Zhang et al. (2024) designed a pneumatic 
flexible manipulator with four fingers. Three piezoresistive sensing units 
were integrated into each finger. The piezoresistive sensitive layer was 
made of gauze-graphene/CNT composite with a sensitivity of 3.30 kPa−1 
and a response time of 0.2 s. The tactile signals from sensor array were 
downscaled by principal component analysis (PCA), and a support 
vector regression (SVR) model was established to achieve simultaneous 
classification of fruit maturity and size. The detection time was signifi­
cantly reduced to 2.5 s. In conclusion, the tactile approach models the 
gripper deformation and the interaction force between the gripper and 
the fruit, which relies on the sensors to feedback accurate electrical 
signals. Attaching too many sensors to the finger surface may affect its 
deformation, leading to challenges in accurate modeling. Li, Zhao, et al. 
(2024) proposed a visual-tactile fusion solution. The robotic arm carried 
a parallel jaw integrated with a tactile sensor. A RealSense camera 
localized the fruit and planned the gripping trajectory. An 8-frame 
sequence of extruded deformation images was captured by an RGB 
camera under constant gripping force. A tactile self-attentive capsule 
network model was developed to analyze the spatial deformation fea­
tures of the sequence and to predict the kiwifruit firmness (R2 = 0.8531, 
RMSE = 2.5078 N/cm2). To further optimize the gripping adaptability, a 
six-degree-of-freedom soft mechanical gripper inspired by the fin ray 
effect was designed (Lin et al., 2023), which reduced the compression 
force on the fruit surface through active deformation. The flexible 
manipulator with the visual-tactile sensing capability was successfully 
extended to tomato and nectarine firmness detection.

4. Existing chemometric methods for in-field data analysis
Nondestructive assessment of fruit quality relies on data obtained 
from mechanical, optical, acoustic, and electrochemical technologies. 
By establishing mathematical relationships between multi-source 
sensing data and target attributes, the internal compositions or 
external characteristics of fruits can be predicted. These models are 
subsequently deployed for the rapid analysis of new samples. The 
modeling process fundamentally relies on chemometric methods, which 
extract meaningful information from complex, multidimensional data­
sets using mathematical and statistical analysis. While conventional 
linear approaches such as PLS, MLR, and PCA are widely used, nonlinear 
algorithms, including SVM, RF, and deep learning, often achieve supe­
rior performance (Yu et al., 2025). Deep learning automates feature 
extraction from raw data, reducing the dependence on manual feature 
engineering, and is advantageous for processing high-dimensional 
complex data.
However, data-driven models are generally highly sensitive to the 
training data distribution and prone to overfitting when trained with 
limited samples. Ensemble learning, Bayesian optimization, and regu­
larization strategies are commonly adopted to improve generalization. 
Despite these efforts, practical deployment still faces challenges, 
including equipment variability, changes in fruit condition, and envi­
ronmental interference, all of which can impair predictive stability 
(Zhang & Yang, 2024). In field applications, phenotypic variability 
arising from diverse cultivars, non-uniform maturity, and microclimate 
differences further complicates model transfer. A core challenge stems 
from the substantial noise inherent in raw data. Instrumental drift, 
illumination changes, and surface contamination constitute interference 
irrelevant to the target attributes and undermine model robustness and 
applicability (Liu, Sun, et al., 2025).
To improve model adaptability, current research focuses on pre­
processing, data fusion, and model optimization. High-quality input 
data is essential for robust models. Preprocessing methods such as 
baseline correction, scatter correction, and smoothing are applied to 
enhance the signal-to-noise ratio by removing non-targeted interference 
(Yan, 2025). Since noise structures vary across datasets, tailored pre­
processing is essential; inappropriate processing may introduce artificial 
bias and degrade model performance. However, the selection of pre­
processing methods remains empirical, making the process inefficient 
and limiting its adaptability to data from new devices or environments. 
Future work could integrate data augmentation approaches, such as 
noise injection, sample synthesis, and style transfer, to diversify training 
samples and improve robustness.
As individual sensing technologies possess limited ability to fully 
capture all fruit quality attributes, multi-source information fusion has 
become a critical pathway for improving prediction accuracy (Zhang, 
Qian, & Zeng, 2025). Integrating mechanical, optical, acoustic vibra­
tional, electrochemical, and environmental data allows for the con­
struction of more discriminative feature sets. For instance, combining 
optical and mechanical responses enables the simultaneous assessment 
of composition and texture, while incorporating ambient data improves 
field adaptability. Fusion can be conducted at the feature level or the 
decision level, and the fusion mechanisms require careful design to 
effectively leverage complementary information.
Even when high-performance models are developed, maintaining 
their reliability across dynamic field environments remains challenging. 
Generalization across devices, environments, and seasons is often 
insufficient, leading to performance degradation under new conditions. 
Traditional transfer methods, such as piecewise direct standardization 
and slope/bias correction, use linear transformations to align instrument 
responses but are limited in handling complex nonlinear variations. 
Recently, transfer learning has emerged as a promising alternative. 
Transfer learning facilitates rapid adaptation to new scenarios with 
limited samples by leveraging pretrained models, feature alignment, and 
domain adaptation, thereby eliminating the requirement for large, an­
notated datasets and training models from scratch (Feng, Zhu, et al., 
2025). Beyond generalization, real-time performance is critical for field

5. Challenges and possible solutions
Significant progress has been achieved in fruit quality detection 
technologies in orchards, which has enhanced their portability, intelli­
gence, noncontact operation, and automation. These advancements 
provide valuable data for improving orchard management efficiency 
and fruit commercial value. However, the practical application of these 
technologies faces significant challenges in achieving scalability and 
high reliability. Their robustness, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness 
under complex orchard conditions remain major hurdles. Examining 
the core limitations of each technology or device and exploring prom­
ising solutions are crucial for further advancement. 
(1) Portable devices provide fruit growers with a cost-effective and 
user-friendly tool for fruit quality assessment in orchards. The 
application and demand for portable devices in orchards are 
growing. However, inherent limitations in current devices 
constrain measurement accuracy and comprehensiveness. 
Detection accuracy will be improved by integrating various 
miniaturized and compact sensors, such as microspectrometers, 
microphones, and cameras. A promising direction involves 
embedding these miniaturized sensors within ubiquitous smart­
phones. Such integration would dramatically improve opera­
tional convenience and accessibility while further reducing 
device size and user barriers.
(2) Emerging wearable sensors enable real-time monitoring of fruit 
quality status. However, high material costs and complex 
manufacturing processes constrain the large-scale production of 
wearable sensors. The long-term stability of wearable sensors is 
also insufficient. They are susceptible to environmental temper­
ature and humidity fluctuations, mechanical deformation, and 
material aging, resulting in signal drift or performance degrada­
tion. Some of the chemicals in wearable sensors may migrate due 
to imperfect material encapsulation or prolonged contact with 
the surface of high-moisture fruits, triggering food safety hazards. 
Besides, traditional batteries are difficult to meet the demand for 
long-term deployment of wearable sensors in the field. Future 
research trends may include: optimizing sensor performance 
through nanomaterials, bionic structures, and fabrication tech­
niques; developing efficient and reliable self-powered methods 
like triboelectric nanogenerators to meet continuous energy de­
mands; exploring biodegradable flexible materials to reduce 
environmental impact; and constructing efficient edge-cloud 
collaborative frameworks for processing massive heterogeneous 
data.
(3) Noncontact devices offer advantages for assessing fruit quality 
across varying canopy heights. However, the effective detection 
capability of existing methods is still insufficient for fruits that are 
heavily shaded by branches and leaves. The real-time variations 
in ambient light intensity seriously affect the accuracy of spectral 
detection. Although calibration by reference plates of different 
gray levels can mitigate this effect, the process is cumbersome. 
Acoustic vibration methods are not affected by lighting 
conditions, but they have low detection efficiency, typically 
permitting only single-fruit measurement per operation. The 
dynamic conditions in orchards can cause fruit movement, which 
may introduce motion artifacts and reduce data quality. More­
over, most existing systems exhibit insufficient automation and 
still require partial manual intervention. Therefore, future 
research might improve fruit tree planting patterns to reduce 
shading, develop effective motion compensation mechanisms, 
enhance the automation of existing inspection methods, and 
design smart aerial or ground inspection robots. The above in­
tegrated approaches will enable more efficient and comprehen­
sive fruit quality monitoring in orchards.
(4) Significant progress has been made in manipulators with quality 
sensing capabilities, especially those incorporating tactile and 
visual technologies. Nevertheless, efficient and reliable detection 
in orchards remains challenging. At the level of detection reli­
ability, the gripping position and contact area significantly affect 
the data quality. Automatic vision-guided gripping is susceptible 
to localization deviations due to occlusion and overlapping 
problems, and even if successful, the lack of a large contact area 
may still distort the detection signal. Although manual manipu­
lation can improve the accuracy, relying on human labour defeats 
the purpose of automation. In terms of efficiency, the existing 
systems generally require 2.5–5 s for single-fruit detection, which 
is lower than the efficiency of manual picking. Furthermore, 
integrating quality-sensing modules often increases manipulator 
size, reducing its dexterity and operational efficiency. In addi­
tion, most of the studies remain in the laboratory validation stage, 
lacking the scale-up validation in the orchard. Future break­
throughs will depend on optimized mechanical structures, 
enhanced control systems, intelligent perception algorithms, and 
modular design principles. The rapid advancement of miniatur­
ized sensors has made it possible to integrate high-performance 
sensing modules into limited spaces. When combined with 
lightweight computational models, this strategy represents a 
promising pathway toward enhanced system practicality.
(5) Achieving nondestructive and accurate assessment of fruit quality 
represents a common objective across various technologies and 
devices. This goal critically depends on well-trained models and 
high-quality datasets. Although classical chemometric methods 
demonstrate effectiveness in predicting fruit quality attributes 
using mechanical, acoustic vibration, optical, and electro­
chemical data, their generalization ability is constrained by data 
variability due to biological factors, instrument configurations, 
and measurement conditions. Deep learning has emerged as a 
cutting-edge tool for enhancing prediction accuracy and robust­
ness with good feature extraction capabilities, data-fusion de­
signs, and flexible architecture. Transfer learning, multimodal 
data fusion, multi-task learning, and knowledge-guided modeling 
provide promising pathways for enhancing model generaliz­
ability. Crucially, highly parameterized deep learning approaches 
typically require large-scale datasets encompassing broad bio­
logical variations, environmental conditions, and instrument 
differences to avoid overfitting. Large-scale datasets can also 
enhance the model’s ability to learn general feature representa­
tions, reducing the need to rebuild models in response to new 
samples. Therefore, strong advocacy for publicly sharing high- 
quality, open-source datasets is essential. This practice will 
significantly accelerate the development, comparison, and vali­
dation of data-driven deep learning models, ultimately acceler­
ating progress in the field of nondestructive fruit quality 
evaluation.
6. Conclusions and future prospects

and precision management in orchards, thereby enhancing fruit quality 
from the source of the supply chain. This paper aims to provide re­
searchers and practitioners with a comprehensive review to accelerate 
innovation in this domain. It discusses the theoretical foundations of 
promising technologies, evaluates the performance of existing in­
struments and data analysis methods, and addresses current challenges 
and future trends. Currently, in-field fruit quality detection primarily 
relies on instrumentation based on mechanical, acoustic vibration, op­
tical, and electrochemical principles. Among these, portable devices 
leverage miniaturization and cost-effectiveness for daily sampling in­
spections, meeting the need for immediate feedback. Wearable sensors 
enable continuous monitoring and accurately track long-term changes in 
fruit quality. Noncontact methods achieve orchard-scale assessments 
through extensive spatial coverage. Flexible robotic manipulators with 
quality sensing ability facilitate integrated harvesting and grading op­
erations. Although each technology has specific limitations, enhancing 
intelligence and detection accuracy represents a common goal for 
development. Integrating multiple technologies tailored to application 
scenarios is expected to provide a more comprehensive analysis of fruit 
quality attributes. Furthermore, the establishment of accurate fruit 
quality prediction models is of vital importance to various technologies 
and devices. Therefore, the open sharing of fruit quality data should be 
encouraged to promote the construction of large-scale datasets and 
significantly accelerate the advancement of robust deep learning 
models. In conclusion, the integration of portable devices, wearable 
sensors, noncontact devices, and flexible robotic manipulators will 
contribute to modern intelligent orchards, ensuring effective quality 
control from the source and the efficient supply of high-quality fruits to 
consumers.
