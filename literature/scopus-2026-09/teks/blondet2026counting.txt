Abstract. Accurate fruit counting is a key component of yield estima-
tion in precision agriculture, enabling the optimization of harvesting, 
logistics, and export processes. In this work, we propose a system for the 
automatic counting of avocados in videos captured in real agricultural 
ﬁelds, combining YOLOv8m for fruit detection with the BoT-SORT algo-
rithm for temporal tracking. 
The system was validated using a custom dataset composed of public 
images and original footage collected in commercial Peruvian orchards, 
under real-world conditions of lighting, occlusion, and high fruit density. 
The detection model achieved a mAP@0.5 of 0.953, a recall of 0.888, 
and an F1-score of 0.919. For cumulative counting, the system reached 
an Absolute Counting Precision (ACP) of 92.68%, along with a Mean 
Absolute Error (MAE) of 2.0 and a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 
3.16, reducing duplicate counts through association and re-identiﬁcation
adjustments.
These results approach the state of the art in controlled environments,
such as the work of Du et al. (2024), who reported an ACP of 95.33%
and an MAE of 3.33. The proximity of our results, despite the more
challenging capture conditions, demonstrates that a design focused on
cumulative counting enables robust metrics and outcomes applicable to
real-world agricultural scenarios, oﬀering a practical solution for produc-
ers and companies in the sector.

Introduction 
Computer vision techniques are increasingly applied in agriculture, driven by 
hardware advancements and mature real-time object detection architectures. 
Automated fruit counting for yield estimation is particularly relevant, optimiz-
ing harvesting, logistics, and exports via accurate data [5,7,10,11,13]. Studies 
show computer vision surpasses traditional methods in accuracy, eﬃciency, and 
scalability, with video-based counting being highly promising for ﬁeld use.

However, Peru, a leading Hass avocado exporter [3], lacks validated auto-

This paper presents a video-based avocado counting model combining YOLO 
(You Only Look Once) for fruit detection in frames and BoT-SORT for robust 
object tracking across sequences. This integration enables accurate fruit identi-
ﬁcation under natural conditions and precise cumulative counting by avoiding
duplicates through persistent tracking.
To validate the system, a custom dataset was built using images from com-
mercial Hass avocado orchards and public repositories, capturing natural vari-
ations and complexities. The model was trained and e valuated using standard
detection and tracking metrics (precision, recall, F1-score, mAP) to balance
accuracy and performance.

Related Work 
Numerous studies have applied YOLO models in agriculture. Osman et al. [10] 
used a custom YOLO for fruit counting via a mobile app. Gremes et al. [5] imple-
mented YOLO with tracking for orange counting, while Parr et al.
[11] used 
YOLO on depth maps from ToF cameras for grape coun ting. Other approaches
reviewed by Zhai et al. [16] include traditional computer vision techniques lik e
HSV color analysis [12] and deep learning methods such as FCNs with hyp er-
spectral imaging for defect detection [15]. Outside agriculture, Tan et al. [14] 
found YOLOv3 outperformed RetinaNet and SSD in sp eed and precision for pill
detection.
A particularly relevant study by Du et al. [1] combined an optimized CS-
YOLOv5s with enhanced DeepSort for green pepper counting. Their model 
included attention mechanisms (CBAM), Slim-Neck structures with GSConv, 
and improved re-identiﬁcation. While achieving high ACP (95.33%) and low 
MAE (3.33), their results were obtained under controlled experimental ﬁeld con-
ditions (stabilized vehicle, segmented lighting). In contrast, our work operates in 
more challenging, uncontrolled commercial orchard settings. Nevertheless, their 
approach underscores the value of i ntegrating advanced detection and tracking
for issues like occlusion and high density, relevant to our environment. Overall,
YOLO architectures are widely favored for their performance and speed across
various applications.

parameters. The input image size was set to 640×640 pixels, and the batch size

3.2
Dataset Preparation 
The dataset contains a total of 1207 images, of which 124 were obtained from 
public repositories and 1083 were captured directly from avocado orchards in 
collaboration with local agribusiness company Frucontrol SAC. These captures, 
focusing on Hass avocados, included both photos and videos recorded around 
noon under direct sunlight. The fruits were at a uniform maturity stage, approx-
imately two weeks b efore harvest. From the videos, one frame per second was
extracted for model training. The recordings were made using a Google Pixel
8a at 1080p resolution and 60 fps. The dataset is publicly available at: https:// 
universe.roboﬂow.com/avocado-workspace/ultimate-avocado/browse. 
To ensure the dataset’s validity and fairness for real-world application, the 
collection process speciﬁcally targeted challenging conditions. This included high 
fruit density, signiﬁcant partial occlusions (with some fruits over 50% obscured 
by foliage), and high chromatic similarity between the dark green fruits and 
the surrounding leaves. This focus on occluded instances was intentional, as 
it presents a more challenging and realistic environment where leaves can be 
easily confused with avocados. W hile the fruits were at a consistent maturity
level, variations in the camera’s distance to the trees provided a diverse range of
apparent fruit sizes within the images. The annotation process was conducted
using Roboﬂow (as shown in Fig. 1), with annotators speciﬁcally trained to label 
partially occluded avocados accurately, ensuring high-quality ground truth data
for these challenging instances.

3.3
Training Process 
The dataset was split into three subsets: 80% for training, 10% for validation, and
10% for testing. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the model was trained for a maximum 
of 100 epochs using YOLOv8m, with early stopping applied if no improvement 
was observ ed on the validation metric for 20 consecutive epochs.
3.4
System Operation Architecture 
Figure 3 illustrates the overall system architecture for inference. This ﬂowchart 
details the operational process from video input to the ﬁnal cumulative count.
The process begins with an input video from the orchard. The video is pro-
cessed frame by frame. For each frame, the trained YOLOv8m model performs 
object detection, generating bounding boxes and conﬁdence scores for potential 
avocados. These detections are then passed to the BoT-SORT algorithm. BoT-
SORT handles the core logic of temporal association, matching detections to 
existing tracks, managing occlusions via its ReID module, and initializing new
tracks for newly detected fruits. The system maintains a global list of unique
track IDs. The ﬁnal output is the total number of unique IDs generated, which
represents the cumulative count of avocados in the video.

3.5
Object Tracking and Counting 
To perform object tracking and cumulative avocado counting, a second pipeline 
was implemented, as illustrated in Fig. 4, based on the BoT-SORT algorithm. 
This approach includes an object re-identiﬁcation module (ReID), which enables 
more accurate association in scenarios involving partial occlusion or temporary 
disappearance. The pipeline takes as input the videos previously processed by 
the YOLOv8m model, which already include detections with bounding boxes
and conﬁdence scores. The output consists of annotated videos with persistent
IDs for each avocado and the cumulative detection count.
Fig. 4. Tracking Pip eline
The BoT-SORT implementation provided by Ultralytics was used, adapted to 
integrate directly with the detection outputs of the trained model. Several hyper-

parameters were adjusted to optimize counting accuracy. Speciﬁcally, the maxi-
mum and minimum association thresholds were set to 0.38 and 0.12, respectively, 
while the threshold for initializing new tracks was raised to 0.6. For track-to-track 
matching, a threshold of 0.4 was applied. Proximity and appearance thresholds
were set to 0.2 and 0.5, respectively, and the ReID module was enabled.
These parameter settings helped reduce the number of false positives during 
object association, prioritizing the accuracy of the ﬁnal count at the expense of 
lower tracking persistence. The inclusion of the ReID module was particularly 
important in agricultural environments, where avocados may become partially or
fully occluded by foliage. This module enables more reliable visual re-association
of previous detections, avoiding the assignment of new, unnecessary identiﬁers.
This behavior is illustrated in Fig. 5, which presents three consecutive video 
frames. In the ﬁrst frame, an avocado is correctly detected and assigned a unique 
ID. In the second frame, the same avocado becomes temporarily hidden by 
branches and leaves, resulting in a missed detection. However, in the third frame, 
once it reappears in the ﬁeld of view, the system correctly reassigns its original
ID. This demonstrates how the system handles temporary occlusions without
compromising tracking continuity or duplicating the count, which is essential for
accurate fruit estimation.
Fig. 5. Example of temporary occlusion in video. Frame 1: the avocado is visible and

Annotation and Evaluation Data 
To validate the accuracy of the counting model, manually annotated videos 
were required. Three representative videos were selected and labeled using the 
CVAT.ai platform, identifying all visible avocados in each frame. This allowed 
for a direct comparison between the system’s cumulative count and the ground
truth, enabling the calculation of speciﬁc tracking and counting evaluation met-
rics.

Experimental Results and Discussion 
4.1
Evaluation Metrics 
Performance was evaluated using standard metrics for detection, tracking, and
counting.
For detection, we used Precision, Recall, F1-score, and mean Average Preci-
sion (mAP), reported at IoU = 0.5 (mAP@0.5) and averaged over IoU thresholds 
from 0.5 to 0.95 (mAP@[0.5:0.95]). Precision measures the accuracy of positive 
detections, Recall measures the proportion of actual fruits found, and F1-score
balances the two. mAP provides a comprehensive measure of detection perfor-
mance across diﬀerent conﬁdence levels and IoU overlaps.
For counting and tracking, following Du et al. [1], we used Absolute Count-
ing Precision (ACP), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Root Mean Square Error 
(RMSE) to assess the ﬁnal count accuracy against the ground truth (GT). Addi-
tionally, Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA), Multiple Object Tracking 
Precision (MOTP), and ID Switches (IDSW) were used to evaluate frame-by-
frame tracking quality, consistency, and spatial accuracy. ACP reﬂects the rela-
tive c ounting accuracy per video, MAE quantiﬁes the average absolute counting
error, and RMSE penalizes larger errors more heavily. MOTA summarizes overall
tracking performance, MOTP measures localization precision, and IDSW counts
tracking inconsistencies.
4.2
Detection Experimental Results 
On the test set, the YOLOv8m model achieved a precision of 0.953, a recall of 
0.888, and an F1-score of 0.919. When evaluated across diﬀerent Intersection 
over Union (IoU) thresholds, the model obtained a mAP@0.5 of 0.953 and a 
mAP@[0.5:0.95] of 0.788. These results demonstrate the robustness of the model
in detection tasks, even under varying levels of overlap between predictions and
ground truth annotations. Figure 6 presents the main visualizations that quan-
titatively and qualitatively supp ort the model’s performance.
According to the confusion matrix shown in Fig. 6e, out of 1646 avocado 
instances, the model correctly detected 1387, while 135 false negatives were 
recorded, mainly due to partial occlusions. Additionally, 124 false positiv es were
identiﬁed, mostly corresponding to leaves with a shape similar to avocados.

Discussion 
A preliminary version of the detection model, trained solely with public images 
of avocados, achieved a mAP@0.5 of 0.61 when validated on the current dataset. 
After incorporating proprietary images captured under real agricultural produc-
tion conditions, this value improved to a mAP@0.5 of 0.953, representing a n
increase of over 56%. This progression highlights the impact of dataset diver-
sity and representativeness on the model’s ability to detect fruits in open-ﬁeld
commercial scenarios.
Among the reviewed studies, the work by Du et al. [1] stands out, as dis-
cussed in the Related Work section. Their approach proposes the CS YOLOv5s 
architecture, an optimized model based on YOLOv5s. It integrates attention 
mechanisms (CBAM) and a Slim-Neck structure using GSConv. The dataset 
used was collected in the experimental ﬁeld of Hunan Agricultural University, 
under controlled conditions: recordings were segmented by time of day to con-
trol lighting, and a remote-controlled vehicle with mec hanical stabilization was
employed. In contrast, the proposed system was trained and evaluated using
data captured in commercial avocado orchards, without artiﬁcial stabilization
or environmental control, representing a more challenging and realistic applica-
tion setting.
To establish a direct comparison with other state-of-the-art architectures, we 
trained YOLOv7, YOLOv11m, and the CS YOLOv5s architecture [1] under the 
same experimental conditions used for our YOLOv8m-based model. The results,

Beyond image-level detection performance, a critical component of the pro-
posed system is its ability to perform reliable cumulative counting across video 
sequences. For this reason, the analysis emphasized speciﬁc metrics such as ACP, 
MAE, and R MSE, which directly reﬂect the accuracy of the ﬁnal count–an essen-
tial requirement in agricultural tasks that involve precise yield estimation.
The system achieved MOTA values ranging from 39.6% to 67.4%, which 
are lower than the approximate 90% MOTA reported by Du et al. [1]. This 
discrepancy is attributed to the challenging conditions of the real-world agri-
cultural environment, including temporary occlusions, variable fruit distances, 
and lighting ﬂuctuations, all of which hinder object visibility and continuous 
tracking. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, the system attained an average 
ACP of 92.68%, along with low MAE (2.0) and R MSE (3.16) values, indicating
high accuracy in cumulative counting. Moreover, the number of identity switches
remained low, reﬂecting consistent identiﬁer assignment over time.
The tuning of BoT-SORT parameters, such as association thresholds and the 
activation of the ReID module, proved essential for enhancing the ﬁnal count 
accuracy (reﬂected in ACP, MAE, and RMSE), even at the cost of frame-to-
frame tracking continuity (partially explaining the lower MOTA compared to 
controlled environments). This trade-oﬀ was intentional; in agricultural appli-
cations for yield estimation, missing occasional detections or track continuity
is preferable to generating duplicate counts, thereby prioritizing the reliability
of the cumulative result over maximizing frame-by-frame tracking metrics like
MOTA.
Overall, both quantitative and qualitative analysis suggest that an eﬀective 
fruit counting system for agricultural production environments requires not only 
a robust detection architecture, but also adapted tracking strategies and a repre-
sentative dataset tailored to the target domain. The integration of these elements

Conclusions and Future Work 
This work presented an automated system for counting avocados in videos from 
commercial orchards using YOLOv8m and BoT-SORT. Key ﬁndings show the 
architecture eﬀectively handled real-world challenges (occlusion, variable light-
ing), ac hieving high cumulative counting accuracy (ACP 92.68%, MAE 2.0)
and strong detection (mAP@0.5 0.953). Comparisons with CS YOLOv5s [1], 
YOLOv7, and YOLOv11m validated YOLOv8m’s eﬀectiveness and stressed the 
importance of training on representative data from the target environment (com-
mercial Peruvian plantations). While frame-by-frame tracking (MOTA 39.6%-
67.4%) was lower than in controlled settings due to ﬁeld complexities, consistent 
tracking (low ID switches, high MOTP 95%) and strategic BoT-SORT tuning 
prioritized ﬁnal count accuracy over continuous tracking, minimizing duplica-
tions. The results validate the proposed architecture as a precise, viable solution
for operational agricultural settings, oﬀering a foundation for future work.
Future work should focus on expanding the dataset with more varied con-
ditions (lighting, occlusion levels, avocado varieties/ripeness) and developing an 
integrated software solution, possibly incorporating a mobile app or drone-based 
capture for easier farmer use and enhanced automation. Further exploration into 
optimizing tracking parameters for diﬀerent environmental conditions could also 
improve frame-by-frame consistency without sacriﬁcing counting accuracy.
