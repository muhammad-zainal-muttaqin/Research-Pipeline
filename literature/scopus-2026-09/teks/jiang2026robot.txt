A B S T R A C T
Cotton boll counting is a critical component of yield estimation and plays a vital role in advancing plant phe­
nomics and precision agriculture. However, the complex spatial distribution of cotton bolls and frequent oc­
clusions in two-dimensional (2D) images pose significant challenges to accurate quantification. To address these 
issues, we developed a novel workflow based on Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), which integrates high- 
throughput robotic image data acquisition, three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction, and cotton boll mapping at 
the plot level in a breeding field. A customized robotic platform equipped with ten cameras mounted around the 
canopy frame was used to capture overlapping red, green, and blue (RGB) images of plot-level cotton plants. 
NeRF was then employed to reconstruct detailed 3D models, and the reconstruction quality was evaluated. 
Furthermore, this study presents a comprehensive 2D-to-3D mapping approach based on an improved FruitNeRF 
algorithm, which effectively overcomes the challenges of 3D plot-level cotton boll annotation and limited 
training data. The proposed approach enables accurate quantification of cotton boll numbers and provides a 
reliable foundation for yield prediction. Experimental results demonstrated that the Lumix-camera configuration 
mounted on the robotic platform achieved the best overall reconstruction performance among the tested con­
figurations, with a peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of 17.21 dB, a structural similarity index (SSIM) of 0.49, and 
a learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) of 0.54. This configuration was also better suited for high- 
throughput plot-level field phenotyping because it enabled more stable and standardized image acquisition. The 
reconstructed plot-level point clouds retained sufficient structural information for downstream cotton boll seg­
mentation, counting, and yield estimation. In the exported plot-level point clouds, both cotton bolls and branches 
are clearly distinguishable. Based on Lumix data, the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for cotton boll 
number and boll weight prediction was 5.43% and 8.78%, respectively. Overall, this approach enables high- 
throughput phenotyping and the generation of plot-level cotton point clouds suitable for downstream boll 
counting and yield estimation, providing a valuable framework for plot-level cotton phenotyping and precision 
management for low-density, post-defoliated plots under the experimental conditions evaluated in this study.
1. Introduction
Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is the most important renewable fiber crop 
globally, with an annual production value exceeding USD 46 billion, 
supporting the livelihoods of 150 million cotton workers across 75 
countries (Devoto et al., 2024; Kun et al., 2025). As the global popula­
tion continues to rise, improving cotton yield has become a critical 
challenge in modern agriculture. Plant phenotypic information plays a 
central role in breeding programs by providing essential data to support 
yield enhancement (Conaty et al., 2022; Voss-Fels et al., 2019). Among 
these traits, boll number is widely recognized as one of the most critical 
indicators of cotton yield in breeding programs. It offers valuable in­
sights into genotype performance under diverse environments and 
serves as a key criterion for selecting high-yielding lines and guiding

genetic improvement (Virk et al., 2024). However, current boll counting 
in cotton predominantly relies on manual field sampling, which is time- 
consuming, labor-intensive, and susceptible to human error, particularly 
under high-temperature field conditions (Chen et al., 2025). These 
limitations underscore the urgent need for efficient, accurate, and reli­
able approaches to boll number quantification.
In cotton breeding and field phenotyping, accurate characterization 
of boll distribution and plant architecture at the plot level remains a 
significant challenge under field conditions. Cotton bolls are spatially 
distributed throughout dense and partially occluded canopies, making 
reliable quantification difficult using conventional manual inspection or 
2D image-based approaches. Manual field scoring is labor-intensive, 
subjective, and difficult to scale to large breeding trials. Unmanned 
Aerial Vehicles (UAV)-based imaging systems primarily rely on top-view 
observations and often fail to capture detailed side-view and under- 
canopy structures that are important for understanding boll distribu­
tion and plant architecture. In addition, conventional 2D image-based 
methods are inherently viewpoint-dependent and are susceptible to 
occlusion and overlapping cotton bolls, particularly in dense field 
scenes. These limitations motivate the need for semantic 3D recon­
struction methods capable of generating detailed plot-level cotton rep­
resentations for downstream phenotyping and yield-related analysis.
Two-dimensional (2D) imaging techniques combined with deep 
learning have been widely applied to cotton boll counting and to fruit 
detection tasks in various crops (Wang et al., 2025). In this context, some 
researchers have investigated segmentation methods based on both fully 
supervised and weakly supervised learning to evaluate boll counting 
accuracy using proximal imagery (Adke et al., 2022). Aerial images have 
also been used to predict cotton boll number using density map-based 
regression approaches, such as DM-Count (Tan et al., 2025). The 2D 
detection-based method has also been applied to fruit detection in many 
other crops. For example, YOLOv5x has demonstrated outstanding 
performance in kiwifruit detection, achieving an average precision of 
99.5% (Gao et al., 2024), while YOLOv8n achieved an average accuracy 
of 91.93% in strawberry identification (An et al., 2024). However, fruits 
with complex spatial arrangements, such as blueberries, grapes and 
apples, present greater challenges for comprehensive detection in 2D 
images due to occlusion issues (Ni et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2024; Yang 
et al., 2025). Cotton bolls are also distributed in a three-dimensional 
(3D) space on the plant, particularly at the plot level, where severe oc­
clusions in 2D images significantly compromise counting accuracy.
Another major challenge in 3D plant phenotyping is the requirement 
for extensive manual annotation of 3D point clouds for semantic seg­
mentation and structural analysis. Compared with 2D image annotation, 
dense point-level annotation in complex plant canopies is highly labor- 
intensive and time-consuming. To address this limitation, recent ad­
vances in foundation models and semantic Neural Radiance Fields 
(NeRF) frameworks provide opportunities to transfer semantic infor­
mation from 2D image space into 3D representations without requiring 
large-scale manually labeled 3D point cloud datasets.
Compared to 2D images, point clouds preserve the 3D spatial ar­
chitecture of plants, which helps mitigate occlusion issues and enables 
detailed 3D analysis of plant traits. Building on this advantage, recent 
studies have employed point cloud instance segmentation to extract a 
variety of phenotypic traits, such as branch length and canopy volume in 
apple trees, and plant height and leaf angle in maize (Jiang et al., 2025a; 
Miao et al., 2021). In fruit phenotyping, segmenting individual apples 
within point clouds enables accurate fruit counting and volume esti­
mation (Dong et al., 2023). High-precision 3D point clouds are a pre­
requisite for successful segmentation. Terrestrial Light Detection and 
Ranging (LiDAR) systems can directly acquire high-quality point clouds; 
however, their high cost still limits their widespread use in practice. By 
contrast, RGB-D cameras provide a more cost-effective alternative, but 
their limited resolution often results in point clouds with substantial loss 
of fine-scale detail (Bloch et al., 2025). Therefore, developing an effi­
cient, low-cost, and high-fidelity 3D reconstruction method is critical for 
advancing plant phenotyping and trait analysis (Jiang et al., 2024). An 
important practical advantage of the proposed workflow is that it en­
ables plot-level 3D reconstruction and cotton boll analysis directly from 
RGB image inputs collected under field conditions, without relying on 
specialized depth sensors or LiDAR systems.
Different sensing platforms present distinct trade-offs for field-based 
cotton phenotyping. UAV-based imaging systems are well-suited for 
large-area field monitoring and high-throughput scouting; however, 
their predominantly top-view observations limit their ability to capture 
detailed side-view and under-canopy structures in dense cotton can­
opies. Handheld smartphone acquisition provides flexibility and rela­
tively low hardware cost, but suffers from reduced repeatability and 
greater operator-dependent variability during large-scale field pheno­
typing. Terrestrial LiDAR systems can directly generate highly accurate 
point clouds with rich geometric detail; however, they generally do not 
provide color and texture information of plants. In addition, their high 
equipment cost and limited scalability remain challenges for practical 
breeding applications.
In this study, a ground robotic platform equipped with multiple RGB 
cameras was selected to balance acquisition cost, reconstruction quality, 
scalability, and repeatability for plot-level cotton phenotyping. 
Compared with UAV-based systems, the ground robot enables more 
stable multi-view acquisition of side-view and under-canopy structures 
that are important for cotton boll distribution and architectural analysis. 
In addition, compared with handheld acquisition, the robotic platform 
provides more consistent imaging trajectories and standardized multi- 
view observations across plots.
Plot-level phenotyping was selected as the focus of this study because 
cotton breeding and field phenotyping are commonly conducted at the 
plot scale, where multiple plants representing the same genotype are 
evaluated collectively for yield-related traits and architectural charac­
teristics. Compared with single-plant reconstruction, plot-level analysis 
introduces additional challenges, including overlapping plant struc­
tures, increased occlusion, and more complex spatial distributions of 
cotton bolls under field conditions. Therefore, developing semantic 3D 
reconstruction methods for plot-level cotton analysis is important for 
practical breeding-oriented phenotyping applications.
Multi-view 3D reconstruction has emerged as a promising approach 
for achieving a balance between sensor cost and model resolution. This 
approach reconstructs the 3D structure of objects or scenes by process­
ing multiple overlapping 2D images captured from different viewpoints. 
The traditional multi-view 3D reconstruction pipeline typically involves 
two distinct stages: the Structure-from-Motion (SfM) algorithm enables 
the recovery of both camera trajectories and the 3D structure of a scene 
from a collection of partially overlapping 2D images (Sch¨onberger and 
Frahm, 2016). The process begins with feature extraction, followed by 
matching corresponding features across overlapping image pairs to 
establish image correspondences. Based on these correspondences, the 
three-dimensional positions of matched features—forming a sparse 
point cloud—can be recovered through an iterative bundle adjustment 
(BA) algorithm (Eltner et al., 2016). Subsequently, a multi-view stereo 
(MVS) algorithm is applied to recover the depth of each pixel in each 
image using consistent matches across views, resulting in a dense point 
cloud (Meinen and Robinson, 2020; Smith et al., 2016). Owing to its 
robustness and compatibility with consumer-grade cameras, this clas­
sical workflow has been widely adopted across a broad range of object- 
and scene-level reconstruction tasks. It has demonstrated strong per­
formance in plant phenotyping applications, including the 3D modeling 
of individual plants such as Carex and kale (Yang et al., 2024a). A va­
riety of software platforms that integrate these workflows—such as 
COLMAP, Pix4D mapper (Pix4D S.A., Lucerne, Switzerland), and Agisoft 
Metashape (Agisoft LLC, St. Petersburg, Russia)—are capable of recon­
structing 3D object models from multiple overlapping 2D images. A 
variety of agricultural studies have adopted these tools for 3D recon­
struction tasks, successfully modeling crops such as cotton and maize 
(Sun et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). Although SfM-MVS-based 3D

which involves multiple discrete stages and increases the risk of recon­

NeRF have emerged as a novel learning-based approach that lever­
ages neural networks to implicitly represent 3D scenes through a 
continuous volumetric radiance field (Mildenhall et al., 2020). The 
NeRF method models the color and density at any 3D location condi­
tioned on a viewing direction using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The 
network is trained to predict view-dependent color and volume density 
from five inputs (spatial position and viewing direction) by minimizing 
the pixel-wise reconstruction error between rendered and ground-truth 
images. This process enables the implicit encoding of scene geometry 
and appearance directly from 2D images. By circumventing traditional 
geometric components such as MVS, surface reconstruction, and texture 
mapping, NeRF simplifies the 3D reconstruction pipeline into an end-to- 
end optimization framework. Once trained, NeRF can synthesize novel 
views with photorealistic quality, even for scenes with complex lighting 
and fine-grained structures. NeRF has been successfully applied to the 
high-fidelity reconstruction of various fruits, including dragon fruit, 
grapes, lychees, and oranges (Hu et al., 2024). This method supports not 
only the 3D reconstruction of individual fruits but also entire plants. 
Moreover, its feasibility has been demonstrated at the plot level for crops 
such as corn and strawberries (Arshad et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). 
Although recent years have witnessed notable progress, the intricate 
architecture of cotton plants and the dense distribution of bolls pose 
significant challenges, highlighting the need for further exploration of 
NeRF's 3D reconstruction capabilities at the plot-level. In particular, 
integrating robotic platforms for high-throughput data acquisition and 
ensuring high-fidelity plot-scale reconstruction remain underexplored 
yet critical directions.
Following the reconstruction of plot-level 3D models of cotton 
plants, the segmentation of cotton bolls is a critical prerequisite for ac­
curate boll counting. To achieve this, two potential approaches can be 
employed for 3D cotton boll segmentation. One common method in­
volves exporting point clouds generated by the NeRF model, which can 
then be used for various point cloud processing tasks. For instance, 
PointNet++ (Qi et al., 2017) has been successfully applied to segment 
bolls from the point cloud of cotton plants (Jiang et al., 2022). However, 
this approach requires extensive annotation of point clouds, leading to 
significant time spent on data labeling, particularly when labeling plants 
at the plot-level, which is even more challenging. Additionally, the 
scarcity of 3D point cloud data is also a notable issue. Another method 
leverages multi-view 3D reconstruction techniques to establish a 
connection between 2D and 3D. The process first obtains 2D masks of 
the target from 2D images, then projects these masks into 3D space, and 
finally segments the object in the 3D space. This processing pipeline has 
already been successfully applied to fruits such as apples and blueberries 
(Gen´e-Mola et al., 2020; Ni et al., 2021), as well as to crops such as rice 
panicles (Yang et al., 2024b). Combining NeRF with object counting 
enables the embedding of 2D semantic information into 3D space, 
resulting in a Semantic Neural Radiance Field that directly outputs 
target-specific point clouds. This approach not only eliminates the need 
for training an additional 3D segmentation model, but also circumvents 
the challenges associated with manual 3D annotation and the scarcity of 
high-quality 3D training data. A key methodological advantage of the 
proposed workflow is that it reduces the dependence on extensive 
manually annotated 3D point cloud datasets by leveraging semantic 2D- 
to-3D mapping through foundation-model-assisted segmentation and 
semantic NeRF reconstruction. Semantic-NeRF extends NeRF by 
enabling the joint learning and representation of scene geometry, 
appearance, and semantic information (Zhi et al., 2021). FruitNeRF is a 
NeRF-based model designed for fruit-centric semantic perception. It has 
been integrated into the Nerfstudio framework (Tancik et al., 2023), 
enabling simultaneous 3D reconstruction and fruit segmentation from 
multi-view RGB images (Meyer et al., 2024). Despite its promising po­
tential for plot-level cotton analysis, this integration of NeRF with 3D 
segmentation remains largely unexplored in existing research.
The overall goal of this study was to utilize a customized multi- 
camera robotic platform for multi-view data acquisition and to employ 
advanced Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) for plot-level 3D reconstruc­
tion and segmentation of cotton plants. A novel method was developed 
for 3D cotton boll segmentation, counting, and yield estimation by 
projecting 2D cotton boll masks into 3D space. The specific objectives of 
this research were to: (1) design an innovative data acquisition method 
using a ground robot equipped with multiple cameras, and to compare 
its performance with that of a handheld smartphone; (2) perform plot- 
level 3D reconstruction of cotton plants using the NeRF method and 
evaluate the reconstruction quality; (3) develop a 2D annotation strat­
egy based on the Segment Anything Model (SAM) for constructing a 
cotton boll segmentation dataset; and (4) implement plot-level cotton 
boll counting and yield estimation based on 3D segmentation.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Experimental field setup and data collection
The data used in this experiment were collected on October 25, 2024, 
in Tifton, Georgia, USA (Coordinates: 31.4371◦N, 83.5855◦W, Fig. 1a). 
The experimental field consisted of 160 plots (Fig. 1b), comprising 32 
different genotypes, with each plot containing a single genotype. The 
plant height of these genotypes ranges around 1 m. The center point of 
each plot was used as a reference, with a row spacing of 6 feet and a 
column spacing of 25 feet (Fig. 1e). Each plot measured approximately 
18 feet in length. A defoliant was applied to the entire field one week 
prior to data collection. In some plots, most of the leaves had already 
fallen off (Fig. 1c), while others still retained a substantial amount of 
foliage (Fig. 1d). The selected experimental conditions, including post- 
defoliation and relatively low planting density, were intended to pro­
vide an initial validation scenario for the proposed semantic 3D recon­
struction workflow under field conditions. Although partial canopy 
occlusions and structural complexity were still present, these conditions 
are less challenging than fully closed, high-density commercial cotton 
canopies. Nearly all bolls had already opened, with only a few remaining 
green. These immature bolls were excluded from yield measurements in 
this study. Data for testing were collected from the first, second, and 
seventh rows (from west to east).
Two data collection methods were employed in this experiment. The 
first was a high-throughput approach utilizing a ground robot equipped 
with multiple cameras, and the second was a low-cost method based on a 
smartphone. The data collection was conducted on a sunny day with 
clear skies. The smartphone data was collected between 11:00 AM and 
2:00 PM Eastern Time, while the ground robot collected data between 
4:00 PM and 6:00 PM. The ground robot, MARS (Xu and Li, 2022), was 
equipped with ten cameras, which were mounted approximately evenly 
around the canopy bracket (Fig. 2a). The two vertical blue support bars 
that hold the camera mounting frame are spaced approximately 2.48 m 
apart. The horizontal blue bar, parallel to the ground and used for 
camera installation, is positioned at a height of approximately 1.97 m 
above the ground. Due to limited camera availability, two different 
camera types were used on MARS. Due to the experimental setup used in 
this study, the robot was operated using manual navigation during data 
collection rather than autonomous navigation. During field data 
collection, the MARS robot was manually operated to move between 
cotton rows to ensure stable multi-view image acquisition across 
different plots. The scanning speed during data collection was approx­
imately 0.15–0.2 m/s. The cameras were mounted at multiple viewing 
directions around the cotton canopy, and the camera mounting angles

2.2. Plot-level cotton plant 3D reconstruction based on NeRF
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) can be briefly summarized as using a 
neural network to implicitly learn a static 3D scene, enabling 3D 
reconstruction of complex environments and novel view synthesis from 
arbitrary perspectives (Mildenhall et al., 2020). The input to NeRF 
consists of a 3D coordinate (x, y, z) and a viewing direction (θ,φ), while 
the output is the color (R, G, B) and volumetric density (σ) at that point 
in the specified direction. By sampling points along camera rays and 
applying volume rendering techniques, NeRF can synthesize photo­
realistic images from arbitrary viewpoints using multiple 2D images, 
while preserving detailed geometric and texture information.
NeRF was employed to reconstruct cotton plants at the plot-level and 
to generate corresponding point clouds in this study. Due to differences 
in video resolution across cameras, images were extracted from the 
recorded videos and uniformly resampled to a resolution of 1920 ×
1080. For plot-level cotton videos collected using multiple cameras, 
approximately 90 frames were uniformly sampled from each video, 
while 500 frames were extracted from the video captured with the 
smartphone. In both cases, the sampling strategy ensured more than 
90% overlap between adjacent frames. During frame extraction, the 
overlap between adjacent sampled frames was estimated based on the 
displacement of the imaging system between consecutive sampled 
frames relative to the approximate image coverage along the scanning 
direction. Given the robot scanning speed of approximately 0.15–0.2 m/ 
s, the video frame rate of 30 FPS, and the scanning duration of 
approximately 40 s per plot for each camera, approximately 90 frames 
were uniformly extracted from each camera video. This sampling 
strategy was expected to preserve more than 90% overlap between 
adjacent sampled frames. The overlap was approximately estimated 
from the acquisition speed, frame rate, and image coverage, rather than 
being calculated using detailed image registration. This high-overlap

2.3. Plot-level 3D cotton boll segmentation and counting
This study proposed a 2D-to-3D mapping-based workflow for plot- 
level cotton boll counting, effectively addressing the challenge of 
limited 3D annotation data. First, a SAM-based semi-automatic data 
labeling method was designed to improve annotation efficiency. The 
labeled data were used to train the YOLOv11x segmentation model, 
which was then used to generate 2D masks of cotton bolls from RGB 
images. Then, FruitNeRF was trained using the RGB images and their 
corresponding binary masks to incorporate cotton boll semantic infor­
mation into the NeRF model, enabling the extraction of cotton boll point 
clouds. Finally, Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Appli­
cations with Noise (HDBSCAN) was applied to the point cloud for 
clustering and boll counting.

trained model was then applied to new, unlabeled data to detect cotton 
bolls and generate corresponding 2D bounding boxes (Fig. 3). YOLO 
(You Only Look Once) is an end-to-end single-stage deep learning model 
that has been widely applied in the agricultural domain. It features a 
well-developed ecosystem, a low entry barrier, simple installation and 
configuration, lightweight architecture, and ease of deployment. 
Moreover, YOLO has strong extensibility—it can not only perform object 
detection to generate 2D bounding boxes, but also support instance 
segmentation to produce 2D masks. These advantages make it particu­
larly well suited for our task. YOLOv11x, an advanced version of the 
YOLO model, was employed in this section to generate 2D bounding 
boxes of cotton bolls for data annotation.
Next, the predicted 2D bounding boxes of cotton bolls were provided 
as prompts to the SAM model (sam_vit_h_4b8939.pth) to generate 
instance-level 2D masks of the cotton bolls. As a foundation model with 
zero-shot segmentation ability, SAM diverges from conventional su­
pervised methods by using prompts—such as bounding boxes, points, or 
text—to initiate segmentation, eliminating the need for dense manual 
labels. Trained on a large-scale dataset containing over 11 million im­
ages and more than 1 billion masks, SAM demonstrates strong gener­
alization to unseen tasks and objects. This extensive training enables 
SAM to perform accurate segmentation without requiring task-specific 
fine-tuning. For the new data collected in 2024, the 2D bounding 
boxes predicted by YOLOv11x were used as prompts, while for the 2022 
data, manually annotated 2D bounding boxes were provided as prompts 
to SAM to generate 2D masks of cotton bolls.
The 2D masks generated by SAM required further manual refine­
ment. When cotton bolls were occluded by the main stem or branches, 
the generated masks were often incomplete and needed manual 
correction. YOLOv11x occasionally produced incorrect detections or 
missed cotton bolls entirely, leading to inaccurate or missing masks. To 
address these issues, the SAM-generated masks and corresponding RGB 
images were imported into Roboflow (https://roboflow.com/) for 
manual refinement by trained personnel. Finally, a 2D segmentation 
dataset of cotton bolls was obtained.
The 2D segmentation dataset of cotton bolls was used to train the

2.3.2. Point cloud semantic segmentation based on FruitNeRF
FruitNeRF (Meyer et al., 2024) is a unified fruit segmentation 
framework that leverages NeRF to construct a 3D representation of the 
scene and incorporates semantic information for exporting the fruit 
point cloud. In this study, the original manual prompt-based mask 
generation process was replaced with an automated workflow using 
YOLOv11x to generate 2D cotton boll masks for semantic supervision. 
The model first takes an unordered set of RGB images and generates 
semantic segmentation masks for the fruits in each image. In the 
workflow of this study, YOLOv11x was used to obtain 2D masks of 
cotton bolls. The RGB images and the corresponding binary masks of the 
cotton bolls were combined to train FruitNeRF, enabling the entire scene 
to be enriched with semantic information of the fruits (Fig. 4). The 
trained FruitNeRF was then uniformly sampled to generate the cotton 
boll point cloud. FruitNeRF also includes a denoising operation for the 
segmented cotton boll point cloud, which filters out points that lack the 
minimum number of neighboring points within a specified radius.
FruitNeRF extends the original NeRF framework by introducing a 
semantic field, in addition to the existing density and appearance fields. 
Inspired by Semantic-NeRF (Zhi et al., 2021), this semantic field encodes 
fruit-related information within the 3D scene, allowing each spatial 
location to be identified and labeled as either a cotton boll or back­
ground. It is implemented using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that 
approximates the semantic logits S(r) for each pixel based on its 3D 
position. To estimate the semantic logits S(r) for a pixel, volume density 
and semantic data are integrated over K sample points along the ray r 
using numerical methods.

2.3.3. Clustering cotton boll point clouds for counting
The cotton boll points for each plot were obtained in the previous 
step. To achieve boll counting, instance segmentation was performed 
using a clustering method. Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering 
of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN) is a density-based clustering 
algorithm that effectively identifies clusters of arbitrary shapes and 
varying densities, while also handling noise points (McInnes et al., 
2017). The core idea of HDBSCAN is to construct a density-based hier­
archical clustering tree and extract the optimal clustering result based 
on criteria such as cluster stability. The algorithm relies on two key 
concepts: utilizing mutual reachability distance to build a dense con­
nectivity graph, and constructing a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) to 
capture the connectivity and evolution of clusters, which is then pruned 
to obtain the optimal clustering outcome.
The HDBSCAN algorithm consists of five key steps.

around each data point and serves as the basis for constructing mutual 
reachability distances. Given a parameter min_samples, the core dis­
tance of a point p is defined as the distance to its min_samples-th nearest 
neighbor.

2.4. Implementation details
The 3D reconstruction of plot-level cotton fields and the segmenta­
tion tasks based on the improved FruitNeRF model were both conducted 
on the HiPerGator high-performance computing cluster, using a 
computing node equipped with 8 AMD EPYC Rome CPU cores and a 
single NVIDIA DGX A100 GPU (80 GB), operating under the Linux sys­
tem. The YOLOv11x segmentation network was also trained on the same 
computational environment. The software stack comprised Python 3.10, 
PyTorch 1.12.1, and CUDA 11.3. Training configurations included an 
input resolution of 1280 × 1280 pixels, a batch size of 8, and a total of 
300 training epochs. All other hyperparameters followed the default 
settings. (The specific dataset can be found at the link: https://figshare. 
com/articles/dataset/2D_labeled_data/32237358?file=64438707). For 
the 3D reconstruction of cotton plants, the NeRF-based experiments 
were conducted using the Nerfstudio-1.0.0 framework with the Nerfacto 
model. The model was optimized using the Rectified Adam optimizer 
with a learning rate of 0.0005. Training was performed for 30,000 it­
erations with a batch size of 1024 rays per iteration. The default loss 
functions provided in the Nerfacto implementation, including RGB 
reconstruction loss and regularization losses, were used during optimi­
zation. The FruitNeRF model was evaluated with its publicly released 
implementation.

3. Results
3.1. 3D reconstruction result evaluation
Three-dimensional reconstruction can be successfully performed 
using 2D images captured by various camera types and configurations. 
In this study, data collection was conducted using two different types of 
cameras on the ground robot MARS. The performance of 3D recon­
struction under different camera configurations was then compared. 
Given the inherently complex and dynamic nature of field environ­
ments, the reconstruction quality exhibited variability among different 
plots. To address this, we selected ten representative plots and calcu­
lated the average performance metrics. The evaluation results indicate 
that while NeRF-based 3D reconstruction can be performed using data 
from all camera configurations, the reconstruction quality varied sub­
stantially depending on the camera setup (Table 4). Among the tested 
configurations, the five Lumix cameras produced the best 3D recon­
struction metrics. The smartphone also delivered promising results, 
particularly in terms of perceptual similarity (LPIPS). In contrast, the use 
of five Fuji cameras or a mixed setup combining two different types of 
cameras (10 cameras: five Lumix and five Fuji), resulted in lower 
reconstruction performance across all evaluation metrics.
It should be noted that PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS were used in this 
study as relative image-level metrics to compare different image 
acquisition configurations under the same reconstruction pipeline. In 
outdoor plot-level cotton scenes, these metrics are strongly influenced 
by complex canopy structures, repetitive textures, partial occlusions, 
shadows, illumination variation, and slight plant movement during 
image acquisition. Therefore, the relatively modest PSNR and SSIM 
values observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of field 
conditions. The practical usefulness of the reconstructed models was 
further validated through downstream cotton boll segmentation and 
counting performance, rather than by the rendering metrics alone.
Image quality affects the performance of NeRF-based 3D recon­
struction. The images produced by the Fuji camera exhibited lower vi­
sual clarity (Fig. S1), which adversely impacted the reconstruction 
results. This is further supported by the image sharpness scores shown in 
Table 3, where the Fuji camera consistently received the lowest scores. 
The variations in camera sensor characteristics contributed to the dif­
ferences in image quality. When both types of cameras were used 
simultaneously, the resulting point cloud exhibits inconsistent and 
visually chaotic color patterns (Fig. 6b). Notably, the color style of the 
point cloud is consistent with that of the corresponding 2D images. 
Quantitative evaluation of the 3D reconstruction using the ten-camera 
setup shows that its PSNR is 2.691 dB lower, SSIM is 0.1117 lower, 
and LPIPS is 0.2006 higher than the best-performing configuration in 
the experiment. These results highlight the importance of maintaining 
sensor consistency in multi-camera data acquisition setups to ensure 
high-quality NeRF-based reconstruction. Therefore, the use of different 
types of cameras is not recommended.
3.2. 2D instance segmentation evaluation
The 2D data annotation approach based on SAM and YOLOv11x 
developed in this study substantially accelerated the labeling process 
and reduced the manual burden for new datasets. Compared with con­
ventional manual labeling methods, this approach significantly 
decreased the labor required. According to our rough estimates during 
the annotation process, the annotation time per image was reduced by at 
least 50%. Furthermore, the proposed workflow is highly adaptable and 
can be easily extended to the annotation of other crop species or data­
sets, demonstrating strong flexibility and scalability.
The YOLOv11x segmentation model achieved strong performance on 
the newly collected 2024 dataset, demonstrating its effectiveness in 
accurately segmenting cotton bolls (Table 5). As shown in the segmen­
tation results of images captured by the Lumix cameras (Fig. 7), cotton 
bolls are correctly segmented from different viewpoints across multiple 
cameras. The instance segmentation results clearly demonstrate the 
robustness of the YOLOv11x model under field conditions. Notably, 
even in the presence of cluttered backgrounds and overlapping struc­
tures such as branches and leaves, the model consistently achieves high- 
precision detection of cotton bolls from multiple viewpoints and suc­
cessfully segments their 2D masks. The results also indicate that cotton 
bolls from adjacent plots were occasionally segmented due to the canopy 
of the MARS system being lifted by wind. However, these instances can 
be manually filtered out during the subsequent 3D mapping process.
3.3. 3D instance segmentation and boll counting
The workflow proposed in this study provides a robust solution for 
plot-level 3D segmentation of cotton bolls, effectively addressing the 
challenges of 2D occlusion and limited 3D annotation. The method 
demonstrates reliable performance on both sparse and dense cotton boll 
distributions, and it can accurately segment cotton bolls regardless of 
whether the data were captured using high-resolution Lumix cameras or 
consumer-grade smartphones. It should be emphasized that the cotton 
boll counting accuracy reported in this section was evaluated indepen­
dently by comparing predicted boll numbers with manually counted 
ground-truth values, rather than being inferred from PSNR, SSIM, or 
LPIPS. By employing the MARS ground robot equipped with a canopy, 
the system can partially mitigate lighting issues commonly encountered 
in field environments. The point clouds reconstructed from Lumix 
camera data exhibit minimal shadows, while those reconstructed from 
smartphone data contain more pronounced shadow artifacts (Fig. 8). 
Moreover, the scanning path of the MARS robot is more stable than that 
of a handheld smartphone.
The final instance segmentation is highly accurate, as illustrated by 
qualitative results from two representative plots acquired under 
different conditions (Fig. 9). A key difference between the two plots lies 
in cotton boll density—Plot1401 features sparse bolls, whereas Plot1332 
contains densely clustered bolls. Despite this difference, the proposed 
method accurately localizes and counts individual cotton bolls in both 
cases, with predicted counts closely matching ground truth. These re­
sults confirm the robustness of the method across varying boll density 
levels.

different combinations of min_cluster_size and min_samples. For the data 
captured by the smartphone, the MAPE of cotton boll counting reached 
its minimum when the parameters were set to a min_cluster_size value of 
9 and a min_samples value of 2. For the data captured by the five Lumix

4. Discussion
Two custom-designed data collection methods were employed in this 
study to support plot-level 3D reconstruction of cotton plants using 
NeRF models and to export colored point clouds. The primary method 
utilizes a custom-developed ground robotic platform, MARS, which is 
equipped with multiple cameras to enable large-scale, high-throughput 
data acquisition in the field. This automated system significantly en­
hances operational efficiency and data consistency, making it particu­
larly suitable for scalable phenotyping tasks (Table 7). In contrast, the 
smartphone-based data collection method offers a low-cost and user- 
friendly alternative, ideal for flexible field sampling scenarios. Howev­
er, it also presents several practical limitations. Data collection using 
smartphones relies on manual operation, which demands substantial 
labor and can introduce inconsistencies—for example, variations in 
camera angles and movement paths during the acquisition process. 
Moreover, smartphones are susceptible to overheating and shutdown in 
high-temperature environments, and their limited battery capacity and 
storage capacity pose further constraints for large-scale or long-duration 
data acquisition. Compared to manual cotton boll counting under high- 
temperature outdoor conditions, the robot-assisted high-throughput 
plot-level 3D reconstruction workflow offers a more efficient, less labor- 
intensive, and more comfortable alternative. The ability to generate 
usable plot-level 3D architectural representations and cotton boll counts 
directly from RGB image inputs further demonstrates the practical 
feasibility of the proposed workflow for field phenotyping applications. 
The outdoor, plot-level data acquisition method adopted in this study 
not only offers broader applicability than indoor or greenhouse methods 
(Liu et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025), but also better reflects plant growth 
under natural conditions, improving the ecological relevance of the 
collected phenotypic traits (Song et al., 2025).
Although smartphone-based acquisition achieved competitive 
reconstruction and counting performance in this study, the primary 
advantage of the robotic platform lies in its ability to provide stable, 
repeatable, and standardized image acquisition for high-throughput 
plot-level field phenotyping. Compared with handheld acquisition, the 
robotic platform can follow consistent scanning trajectories, maintain 
more stable viewpoints, and reduce operator-dependent variability 
across plots and experiments. In addition, the robotic platform enables 
more consistent image acquisition beneath and inside dense cotton 
canopies, where handheld smartphone acquisition may be more limited. 
Therefore, the value of the robotic platform is not solely reflected by 
reconstruction accuracy, but also by its scalability and acquisition

plant movement. Multi-view 2D counting would also require reliable 
association of the same cotton boll across different viewpoints to avoid 
repeated counting. Therefore, a simple 2D baseline based on a single 
selected image, stitched images, or direct summation across multiple 
views would not provide a fair or reliable comparison with the proposed 
3D workflow. In contrast, the proposed workflow enables semantic in­
formation from multiple viewpoints to be integrated into a unified 3D 
representation, providing semantic cotton boll point clouds suitable for 
downstream phenotyping analysis.
The relatively modest PSNR and SSIM values should be interpreted in 
the context of outdoor plot-level crop reconstruction. Unlike controlled 
indoor scenes or rigid objects commonly used in computer vision

shadows, changing illumination, and slight canopy movement during 
image acquisition can further reduce view-to-view consistency. Under 
such conditions, image-level rendering metrics may underestimate the 
practical utility of the reconstructed models for downstream pheno­
typing tasks. In this study, PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS were used only to

NeRF is an open-source method and has been shown to achieve su­
perior 3D reconstruction performance in recent plant phenotyping 
studies (James et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025c). Although UAVs have 
been used in some studies for rapid data collection, the resulting point 
clouds often lack structural completeness, particularly in capturing 
branches and the undersides of cotton bolls (Xiao et al., 2024), whereas 
our proposed method is capable of reconstructing the branches and fine- 
grained architectural traits (Fig. 13a). Other studies have explored the 
use of NeRF on outdoor crops with relatively simple structures, 
demonstrating its feasibility, but often stopping at 3D reconstruction 
without progressing to segmentation or trait extraction (Zhang et al., 
2024). The entire workflow designed in this study enables accurate 
cotton boll counting.
The workflow proposed in this study enables accurate cotton boll 
counting and addresses the challenges associated with the complexity 
and labor-intensive nature of 3D cotton boll annotation, as well as the 
limited availability of plot-level cotton point cloud data. It offers clear 
advantages over direct 2D image-based counting methods by effectively 
overcoming occlusion issues. In boll counting across different plots, our 
approach achieved a MAPE of 5.29% using smartphone data and 5.43% 
using Lumix camera data, whereas a previous study relying solely on 2D 
images reported a MAPE of 8.92% (Sun et al., 2019). Compared with a 
previous study on 3D plot-level analysis, our method eliminates the need 
for manually designed features during training, operates on larger-scale 
plots, and produces more accurate counting results (Sun et al., 2020). 
Overall, the method developed in this study holds great promise for 
applications in cotton boll yield estimation.
The lower reconstruction performance observed in the mixed 10- 
camera setup should not be interpreted as evidence that fewer cam­
eras are inherently better for NeRF-based reconstruction. Instead, the 
results suggest that image consistency across views is more important 
than simply increasing the number of cameras under the current 
experimental conditions. As shown in Table 3, the Lumix cameras pro­
duced higher-quality and more consistent images. In contrast, the mixed 
setup combined two different camera types (Lumix and Fuji), which 
introduced differences in image sharpness, color response, and imaging 
characteristics. Therefore, the mixed-camera configuration should be 
considered a limitation of the current data acquisition setup. In future 
experiments, all cameras will be standardized to the same camera type 
to improve consistency across views and further enhance reconstruction 
performance.
Although this study achieved plot-level 3D reconstruction of outdoor 
cotton plants, using cameras with different models and resolutions 
resulted in reduced point cloud quality. Consequently, only the data 
captured by five Lumix cameras were used for cotton boll counting. 
However, these five cameras provided only a side view and a top view of 
the cotton bolls, lacking imagery from the opposite side. This led to 
partially incomplete point clouds. As shown in Fig. 13 (b, c), two side 
views of the same point cloud are presented, and the enlarged views of 
the cotton bolls from different angles illustrate the missing regions. 
Nevertheless, this did not affect the accuracy of cotton boll counting, as 
the minor incompleteness of individual bolls had negligible impact. 
Furthermore, the ability of the proposed method to reconstruct complete 
plant structures highlights its potential for future phenotypic analyses of 
main stems and branches.
The accuracy of cotton boll counting is not only related to the seg­
mentation precision of FruitNeRF, but also depends on the clustering 
results of HDBSCAN. Fig. 14 illustrates a common issue in the clustering 
step of the cotton boll counting pipeline. As highlighted by the red circle, 
two adjacent cotton bolls are incorrectly grouped into a single cluster by 
HDBSCAN. This occurs due to the close spatial proximity and partial 
occlusion between the bolls, which makes it difficult for the density- 
based algorithm to distinguish them as separate instances. Such cases 
can lead to slight underestimation in the final boll count and suggest the 
need for further refinement in clustering parameters or post-processing 
strategies.
Several limitations of the current study should also be acknowl­
edged. First, the experiments were conducted under post-defoliation 
conditions with relatively low planting density, which reduced canopy 
closure and partially alleviated severe occlusion. Although these con­
ditions remain representative of certain breeding and phenotyping 
scenarios, they are less challenging than dense commercial cotton fields 
with fully closed canopies. It should also be emphasized that the current 
study primarily focused on plot-level phenotyping scenarios in cotton 
breeding trials, where relatively lower planting density and plot-based 
evaluation are commonly adopted experimental conditions. Under 
high-density field conditions, severe occlusion, incomplete viewpoint 
coverage, and reconstruction instability may substantially increase the 
difficulty of semantic 3D reconstruction and cotton boll separation. 
Therefore, the conclusions of the present study should be interpreted 
within the boundary conditions of the current experimental setup. 
Future work will investigate the robustness of the proposed workflow 
under denser and more complex canopy conditions.
In addition, several limitations related to the camera comparison 
experiments should also be noted. The datasets were collected under 
different field conditions and acquisition trajectories. Variations in 
illumination, collection timing, and scanning paths may have influenced 
the reconstruction and counting performance observed among different 
camera configurations. Therefore, the current comparisons should not 
be interpreted as strictly controlled evaluations of camera hardware 
performance alone. Future studies should perform fully controlled ex­
periments under standardized illumination and acquisition conditions to 
enable more rigorous camera and sensor comparisons. Another limita­
tion of the current workflow is its relatively high computational demand 
associated with NeRF optimization and semantic reconstruction. 
Although the proposed framework demonstrated promising perfor­
mance for plot-level cotton phenotyping, the current implementation is 
not yet suitable for real-time or large-scale high-throughput field 
scouting applications.

acquisition process will be further optimized by using a uniform camera

5. Conclusions
This study proposes a ground robot-assisted high-throughput data 
acquisition system and establishes a novel workflow for NeRF-based 
plot-level 3D reconstruction of cotton plants, boll counting, and yield 
estimation in the field. The proposed workflow demonstrates the feasi­
bility of generating semantic plot-level 3D cotton models and perform­
ing cotton boll counting directly from RGB image inputs collected under 
real field conditions. By obtaining high-quality 2D masks of cotton bolls 
from RGB images and projecting them onto the 3D point cloud using 
FruitNeRF, the method effectively bridges 2D segmentation with 3D 
analysis to achieve plot-level boll counting and yield estimation. A 
comparative analysis showed that the data collected by the Lumix 
camera and the smartphone resulted in comparable cotton boll counting 
accuracy; however, the Lumix-camera configuration integrated with the 
MARS robotic platform was better suited for high-throughput field 
deployment because it provided more stable, repeatable, and stan­
dardized image acquisition. The proposed approach offers a valuable 
solution for high-resolution plant mapping and 3D phenotyping, and it is 
adaptable to a wide range of crops. In future work, we will adopt a 
standardized setup using ten identical cameras for data acquisition and 
focus on extracting plant architectural traits from field data, such as 
branch angles and main stem height at the plot level.
