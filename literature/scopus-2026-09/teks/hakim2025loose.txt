Loose Oil Palm Fruitlet Detection Using YOLOv8 Nano Model By
Layer Freezing Training for Better Accuracy with Small Dataset
Muhammad Najmi Hakim Nasir∗1,2, Wan Nurshazwani Wan Zakaria1, Ryosuke Horio2 and Naoki Uchiyama2
Abstract— Palm oil is a vital commodity for Malaysia, con-
tributing 30% of global vegetable oil production. Beyond fresh
fruit bunch (FFB) harvesting, loose fruitlets play a crucial role
in palm oil production. Automating their detection is essential
for sustainable agricultural robotics in addressing ongoing
labour shortages. Traditional image processing methods rely
on manually engineered features, which may not accurately
represent fruitlets and it is very time-consuming to implement.
This study evaluates the use of layer freezing of YOLOv8
nano for loose fruitlet detection, using mean average precision
(mAP50-95) as the performance metric. Results show a 0.251
difference in mAP50-95 between large and small datasets, in-
dicating potential overﬁtting with smaller datasets. To mitigate
this, pre-processing and dynamic augmentation were applied to
a 280-image dataset. Models were trained using the AdamW
optimizer, with the YOLOv8-Nano model achieving the highest
mAP50-95 of 0.75 on the smaller dataset. A training-validation
gap of approximately 0.1 for DFL loss and 0.2 for box loss over
100 epochs suggests reduced overﬁtting.
I. INTRODUCTION
The production of palm oil has a signiﬁcant impact on
Malaysia’s GDP. Malaysia is the world’s top producer of
palm oil [1]. Recent research has emphasized that the loose
fruitlets may be used to make biodiesel as a way to address
the demand for renewable energy from non-food crops [2].
However, picking loose fruit is a labor intensive task that may
cause discomfort for workers. Additionally, it is anticipated
that there may be a manpower scarcity in the future, thus
research nowadays is focused on developing automated and
mechanized techniques for gathering fruitlets [3].
A. Automation in Loose Fruitlet Collection
Numerous mechanical solutions have been created, rang-
ing from basic picking mechanisms to autonomous systems
for the collection process. Using vacuum-type collectors
[4] for the robot end effector is one of the well-known
approaches [5]. However, using this method increases the

B. Object Detection with Deep Learning
For robotic applications, single-stage object identiﬁcation
algorithms have gained popularity due to their exceptional
accuracy and real-time functionality [6]. Version 8 iteration
of the YOLO architecture utilises a Feature Pyramid network
(FPN) with a Path Aggregation Network (PAN) for multi-
scale detection and a CSPNet backbone [7] for effective
feature extraction.
C. Limitation of Training Method
This study focuses on the training strategy of the YOLOv8
model. Other deep learning models that are similar to YOLO
that is used for object detection often require different tuning
and adjustment for the best performance with a limited
dataset. In the context of training YOLO based architecture
for object detection, this method of training is only applicable
with the nano model under the condition of small available
data with one class of objects for detection. The method
also does not focuses on the the implication of the negative
impact of class imbalance between null images used during
training.

image used for our dataset is taken from a Redmi Note 10 Pro
smartphone model. The raw image resolution is 1080x2400

C. Training of YOLOv8 Architecture
To implement layer freezing in YOLOv8 using PyTorch,
the process involves selecting a portion of the model’s
early layers and disabling their ability to update during
training. This is done by accessing each of those layers
and turning off their gradient calculations showed in Figure
2. This effectively locks the weights during training. As a
result, these layers retain the features they learned during
pretraining such as edges, textures, and basic shapes. The
other layers are trainable and can adapt to the new task.
This method mimics the internal behavior of YOLOv8 when
using the built-in freeze=N option and is especially helpful
when ﬁne-tuning on a small or domain-speciﬁc dataset.
To investigate the impact of layer freezing on YOLOv8
performance, a baseline is established using the standard
YOLOv8 nano architecture (Figure 3). This architecture,
with its varying depth and width multiplier that deﬁnes the
number and size of layers respectively, offers a ﬂexible struc-
ture for feature extraction (neck), and object detection (head).
The ratio parameter governs the scaling of these layers across
different model sizes. For all experiments, input images from
the dataset are resized to YOLOv8’s standard input size of
640x640 pixels. Subsequent experiments involve selectively
freezing speciﬁc layers or blocks within the backbone, neck,
or head of this architecture to analyze the effect on training
efﬁciency and detection accuracy.

B. Analysis of mAP Results
As a single-stage detection, YOLOv8 algorithm utilizes
mean Average Precision (mAP) as a key performance met-
ric. Speciﬁcally, mAP50-95 evaluates the model’s precision
across multiple IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95, providing
a more stringent and comprehensive assessment. In deep
learning, larger datasets typically lead to better generalization
and performance [17]. However, in scenarios with limited

C. Layer Freezing Analysis
Freezing the early layers of the pre-trained model helps
preserve general object feature. This approach allows only
the later layers to be ﬁne-tuned, encouraging the model to
focus on task-speciﬁc features such as fruitlet color and size
while retaining foundational representations. In this case,
freezing more than 7 layers of the Nano model reduces
the model’s ability to adapt to unique characteristics of the
dataset, such as the speciﬁc texture of oil palm fruitlets,
variations in lighting and angles not seen during pretraining.
Based on training results, precision drops from 0.75 to 0.72.
This trend suggests that freezing more layers introduces
model underﬁtting due to limited learning capacity. The
biggest model that was not trained with layer freezing
method have lower precision of 0.668. This proves that layer
freezing method can give better training results.

D. Analysis of Model Overﬁtting
YOLOv8 is known to out perform its predecessors in
having higher precision when it comes to small object
detection [18]. This is beneﬁcial for detecting loose Oil
Palm fruits. With limited dataset, overﬁtting can be a major
problem for a deep learning model [6]. Overﬁtting can be
observed by the behaviour validation loss graph which is
deﬁned by a loss function. Generally it is understood that
the divergence of the training and validation loss indicates
overﬁtting.
1) Distribution Focal Loss Function: The Distribution
Focal Loss (DFL) is an improvement of YOLOv8’s loss func-
tion from previous models [19]. This loss function addresses
localisation issues of Oil Palm fruits in densely packed scene
[20]. Li et al. [21] proposed a loss function that models
bounding box coordinates as probability distributions over
discrete values, optimized using a focal loss-like approach
[22]. Figure 7 compares the training and validation loss for
the DFL function.
Training loss starts gradually with rough ﬂuctuations after
reaching the highest peak value. It shows that the model
is trying to improve the prediction of the bounding box
distribution on training data. The decreasing trend indicates
that the model struggles to apply what it learns to data it
hasn’t seen before. The relatively small gap suggests that
overﬁtting is not severe.
2) Bounding Box Loss Function: The bounding box re-
gression loss, quantiﬁes the difference between predicted and
ground truth bounding boxes in terms of spatial properties
such as position, size, and shape. Various object detection

IV. CONCLUSION
This study aimed to identify the most suitable YOLOv8
model for detecting loose oil palm fruitlets under limited
training data conditions. The results indicate that the Nano
model offers an effective trade-off between detection accu-
racy and the constraints posed by small datasets. While some
signs of overﬁtting are observed due to data scarcity, the
model still maintains strong accuracy. As shown in Table III,
the performance gap between the best-performing models
for BD and SD scenarios is relatively small (0.207), further
supporting the model’s consistency.
Importantly, this work does not suggest that the layer
freezing strategy is superior to other techniques such as ar-
chitectural modiﬁcations. Rather, introducing layer freezing
as a potentially useful base method that could be combined
with ablation modules to enhance overall mAP performance
for training. Future research could explore this integrated
approach alongside other strategies, such as synthetic data
augmentation, layer pruning, and hyperparameter optimiza-
tion. Together, these directions contribute to more efﬁcient
and sustainable automation in oil palm fruitlet collection.
