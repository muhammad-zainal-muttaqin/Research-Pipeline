Using Green Atlas Cartographer to investigate orchard-
specific relationships between tree geometry, fruit 
number, fruit clustering, fruit size and fruit colour in 
commercial apples and pears 
A. Scalisi1,a, L. McClymont1, M. Peavey1,2, P. Morton3, S. Scheding3, J. Underwood3 and 
I. Goodwin1,2 
1Tatura SmartFarm, Agriculture Victoria, Tatura, Victoria, Australia; 2Centre for Agricultural Innovation, The 
University of Melbourne, Australia; 3Green Atlas, Alexandria, New South Wales, Australia. 
Abstract 
This study investigated the adoption of a commercial, sensorised platform – 
namely Green Atlas Cartographer, equipped with a network of proximal sensors 
(including cameras and LiDAR) and machine learning algorithms – to evaluate orchard-
specific relationships between estimated tree geometry, fruit number, fruit clustering, 
fruit size and fruit colour in commercial apple and pear orchards. The study was 
conducted at three commercial apple and pear sites in the Goulburn Valley (Victoria, 
Australia) during the 2021-2022 season. Estimations of canopy area, canopy height, 
canopy density and cross-sectional leaf area were generated using LiDAR technology. 
Fruit number, fruit clustering, fruit colour development and fruit diameter were 
estimated using a combination of machine vision and deep learning. The geo-
referenced data points generated by Cartographer were grouped into spatial plots and 
statistics by plot were obtained. Correlation and principal component analyses of 
harvest crop parameters unveiled underlying relationships between tree geometry, 
productive performance and fruit quality attributes. The relationships obtained in this 
study can drive orchard design strategies and dictate management decisions so that 
trees can be standardised to consistently produce high-quality fruit over the lifespan of 
modern apple and pear orchards. 
Keywords: AI, ground-based platform, LiDAR, machine vision, precision horticulture 
INTRODUCTION 
Precise orchard management benefits significantly by integrating sensing technology to 
improve the objective automation of operations that are tailored to crop and cultivar 
responses to various soils and environments. Often, the physiological and productive 
performances that a crop or cultivar expresses in an orchard are not easily reproduced in 
different orchard blocks (Aggelopoulou et al., 2010). This is due to the complex interaction 
between soil conditions (e.g., structure, texture, drainage), orchard microclimate, pollination 
(both polleniser and pollinator variability), biennial bearing cycle and pest and disease 
pressure. Hence, it is important to derive orchard-specific relationships between crop 
productive performance and tree size/geometry in addition to attempting to define 
universally valid cultivar-specific management strategies. 
The integration of technologies that are already adopted in other industries into 
horticulture systems aims to increase resource use efficiency – including labour – and make 
orchards more profitable (El-Naggar et al., 2021; Norasma et al., 2019). Aerial or ground-
based platforms are becoming commercially available to estimate several crop parameters 
with proximal or remote sensing. The Green Atlas Cartographer represents one of the most 
sophisticated ground-based platforms in the market, allowing simultaneous and geo-
referenced estimations of tree geometry parameters (e.g., canopy area, canopy density,

canopy height, cross-sectional leaf area), flower and fruit number, yield, (Scalisi et al., 2021; 
Islam et al., 2022), fruit cluster size (number of fruit per fruit cluster in an image), fruit size 
and fruit skin colour, and some experimental features currently under investigation such as 
canopy temperature. Geo-referencing a multitude of crop parameters enhances the 
investigation of orchard-specific relationships by studying the correlations between the 
estimated parameters within orchard zones. 
This study investigated the use of the ground-based platform Cartographer to derive 
orchard-specific correlations between tree geometry parameters, productive performance 
and fruit quality parameters in three commercial orchards – two pear and one apple cultivars. 
The aim was to determine whether Cartographer can support the identification of correlation 
trends between crop parameters that can be used to reduce spatial variability and inform 
orchard operations such as thinning, pruning, hedging and irrigation to achieve the best 
compromise between high yields and fruit quality. 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Experimental sites in commercial orchards 
The experiment was conducted in 2021-2022 in two pear orchard blocks (‘ANP-0131’ 
marketed as Ricó ®, and ‘PremP009’ marketed as Piqa® Boo®, Calimna Orchards, Ardmona, 
Victoria, Australia) and in one apple orchard block (‘Ruby Pink’, marketed as Pink Lady®, 
Plunkett Orchards, Ardmona, Victoria, Australia). Details of the orchards used in this study are 
reported in Table 1. Measurements were collected at harvest.

Mobile platform and estimated crop variables 
The mobile platform Cartographer used in this study was equipped with two RGB 
cameras, four custom-built strobe lights for continuous canopy illumination to enhance 
machine vision, LiDAR and an onboard GPS antenna interfaced with an offboard GNSS (global 
navigation satellite system) for real time kinematic (RTK) corrections (Emlid Ltd., Hong Kong, 
China). Green Atlas uses a proprietary convolutional neural network algorithm to train 
detections of fruit. LiDAR data are used to extrapolate canopy height, area and density, and 
cross-sectional leaf area (CSLA, a proxy of leaf area). The following variables were measured 
at harvest: i) fruit number/tree, ii) cluster size (number of fruit/fruit image cluster), iii) fruit 
diameter, iv) fruit skin colour development index (CDI, i.e., a skin colour attribute derived from 
hue angle that ranges from 0 (pure green) to 1 (pure red) – see Scalisi et al. (2022)), v) canopy 
height, vi) canopy area, vii) canopy density, and viii) CSLA. For an explanation of these 
variables and for their validation see Scalisi et al. (2021). 
Geoprocessing and spatial analysis 
Data points obtained with Cartographer were uploaded into QGIS (v. 3.6) and colour-
coded spatial heatmaps of the orchards were obtained using a five-class quantile 
representation (0-20th, 20-40th, 40-60th, 60-80th, and 80-100th percentiles). Data points were 
subsequently overlaid with a grid of pseudo-plots (i.e., plots subjectively drawn with 
horizontal spacing equivalent to 3× row spacing and 10× tree spacing in ‘ANP-0131’ and 
‘PremP009’ and 2× row spacing and 5× tree spacing in ‘Ruby Pink’, to obtain a number of 
experimental units for correlation purposes >200). Medians of the measured variables were 
extracted from each pseudo-plot to derive orchard-specific correlations.

Statistical analysis 
Crop parameters were correlated using Pearson’s correlation matrices. Relationships 
between crop parameters were subsequently analysed with a principal component analysis 
(PCA) to summarise the relationships between crop parameters using a dimension reduction 
approach. PCA loading plots were used to show the direction of relationships. Statistical 
analyses and graphs were produced using a combination of R (v. 3.6.1, The R Foundation for 
Statistical Computing) and Jamovi (v. 2.2.5, The Jamovi Project), and the factoextra 
(Kassambara and Mundt, 2020) and snowCluster (Seol, 2022) packages. 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Spatial maps 
Colour-coded spatial heatmaps of canopy density, fruit number per tree, CDI and fruit 
diameter obtained in the ‘PremP009’ orchard are shown in Figure 1 as an example of the data 
points generated using Cartographer. Figure 1A revealed a tendency for ‘PremP009’ trees to 
have higher canopy density in the south-central area of the orchard block that visually 
matched lowest fruit numbers per tree (Figure 1B) and reduced CDI (Figure 1C). Both the fruit 
number per tree and CDI maps showed a horizontal stripe in the middle of the orchard 
corresponding to pollenisers in the block that had higher crop loads and greener skin. The 
fruit diameter map (Figure 1D) did not unveil a clearly recognisable spatial trend. 
Orchard summary and variability 
Average crop parameters and their variability obtained in the pear and apple orchards 
are summarised in Table 2. In 2021-2022, the orchards were in an OFF season within the 
biennial bearing cycle. Thus, fruit numbers were relatively low, particularly for ‘PremP009’. 
The variability of fruit number per tree and cluster size was particularly high in ‘ANP-0131’ 
pears (coefficient of variation = 75%) due to an ongoing trial with thinning chemicals in the 
orchard. ‘PremP009’ also showed reduced fruit set compared to the other two cultivars that 
led to the lowest cluster-size. ‘PremP009’ had redder fruit and the lowest CDI variability 
compared to ‘Ruby Pink’ and ‘ANP-131’, in line with expectations based on cultivar-specific 
skin colour characteristics. Fruit diameter variability in the three orchards ranged from 9 to 
11%. The highest canopies were detected in ‘Ruby Pink’ trees but canopy area, density and 
CSLA averages and coefficients of variation were similar to ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’.

plots as in Figure 2. Grids consisted of 244 (12×15 m), 300 (12×12 m) and 266 (7×7.5 m) 
pseudo-plots for ‘ANP-0131’, ‘PremP009’ and ‘Ruby Pink’, respectively. Correlation analysis

Fruit number per tree was consistently inversely correlated (p<0.05) with canopy 
height, area, density and CSLA in ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’ (Table 3), corroborating visual 
inferences from ‘PremP009’ maps (Figure 1A, B). In ‘Ruby Pink’ trees, fruit number per tree 
and cluster size had no or poor significant correlation with canopy geometry. Fruit number 
per tree and cluster size were expectedly strongly correlated with each other in the three 
cultivars (r>0.890). Fruit diameter had the most relevant correlations with fruit number per 
tree and cluster size (inverse association). CDI was mostly driven by an inverse relationship 
with canopy size in ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’, with a consistent loss of fruit redness in trees 
with higher canopy height, area, density and CSLA. Fruit redness in ‘Ruby Pink’ was less 
negatively influenced by increasing canopy size, instead increasing fruit number per tree 
combined with cluster size contributed to lower fruit skin redness. The weak, although 
significant, positive correlations between CDI and fruit number in ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’ 
pears were likely to be driven by the low fruit number and yield as in these circumstances 
trees become more vigorous and fruit are not as exposed to the sun, leading to a reduction of 
skin redness expression. Fruit diameter had poor or no significant association with fruit 
redness in ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’ pears. 
PCA showed that most of the variance in each cultivar was characterised by the first two 
principal components (eigenvalues >1.0; Table 4). The PCA loading plots of the two principal 
components in ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’ (Figure 3A, B) suggest that crop parameters were 
grouped into four clusters – i) fruit number tree-1 and cluster size; ii) CDI; iii) fruit diameter; 
and iv) canopy height, area, density and CSLA. In this case, the first two components both 
identified the inverse relationship between (a) CDI and canopy geometry parameters, and (b) 
fruit diameter and fruit number and cluster size. These observations confirm the trends of the 
correlation analyses in the two pear cultivars (Table 3).

The loading plot of the two principal components derived from crop parameters in the 
‘Ruby Pink’ orchard showed three clearly defined clusters (Figure 3C) – i) fruit diameter and 
CDI; ii) fruit number tree-1 and cluster size; and iii) canopy height, area, density and CSLA. The 
cumulative variance explained by the two first principal components was lower (70%) than 
in the pear orchards (86 and 77% for ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’, respectively), but the 
loading plot confirmed the direction of the relationships derived from Table 3. The PCA helped 
to visually cluster crop parameters within the four loading plot quadrants and suggested that, 
although genetically different, the two pear cultivars have similar relationship directions 
between canopy geometry, productive performance and fruit quality, which may lead to 

 
209 
 
similar orchard management practices. On the other hand, ‘Ruby Pink’ apple relationships 
appeared different, as fruit colour is more directly linked to fruit size compared to the pear 
cultivars. 
Relationships summarised in this work highlighted how, despite their different fruit 
quality characteristics and genetics, the trends and correlation directions of crop parameter 
relationships in ‘ANP-0131’ and ‘PremP009’ remained consistent. We demonstrated that even 
in ‘PremP009’, a pear with uniform red skin, CDI was significantly affected by canopy size, 
similar to ‘ANP-0131’ which is a blush pear. This is consistent with the requirement of fruit 
exposure to solar radiation for the development of colour in ‘ANP-0131’ (Peavey et al., 2020). 
The significant correlations can be further investigated to derive optimal values of canopy 
geometry and fruit number to improve profitability. These relationships can also be used to 
standardise orchard conditions in order to simplify machine-driven management.

CONCLUSIONS 
In summary, this study identified orchard-specific relationships using large data sets of 
canopy geometry, yield and fruit quality parameters obtained with the ground-based platform 
Cartographer. Using the methodology proposed in this study, relationships between absolute 
or relative crop parameters can be estimated to reduce spatial variability and achieve the best 

 
210 
yield and fruit quality. It is important that more years of data are considered to quantify 
accurate orchard-specific relationships to be used for orchard management, particularly for 
alternate bearing crops and cultivars. 
In future studies, attempts will be made to objectively determine thresholds of canopy 
geometry parameters to optimise return bloom, fruit number and fruit quality to inform 
orchard automation of essential operations such as thinning, pruning, spraying and irrigation.
