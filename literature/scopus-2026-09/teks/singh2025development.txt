A B S T R A C T
Timely blueberry yield monitoring is critical to guide harvest planning, optimize marketing, and support 
breeding programs. Traditional yield assessment methods, either through hand harvesting or visual assessment, 
are often labor intensive or error prone. This study developed and evaluated a pipeline to predict individual 
blueberry plant yield from smartphone images by combining deep learning-based fruit detection with key crop 
data. YOLOv8 models were fine-tuned to detect ripe and unripe berries in side-view field images of southern 
highbush and rabbiteye genotypes, with the best model (YOLOv8-x) achieving a mean average precision (mAP50) 
of 0.708. The YOLO-derived visible ripe berry counts, along with manually measured average berry weight and 
genotype data, were used to train 42 machine learning regression models. Additionally, three strategies for using 
the two-side ripe berry counting data were explored. A histogram-based gradient boosting regressor performed 
best, predicting yield with an R2 of 0.80, a bias of 16.48 g, and limits of agreement of [−440.28 g, 473.24 g]. 
Notably, excluding genotype from model inputs substantially decreased prediction accuracy (R2 = 0.47). Sig­
nificant variations in plant architecture and mean ripe berry occlusion were observed across genotypes. This 
suggests that image-derived architectural features could serve as a practical proxy for genotype in future yield 
prediction models. To make this developed tool accessible, the image processing and modeling pipeline was 
packaged into the "Blueberry Yield Analyzer," a user-friendly Android app on the Google Play Store that enables 
real-time yield monitoring for growers and researchers.
1. Introduction
Blueberry is often considered a “super-fruit” for its high nutritional 
content and anti-inflammatory actions [1]. The United States is a lead­
ing blueberry producer in the world, which contributes around 322,000 
t of blueberries annually to the global market [2]. The primary regions 
for blueberry production in the United States include the Northeast, 
Southeast, Northwest, West, and the Midwest, owing to their favorable 
growing conditions that support large-scale blueberry cultivation [3]. 
Three major types of blueberries are commercially produced for the 
fresh fruit market in the United States [4]. The northern highbush 
blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.) is mainly produced in the northern 
region due to its requirement for long chilling hours [4]. On the other 
hand, southern highbush (V. corymbosum interspecific hybrids) and 
rabbiteye blueberry (V. virgatum Ait.) are commonly produced in the 
southern region for their lower chilling requirement and adaptation to a 
warmer climate [4].
Accurate yield and maturity estimation before harvest is important 
for blueberry growers to plan harvesting operations and optimize mar­
keting strategies. Moreover, accurate yield data is critical for breeders 
and researchers to evaluate blueberry germplasm and conduct various 
research initiatives to improve crop management and other related 
areas. Traditionally, blueberry yield is measured by manually or me­
chanically harvesting and weighing berries, which is labor intensive and 
time consuming. Pre-harvest estimation relying on visual assessment is 
largely inaccurate due to difficulties of counting numerous berries on 
mature plants. Image-based plant analysis assisted with deep learning 
(DL) methodologies emerged as a promising tool for accurate blueberry

detection [5]. DL is a computational approach that imitates human brain 
learning through examples. DL has become instrumental in diverse ap­
plications such as self-driving cars, facial recognition, and various 
classification/detection tasks, owing to its ability to glean meaningful 
insights from large datasets [6]. In agriculture, computer vision-based 
DL techniques have been applied in disease detection, weed identifica­
tion, land cover classification, vegetation composition assessment, 
weather forecasting, and the estimation of crop height and yield [7–10]. 
As a standard for real-time object detection, YOLO models have been 
extensively used in various real-time object detection tasks, such as 
autonomous vehicles and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV)-based sur­
veillance systems [11]. Unlike Region-based Convolutional Neural 
Networks (R-CNNs) which use a two-stage detection pipeline, YOLO 
models predict bounding boxes and class probabilities in a single pass, 
making them faster and more efficient for real-time applications [12].
The distinctive color and shape of blueberries make them prime 
candidates for image-based analysis for yield prediction and maturity 
estimation. Previous studies, although limited, have exhibited prom­
ising outcomes in both wild and cultivated blueberries. Based on the 
percentage of blue pixels in field images, Swain et al. [13] employed 
linear regression models in an automated yield monitoring system for 
wild blueberries (V. angustifolium Ait.), which achieved R2 = 0.967 be­
tween predicted and actual fruit yield. Qu et al. [14] developed XGBoost 
models to accurately predict wild blueberry yield (R² = 0.89) based on 
UAV images. MacEachern et al. [15] evaluated six YOLO CNN models 
for wild blueberry detection across three maturity stages, with the best 
performing model achieving 79.79 % mAP50. For cultivated blueberries, 
Ni et al. [16] trained Mask R-CNN for southern highbush blueberries, 
demonstrating higher accuracy than manual counting (R² = 0.886) and 
strong correlation for maturity estimation (R² = 0.908). Additionally, 
Deng et al. [5] developed the first open-access image dataset for rab­
biteye and highbush blueberries, containing 17,955 manually annotated 
berries. The YOLOv8l model developed by [5] achieved approximately 
93 % mAP50 score in distinguishing between ripe and unripe blueberry 
fruits.
In addition to the fruit detection approaches, other yield estimation 
methods have been developed that leverage remote sensing-derived 
canopy structure metrics. Bai et al. [17] integrated multi-temporal 
remotely sensed leaf area index (LAI) observations into the WOFOST 
crop growth model using the SUBPLEX optimization algorithm, 
enhancing the accuracy of jujube yield forecasts at the field scale. More 
recently, Chen et al. [18] combined UAV multispectral imagery with 
light detection and ranging (LiDAR) data to extract detailed spectral and 
morphological traits of individual apple trees, which were then used to 
train ensemble learning models for yield prediction tasks. By integrating 
vegetation indices with structural parameters such as crown projection 
area and crown volume, this method achieved high prediction accuracy 
at the individual-tree level. These methods show the promise of 
combining spectral, structural, and phenological data for yield predic­
tion but often rely on specialized sensors, repeated seasonal data 
collection, and complex processing. The integration of remote sensing 
and crop modeling also demands extensive calibration for specific cul­
tivars, is sensitive to field-scale variability, and typically yields 
coarser-resolution estimates.
Despite these advances, significant gaps persist in field-based appli­
cations for yield prediction for cultivated blueberries. Blueberries pre­
sent unique challenges for yield prediction due to their small berry size, 
dense clustering, complex canopy structure, and the presence of multi­
ple maturation stages within the same plant. Furthermore, even the deep 
blue to purple hues of mature berries can be easily mistaken for foliage 
shadows, which reduces the accuracy of berry detection [19]. While 
YOLO models have proven effective for large fruits like mango [20,21], 
their transferability to blueberry yield prediction remains sparsely 
documented, primarily due to challenges mentioned above. Addition­
ally, large DL models that require substantial computational capacity are 
not accessible for growers, and currently, no user-friendly applications 
allow growers to easily estimate yield in the field setting. To address 
existing research gaps, the objectives of this study were to: 
1) Evaluate the performance of YOLOv8 models for image-based blue­
berry detection of multiple cultivars under field conditions,
2) Assess the feasibility of ML-based blueberry yield estimation using 
image-derived berry count, average berry weight, and genotype in­
formation, and
3) Develop a user-friendly smartphone application that facilitates real- 
time yield and maturity monitoring in the field setting, serving both 
growers and researchers.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data collection
Field data was collected in 2022 and 2023 from three blueberry 
farms in Georgia and Alabama in the United States, including the Blue 
River Legacy Farms in Lake Park, GA; Bartley Blueberry Farm in Auburn, 
AL; and the Gulf Coast Research and Extension Center of Auburn Uni­
versity, Fairhope, AL (30.54574, −87.88099). The dataset includes 178 
images of 89 blueberry plants. The plants consisted of 17 genotypes, 
including 9 cultivars (southern highbush: ‘Cielo’, ‘Farthing’, ‘Jewel’, 
‘Meadowlark’, ‘Star’, ‘Springhigh’; rabbiteye: ‘Alapaha’, ‘Premier’, 
‘Powderblue’) and 8 advanced selections (Table 1). Farms in Lake Park, 
GA and Fairhope, AL were managed following standard practices. Plants 
were grown on raised beds amended with pine barks. Space between 
plants was around 0.91 m. The Bartley Farm in Auburn, AL was 
managed with less intensity, with less fertilization and forgoing drip 
irrigation. Space between plants was approximately 1.8 m, allowing 
individual plants to develop into larger bushes.
Phenotypic data collection for each location was performed in 
following steps. First, three to twelve healthy blueberry plants were 
chosen for each cultivar or genotype. Second, parking sticks with 
AprilTags were used to mark the edges of each plant, ensuring consis­
tency in image framing. Additionally, plant labels were included in each 
image to correctly identify individual plants. The AprilTags on the 
parking sticks enable automated processing, linking plant IDs to the 
labels for efficient identification during data analysis. Plant height and 
width were manually measured with a tape measure for data collected in 
2023. Prior to harvesting, two images were captured from the sides of 
each plant. Images were taken using a 50-megapixel camera on a 
smartphone (Pixel 6 Pro, Google, Mountain View, CA, USA). The images 
were captured from a height of around 1 m above the ground and at a 
distance of approximately 2 m from the plant ensuring the entire plant 
was captured in each frame. Images were saved on the smartphone in . 
JPG format with dimensions of 3024 × 4032 pixels each. After image 
collection, ripe berries were manually harvested from each side of the 
plant. Berries harvested from each side were manually counted and 
weighted separately to obtain average berry weight and yield for each 
side. Yield from two sides were summed to obtain the total yield of each 
plant.
2.1.1. Data pre-processing
Original images were cropped prior to analysis to remove back­
ground and neighboring plants. The preprocessed images were further 
tiled into sub-images to preserve the original spatial resolution, as the 
berries are small and require finer detail for accurate training of the DL 
models. The edited images were divided into smaller sub-images 
measuring 224 × 224 pixels each, which resulted in a dataset contain­
ing a total of 17,432 images. Images were manually annotated using the 
“Smart Polygon” tool available on Roboflow [22]. Berries were classified 
into two maturity groups, ripe and unripe based on berry colors (Fig. 1). 
The annotation files in .txt format and the corresponding YAML 
configuration file, compatible with YOLOv8, exported from the Robo­
flow software were used for model training.

2.2. Blueberry detection using YOLOv8 models
Over time, researchers have continued to refine and enhance the 
YOLO methodology, introducing various model iterations with 
improved performance and capabilities. The recent versions released 
under the YOLO family have focused more on detection of smaller ob­
jects with higher accuracy [23]. The YOLOv8 model released by 
‘Ultralytics’ boasts superior detection accuracy and speed, further so­
lidifying the YOLO series. This balance of speed and precision has made 
YOLO increasingly versatile, and applicable in diverse fields like ro­
botics, surveillance, and precision agriculture, where quick and accurate 
detections are crucial for effective decision-making [12].
The YOLOv8 network architecture comprises three main compo­
nents: the backbone, the neck, and the head. To extract features from 
images, the YOLOv8 model utilizes a modified CSP Darknet architecture 
as its backbone [24]. Within the backbone, the CBS module conducts 
convolutional operations on the input data, the C2f module enhances the 
flow of feature extraction, and the SPFF layer connects the max pooling 
layers to reduce latency. The Neck layer is structured with a PAN-FPN 
(Path Aggregation Network-Feature Pyramid Network) design, con­
structing both top-down and bottom-up networks to generate a more 
diverse and comprehensive feature representation. This enhanced 
feature representation facilitates the model’s ability to interpret visual 
data, resulting in improved performance in object detection-related 
tasks. The multi-scale feature fusion in the PAN-FPN, combined with 
the C2f module’s richer gradient flow, enables YOLOv8 to capture 
fine-grained details, making it particularly effective for detecting 
smaller objects in complex plant images. For the detection component, 
YOLOv8 employs a decoupled head structure. This structure utilizes 
binary cross-entropy loss for classification tasks and employs distribu­
tion focal loss and class Intersection over Union (IoU) loss for predicting 
bounding box regression tasks [25,26]. These components collectively

The performance of the YOLOv8 models was evaluated using several 
key metrics: Precision, Recall, and mean Average Precision (mAP). 
Precision measures the proportion of true positive detections among all 
positive predictions. Recall quantifies the ratio of true positive de­
tections to the total number of ground truth objects, and mAP assesses 
the average precision across all classes at specific Intersection over 
Union (IoU) thresholds, including mAP50 (considering an IoU threshold 
of 0.50) and mAP50–95 (considering a range of IoU thresholds from 0.50 
to 0.95) [5,15]. These evaluation metrics provide insights into the 
model’s ability to accurately detect and classify objects within the given 
dataset. Precision measures the correctness of the positive predictions, 
while recall measures the completeness of the model’s detections. The 
mAP metrics assess the model’s overall performance, considering both 
the accuracy and the localization of the detections.
2.3. Machine learning-based yield prediction
Occlusion was the major obstacle for yield prediction, as a substan­
tial number of berries were concealed by branches, foliage, and clusters 
(Fig. 2). Performance of the berry detection model was evaluated based 
on its capacity to identify visible berries, rather than providing an 
exhaustive count of all berries on each plant. To account for obscured 
berries and other factors for yield prediction, we constructed several ML 
models to predict the total yield for blueberry plants. ML-based regres­
sion models were trained to predict yield by understanding the re­
lationships between input features and the continuous target yield 
variable. These models employ optimization algorithms to fine-tune 
their parameters, aiming to minimize the discrepancy between the 
predicted and actual yields during training. A correlation matrix was 
utilized to identify the features most directly linked to yield to improve 
the models’ performance and interpretability. This feature selection 
method ensures that only the most relevant predictors are included in 
the final models.
Two types of yield prediction models were developed and evaluated. 
The full prediction models used three parameters for yield prediction: 
average berry weight, image-derived ripe berry count, and genotype to 
predict yield. Simplified models used only the average berry weight and 
DL-detected ripe berry count for yield prediction, making it more 
practical for applications where prior knowledge on the cultivar or ge­
notype is not available. To maintain data integrity and avoid overfitting, 
consistency was maintained throughout the evaluation process by 
ensuring that images from the same plants were used either in the 
training or the testing datasets.
We assessed three distinct yield prediction approaches for both types 
of yield prediction models, utilizing images captured from one side of 
the plant as well as from both sides. The Single Image – Single Side 
(SISS) strategy predicts yield from one side of the plant based on 
detected ripe berry count from the image taken from one side of the 
plant, alongside other traits. The total yield for the plant was then 
calculated by adding the yields predicted from each side. The Single 
Image – Whole Plant (SIWP) strategy predicted the yield based on ripe 
berry count from the image taken from only one side of the plant. This 
approach was applied to both sides of each plant individually, resulting 
in two separate yield estimates per plant, which were then averaged to 
approximate the yield. The third strategy, Dual Image – Whole Plant 
(DIWP) used the combined count of ripe berries from both sides of the 
plant, based on images taken from two sides, to predict the yield per 
plant. A schematic representation of this methodology is presented in 
Fig. 3, which outlines the step-by-step process of yield prediction model 
development, evaluation, training, and statistical analyses.
ML-model training was facilitated by the ’LazyPredict’ library in 
Python [27], designed to utilize both the phenotypic characteristics of 
blueberry plants and DL-detected ripe berry counts. LazyPredict auto­
mates training 42 different ML models spanning multiple categories 
such as linear models (Linear Regression, Ridge Regression, Lasso 
Regression), ensemble methods (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, 
HistGradientBoosting, Extra Trees), tree-based models (Decision Tree 
Regressor, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost), support vector machines, 
k-nearest neighbors, and neural network-based regressors. Once the 
top-performing model was identified, it was optimized using the Grid­
Search algorithm. This additional step helped to verify and potentially 
enhance the model’s performance while also identifying the optimal set 
of hyperparameters that produce the best results. By combining ML and 
DL techniques, this integrated approach seeks to accurately estimate 
blueberry yields from a range of input variables.

Coefficient of Determination (R²), on the test dataset. Cross-validation 
techniques were employed to compare the performance of different 
regression-based ML models. These metrics provide insights into the 
accuracy, precision, and overall performance of the ML models in pre­
dicting the blueberry yield. The MSE, RMSE, MAPE, and R² were eval­

2.4. Quantification of ripe berry occlusion patterns
In many cases, multiple ripe berries within the plant canopy are 
occluded by overlapping foliage, berry clusters, or structural compo­
nents of the plant, making them invisible to detection algorithms. This 
occlusion presents a significant challenge in accurately identifying and 
counting ripe berries, as the extent of occlusion can vary due to differ­
ences in plant architecture, foliage density, and berry arrangement. 
These variations are further influenced by the specific genotype of the 
plant, as each genotype exhibits unique structural characteristics. To 
better understand the extent and variability of occlusion across different 
cultivars, the Ripe Berry Occlusion Ratio was calculated using Eq. (5).  
This metric provided a quantitative measure of the proportion of ripe 
berries that were occluded and, therefore, not visible in the captured 
images. The ripe berry occlusion ratio was determined for plants rep­
resenting six southern highbush blueberry cultivars, using image data 
collected during the 2023 growing season. This exploration aimed to 
systematically assess occlusion trends and establish a baseline for un­
derstanding how different genotypes influence the visibility of ripe 
berries under natural field conditions.
2.5. Android application development
An Android application was developed using the Android Studio 
software (version 2023.1.1) with Kotlin to allow timely yield and 
maturity monitoring under field conditions. The trained YOLOv8 model 
was first exported to ONNX format and subsequently converted to 
TensorFlow Lite (.tflite) using the TensorFlow converter to reduce model 
size and improve inference efficiency on mobile devices. The ‘.tflite’ 
model was integrated into the app using the TensorFlow Lite Interpreter, 
allowing on-device inference without internet connectivity. For optimal 
performance during real-world use, users are advised to capture images 
such that each frame contains only a single plant, which improves 
detection accuracy for estimating blueberries per plant. The app was 
tested on multiple Android smartphones (API level 24 and above). This 
mobile deployment supports in-field decision-making by providing 
rapid, accessible, and cultivar-independent yield insights.
3. Results
3.1. Key phenotypic data across datasets and genotypes
Phenotypic data exhibited large variability across different datasets 
and genotypes. Average plant yield ranged from 99.9 g (AU1, collected 
from Fairhope, AL in 2022) to 939.25 g (Jewel, collected from Lake 
Park, GA in 2023) (Table 1, Fig. 4). Average berry weight ranged from 
1.27 g (Premier, collected from Auburn, AL in 2022) to 2.9 g (AU8, 
collected from Fairhope, AL in 2022) (Table 1, Fig. 4). The substantial 
variability within this dataset, stemming from differences in genotype, 
plant age, management practices, and environmental conditions, is 
intentionally selected to facilitate the development of a robust yield 
prediction model applicable to various scenarios. Variations in yield and 
average berry weight may be influenced by both genotypic differences 
and environmental factors. For instance, the ‘Alapaha’, ‘Premier’, and 
‘Powderblue’ plants in Auburn, AL faced drought stress in 2022, 
resulting in lower yields and smaller berries compared to regular sea­
sons. Additionally, plants grown in Fairhope, AL were affected by a 
spring frost in 2022, resulting in a substantial decrease in yield. There­
fore, the focus of this study is not to compare the performance of 
different genotypes but rather to evaluate the accuracy of the yield 
prediction model across a variety of conditions.

detection dataset, the YOLOv8-x model, which has the largest number of 
trainable parameters, emerged as the top performer on the validation 
dataset. It achieved a mAP at an IoU threshold of 0.5 (mAP50) of 0.708. 
In contrast, with fewer parameters, the lightweight YOLOv8-n model 
achieved a slightly lower mAP50 score of 0.636. This trend illustrates 
that larger models generally result in better detection accuracy. How­
ever, the increase in accuracy is not strictly proportional to the model 
size, as the performance gains taper off with larger models. The model 
size increased significantly between YOLOv8-m (53 MB) and YOLOv8-x 
(140 MB), the mAP50 improvement was relatively modest (0.681 to 
0.708). For real-time applications where execution time is crucial, 
deploying the YOLOv8-s or YOLOv8-m models would be advantageous 
due to their faster inference times. However, for scenarios where 
achieving higher or more accurate blueberry detections is paramount, 
deploying the YOLOv8-l or YOLOv8-x models would be beneficial. The 
top-performing DL model, likely the YOLOv8-x, achieved an overall 
blueberry detection accuracy of 70.8 %. The model performed better in 
accurately detecting ripe berries, with an accuracy of 77 %, compared to 
64 % for unripe berries. The better performance in detecting ripe berries 
is potentially due to their distinct blue or black color in contrast with the 
background of leaves and soil, which the DL model could learn more 
easily. The similarity in color between green berries and leaves could 
have contributed to potential confusion for the DL models in detecting 
green berries accurately during testing. In the subsequent sections of the 
results, the YOLOv8-x model was utilized to generate predictions for 
visible berry counts and yield estimates, leveraging its superior perfor­
mance among the evaluated YOLOv8 versions.

distinct black/violet color, which contrasts with the green or red/pink 
color of the unripe berries. However, detecting unripe berries is often 
less accurate due to their color similarity to the leaves. There is a notable 
trade-off between the lightweight and the DL model, which is comprised 
of higher parameters leading to a larger model size than the prior model. 
While lighter models like the YOLOv8-n offer faster processing speeds, 
which is beneficial for real-time applications requiring lower latency, 
they often achieve lower accuracy than their heavier counterparts like 
the YOLOv8-x. Thus, the model choice depends on specific application 
needs, balancing accuracy and speed. Although slower, models 
requiring a higher number of trainable parameters tend to deliver more 
accurate results, making them suitable for applications where precision 
is prioritized over speed. This trade-off between speed and accuracy is 
crucial in deploying models in different operational contexts, especially 
when timely data processing is as important as detection accuracy. To 
develop reliable ML models for yield prediction tasks, we used the 
trained YOLOv8-x model to generate the count of ripe blueberries 
detected in the images which is used as one of the features to train the 
ML models as discussed in the earlier section.

identify ripe blueberries from field-acquired images. Importantly, these 
predictions were made on a dataset the model had not encountered 
during training, confirming its ability to generalize well to unseen data. 
This accomplishment is noteworthy, given the complexity of detecting 
blueberries in natural environments. Despite the small size of the 
berries, the model effectively identifies them, aided by an IoU threshold 
of 0.2, allowing for more flexible detection of smaller objects during 
analysis.

3.6. Smartphone android application
The Android application (app) was designed with a focus on user- 
friendliness. It guides the user through the process of selecting an 
image, which the integrated DL model then analyzes to detect blue­
berries and predict yield. The app interface is intuitive, allowing users, 
including farmers and agricultural professionals, to navigate and use the 
app’s features without needing technical expertise. The app’s workflow 
is detailed below with Fig. 16 showing the screenshot of each step: 
1. The user interface includes a button to "Select Image from gallery".
2. Users can choose an image from the smartphone’s camera gallery or 
import images from cloud platforms like Google Drive or Google 
Photos. Upon image selection, the embedded DL model runs in the 
background to detect the ripe and unripe (green and half) blueberries 
in the image, and the following steps are followed – 
• The original image is divided into sub-images of 224 × 224 pixels.
• The DL model performs predictions on all the sub-images. The ripe 
and unripe blueberries are detected, and the counts are accumu­
lated during the process.
3. The berry counts from all the sub-images are added and displayed to 
the user, along with a maturity index based on the ripe to unripe 
berry ratio.
4. The app features an "Enter average blueberry weight" button, 
allowing users to input the approximate/actual average berry weight 
for further analysis.
5. After inserting the average berry weight and receiving the berry 
count results, users can click "Calculate Yield" to view the yield 
prediction estimate generated by the ML model, based on the ripe 
berry count and the provided average berry weight. 
• The coefficients of the average berry weight and ripe berry count 
from the best-performing ML model were used in the smartphone 
app to calculate the final yield.
• This method ensures that the app’s predictions are reliable and 
based on real data and insights from the ML model’s analysis, of­
fering reliable yield estimates.
The Android application “Blueberry Yield Analyzer” is publicly 
available on the Google Play Store.

The YOLOv8 models, particularly the YOLOv8-x version, demon­

stages. With a mAP of 0.708 at 50 % overlap, this model effectively 
distinguished between ripe and unripe berries. The enhanced perfor­
mance in detecting ripe berries, characterized by distinct color differ­
ences compared to their unripe counterparts, highlights the potential of 
DL models in managing complex agricultural tasks. However, the ac­
curacy in detecting unripe berries was lower, likely due to the color 
similarity between green berries and the surrounding foliage. This lim­
itation indicates that further refinement in the model’s training process 
or additional image preprocessing techniques could enhance detection 
accuracy for less visually distinct blueberries. Additionally, occlusion 
remains a significant challenge in detecting blueberries. Blueberries 
hidden under the canopy, obscured by leaves and branches, pose a major 
obstacle, and their small size makes them difficult for the DL model to 
detect. Addressing these challenges is crucial for improving the model’s 
overall performance in real-world scenarios [30].
Yield prediction models based on genotype, average berry weight, 
and DL-derived berry count exhibited the highest accuracy, with R² 
values of up to 0.80 and a MAPE as low as 26.17 % compared to 
simplified models not considering genotype with R² values ranging from 
0.47 and a MAPE score of 53.19 %. The higher prediction accuracy of the 
full models can be attributed to the strong correlation between genotype 
and total yield in this dataset (r = 0.87), likely for two reasons. First, 
plants of the same genotype possess identical genomes, which contribute 
to their similar yield potential. Second, individuals of the same genotype 
were cultivated and managed under the same environmental conditions 
in this study, contributing to similar performance. The reliability of 
genotype as an indicator of yield may be somewhat limited when plants 
are cultivated under varying conditions or at different ages. However, 
our dataset presents limitations to assess genotype and environment 
interactions because of the lack of overlapping genotypes across the 
different environments. Future development genotype-specific yield 
prediction models will benefit from including phenotypic data from a 
wide range of environmental conditions and different growth stages.
ML models trained without genotype information provides a more 
practical solution for yield prediction when prior knowledge of the 
cultivar or genotype is not available. However, the limited accuracy of 
these simplified models presents a challenge for precise yield prediction. 
This limitation is primarily due to difficulties of accurately estimating 
the occlusion ratio. The degree of occlusion varied greatly across culti­
vars, which might be influenced by plant architecture, berry cluster 
type, plant age, maturity level, and environmental conditions. Plants 
with dense foliage and tighter clusters could lead to greater occlusion, 
while those with fewer leaves and/or more exposed clusters allow for 
better detection. To better estimate occlusion ratios, future studies 
should incorporate a broader range of genotypes and maturity levels, 
along with a more in-depth analysis of plant architecture affecting berry 
occlusion. Additionally, it would be beneficial to investigate if addi­
tional features such as bush density and berry distribution can be 
extracted from the images to better predict occlusion ratios.
Berry detection models developed in this study also allowed maturity 
estimation, which showed a similar but slightly lower mAP50 score 
compared to a previous study on wild blueberry maturity estimation.

maturity stages of wild blueberries, where yield was determined based 
on the weight in kilograms per hectare. The research primarily focused 
on detecting ripeness stages and estimating yield on a smaller scale, 
targeting individual quadrants within fields. Their best model, 
YOLOv4-small, achieved a mAP50 of 79.79 % for the three-class model

research adopts a more accessible single-view approach using smart­
phone cameras, making it practical for real-world applications. 
Although our model’s mAP50 score is slightly lower at 70 %, it differ­
entiates between ripe and unripe berries, providing clearer insights into 
yield prediction and plant maturity. By integrating genotype informa­
tion, which strongly correlates with yield (R² of 0.80), and specifically 
targeting ripe berries, our model provides more accurate yield pre­
dictions and clearer maturity assessments. Our model balances accuracy 
with practicality by leveraging DL-derived berry counts and simple 
phenotypic traits like average berry weight to offer a scalable and easily 
deployable solution for blueberry yield prediction through an Android 
application, making it suitable for diverse agricultural settings.
Several yield prediction models for wild blueberries have been 
developed, often relying on simulated data to train ML algorithms. A 
study conducted by Seireg et al. [32], utilized ensemble ML techniques 
like stacking and cascading regression combined with computer simu­
lation data to predict wild blueberry yield. Their research demonstrates 
a high predictive accuracy (R² of 0.985) using simulation data combined 
with various meteorological and biological factors, achieving high per­
formance through advanced ML models. In another research conducted 
by Obsie et al. [33], bee species composition and weather information 
were used to train ensemble ML algorithms for wild blueberry yield 
prediction using simulated data, which also achieved a high prediction 
accuracy (R² of 0.938). However, model validation with real data would 
be helpful to ensure its effectiveness in field applications.
Investigation of blueberry detection patterns exposed notable 
genotype-dependent variations in occlusion and fruit visibility, 
emphasizing the influence of cultivar-specific plant architecture on 
detection accuracy. The relationship between occlusion ratio and visible 
berry count showed distinct negative correlations across cultivars, 
indicating that increased plant foliage affects berry visibility differently 
among cultivars especially in mature plants. These cultivar-specific oc­
clusion patterns proved vital for yield estimation, as demonstrated by 
our prediction models: incorporating genotype as a feature significantly 
improved model performance (R² = 0.80) compared to models based 
solely on visible berry count and average berry weight (R² = 0.47). This 
substantial improvement highlights how architectural traits unique to 
each cultivar influence berry occlusion and, consequently, the accuracy 
of computer vision-based yield estimation systems. Interestingly, the 
simplified models that excluded genotype underperformed relative to 
using the image-derived ripe berry count alone r = 0.77 (Fig. 7), which 
would yield an expected R² ≈0.59. This finding further reinforces the 
critical role of genotype in capturing underlying structural traits such as 
occlusion, density, and fruiting patterns, which strongly determine yield 
outcomes. For practical yield prediction systems, however, end-users 
often lack prior knowledge of cultivar identity. Thus, developing tools 
to automate genotype prediction becomes essential for broader appli­
cability of such models. Future research could incorporate depth esti­
mation approaches, as proposed by [34,35] to better characterize 
canopy structure and occlusion patterns, potentially enabling automated 
genotype identification and further improving yield prediction accuracy 
across different blueberry cultivars.

mance in detecting blueberries, but several challenges were encountered 
while deploying it on an Android app. The necessary conversion to a ‘.

Furthermore, our initial ML models for yield prediction incorporated 
genotype as a key feature alongside other phenotypic traits, leading to 
high accuracy. However, recognizing that genotype information is often 
unavailable in practical scenarios, we developed an alternative model 
using only two features: ‘average berry weight’ and ‘DL-derived berry 
count’. This model, trained using the SIWP strategy, was ultimately 
deployed on the Android app. The ‘LGBM Regressor’ emerged as the best 
performer among the various ML models trained using this strategy. Its 
ability to capture non-linear relationships between berry weight, berry 
count, and yield made it particularly well-suited to the problem. This 
deployment illustrates how advanced ML models can be effectively 
translated into real-world tools, ensuring growers benefit from accurate 
yield predictions in practical field conditions.
The visual differences across blueberry cultivars align with the 
cultivar-specific patterns of berry occlusion observed in our study. 
Various occlusion ratios could be due to plant architecture, berry cluster 
structure, leaf density, and maturity level. ‘Cielo’ plants displayed an 
open, uniform canopy structure, contributing to its lower occlusion 
ratio. In contrast, ‘Springhigh’, ‘Farthing’ and ‘Jewel’ plants had denser, 
more complex canopies, corresponding to their higher occlusion levels. 
These architectural variations directly impact berry visibility, posing 
challenges for automated detection using DL-based blueberry detection 
models. Cultivars with higher occlusion, present increased difficulty in 
accurately identifying and counting berries, as many may be obscured. 
This finding underscores the importance of incorporating genotypic 
differences in plant architecture when developing yield prediction 
models. Understanding the relationships between genotype, architec­
ture, and occlusion, researchers and growers can optimize yield esti­
mation systems to the unique traits of each cultivar. This knowledge can 
lead to more reliable predictions, supporting informed decision-making 
and improving blueberry production efficiency.
Expanding the dataset with images from different blueberry cultivars 
could enhance the training process by exposing the DL model to a wider 
variety of data scenarios. This exposure is crucial because it trains the 
model to recognize and adapt to the natural variations within the spe­
cies, such as variations due to geographic, climatic, or genetic differ­
ences. Each cultivar may present unique challenges in terms of berry 
appearance some might be clustered more closely, others might have 
varying hues at maturity, and some might be obscured by foliage 
differently based on their growth habits. Furthermore, a diverse dataset 
also helps mitigate the risk of overfitting, which occurs when a model 
becomes too finely tuned to the specific characteristics of its training 
data. By training on a broader set of examples, the model learns to 
identify the essential features that truly matter for detection and clas­
sification, making it more reliable [8]. Enhancing the dataset’s diversity 
can improve the model’s utility across agricultural practices and man­
agement scenarios. For instance, blueberry farmers worldwide could 
confidently utilize such a model, knowing it has been validated across 
various conditions and cultivar characteristics.
In reference to the smartphone application development, expanding 
the app’s availability to iOS users by creating a similar application for 
Apple devices like iPhone and iPad can significantly increase its acces­
sibility and usability. This move would allow a wider audience to benefit 
from the technology, potentially reaching more farmers and agricultural 
professionals who use Apple devices. Developing an iOS version of the 
app could also open additional market opportunities and enhance user 
engagement by providing a platform-specific user experience that caters 
to the iOS ecosystem.
5. Conclusion
Rapid blueberry yield monitoring is essential for growers seeking to 
optimize production and marketing strategies and for breeders to 
improve selection efficiency. The complexity of yield prediction arises 
from challenges such as occlusion, small berry size, and variability in 
berry shape and color. This study demonstrates the potential of inte­
grating DL-based blueberry detection with key crop traits for improved 
yield prediction modeling. Although occlusion remains a significant 
challenge for image-based berry detection, our findings provide insights 
into how cultivar-specific characteristics influence blueberry yield pre­
diction accuracy. To enhance prediction performance beyond computer 
vision alone, ML algorithms were trained using the berry count pre­
dicted by the DL model, along with average berry weight, and/or ge­
notype, to further improve the accuracy of yield under real-world field 
conditions. While genotype was found to be a critical feature for accu­
rate yield prediction, future research will explore image-derived plant 
architectural features to estimate berry occlusion and substitute geno­
type for better generalizability. Finally, the trained DL model was 
deployed on a custom-built, user-friendly Android application, serving 
as a practical tool for end-users, including growers and breeders, to 
streamline tasks related to yield prediction and maturity assessment in 
blueberry.
