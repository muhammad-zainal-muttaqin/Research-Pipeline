Abstract — The significance of palm oil, which contributes 
30 % of the world's total vegetable oil production, cannot be 
overstated. Its numerous applications, ranging from soap to 
cosmetics, have increased demand, thereby increasing the 
importance of yield management. Human graders have 
traditionally been responsible for determining the ripeness of oil 
palm fresh fruit bunches (FFBs), a task upon which the oil 
extraction rate (OER) relies heavily. This rate has significant 
economic implications: a 0.13 % drop in OER due to unripe 
fruits can result in a staggering RM 340 million loss. Precision 
is stressed, prompting automated detection research. Computer 
vision and Artificial Intelligence are becoming more effective at 
assessing oil palm fruit ripeness. However, many methods 
require complex operations, controlled settings, or manual 
calibrations. Although innovative, microwave sensors and 
inductive techniques have drawbacks like sample preparation 
and equipment dependence. This study investigates the potential 
of the YOLOv8 framework, particularly its YOLOv8m variant, 
for ripeness classification and detection. This model's mAP50-
95 of 0.927 balances computational efficiency and accuracy, 
indicating its potential to revolutionize the palm oil industry's 
fruit assessment procedures. The findings here shed light on the 
model's efficacy and highlight its potential as an industry-
standard solution, bridging gaps in ripeness detection 
methodologies. 
Keywords—palm oil ripeness, object detection, computer 
vision, deep learning, YOLOv8. 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
Malaysia is one of the world's leading palm oil producers, 
and the industry significantly impacts the country's GDP. The 
industry's future is bright, with palm oil companies owning 
more than a million hectares of land designated for producing 
fresh fruit bunches (FFBs). Effective oil extraction depends on 
carefully selecting ripe FFBs per Malaysian Palm Oil Board 
standards (MPOB). FFBs are harvested primarily from mature 
oil palm trees three years after planting. Field workers identify 
ripe FFBs based on visual cues such as their color, detached 
fruitlets, and formed sockets during the 10–14 day harvest 
interval [1]. Once harvested, these FFBs are typically sent for 
processing 
within 
24 
hours; 
however, 
unforeseen 
circumstances can occasionally cause this to be delayed, 
compromising the quality of the oil. 
The oil palm industry heavily relies on technological 
advances to combat labor shortages and improve productivity. 
Implementing autonomous mobile robots for FFB harvesting 
is gaining traction to maintain and possibly increase 
productivity. This technology addresses labor constraints and 
reduces human errors that could compromise production 
efficiency. The most significant difficulty in developing an 
autonomous FFB harvesting system is creating a mechanism 
that can objectively and precisely identify FFBs that are ripe. 
Over the past decade, researchers have developed various 
techniques to determine the maturity of FFBs, allowing for 
optimal harvesting times. 
Several techniques have been proposed, with computer 
vision emerging as a primary method for determining 
ripeness. This technology and artificial intelligence provide a 
nuanced evaluation of palm fruit ripeness stages [2, 3]. 
However, 
conventional 
systems 
necessitate 
intricate 
procedures, 
controlled 
environments, 
and 
manual 
interventions for calibration [4, 5]. Other proposed methods, 
such as microwave sensors and inductive methodologies, 
come with challenges, including laborious sample preparation 
and the need for specific equipment [6, 7]. 
The You Only Look Once (YOLO) architecture has 
emerged as a pivotal tool in computer vision, especially 
concerning object detection tasks [8]. When applied to palm 
oil, YOLO and its subsequent versions have provided 
groundbreaking solutions in detecting and categorizing the 
ripeness stages of palm fruits [9]. While traditional methods 
heavily rely on human graders to visually determine ripeness, 
which is both time-consuming and subject to error, YOLO 
offers an automated, fast, and efficient approach. As the 
versions of YOLO have evolved, improvements in detection 
speed, accuracy, and the ability to recognize minute 
differences in fruit color and texture have emerged. This 
progression enables palm oil producers to make accurate 
harvest decisions, optimize the oil extraction rate, and enhance 
the industry's productivity and sustainability. Leveraging 
YOLO's real-time processing capabilities, stakeholders in the 
palm oil industry are now better equipped to streamline their 
operations and achieve more consistent outcomes in fruit 
ripeness categorization. 
Despite advancements in computer vision for agricultural 
applications, there remains a significant research gap in 
identifying and classifying palm fruit ripeness stages using 
cutting-edge detection systems. Despite their success to some 
degree, previous models frequently encountered difficulties in 
real-time processing, precision, and adaptability to diverse 
plantation environments. The primary objective of this 
research is to exploit the capabilities of the most recent 
YOLOv8 architecture to address this deficiency. By 
integrating YOLOv8, we hope to achieve improved accuracy, 
faster detection speeds, and more reliable categorization of 
ripe palm fruit. This research aims to fill a research void and

II. 
PALM FRUIT RIPENESS DATASET 
We used the dataset published in [10] for our research, as 
it combines multiple datasets into one comprehensive dataset. 
Traditionally, computer vision has focused on the color 
detection of individual fruits, while light sensor research has 
analyzed the spectrum emitted by the fruit. Prior research 
focused primarily on single image datasets or the color 
spectrum, employing diverse techniques such as SVM and 
YOLOv4. The multimodal dataset of videos and images of oil 
palm fresh fruit bunches at six validated maturity levels is 
novel to this paper. This large dataset helps application 
developers and agronomists create practical machine learning 
and deep learning models for smartphone apps and robotics. It 
addresses video issues like dynamic luminance and obscured 
objects for real-world use. Palm oil mills need consistent palm 
fruit maturity assessment to reduce errors and losses.

transforming raw data captured in mp4 format at a resolution 
of 1280×720 on a smartphone into data that deep learning 
models can use. The unprocessed video data were converted 
into sequential images by extracting frames every second, 
resulting in images with a resolution of 416×416. DarkLabel, 
a tool designed for annotating object detection, was used to 
annotate these images with bounding boxes. Each image's 
YOLO annotation contained information about the class and 
coordinates of the object it depicted. Two types of data were 
compiled: video datasets and image datasets. The video 
datasets included recordings displaying a 360° rotation of oil 
palm FFBs lasting 10 to 15 seconds. From these videos, 4160 
images were extracted, containing 14559 objects within 7171

III. 
YOLOV8 ARCHITECTURES 
YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a well-known object 
detection and image segmentation model developed at the 
University of Washington in 2015 by Joseph Redmon and Ali 
Farhadi. YOLOv2 introduced batch normalization and anchor 
boxes in 2016, while YOLOv3, released in 2018, incorporated 
an improved backbone network and spatial pyramid pooling. 
2020's YOLOv4 introduced Mosaic data enhancement and a 
redesigned detection head. Following the adoption of 
YOLOv6 by Meituan for their autonomous delivery robots, 
YOLOv5 featured hyperparameter optimization and export 
flexibility. YOLOv7 commenced pose estimation. YOLOv8 
by Ultralytics is a state-of-the-art model encompassing 
detection, segmentation, pose estimation, tracking, and 
classification, demonstrating its adaptability for a wide range 
of vision AI applications [8]. 
Ultralytics' most recent state-of-the-art model, YOLOv8, 
as shown in Fig. 2, was designed for object detection, image 
classification, and instance segmentation. As successors to the 
influential 
YOLOv5, 
YOLOv8 
introduces 
several 
architectural 
improvements 
and 
developer-focused 
enhancements. Even though it is currently undergoing active 
development, with Ultralytics continuously refining and 
enhancing its features in response to community feedback, it 
already has impressive credentials. Specifically, as indicated 
by COCO metrics, YOLOv8 is more accurate than YOLOv5 
in evaluations using the Roboflow 100 dataset. Notable 
characteristics of the model include an intuitive command-line 
interface and a streamlined Python package designed to 
simplify the developer experience. Its large and expanding 
community ensures developers can quickly locate guidance 
and assistance for their computer vision projects. 
Ultralytics has released various pretrained YOLOv8 
models on COCO datasets, each varying in performance and 
computational metrics. While all these models are optimized 
for a size of 640 pixels, their performance and speed metrics 
differ. Specifically, YOLOv8n has a mAPval of 37.3 with a 
speed of 80.4ms on a CPU ONNX and 0.99ms on an A100 
TensorRT, having 3.2 million parameters and 8.7 billion 
FLOPs. YOLOv8s registers a mAPval of 44.9, YOLOv8m 
achieves 50.2, YOLOv8l scores 52.9, and YOLOv8x tops 
with 53.9. Their speeds and computational complexities 
increase respectively, with YOLOv8x being the most 
resource-intensive at 479.1ms on CPU ONNX, 3.53ms on 
A100 TensorRT, boasting 68.2 million parameters, and 257.8 
billion FLOPs. These models are pretrained on the COCO 
dataset for detection, segmentation, and pose estimation tasks, 
while classification models utilize the ImageNet dataset. 
IV. 
IMPLEMENTATION AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 
We utilized a collection of tools and libraries for the 
software implementation to ensure experiment robustness and 
efficacy. Our codebase was founded on Python version 3.9.16. 
For image processing and computer vision tasks, version 4.6.0 
of OpenCV was used. PyTorch, version 2.0.1, was the primary 
framework for deep learning utilized for the project. We 
utilized version 11.8 of the PyTorch-CUDA package for GPU 
acceleration and improved computational performance. 
Furthermore, we incorporated torchvision version 0.15.2 for 
various vision-related utilities and pretrained models. For the 
software stack to work efficiently with the YOLO architecture 
and its variants, the Ultralytics package, version 8.0.180, was 
crucial.

computational resources are frequently in short supply.
